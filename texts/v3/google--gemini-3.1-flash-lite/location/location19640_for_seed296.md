The city of Oretide is not built *on* the mountain; it is the mountain, eroded into a skeletal, vertical metropolis that has been cooling for eons. It perches at a staggering elevation, where the air is thin, silver-grey, and perpetually damp. The geography has reached a state of "primordial stability"—the cliffs here are so ancient that they have been worn down into soft, rounded geometry, like the knuckles of a giant, covered in a velvet skin of moss and lichen.

Everything is dense. Buildings are not merely stacked; they are grafted onto the stone. The architecture is a frantic, multi-layered sprawl of iron gantries, rusted copper piping, and basalt-hewn chambers. There is no clear distinction between the geology and the machinery; steam-vents puff rhythmically from fissures in the rock, and the rhythmic *thrum-thrum* of deep-earth turbines mimics the heartbeat of the peaks.

The light is a permanent, bruised twilight. Because of the elevation and the constant condensation—a fine, cold mist that clings to every surface—there is no direct sunlight. Instead, illumination comes from the "phosphor-pipes." These are long, glass-encased conduits snaking through the city's alleyways, filled with a viscous, luminescent algae that glows with a dim, sickly-green hue. It creates an environment of shadows and shifting, oily reflections.

The ecology is quiet, almost reverent. Because the environment is so demanding, life has retreated into the architecture. Lush, hanging gardens of hardy alpine ferns and bioluminescent ivy choke the narrow walkways, drinking deeply from the mist. Fauna is rare and strange: you might see "Glass-Skimmers," insects with translucent wings that vibrate at a frequency just barely audible to the human ear, darting between the steam-vents to collect the mineral-rich condensation. Or perhaps a scavenger-cat, its fur matted with silver damp, moving with a fluid, liquid silence across the wet iron gantries.

The city is active, though it moves with the slow, deliberate pace of a machine that has been running for a thousand years. It is a place of *maintenance*. The soundscape is dominated by the hiss of condensation being drawn into filtration stacks, the groan of ancient metal thermal-expanding under the weight of the cooling mist, and the intermittent drip-drip-drip of water coalescing on high-altitude spires.

There are rules here, etched into the very rust: *Keep the pipes clear. Respect the moss. Never extinguish the green light.* 

Oretide feels like the end of a long, industrial dream. It is a city that has stopped expanding upward and has begun to grow inward, folding its own debris into its foundation. Here, the boundary between the natural world and the industrial process has frayed away; the moss eats the rust, the rust feeds the iron, and the river of condensation flows through the middle of houses as if it were a natural tributary of the street. It is a breathless, cooling, and deeply settled place—a monument to the final state of all things.
