The air here is neither gas nor liquid, but a dry, heat-shimmering aerosol of suspended conductive dust. There is no water; the chemistry of this world is built on high-energy polymers and lattice-structured silicates that pulse with the rhythm of distant, cosmic machinery.

You stand in a vast, low-lying basin where the horizon does not curve—it simply repeats. The terrain is composed of crushed white bismuth and jagged, freshly shattered obsidian glass. Because the erosion is minimal, the landscape feels unnervingly new, as if it were extruded from the crust only moments ago. Every ridge is a razor; every crevice is a clean, sharp geometry that has never known the softening touch of wind or rain.

Despite the total lack of water, the land is lush. It is choked with "vegetation" that is not biological in the terrestrial sense. Great forests of fractal antennas—bronze-colored, branching stalks that thrum with a low, sub-audible vibration—crowd the lowlands. These structures spiral upward, leaves blooming into intricate, iridescent geometric shielding that catches the brilliance of the high, white light above. The density is stifling; these metallic thickets are packed so tightly you must weave through the gaps, your skin humming as you brush against stalks that hold enough localized computational power to map a star system.

There are no buildings, no ruins, no footprints. Yet, the tech level is absolute. The ground itself is "smart," a programmable substrate of non-local matter. If you look closely at the white bismuth beneath your boots, you realize the pebbles are shifting to accommodate your weight not through friction, but through quantum realignment. The reality here is breaking down; in the periphery of your vision, the forest seems to fold in on itself, creating impossible corridors that loop back to your starting coordinates. 

There are no animals—at least, nothing that breathes or bleeds. Occasionally, a shadow detaches itself from a cluster of fractal stalks, a glitch in the light where the world’s rendering seems to falter. It lingers for a pulse, a flicker of non-Euclidean geometry, before the local physics snaps back into place, smoothing the anomaly away.

The atmosphere is profoundly still. There is no weather, no breeze, no seasons. It is a frozen equilibrium, a static masterpiece of high-tech permanence. The only sound is the occasional *click-tink* of a falling metallic shard, a sound so lonely and precise that it feels like a period at the end of a sentence that has been running for a thousand years. Everything is waiting, held in a state of eternal, saturated, and terrifyingly efficient potential. You are walking through the interior of a machine that has forgotten it is being used.
