You are standing inside a throat of basalt, a vertical chimney carved into the highest, most jagged reach of an unnamed range. The air is perpetually heavy, not with rain, but with a lingering, humid metallic dew that coats every surface in a slick, cold film. It is a place of needle-sharp crags, fresh and untempered by time. The rocks here are not rounded by eons of wind; they are crystalline, thrust upward in geometric blades that look like shattered teeth.

There is no sky here, only the suffocating pressure of rock pressing in from all sides. At this scale, the world is a single, winding atrium of obsidian and grey slate, no larger than the nave of a great, collapsed cathedral. 

It is dark, but the darkness is crowded. This is a place of staggering biological density. 

The walls are not bare; they are quilted with life. A specific, velvet-black moss thrives in the near-total dark, pulsing with a faint, bioluminescent violet rhythm that beats once every few minutes. Thick, fleshy tubers sprout from the crevices of the sharp rock-needles, dripping a viscous, translucent fluid that smells of ozone and wet soil. Every square inch of the terrain is occupied by a layer of clinging, huddled biology—a carpet of overlapping, sensitive organisms that react to the tremor of your heartbeat.

The fauna is overwhelming. You are not alone, though "not alone" is a violent understatement. The space is filled with thousands of chitinous, multi-legged skitterers, the size of human palms, which cling to the walls like living wallpaper. They do not walk; they shimmer, vibrating in place. Their collective sound is a low-frequency, dissonant hum, a mechanical grinding like stones rubbed together under water, loud enough to rattle the fillings in your teeth. They are primitive, rhythmic, and utterly indifferent to you.

The air is cold—biting, thin, and damp—keeping the metabolism of the space at a crawl. Events transpire here with glacial patience. You watch a bloom of pale, ghostly fungi unfurl over the course of hours, a slow, hypnotic unfolding of translucent petals that weep tiny, glowing spores. 

Because the erosion is so young, the landscape feels unfinished, as if the mountain sprouted in a frantic, tectonic outburst only yesterday. There are no paths, no artifacts, no legacy of mind. There is only the *huddle*. 

If you reach out to touch the wall, the creatures do not flee; they simply tighten their grip, a million tiny legs bracing against the rock. There is a strange, wet suck of suction cups against stone. The atmosphere is thick with the smell of wet mineral and the sharp, ammonia-rich scent of the fauna. Everything is held in a state of precarious, frozen excitement, as if the entire room is a single coiled muscle, waiting for a prompt that has not arrived for ten thousand years. 

You feel the scale of it: a small, dark, and crowded pocket of the world, suspended at an impossible height, vibrating with a life that doesn't know what light is, but knows exactly where you are standing.
