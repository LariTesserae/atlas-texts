The air at this altitude is a physical weight, thick with the smell of scorched ozone and wet sulfur. You are standing on a serrated spine of obsidian and basalt, thousands of meters above the choked, hazy basins of the lowlands. The heat here is not a gentle warmth; it is a radiant, vibrating pressure that bleeds up from the rock itself, constant and unforgiving.

To call it a "mountain" is to miss the point. It is a vertical factory, a calcified geography of industry. 

Everything is packed tight. There is no empty space here; every square centimeter of the dark, weathered rock is occupied by colonies of bioluminescent moss that pulsate in a rhythm slightly out of sync with the wind. Between these organic mats, the terrain is pitted with the evidence of a deep, ancient industriousness. There are rusted brass conduits, half-fused into the stone, that act as natural cooling vents for the planet’s core; they hiss periodically, spitting thin, superheated jets of steam that turn immediately to condensation on the cooler, shadowed underside of the cliffs.

Because the heat is so intense, the water does not flow in rivers. It *migrates*. You see it in the way the moisture clings to the machinery embedded in the rock—tiny, sweating droplets that bead on polished copper surfaces before being pulled toward the next rusted junction. It is a landscape that sweats.

The life here is hardy and specialized. You hear the rhythmic *clack-whirr* of the "Crawler-Crabs"—crustacean-like scavengers with shells that look suspiciously like riveted sheet metal. They move in swarms across the vertical faces, their pincers busy stripping oxidized minerals from the defunct industrial remnants that litter the slope. They are the primary custodians of this place, keeping the machinery clear of debris. Occasionally, a larger predator—a wingless, leathery thing that glides on the thermal updrafts—will dive beneath the cloud line, its screech muffled by the sheer thickness of the atmosphere.

The built environment is sparse, composed of lonely, skeletal structures: a lone observation tower that leans precariously over an abyss, or a cluster of corroded silos that once held fuels for forgotten engines. They are not ruins in the sense of abandonment; they are components of a machine that never fully turned off. You can feel the vibration through your boots, the low-frequency hum of a central engine still laboring somewhere deep beneath the crust, processing God-knows-what, feeding the heat that keeps the moss alive.

Despite the industrial presence, the world feels uncannily quiet. The sounds are granular—the ticking of cooling metal, the wet suction of the Crawlers moving over damp stone, the soft *hiss* of steam. There is no wind howling, only the heavy, persistent movement of hot air rising toward the blue-white sky. 

You feel exposed, yet constrained. There is nowhere to hide in such a dense, vertical theatre. Every movement you make is observed by the millions of small, scurrying lives that inhabit these cliffs. You are merely one more artifact in a landscape that has long ago settled into a balance between the mechanical and the biological, a world that has decided it has no need for humans to keep the gears turning.
