This is the High Plateau of Oros, a slab of continental crust so ancient it has forgotten how to be stone. It is a place of profound, primordial exhaustion, where the geography has been carved away by eons of invisible friction until the peaks are no longer jagged, but rounded into the soft, slumped shapes of sleeping giants.

The air here is punishingly cold, a thin, crystalline chill that tastes of ozone and stagnant frost. It is a vast, horizontal world; the horizon line is so far away that it curves beneath the weight of its own emptiness. Because the elevation is extreme, the atmosphere is thin, yet it remains perpetually damp. Clouds do not drift above the surface here; they *are* the surface. A low, grey condensation clings to the low-lying basins, clinging to the lichen-slicked basalt like a cold, wet shroud.

There is no animal life. Not a bird, not an insect, not a beetle. The silence is absolute, broken only by the rhythmic, geological *thrum* of the landscape’s machinery. It is a place of active, rhythmic decay. 

The vegetation, unexpectedly lush, is not green. It is a riot of violet, slate-grey, and translucent white fungi that have colonised every inch of the ancient, flattened stone. These are not plants in the traditional sense, but massive, slow-growing colonial growths—some reaching the size of small villas—that pulse with a faint, bioluminescent dimness. They drink the condensation directly from the air, swelling and contracting in a cycle of hours, exhaling heavy, freezing spores that hang in the breathless air like glitter.

Nestled within these sprawling fungal colonies are the Cities of the Scrim. They are not built upon the land, but *grown* into the eroded valleys. The architecture is a marvel of primitive, manual labor: structures fashioned from dried, resin-hardened fungal stalks and smoothed river stones, stacked in precarious, spiraling towers that defy traditional geometry. There is a sense of "urban density" here, yet it is profoundly sparse; whole districts consist of fifty-story towers separated by miles of barren, wind-scoured scree. The inhabitants—entities of woven fiber and articulated shadow—move with a deliberate, haunting slowness. They are the tenders of the erosion.

The reality of this place is brittle and prone to shattering. Because the plateau is so ancient and the physics so stretched, the "Rules" often fail. In the deep depressions—the places where the erosion has carved perfect, circular bowls into the bedrock—space itself sometimes folds. You might walk toward a cluster of white-stalked towers, only to find yourself stepping out of a shadow miles behind your starting point. Time does not flow; it leaches out of the ground like moisture. One might watch a fungal bloom unfold over the course of a day, only to realize, upon looking back, that the structure has been there for a thousand years, or perhaps, only since the last pulse of the wind.

There is no sun, yet the light is constant—a diffuse, milky radiance emanating from the sky itself. It catches the moisture on the stones, turning the entire plateau into a field of flickering mirrors. 

Everything here is in a state of finality. The mountains are finished with their climbing; the rivers have long since vanished, leaving behind vein-like scars filled with pockets of permafrost and stagnant, mineral-heavy water. It is a world that has reached the end of its history, and yet, because it is trapped in this state of perpetual, active weirdness, it refuses to go quiet. The silence is not an absence of sound; it is a waiting. It is the sound of a world that is so heavily settled into its own form that it has begun to dream of landscapes that never existed.
