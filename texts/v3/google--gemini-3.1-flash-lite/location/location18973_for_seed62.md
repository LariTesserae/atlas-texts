The air here is held in a state of suspended condensation. It is cool—a pervasive, mountain-chill that clings to the coat—but the light is brilliant, refracting through the endless microscopic beads of moisture that hang in the atmosphere like static diamonds. Because the humidity exists as a suspension rather than a downpour, everything is perpetually slick, a world wrapped in a glistening, pearlescent film.

This is a landscape of sharp edges and fresh fractures. The mountains are tectonic infants, thrusting upward in jagged, unweathered blades of obsidian and basalt. There is no softness in the stone; it has not yet been humbled by wind or water. The geography is regional, a vast, terraced bowl of high-altitude basins that stretch toward the horizon, dense with a verdant, almost aggressive botany.

The flora here is riotous. Ferns with translucent, succulent fronds grow in such tight, overlapping layers that they form a subterranean floor of green scales. Every vertical surface dripped with moss that pulses with a faint, bioluminescent electric blue—a parasitic shimmer that seems to draw its energy from the metallic minerals in the rock.

Yet, pushing through this biological wealth is the cold, unyielding geometry of industry. Scattered throughout the basin are the "Foundries of the Drift." These are not sprawling cities, but solitary, vertical structures of blackened steel and pressurized copper piping, piercing the canopy like needles. They sit silent, mostly. There is no roaring of furnaces or howling of steam whistles. Instead, the industrial presence feels like a long-forgotten mechanism, a clock frozen mid-tick. 

Occasionally, a heavy piston—the size of a silo—will descend a fraction of an inch into the ground, driven by some deep-earth slow-cycle motor that operates on a timescale of days rather than seconds. The sound is not a noise, but a vibration—a low-frequency thrum that you feel in the marrow of your teeth, barely audible, as if the planet is groaning under the sheer weight of being redesigned.

There is a distinct lack of fauna. One might expect birds in such high, lush greenery, but there is only the occasional, heavy-bodied insect, armored in iridescent chitin, clinging to a steam valve. They move with crystalline precision, never startling. They do not flee because the concept of "threat" does not exist in a silence this profound.

The rules of this world are dictated by the moisture and the machinery. The condensation acts as a superconductor; the electric glow of the moss and the faint, flickering light-strips along the industrial monoliths suggest that the entire basin is a circuit. You walk delicately, mindful that the lush moss is merely a layer of carpet over deep, complex tangles of wire and brass tubing. 

It is a place of slow, patient synthesis—where the rock is being ground down by the earth’s own quiet hunger, filtered through the pipes of abandoned engines, and turned into something else, something harder, something greener. The process is so gradual that to witness it is to witness nothing at all, only the stillness of a world that is quietly, methodically, finishing its own construction.
