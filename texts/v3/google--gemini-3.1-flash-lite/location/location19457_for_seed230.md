This is the city of Oakhaven-on-the-Crag, a place that does not grow, does not decay, and does not welcome the footfall of the hurried.

The settlement is perched at a high, thin-aired elevation, nestled into a natural horseshoe amphitheater of weathered, pale-grey schist. The scale is intimate—the entire town could be circumnavigated in ten minutes of walking. It is a place of permanent equilibrium; the stone has been shaped by ancestral hands into rounded, overlapping tiers, and here the erosion has ceased. It is as if the wind itself decided, centuries ago, that this arrangement of rock and timber was the final, optimal state of the mountain.

The air is cool, hanging in a state of suspended dampness. It is not exactly rainy, but the atmosphere is perpetually heavy with a fine, silver-grey condensate. Water does not flow in streams here; instead, it beads upon the smooth, lichen-speckled masonry of the dwelling-hives. Every surface feels slick, cold to the touch, and perpetually refreshed by a mist that neither intensifies nor dissipates.

The architecture is the dominant feature. It is a dense, layered hive of dry-stone walls and rough-hewn timber rafters, all built without mortar. The masonry is primitive but meticulous, possessing the weight of permanence. Staircases carved directly into the living granite connect one level of the city to another, their edges softened by centuries of soft friction, though no one is seen polishing them. There are no straight lines here, only the stubborn, curvaceous geometry of stones that have finally settled into their hollows.

Vegetated life is held in a state of near-absence. Sparse, wiry tufts of desaturated moss cling to the northern faces of the huts, and here and there, a solitary, stunted mountain-fern emerges from a crevice. These plants do not rustle; they are stiff, existing as part of the geography rather than as living, growing things. There is no fauna to be seen—no birds in the pale sky, no insects in the damp air. The silence is profound, broken only by the occasional *tock* of a cold droplet hitting a slate roof, followed by the long, hollow sound of water sliding down a gutter worn smooth by antiquity.

Everything operates on a scale of profound stillness. The inhabitants, if one catches a glimpse of them, are few and scattered. They move with the fluid, calculated economy of creatures who know that the setting does not reward agitation. They carry simple tools of wood and polished bone, shaping things only to repair the slow wear of the centuries, never to add or expand. 

There is a sense of "held breath" to the light—it is clear, diffuse, and steady. It reveals the cracks in the stone and the texture of the moss with clinical, unchanging clarity. One feels that to shout would be a fundamental error in etiquette, not because anyone would be offended, but because the landscape is perfectly tuned to its own quiet frequency, and a discordant sound might simply vanish into the stone, leaving no echo behind. 

Time here is not a river; it is a stagnant pool. The city does not wait for morning or dread the night; it exists in a state of static grace, a carved, damp, and lonely monument to the idea that some places, once finished, should never change again.
