The horizon does not sit still, though the land itself is ancient, weathered into submission by a sun that never tilts. This is the High Forge—a plateau of jagged, oxidized obsidian and crushed copper ore, suspended miles above some impossible, lightless sea. There is no water here; the chemistry of the world is purely metallic and thermal. The air is a thin, dry rasp of ionized dust, shimmering with a permanent, aggressive heat that warps the perspective of the vast, open plains.

Across this gargantuan expanse, there is an crushing density of life that defies standard biology. These are not beasts of flesh, but of clockwork, filament, and volcanic glass—the "Inhabitants of the Gear." Millions of them navigate the terrain, chittering in high-frequency metallic drones that vibrate the very bones. They are the fauna of the forge, vast shoals of insectoid constructs that move in hypnotic, slow-motion cycles. They possess no masters, building nothing, yet they hum with the terrifying complexity of a machine that has been running for a billion years.

The sound is a constant, rhythmic grinding. It is the sound of tectonic plates scraping against one another with the precision of a watchmaker’s tools. Beneath the plains, titanic, unseen pistons pulse against the crust, regulating the heat. This is an industrial landscape without an industry, an effect without a cause. The "engines" are woven into the geology; a jagged mountain range might, at any moment, hum with the internal rotation of a great basalt gear. 

Nature here is a reality-glitch. Because the density of matter is so low, the landscape often fails to resolve. One might walk toward a ridge of splintered copper, only to find the ridge flickering out of sequence—a shimmering ghost-image of a mountain that exists three seconds into the future. Sometimes, the sky does not merely hold light, but refracts it into impossible geometries, cutting the plateau into hexagonal slices of blinding white and shadow. 

There are no plants to soften the edges, no brooks to carve paths. The wind, when it comes, carries no moisture, only the scent of ozone and cooling sparks. The inhabitants follow ancient, invisible circuits etched into the bedrock. They gather in hives of sparking quartz, cleaning the obsidian with delicate, spindly limbs. It is a world of overwhelming presence and utter emptiness, a vast, open clockface where the gears have rusted into the land itself, and the ticking is the only god left to worship.

In the center of the plateau, a great crack in the mineral crust reveals the internal workings: deep shafts pulsating with a dull, orange, synthetic warmth, like the throat of a furnace. Around these craters, the fauna congregates, their glass bodies glowing as they absorb the radiation. They move with such patient intensity, repairing infinitesimal fractures in the world’s surface with tongues of liquid gold. Everything here is in a state of terminal, slow-motion labor. The plateau is a factory that has forgotten its product, a temple of pure motion, enduring in the heat, waiting for a clock-turn that never arrives.
