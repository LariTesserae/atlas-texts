The world is not dark because of a lack of sun, but because of a surfeit of density in the air. Here, at an elevation that should be thin and crisp, the atmosphere is a pressurized, tepid soup of suspended moisture and microscopic, bioluminescent machinery. It is a warm, weeping cathedral of stone that has forgotten how to be sharp.

The terrain is ancient beyond geology. The mountaintops have been worn down by eons of slow-motion chemical hunger, leaving behind rounded, succulent hills of hummocky silt and mineral paste. There are no jagged peaks; there are only great, soft knuckles of land covered in a velvet of impossibly lush mosses and epiphytes that feed directly on the heavy, atomized vapor.

Because the space is vast in its emptiness but small in its boundaries, the silence—or the lack of human-audible noise—is shattered by a constant, rhythmic **thrum**. It is the sound of the *infrastructure*. The ground beneath is not just soil, but a porous, reactive substrate. Every few meters, a "weeping rock"—a translucent, calcified spire of ancient tech—pulses with a deep, subsonic vibrations that rattle the marrow. This is the sound of the world’s self-maintenance: the crunching of silt, the filtration of heavy water, the slow, atomic re-knitting of the landscape.

You are never truly alone, though you feel like the only occupant. Small, bioluminescent entities—part biological, part data-lattice—drift through the damp air like embers from a dying fire. They do not possess eyes; they perceive the world through the oscillation of the moisture. When they move, they hum at a pitch that resonates with the weeping rocks, a loud, harmonic chord that fills the hollow space between the growths.

The remnants of "built" life are scarce, appearing as half-sunken, translucent ribs of a white, ceramic polymer that look like the fossils of a forgotten, gargantuan organism. These structures are not ruins; they are being slowly digested by the moss. They are archives. If you touch one, the moisture on your skin conducts the signal, and you feel the history of the place—a slow-drip of data, a million years long, detailing the cooling of the mountain, the settling of the stars, and the precise chemical composition of every droplet that has fallen here since the beginning.

It is a world of patient processes. The erosion is so advanced that the land is almost fluid, shifting by millimeters every century. Yet it is a place of profound abundance. Life here is not frantic; it is structural. The greenery holds the earth together, and the weeping rocks hold the sky in place. 

Everything is wet, everything is warm, and in the total darkness, the air glows with the pale, sickly neon of a civilization that finished its work millions of years ago and left the lights on, only to have the jungle reclaim the corridors. You walk through a room-sized grove, your feet sinking into soft, ancient loam, hearing the mountain groan as it breathes, sensing that if you stayed for a thousand years, you would eventually dissolve into the hum, becoming just another ripple in the warm, weeping dark.
