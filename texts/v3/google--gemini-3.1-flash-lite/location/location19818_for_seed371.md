This is the Throat of the Spire, a jagged needle of black, volcanic basalt piercing the belly of a perpetual, soot-heavy cloud layer. At this altitude—higher than the circling birds, higher than cold air has any right to be—there is no stillness.

The landscape is a vertical labyrinth of razor-edged obsidian. Because the terrain is geologically young, the rock has not yet succumbed to the softening touch of time; it is fractured into impossible, interlocking geometries—sheer cliffs, overhangs that jut out like serrated blades, and narrow, claustrophobic chimneys where the walls seem to press inward, hungry for space.

It is here that the industrial paradox lives. Though there is no human hand, no artifact, and no map, the mountain itself serves as a colossal, pressurized mechanism. Thick, pressurized steam—the "dampness" of this world—hisses violently from deep, rhythmic fissures in the basalt. It is not a gentle mist; it is scorching, high-velocity vapor that coats the walls in a slick, metallic condensation before freezing into fragile, jagged fractals of rime ice.

The density is suffocating. The air is so thick with iron-rich particulate and billowing clouds of industrial steam that you cannot see more than a few feet in front of you. And it is loud. The sound is not of wind, but of the mountain’s internal combustion: a subterranean, grinding roar, the clatter of loose scree falling into unseen depths, and the percussive, deafening *thrum* of high-pressure geysers venting through narrow stone apertures. It sounds like an assembly line for the world, a factory floor made of tectonic plates and steam power, running at a frantic, breakneck cadence.

The vegetation is the only soft thing, and it is aggressive in its vulnerability. Thick, bioluminescent mosses and velvet-black liverworts carpet every inch of the damp basalt. They do not grow toward the sun—there is no light here—but toward the heat of the vents. They are layered so densely that the moss is often a foot thick, a spongy, shivering carpet that pulses in rhythm with the mountain’s vibrations. These plants are the lungs of the place, constantly drawing in the heavy, mineral-laden air and exhaling it back out as pressurized moisture, a closed-loop biological engine.

There is no animal life. Nothing could survive the turbulence of this place. If you were to stand here, you would feel, above all, the sense of an urgent, unfinished project. The sharp, fresh edges of the rock suggest that the mountain is still currently being pushed upward by some enormous force, twisting and tearing as it rises. It is a place of brutal, unthinking creation, where the earth is hardening under immense pressure, screaming in the process, and covering its naked, new-born wounds in a thick, wet skin of neon-green life. It is small, a contained hamlet of chaos, hidden within the shroud of a dark and frozen sky.
