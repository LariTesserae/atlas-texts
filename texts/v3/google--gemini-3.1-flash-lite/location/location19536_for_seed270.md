You are standing inside the hollowed-out skull of something continental, or perhaps inside the cooling mantle of an ancient, failed engine. The space is no larger than a cargo container, yet it feels infinite because it is packed to the ceiling with movement.

There is no water here; the physics of the pocket do not permit it. In its place, the air is thick with a fine, pressurized silicon dust that behaves like a fluid. It hangs in the air, shifting in rhythmic currents, vibrating with the constant, low-frequency hum of a trillion miniature pistons ticking over. 

The terrain is a chaotic, layered architecture of industrial decay and hyper-evolved biology. Every square millimeter of the floor is a carpet of jagged, iron-rich filaments and obsidian-like growths. There is no soil; the ground is "built"—a compacted sludge of discarded alloy shavings, oxidized circuitry, and the molted chitinous shells of the occupants. Beneath your feet, you can feel the rhythmic *thrum-thrum-thrum* of deep-seated machinery, a mechanical heartbeat that has been grinding against these mineral surfaces for eons, eroding everything into a state of perfectly weathered, rounded perfection.

The "fauna" is inescapable. They are neither animal nor machine, but a dense intersection of both: twitching, crab-like scavengers of heavy metal, no bigger than your thumbnail, armored in iridescent volcanic glass. They are everywhere—crawling over one another in a frantic, ceaseless industry. They feed on the friction between the surfaces, and as they move, they emit sharp, crystalline chirps—a chorus so dense it creates a wall of white noise that vibrates in your chest. They are hyper-active, constantly harvesting, grinding, and reassembling the metallic debris of the floor into fragile, fleeting geometric towers that rise an inch and then topple under the weight of the next wave of scavengers.

The atmosphere is cool—uncomfortably so—a damp, biting chill that seems to emanate from the very walls, which are coated in a thin, frost-like layer of particulate metal. There is an occasional flicker of light, regular as a heartbeat, cast by the grinding, sparking joints of the walls’ architecture. Whenever a gear shifts or a pressure valve releases, a spark—brilliant, electric blue—arcs across the space, illuminating the frantic, overlapping bodies of the myriad tiny residents.

There is no sky, only the ceiling of the pocket, which is hung with "cables" of calcified nervous tissue that pulse rhythmically. Drops of dark, viscous lubricant fall occasionally from these bundles, sizzling as they strike the mineral floor, creating tiny, drifting plumes of acrid, orange smoke.

It is a world of total conservation: nothing is ever truly discarded, only repurposed. The rust of a thousand years ago is the skin of a creature today, which will be the lubricant of tomorrow. The noise, the movement, the shivering cold—it is the frantic, claustrophobic peace of a machine that has forgotten the purpose for which it was created, but will never stop running.
