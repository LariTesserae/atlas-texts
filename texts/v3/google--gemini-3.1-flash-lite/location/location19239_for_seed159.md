The world here does not happen on the surface, but in the vast, hollowed-out crawlspace of a planet that has not yet finished cooling. 

It is a cavernous, subterranean expansion—a valley of raw, unweathered geology where the ceiling is a dizzying, jagged expanse of obsidian and raw hematite. The air is heavy and humid, saturated with a warmth that seems to seep from the very floor, which is composed of tectonic plates still grinding against one another with a low, rhythmic thrumming.

There is no sunlight, yet the space is bright. The light does not come from fixtures; it comes from the *fauna*. 

This is a world of overwhelming biological density. Millions upon millions of translucent, segmented creatures—roughly the size of hounds—scuttle across the walls, floors, and ceilings. They are semi-gelatinous, pulsating with bioluminescence that shifts from pale turquoise to a hot, dangerous amber. Their internal organs glow, refracting through their skins like liquid lanterns. They are not plant-like, nor are they strictly animal; they are an emergent biological swarm, a living circuitry that covers almost every square inch of the subterranean expanse.

The turbulence is constant. The creatures move in a state of hyperactive migration, climbing over one another to form living bridges between the shards of raw basalt. When they congregate, they produce a sound like the rushing of a thousand heavy silk curtains being whipped in a windstorm—a dry, scuttling hiss that occasionally swells into a thundering roar as the ground shifts.

In the midst of this chaotic swarm, moisture is a structural necessity. Every few minutes, condensation drips from the jagged ceiling, cooling the overheated creatures below and causing them to emit a sharp, choral chime as the droplets evaporate on their heat-generating skins. These pockets of moisture pools in the natural basins of the stone, shimmering with the reflected light of the swarm-tide.

The built environment is primitive, almost incidental. Here and there, tucked against the base of a great obsidian pillar or nestled in a fold of the rock, there are scattered artifacts of a soft, malleable clay. These small, crude structures—some hollowed-out hemispheres, others simple, flattened terraces—are not shelters for the creatures, but nests for the discarded shells of the swarm. They are marked with simple, rhythmic indentations, the work of something manual, something that has learned to harvest the chitinous debris of the fauna to build small marks upon the world. 

There is no erosion yet, because nothing has lasted long enough to be worn down. The stone itself is still coming into being, pushed upward by volcanic pressure, only to be immediately colonized and covered by the translucent, glowing tide. 

It is a world of high-saturation existence. To stand here is to feel the pressure of the swarm against your own skin—the heat, the vibration, the constant, shifting light of a billion glowing pulses. It is a place that is perpetually mid-breath, a cathedral of raw energy held together by the frantic, beautiful chaos of a life-form that has never known a sky.
