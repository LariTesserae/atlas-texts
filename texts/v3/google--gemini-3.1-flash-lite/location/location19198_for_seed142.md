The world sits at the end of time, held in a state of absolute, petrified equilibrium. It is a high-altitude plateau of obsidian glass and pulverized silicates, existing in a permanent, suffocating darkness. There is no moisture here; the atmosphere is a thin, dry slurry of inert noble gases that taste like ozone and long-dead static.

The scale is gargantuan, an open expanse of jagged mineral ridges that stretch toward a horizon that never glows. Because there is no sun, no heat convection, and no fluid dynamics, the landscape has been eroded by a billion years of slow, abrasive gravity. Everything is rounded, softened, and smoothed into the final, tired posture of a planet that has finished its work.

Scattered across this dark plateau is the Necropolis City—a sprawling, industrial skeleton of a settlement. The structures are not built; they are extruded. They are layered towers of calcified metal and black volcanic slag, rising like frozen, leafless trees from the dust. There are no streets, only structural interfaces where one derelict hive-tower meets the next. The "urban" density is a cruel trick of geometry; there is infrastructure everywhere, but it is hollow, echoing, and entirely disconnected from utility.

Between these towers, the silence is near-total. Occasionally, a low, rhythmic thrum emanates from deep within the earth—a mechanical shudder, a relic of an engine that has been running for eons, driving nothing. It is a tectonic heartbeat that never fluctuates, never speeds up, never dies. It is the sound of a world that is trapped in a loop.

Technology here is a matter of fossilized intent. Copper wires hang loose from the ceiling of the void, oxidized into brittle ribbons. Massive, rusted cogs, the size of cathedrals, are locked in permanent mid-rotation, fused by sheer age into the bedrock. There is no fire, no light, no energy discharge. The only illumination is a perceived, faint ultraviolet flicker that occurs when the rare, phantom fauna—vague, kite-like impressions of magnetism—drift through the spires. These creatures are not biological; they are localized glitches in the local density, sparks of memory trailing behind them like fading smoke.

If a stray particle of dust settles on the black glass floor, it stays there for an era. There is no weather, no wind to stir it, no rain to wash it away. The landscape is a library of every movement ever made, a physical record of near-nothingness. 

To stand here is to recognize the ultimate destination of industry. It is the final stage of "built" reality—where the machinery has eventually become indistinguishable from the mountain, where the town *is* the geology, and where the temperature is kept at a lukewarm, indifferent mid-point by the sheer mass of the ancient, unmoving world. Nothing is starting. Nothing is finishing. The city is a monument to a function that was forgotten before the first star went cold.
