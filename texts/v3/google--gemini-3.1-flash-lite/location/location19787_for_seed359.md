This is not a landscape of geography, but of joinery. The floor beneath your feet is not ground, but a tightly interlocking mosaic of hand-polished ceramic tiles, each the size of a dinner plate, fitted with a precision that defies the lack of industrial machinery. They are held together by a translucent, amber-hued resin that smells faintly of dried cedar and calcified honey.

There is no water here. The atmosphere is instead saturated with "phlogiston-mist"—a slow-moving, golden vapor that acts as both lubricant and nutrient for the dense, vertical proliferation of the world.

The space is roughly the size of a grand cathedral, bounded by fluted walls of woven glass-fiber and hardened clay. It is a "closed-loop" environment, a terrarium of the absurd. Because there is no raw substrate, every stalk of vegetation is a feat of primitive engineering. The flora—dense, spiraling towers of semi-organic, semi-knitted textiles—springs directly from the seams of the floor. They are lush, vibrant, and impossible; they possess the texture of velvet but the structural rigidity of iron-wood. They grow not by photosynthesis, but by consuming the ambient golden vapor, their leaves unfolding in slow, jerky motions, like the hands of a clock moving through molasses.

Reality here is thin, worn to a translucent sheen. If you stare too long at the junction where a textile-vine meets the ceramic floor, the colors begin to bleed. You might see a shadow detach itself from a leaf and crawl across the floor to hide in a tile-gap, or hear the "clack-hiss" of a non-existent machine echoing from behind the walls. 

The fauna is abundant, yet unnervingly quiet. Small, multi-legged constructs—part-crustacean, part-clockwork toy—scuttle through the dense thickets. They have bodies made of smoothed bone and scavenged pottery shards, tied together with sinew-string. They do not click or whir; they move with an absolute, soft-footed silence. They are the gardeners of this place, perpetually brushing the dust from the tiling or re-knitting a fraying leaf-seam with obsidian needles. 

The warmth feels mammalian, like the heat trapped inside a wool blanket left in the sun. Everything here is built, yet everything feels alive. There is no horizon, only the verticality of the woven trees and the recursive patterns of the floor that seem to repeat at impossible angles—if you walk straight forward for a hundred paces, you find yourself staring at the back of your own neck, seeing the world from a vantage point that shouldn't exist.

It is a slow-motion clockwork garden, a dream constructed by artisans who had no access to the outdoors, building their own lush, arid heaven inside a box the size of the world. Time does not flow here; it accumulates, settling into the corners like the fine gold dust that coats the trembling, synthetic petals of the un-natural world. You are standing in a cradle of someone else's meticulous, patient, and deeply strange devotion.
