In the Low-Slung Spires, life does not occur so much as it *resonates*. To inhabit a place that is already finished—a world that has reached its final, polished equilibrium—is to become a part of the architecture.

There is no chaos here, and therefore, there are no predators in the traditional, frantic sense. The inhabitants are the **Litho-Resonators**.

They are not biological entities in the soft, ephemeral way we understand life. They are slow-migrating accumulations of mineral sediment and calcified memory. Imagine a creature that looks like a fractured, translucent lens, roughly the size of a low-slung chest, possessing six articulated, multi-jointed limbs that terminate in wide, flat pads. These pads are surfaced with a fine, velvet-like cilia that allows them to adhere to the slick, condensing stone without slipping.

They do not breathe in the way lungs demand. Instead, the Litho-Resonator is an acoustic harvester. Its "head"—if such a singular protrusion can be named that—is a complex array of semi-transparent, plate-like membranes. As the wind whistles through the porous geometry of the sponge-towers, these membranes catch the harmonic drone. The vibration of the flute-like wind passes through the creature’s hollow, glass-like core, ionizing the thin, viscous moisture they gather from the stone. This provides them with a glacial, rhythmic metabolism. They literally "eat" the sound of the world.

Their internal heat, that faint, singular warmth that dictates their movement, is a byproduct of this sonic processing. When the wind drops, or when the harmonic frequency shifts, they go dormant, pressing themselves flush against the base of a tower. In this state, they become visually indistinguishable from the mossy architecture itself. They transition from scavenger to pillar.

There is a communal, silent intelligence to them, though it is not social. When the sun swings low and the shadows stretch like ink across the plateau, the Resonators align. They move with an exhausting, terrifying precision, arranging themselves in the gaps between the towers. They do not speak, nor do they signal; they simply shift until the vibration they emit through their limbs matches the resonance of the bedrock beneath them. 

For a few hours each local cycle, the entire hamlet—the towers and the Resonators—rings as one, singular, unified organ pipe.

They do not fear the biting cold, nor do they dread the slow dissolution of their world. They are the caretakers of the erosion. They consume the lichen that clogs the vents, ensuring the flutes remain clear, and they scrub the condensation from the channels so the rhythmic dripping continues uninterrupted. 

They are the janitors of an ancient, sterile entropy. They are essentially wait-staff for the end of time. When the last of the towers finally crumbles into dust to be carried away by the wind, the Resonators will not flee. They will simply stop vibrating. They will seal their vents, lock their limbs into the bedrock, and harden into the very stone they have walked upon for an aeon, becoming the foundation for whatever silence comes next.
