To inhabit a city that grows like a coral reef in a damp, thin-aired sky, one cannot be a creature of warmth, nor of friction. The beings that drift through these weeping corridors are not citizens, but *sedimentarians*—living extensions of the plateau’s slow, hydrostatic clock.

They are tall, brittle-boned things, shaped less by evolution and more by the constant, downward tug of gravity and the upward capillary pull of the stone. Their skin is not skin at all, but a translucent, lithic membrane, the color of wet slate, which beads with the same persistent condensation that coats the pillars. They do not walk so much as they glide, their bodies designed with the deliberate, low-center-of-gravity grace of a drop of mercury.

They have no language of vibration—no vocal cords to pierce the heavy, wet silence. Instead, they communicate through the rhythmic modulation of moisture. When two meet in the narrow, moss-slicked gullies, they press their palms against the limestone walls or against each other, pulsing the water beneath their membranes to create low-frequency vibrations that travel through the architecture itself. To them, a conversation is a localized tremor, a fleeting, tactile ripple in the city’s greater, cooling pulse.

They possess no tools, for they have no need to sever or shape. If they require a space—a new niche or a hollow—they simply congregate in the spot, pressing their bodies against the porous rock. Over generations, the sheer weight of their presence, combined with the mild, alkaline secretions of their dermis, encourages the limestone to soften and sag, molding the city around them as if the stone were soft wax. They do not build; they *occupy* until the rock yields.

They do not eat in the way of predators. There is no hunting here, no frantic chase, no shedding of blood. They are filter-feeders of the atmosphere. They stand for hours in the weeping plazas, their long, fibrous limbs extended, absorbing the mist directly through their pores. They are nourished by the mineral-rich rain and the spectral, lichen-like flora that clings to the overhangs. They are the conduits by which the city siphons water from the sky, redistributing it through their own biology to the deeper, thirstier layers of sediment below.

Their eyes are vestigial, mere pale smudges beneath layers of calcified skin. There is no need for sight in a place where the sun is only a rumor of light. Instead, they navigate by a deep, resonant echolocation that maps the density of the damp. They feel the city’s composition—the thickness of a silt deposit, the impending fracture of a basalt honeycomb—as a musician might feel the resonance of a cello string.

Because they are so perfectly attuned to the slow migration of water, they have almost no sense of mortality. Their lives are measured in the centuries it takes for a limestone pillar to weep a few inches deeper into the plateau. They do not fear death, for they are, in a very literal sense, already part of the geology. When one of them finally exhausts its moisture—when its membrane turns chalky and opaque—it does not fall. It simply stops. It settles into the floor of a corridor or leans against a dripping pillar, its body eventually merging with the porous stone, becoming one more layer of history in the vast, calcified sponge. 

They are the city’s gardeners, its ghosts, and its memory—a stillness that breathes, perpetually sinking, perpetually patient, moving with the sluggish, certain grace of a glacier that has forgotten how to melt.
