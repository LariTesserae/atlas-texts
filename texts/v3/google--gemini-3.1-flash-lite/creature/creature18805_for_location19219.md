The dwellers of this Cordy-quartz labyrinth are not settlers; they are *osmotic residents*. To live here is to surrender the upright, assertive posture of the surface world and adopt a slow, drifting biology that mimics the city itself.

They are tall, incredibly lean figures, their limbs elongated by a lifetime spent navigating vertical shafts and narrow, constricting corridors. When you first observe them, you might mistake them for shadows detached from the walls. Their skin has lost all pigmentation, bleached to the color of curdled milk, and rendered semi-translucent by the constant, aggressive humidity. Under the bruised, indigo light, you can see the faint, dark pulsing of their own internal systems—the rhythmic slow-thrum of organs that have learned to sync their pace to the systolic push of the city’s water-veins.

They do not wear clothes in any traditional sense. Instead, they are wrapped in layers of shed fibers from the prehistoric ferns—stiff, organic tatters that hold the moisture of the walls. These garments are not for warmth, but for absorption; as they move through the city, the fibers pull the condensation from the air, keeping their skin perpetually lubricated so they may slip through the narrow, constricting apertures when the city decides to "breathe."

Their faces are featureless expanses of pale membrane, save for wide, lidless eyes that have adapted to the deep-sea gloom. They do not blink; they rotate their gaze with mechanical, fluid precision, tracking the flow of the luminous mucus trails left by the gastropods. They do not speak. The air here is too heavy for sound, and the city’s structural memory is too impatient for conversation. Instead, they communicate through bio-electrical tremors. When two residents meet in a hallway, they press their palms against the Cordy-quartz walls; their bodies pulse with that same indigo light, sending vibrations through the cartilaginous floor. A conversation is a series of shimmering, subsonic hums that ripple through the vegetation, felt by the other in the soles of their feet.

The residents are essentially "hollow." They consume almost nothing. They survive by pressing their backs against the sweating surfaces of the residential nodes, allowing their pores to drink the mineral-rich slime that the moss exudes. They are sedentary in the extreme. Sometimes, a resident will find a niche that suits their frame and simply remain there for weeks, allowing the city to grow around them. You might pass the same figure in the same alcove for a month, only to find them gone one morning, the wall behind them having sealed over, leaving behind a subtle, human-shaped indentation in the translucent quartz.

They are not the keepers of this place, nor are they its parasites. They are its ghosts—the only things here that possess a semblance of consciousness, yet they possess no ambition. They spend their cycles watching the gastropods, studying the slow, intentional geometry of their grazing. When a gastropod clears a path, the resident follows in its sluggish wake; when the city constricts to force a passage shut, the resident curls into a ball, waiting with infinite patience for the wall to push them into a new, unfamiliar gallery.

They have forgotten the sun. They have forgotten the language of wind and open sky. They live in the state of a dream half-remembered, their identities blurring into the indigo light until they are indistinguishable from the veins of the city itself. If you were to touch one, you would feel no heat, only the cool, spongy resilience of a creature that has become a permanent, living fixture in the anatomy of the mountain.
