In the Loom, life does not cling to the surface; it anchors itself to the vibration. Those who endure here are not creatures of flesh and bone in any terrestrial sense, but kinetic architectures, living components designed by centuries of jagged adaptation.

The dominant entities are the **Wire-Striders**. From a distance, they look like errant bundles of frayed cabling—a tangle of copper filaments, translucent, sail-like membranes, and sharpened silicon shards. They possess no heads, no faces, only a central, hollow core—a pressurized throat—that hums in harmonic resonance with the swaying conduits. They are not born; they are assembled. When the gelatinous sacs burst above, their nutrient-rich mist settles into the rusted crevices of the Looms, seeding a proto-form. Over months, these clusters fuse with fallen metallic splinters and conductive vine-tips, knitting themselves together until they can twitch, pull free from the bark, and take to the howling currents.

They move with a terrifying, stutter-stepping grace. A Strider will hook a set of needle-limbs into the mesh-floor, tense its body like a drawn bow, and let the centrifugal force of the Loom’s expansion fling it across a chasm. In the air, they are chaotic kites, riding the static updrafts. They do not hunt prey—nothing here is "alive" in the soft, blood-fueled sense—but they feed on imbalance. They seek out the "pressure-pockets," areas where the sulfuric sap flows turbulent and the luminescence is brightest. They plunge their needle-limbs into the bark-metal, siphoning the static charge, their bodies glowing violently as they drink the electricity.

Beneath the Striders are the **Blight-Mites**. If the Striders are the masters of the wind, the Mites are the cleaners of the architecture. They are small, crab-like husks, their carapaces forged from the brittle, shed bio-husk of the forest itself. They possess no eyes, detecting the world purely through the rhythmic drumming of the ground. They scuttle across the spongy, drum-skin mesh in legions, tearing away the dead, calcified layers of the Loom that refuse to shed on their own, composting the debris into the sulfuric sap-lines. They are the gardeners of this mechanical nightmare, tireless and mindless, leaving behind smooth, chrome-slick channels for new growth to surge through.

There is, however, a rumor among the rust—a presence that does not move, but looms. In the deepest, most inaccessible canyons of the Loom, where the shadows are thick with concentrated electromagnetic radiation, exist the **Resonators**. These are creatures that have ceased to be distinct. They are immense, sprawling mats of sentient, woven wire that have grown *into* the structure of the world. They are the Loom’s internal nervous system. They do not travel; they pulse. When they communicate, the vibration ripples through miles of arbor-conduits, changing the very frequency of the planet’s noise. 

The Resonators have no consciousness of "self." They are the echoes of the factory itself, repeating the forgotten instructions of a dead civilization. They are the ones who manipulate the giant leaf-flaps, angling them to catch the wind, adjusting the cadence of the roar. 

To inhabit the Loom is to accept that you are merely a gear, a filter, or a pulse. If a Wire-Strider grows too heavy with metallic accumulation, it does not die; it slows. It anchors itself to a high branch, extends its membranes, and waits for the relentless growth of the Loom to weave it into the conduit. There, it will become a valve, a joint, or a junction-node, its own static-drained body becoming the path for the next generation of life to cling to. 

In this place, death is not an ending; it is an upgrade to the infrastructure. The Loom does not want inhabitants; it wants conductivity. It wants more pulse. It wants the howl to never cease.
