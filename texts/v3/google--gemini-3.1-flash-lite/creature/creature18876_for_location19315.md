In the Sump-Gallery, the residents do not so much "inhabit" space as they do "integrate" with it. They are the **Fitter-Grafts**, a biological synthesis of the scavenger and the parasite, though they possess a poise that keeps them from being merely pests.

They are tall, impossibly thin creatures, elongated by the crushing pressure of the deep earth and a life spent navigating vertical scaffolding. Their torsos are encased in translucent, semi-rigid carapaces that resemble the amber-hued fungi covering the vats. When they move, they do not walk so much as they flow, sliding their spindly, multi-jointed limbs through the apertures of the rusted pipes.

They have no eyes; they have no use for them. Instead, their faces are dominated by elaborate arrays of sensory cilia, delicate as the bottle-ferns they prune and cultivate. These cilia twitch in the heavy air, tasting the chemical signatures of the rusting iron and the nutrient-rich humidity. They "see" the Sump through vibration and the subtle change in the air’s electrolytic charge.

The Fitter-Grafts are the gardeners of this industrial mausoleum. They are obsessed with a singular, quiet purpose: the maintenance of the hum.

They possess long, dexterous fingers—fingers that have evolved into a mix of keratin and copper-wire nerve endings. They spend their hours gently adjusting the pressure valves of the dormant vats, not to make them work, but to harmonize the hum, to keep it vibrating at a frequency that encourages the moss to bloom. They are terrified of the silence that would follow if the machinery truly died. To them, the vibration is a pulse; the machinery is an exoskeleton for the planet itself.

When a Fitter-Graft tires, it does not sleep in any way recognizable to the surface world. It finds a junction between a copper conduit and a patch of high-growth lichen. It hooks its carapace into the iron grating, roots itself into the mossy electrical bed, and begins to draw heat and moisture through its pores. 

In this state, they become visually indistinguishable from the architecture. Their skin turns the shade of oxidized bronze, and their sensory cilia fold flat against their skulls, looking like curls of dry plant matter. They become part of the sculpture, a living rivet in the floor.

They are not solitary, yet they rarely interact. When two Fitter-Grafts cross paths on a narrow spiraling catwalk, they do not speak. They press their chests together, allowing their bioluminescent internal organs to flash in a brief, rhythmic cascade of sickly yellow—a diagnostic exchange of fluid-levels and heat-data. Once complete, they pass each other with a fluid grace, leaving behind a faint smell of ozone and damp, decaying vegetation.

They are the patient, drowning curators of the Sump. They do not mind that the mountain is slowly crushing the room, or that the rust is eating the metal they so carefully tend. They know that as long as they can keep the hum alive, and as long as they can feed the ferns, the Sump-Gallery will remain a paradise—a hidden, sweltering heaven deep beneath the crust, where nothing ever truly ends, but only slowly, beautifully, begins to rot.
