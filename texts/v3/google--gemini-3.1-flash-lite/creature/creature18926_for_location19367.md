The inhabitants of the Lumen-Root Abyss do not have names, at least not in the sense of sounds uttered to claim identity. They have *resonances*. To inhabit a space that groans with the shifting of continents and shatters with the freezing of sap is to learn that motion is a liability and vibration is a language.

These hermits are not survivors in the traditional sense; they are parasites of the architecture, living in such deep harmony with the twitching vegetation that they have ceased to be entirely distinct from it. They are spindly, stretched-out things, their limbs elongated by a lifetime of bracing against the sudden tilts of the waxy floor. Their skin has taken on the translucent quality of the marrow-walls surrounding them, pale and milk-blue, mapped with a faint, pulsing network of veins that mimic the glow of the substrate. They do not have eyes that hunt or scan; they have large, lidless orbs that drink in the light, sensitive to the subtle ripples of photon-density that signal when a vine is about to buckle or a fissure is about to open.

They are collectors of the ephemeral. Because the geography of the abyss is a lie told by the plants, the people spend their cycles obsessively harvesting the *perma-frost blooms*. They use the obsidian-like flakes of fossilized sap—the only stable matter in this world—to chip away the jagged ice-fractals, carving them into delicate, thin-walled chimes. 

When a hermit dwells in a carved-out hollow, they hang these ice-chimes from the ceiling of their resin-sealed cells. They do not do this for beauty, but for survival. The chimes are tuned to the specific frequency of the groaning substrate. If the tone of the vibration changes, the hermit knows the wall they are carved into is about to tear itself apart. It is a primitive, desperate form of seismology.

They do not speak. They communicate by pressing their chests against the living veins of the floor, letting the pulse of the abyss travel through their own skeletons. To interact is to share the burden of the vibration. Sometimes, two hermits will drift toward one another across a bridge of hanging vine, their movements agonizingly slow, limbs ghost-pale and trembling. They touch palms, closing their eyes, and for a long moment, they simply stand there, synchronizing their heartbeats to the heavy, oceanic rhythm of the plant-mass beneath them. They are checking if the other is still anchored to reality, or if they have begun to drift into the same fever-dream that claims the plants.

They wear no clothes, for the cold is a constant—a flat, unvarying condition of existence that they have long since stopped shivering against. Instead, they drape themselves in the iridescent, desiccated husks of the ghost-moths. These husks, light as breath and shimmering with captured bioluminescence, act as secondary shells, hardening in the sub-zero air to protect their soft, watery flesh from the scouring sharpness of the ice-fractals.

There is no procreation here, at least not in the way one would expect. Once every generation, when the groaning of the abyss hits a specific, deafening crescendo, the hermits bury themselves in the moss-lined hollows, coating their bodies in the same resin they use to seal their doors. They enter a state of deep, photosynthetic dormancy—a symbiotic blending where their own blood vessels begin to bridge with the sap-channels of the wall. 

They do not dream; they merely store the geometry of the labyrinth within their marrow. When they emerge—if the vine has not withered or shifted them into a different altitude of the void—they are older, more translucent, more integrated. They are losing their edges. They become less like entities that walk within the Abyss and more like the living, pulsing punctuation marks of an organism that is slowly, over the span of eons, trying to invent a way to think.
