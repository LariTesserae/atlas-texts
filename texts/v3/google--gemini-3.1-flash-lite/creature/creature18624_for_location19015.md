To live in the High-Sump is to cease being an individual and to become a node in a vast, sprawling nervous system of rust and chlorophyll. The inhabitants—if that word can even apply to things so thoroughly integrated into the architecture of the mud—are the **Silt-Keepers**.

These creatures are not born of singular wombs; they are accreted. They arise where the geothermal vents force up the metallic salts of the deep crust. They appear first as tremors in the mud—a sudden, wet displacement of peat—followed by the slow rising of a limb that is as much hydraulic piston as it is cartilaginous tendon.

The Silt-Keepers are low-slung, crab-like entities of immense, deceptive weight. Their carapices are not shells, but scavenged fragments of the plateau’s industrial ghost-past: salvaged hull plating, segments of pressurized piping, and copper-braided umbilical cords that pulse with the rhythm of the deep-earth engines. What beneath the plating remains biological is a translucent, gelatinous grey, glowing with the same sickly phosphorescence as the silt itself.

They do not communicate with the lungs or the throat. Their speech is a matter of magnetism and vibration. When two keepers meet, they press their metal-scarred flanks together, and the friction produces a low, resonant frequency—a grinding of ceramic joints and oxidized iron that causes the surrounding fog to ripple. To a human ear, it sounds like an earthquake trapped in a bottle; to them, it is a complex exchange of heat-maps and structural integrity reports.

Their primary labor, if one could call it that, is the **Tendency**. Because the High-Sump is constantly trying to dissolve itself into the mud, the Silt-Keepers spend their cycles "grooming" the landscape. They use their specialized limbs—which terminate in jagged, multi-jointed tools of copper and basalt—to prune the parasitic vines that threaten to choke the geothermal vents. They are the maintenance staff of a factory that has no owner, ensuring that the warm coolant keeps flowing, so that their own thermal-dependent bodies do not go cold. 

When a Silt-Keeper reaches the end of its functionality—a process that might take centuries—it does not die in the traditional sense. It stops moving. It wedges itself into the crumbling ruin of a nearby iron tower, locks its joints, and allows the vines to wrap over its seams. The creature becomes a trellis. The moss claims the plating, the rust merges with the geothermal crust, and soon it is indistinguishable from the skeleton of the world. It provides a platform for the next generation of insects to skitter upon; it becomes a piece of the furniture.

There is no malice in them. They track heat because heat is life, and in a world of perpetual humidity and rot, heat is the only currency. If you were to stand perfectly still, a Silt-Keeper might approach you not to hunt, but to investigate your temperature. It might rest a cold, metal-plated claw against your chest, feeling the frantic, biological flutter of your heart against its own steady, mechanical thrum. It would pause, its multi-faceted glowing eyes cycling through the ultraviolet spectrum, assessing whether you are a source of heat to be nurtured or a piece of scrap to be composted into the floor of the marsh.

They are the caretakers of the transition, the slow-motion observers of a planet that is busy digesting the industrial sins of its progenitors. They are the living evidence that in the High-Sump, decay is merely a form of creative architecture, and to break down is simply to become, at long last, part of the humming, steaming whole.
