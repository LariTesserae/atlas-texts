The inhabitants of Oulo, known to themselves as the *Vane-Kin*, move through the slurry with a grace that suggests they are not merely residents, but extensions of the cyclone’s own circulatory system. To behold them is to see a physical translation of the environment: their skin, translucent and veined with the faint, bioluminescent indigo of the moss they tend, is perpetually slicked with the nutrient-rich condensation of the atmosphere.

They are a people of long, sweeping reach. Their limbs are elongated, possessing an extra joint in the wrist and ankle that allows them to hook onto the calcified bark of the Lumen-Oaks like climbing ferns. They do not wear clothes in the traditional sense; instead, they drape themselves in shrouds of woven vine-silk, iridescent and porous, which catch the particulate moisture and pull it toward their skin, feeding them even as they sleep. 

Their ears have receded, replaced by sensitive, ossified ridges along the jawline that act as tuning forks for the city’s low-frequency thrum. They do not speak in words formed by breath; rather, they vibrate their sternums, producing melodic, humming chords that resonate with the wood, allowing their thoughts to ripple through the tree trunks to their neighbors blocks away. 

A Vane-Kin at labor is a study in stasis. You might find a master weaver sitting upon a protruding blister-chamber, her fingers flying through the air as she knots a suspension bridge. She does not look at her hands. She is watching the *Cloud-Skimmers* pass. When a Skimmer drifts near, she does not grab at it; she merely extends a webbed palm, and the creature, sensing the vibration of her pulse, brushes against her, leaving behind ribbons of harvested resin. She harvests this with a meditative slowness, folding the resin into her sash as if she were sculpting time itself.

There is no concept of "owning" these chambers. A Vane-Kin inhabits a dwelling only so long as the tree invites it—until the bark begins to shift or the leaf-veins turn from emerald to bruised gold, signaling that the room is ready to be reclaimed by the branch. When that day comes, the dweller simply unknots their tether, packs their polished ballast-stones, and drifts into the currents to find a new mooring. To be homeless in Oulo is a joyous thing; it is merely to be "in transit," a traveler between the lungs of the world.

The Vane-Kin keep no records of history. They believe the trees remember everything. When they need to recall a name or a cycle of the wind, they press their foreheads against the deepest, oldest knots of the trunk and hum. The wood hums back, a memory retrieved from the sap of four thousand years. 

Their only fear—if the Vane-Kin know fear at all—is the stillness of the heart. For if one’s own internal rhythm falls out of sync with the booming, rhythmic respiration of the Lumen-Oaks, the suspension of the slurry loses its hold on them. They become heavy. They begin to sink. And so, they spend their lives in constant, subtle motion, a perpetual dance of breaststroke and pull, ensuring they always drift at the height where the moss-light is brightest.

They are the guardians of the damp. When they look across the chasm at the *Lost Guests*—those flickering, upward-drifting shadows who lost their tethers—the Vane-Kin feel no sorrow. They simply hum a low, resonant note of farewell, watching as their kin ascend into the higher, thinner strata, fading into the pearlescent veil until they are nothing more than a new, slow-moving light in the canopy of the infinite storm.
