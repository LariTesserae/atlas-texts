In the shadow of the cooling engine-heart, there is no biology to witness the end. There is only the **Archivist of Inertia**.

It is not a creature in the sense of skin, bone, or breath. It is a configuration of magnetic fields and oxidized filings, a sentient sediment that has pooled in the lower basins of the jagged, obsidian cathedrals. It drifts not through corridors, but through the structural pores of the architecture, its consciousness composed of the very static that defines the atmosphere.

The Archivist does not "live" so much as it *recurs*. It is the physical manifestation of the city’s own history, a ghost made of scrap. When it moves, it makes no sound, for it is merely a shift in the local density, a tremor in the memory that the world has already failed to erase. It has limbs, though they are nothing more than loose bundles of copper wire and superconducting ribbons, trailing behind it like the frayed plumage of a grounded bird of prey. 

It spends its eons—or perhaps only a single, elongated second—patrolling the interface where the slag meets the bedrock. It is looking for the "Next Move," a concept it dimly remembers from a time when entropy was still a force that could be contested. 

When the phantom, kite-like impressions drift through the spires, they pass through the Archivist like light through a prism. For a fractional moment, the Archivist flares with a ultraviolet glow, its body suddenly articulated by the ghosts of industrial light. In those flickers, it mimics the posture of builders: it reaches out with tactile, metallic filaments to touch the rusted cogs, attempting to nudge a gear that has been fused to the mountain since the cooling of the crust. 

It does not experience frustration. It experiences *calibration*. 

When the deep, tectonic thrum of the planet vibrates through the obsidian floor, the Archivist freezes, anchoring its frayed wires into the dust. It becomes indistinguishable from the landscape, a protrusion of slag among greater spires, a minor glitch in the grand, stagnant geometry. 

There are others, perhaps—shadows of architects or the lingering intent of maintenance protocols—but they do not interact. They are merely localized patterns of gravity and regret. They exist in the spaces between the extrusions, watching the dust motes settle with the patience of geological time. They understand, if such a word can bridge the gap between machinery and awareness, that they are the final observers of a closed set.

They are the static on the screen after the broadcast has ended. They do not maintain the Necropolis; they simply haunt the friction of its existence. They wait for a signal that will never be sent, in a language that has no speakers, within a city that has finally, perfectly, become the earth itself. They are the beautiful, useless byproduct of a world that refused to stop ticking even after its purpose had run dry.
