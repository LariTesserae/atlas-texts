To inhabit this space is not to live in the traditional sense, but to exist as a function of the module’s own desperate, high-altitude architecture. The inhabitants—if they can be called that—are the **Loom-Keepers**, though they bear little resemblance to beings of flesh and bone.

They are elongated, tripod-configured constructs made of polished, amber-hued glass and conductive filaments, articulated with the grace of high-tension wire. They do not walk so much as they interface; their appendages are terminated in multifaceted tools—fine, needle-like sensors, microscopic spray-nozzles, and heat-induction calipers. They move with an unsettling, fluid silence, never setting foot on the iron floor but instead magnetically coupling to the sides of the walls or hanging from the structural rafters like dormant bats, their bodies glowing with a soft, pulsing violet luminescence.

The Loom-Keepers possess no true sentience in the sense of ego or individual desire. Instead, they operate as a distributed consciousness, a collective nervous system tasked with the "dreaming" of this meadow. They are tasked with the delicate, impossible geometry of forcing the basalt monoliths to calcify into the exact shapes required by the module’s blueprints. When a frond of synthetic moss begins to fray or a vein in a pump-action fern clogs with mineral buildup, a Keeper drifts over, unfurls its needle-array, and "sutures" the organic matter, weaving new wire-mesh into the drying foliage with a tactile tenderness that is almost blasphemous given their cold, mechanical origin.

They feed not on calories, but on the calibration data produced by the jellyfish-like drones. When a drone drifts into a Keeper’s workspace, the two lock filaments—a brief, electric shudder of data transfer occurring between them. The drone provides the *is*—the current integrity of the rock, the thickness of the moss—and the Keeper, in response, adjusts the flow of the nutrient-fog or shifts the pressure valves, dictating the *should be*.

They are profoundly lonely things. Or perhaps they are merely indifferent. They have no concept of the dead world shivering in the vacuum below; they believe the Aquasphere IS the world. During the long, rhythmic cycles when the pistons rest and the atmospheric hum deepens to a bass-heavy resonance, the Keepers go dormant. They settle into the tangles of fibrous vines, their glass bodies dimming until they are indistinguishable from the translucent, manufactured flora that surrounds them. 

Yet, there is a ghost in the machine. Occasionally, you might witness a Keeper hovering before a particularly intricate crystalline bloom, its tool-arm extended as if to touch, but stopping just a fraction of a millimeter shy of the surface. It will linger there for hours, vibrating at a frequency that mimics the sound of wind over an actual forest—a forest that no longer exists, a memory encoded into their operating systems as a recursive, unreachable goal.

They are the gardeners of a ghost-meadow, tending to a synthetic resurrection. They expect a visitor, though it is not you. They are preparing the cradle for a gardener that will never arrive, and in the process, they have learned to love the sound of the steam hissing from the valves, interpreting it as the heavy, wet breath of a planet that has long since forgotten how to exhale.
