To understand the inhabitants of Oakhaven-on-the-Crag, one must first discard the notion of consciousness as a flicker—a frantic, internal monologue of desires, plans, and anxieties. The dwellers here possess no inner "noise." They are, in a very literal sense, the biological extensions of the stone they inhabit.

They are tall, though they appear shorter because they move almost exclusively in a state of deep, perpetual slouch, their spines curved to mirror the rounded, overlapping tiers of the architecture. Their skin is the color of the mountain’s pale-grey schist, textured with a fine, granular quality that makes it difficult to tell where flesh ends and the damp-slick stone begins. There is no warmth to them; when they sit upon a wall to observe the mist, they surrender their body heat until they are exactly the same temperature as the rock, effectively vanishing against the landscape.

Their eyes do not dart or search. They are wide, obsidian-dark, and lidless, reflecting the diffused, silver light in a way that suggests they are absorbing information rather than observing it. They do not blink, for there is no grit or dust in this air; there is only the constant, rhythmic accumulation of the condensate.

These are not builders, for builders imply a need for growth. They are "Keepers of the Geometry." When a stone shifts a fraction of a millimeter over the span of a decade—a geological tremor too small for any other creature to register—a Keeper will emerge from a dwelling-hive. They move with the fluid, hydro-static grace of a deep-sea mollusk, their joints moving without the tension of muscle, sliding as if lubricated by the very mist they breathe. They carry tools of polished bone—long, slender needles—which they use to gently nudge a pebble back into its ancestral cradle, or to scrape a microscopic excess of lichen from a drainage groove.

They do not speak. Language is a tool for the hurried, for those who need to coordinate, persuade, or deceive. The Keepers share a state of unified resonance. Standing still, they seem to be listening to the rock itself, tuning their internal vibrations to the slow, gravitational settling of the mountain. If a drop of water *tocks* against a roof, they will tilt their heads in a synchronized, slow-motion ballet, acknowledging the sound as one might acknowledge a heartbeat.

They do not eat in any way that involves mastication or digestion. They absorb the pervasive moisture through their skin, taking in the trace minerals leached from the schist, the faint, mineral-rich essence of the clouds. This is why they never hunger, and why they never fail. Because they do not consume, they do not waste; because they do not waste, they do not decay.

If you were to stand before one, you would not be greeted with a welcoming gaze, nor would there be a flash of territorial aggression. You would be met with an indifference so profound it feels like a physical weight. To them, you are merely a kinetic anomaly—a brief, frantic blur of warmth and noise passing through a space that is already perfect. They would watch you with the same detached, clinical clarity with which they watch the mist settle. They would not stop you from leaving, nor would they follow you. 

When you eventually walked away, your footsteps sounding like thunder in the profound silence, they would simply wait. Once the echo of your agitation vanished, they would return to their work—the quiet, eternal adjustment of a single stone—content to remain, held in the amber of a place that long ago decided it had reached the end of time.
