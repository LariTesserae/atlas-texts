To live in Oretide is to exist in a state of perpetual integration. The inhabitants—the *Oretideans*—are not merely citizens of a mountain; they are extensions of its metabolism.

They do not walk through the city so much as they navigate its circulatory system. In appearance, they carry the weight of the elevation: their skin is pale and cool to the touch, often dusted with the fine, grey soot that falls from the higher steam-vents. Because the oxygen is thin and the labor is constant, their chests are broad and their breathing is rhythmic, punctuated by the same hitch and release as the turbines that hum beneath the floorboards.

They are a people defined by their tools and their temperaments. You will often see them wearing heavy, oil-streaked leathers reinforced with salvaged copper—garments that look more like plumbing than clothing. Their hands are their most important feature; these are not soft laborers, but "conduit-tenders," calloused and perpetually stained a permanent, dark iron-grey from years of polishing the phosphor-pipes. They move with an economy of motion born of a thousand years of practice. There is no urgency in Oretide, for to rush is to waste heat, and in a city that is slowly cooling, heat is the only currency of value.

They are keepers of the slow watch. A child in Oretide does not play with toys; they learn to read the "weep" of the stone, listening to the pitch of the dripping condensation to tell if a pipe is clogged or if the mountain is settling. They are quiet, not because they are mournful, but because the city is a conversation between stone and machine, and they have learned that to speak too loudly is to drown out the necessary hum of the infrastructure.

But look closer, and you will see the tenderness of their existence. Because the light is that sickly, radioactive green, the Oretideans have cultivated a strange, meditative relationship with beauty. They cherish the "Gardens of the Gantry"—the hanging clusters of bioluminescent ivy and ferns that they tend with the same devotion they give the steam-valves. They collect the Glass-Skimmers in glass vials, not to cage them, but to watch the rhythmic cooling of their translucent wings, finding solace in the tiny, flickering lives that occupy the cracks in their world.

They do not sleep as we do. They "hibernate" in the basalt-hewn chambers, wedged into niches carved into the rock, wrapped in thick furs that smell of mountain damp and ozone. They dream in the colors of the phosphor-pipes—shifting, oily greens and bruised violets.

They are the mountain’s immune system. They feel the rust on a beam as a mild irritation. They feel the health of the filtration stacks as a personal vitality. When a pipe bursts, they don’t just repair it; they tend to it, patching the leak with a sort of surgical reverence, as if they are stitching a wound in the skin of a god.

They understand that they are the end-point of an industrial dream. They know that one day, the turbines will fall silent and the phosphor-pipes will grow dim, and the moss will eventually cover them as completely as it covers the cliffs. They don’t fear this—they embrace it. Their lives are a long, slow act of stewardship, a gentle ushering of a great, metallic titan into its final, quiet rest. They are the last mechanics of an ancient machine, moving with a fluid, liquid silence, forever tending to the slow, cold thrum of a world that has decided, quite happily, to stop growing and start being.
