To inhabit the chimney is to exist as a single cell within a larger, twitching organ. 

The primary residents are the *Litho-weavers*. They are segmented, roughly the size of a human hand, possessing a carapace the color of obsidian, polished by the friction of siblings and the relentless passage of moisture. They do not walk so much as they anchor; each of their twelve hook-like limbs is tipped with a microscopic, sticky cilia that allows them to cling as easily to the vertical ceiling as to the damp floor of a horizontal shelf. Their bodies are flattened, aerodynamic, and perpetually cool to the touch, save for the feverish internal heat they harbor—a biological convection that keeps the chimney’s temperature a constant, swampy tepidity.

The Litho-weavers move in tides. A single creature is a stuttering, uncertain thing, but the collective is fluid. They migrate across the basalt faces in vast, shimmering carpets, their multiple limbs creating a sound like a torrential downpour hitting a tin roof. When they harvest, they do so with a synchronized, rhythmic scraping that makes the very stone hum. They hold their volcanic glass shards—jagged, translucent slivers—at precise angles, peeling back the biofilm of moss and lichen with the surgical indifference of a stone mason. 

Among them, there is no hierarchy of individual, only a station of purpose. The *Harvesters* occupy the middle rungs of the chimney, doing the labor of scraping the stone. They are the ones who vibrate with the white-noise roar, their mandibles clicking a rapid, percussive code that translates into the base frequency of the mountain.

Above them, near the pressurized cloud-shroud where the mist is thickest, dwell the *Collectors*. These are slightly larger, their chitan thinner and translucent, their bodies bloated with the water they hoard in specialized abdominal sacs. They act as the chimney’s circulatory system. As the water drips down from the high reaches of the spire, the Collectors lean out from their porous hollows, their bodies acting as living funnels. They catch the water, store it, and eventually move downward—or wait for the Harvesters to reach up—to share the moisture through a process of shared fermentation. They are the ones who maintain the "plumbing," physically guiding the path of the drips by shifting their own bodies to alter the flow, ensuring that every basin remains filled and every life-supporting patch of moss stays damp.

Deepest in the cool, dark marrow of the basalt, tucked away in the deepest honeycomb voids, are the *Ancestors*. These are the elders of the chimney, grown sluggish and heavy. Their bodies have become encrusted with the very mineral salts they have filtered from the stone for cycles beyond counting. They barely move; they are nearly indistinguishable from the rock itself, sensing the life of the colony through the vibrations carried in their own fused limbs. They are the keepers of the rhythm. If the collective scraping slows, if the water-rhythm falters, the Ancestors emit a low, infrasonic pulse—a thrumming that ripples up through the rock, forcing the Harvesters back into alignment, back into the patient, brutal, and eternal cadence of the chimney. 

In this place, death is not loss; it is merely a redistribution of mass. When a Litho-weaver’s limbs fail, it does not fall. It is consumed where it clings, broken down by its neighbors to patch the crevices, its remains becoming the substrate upon which the next season of bioluminescent lichen will bloom. 

To look at them is to see a civilization that has realized it cannot grow outward, so it has decided to grow inward, deeper and deeper into the silence of the stone. They are the sound of the mountain breathing; they are the patience of the rain made flesh. They have no name for their home, for they do not distinguish between the basalt that houses them and the bodies that sustain them. They are the chimney, and the chimney is a living, wet, and perfectly finished thought.
