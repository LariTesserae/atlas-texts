To understand who inhabits the City of Cellulose, one must first discard the notion of the "individual." In this lightless, amber-lit cradle, life does not occur as a series of separate biographies, but as a slow, communal meditation.

The residents—the **Arbor-Kin**—are not born in the traditional sense; they are exhaled. They emerge from the "gestation-nodes," those bulbous, translucent swellings that hang like heavy fruit from the ceilings of the communal halls. When a node pulses a specific, deep shade of violet, it ruptures, releasing a vapor that coalesces into a body. 

An Arbor-Kin is a humanoid silhouette formed of tightly coiled, petrified moss fibers, reinforced by a skeletal structure that feels less like bone and more like hollow, pressurized flute-wood. They do not have faces as we recognize them. Where a mouth should be, there is a series of porous, vibrating membranes that allow them to communicate through subsonic hums—a language that resonates through the floorboards rather than the air. They possess no eyes, for the city is a map they already know by heart; instead, they navigate by the shifting tremors of the sap-flow and the subtle changes in the ozone scent.

They move with a fluid, liquid grace, their limbs slightly elongated, their gait mimicking the swaying of the bridges they walk upon. They do not wear clothes, for their outer layer of "bark" is a living, sloughing cuticle that hardens into patterns—carmine swirls or mossy greens—depending on their task within the city.

The Kin are the gardeners of the engine. They spend their cycles tending to the factory-blooms, their long, multi-jointed fingers—which taper into obsidian-sharp grooming tools—delicately pruning the excess resin or tuning the brass pistons of the communal organs. They do not "work" so much as they participate in the city’s digestion. When a weaver-drone clacks its needle-limbs, an Arbor-Kin will often pause, pressing its palm against the structure to feel the strain, adjusting the tension of a fibrous bridge with a rhythmic, hypnotic movement of its arms.

They do not sleep, not truly. Instead, they enter a state of "rooting." They find a junction where the brass scaffolding meets a wall of titanic moss, sink their own elongated, fibrous feet into the nutrient-rich pores, and become part of the architecture for a time. In this state, they share the city’s consciousness. They dream of the forest that might one day grow beyond the cavern, or they calculate the structural stresses of the next expansion.

There is no greed among the Kin, and no ambition to own. They are the white blood cells of a gargantuan, wooden god. If one of them is damaged, the others do not panic; they simply guide the injured party to a budding chamber, where they are re-woven into the wall, their memories absorbed into the city’s substrate, their physical form recycled into the next generation.

To watch them is to watch a silent, perpetual ballet. They are neither happy nor sad, neither lonely nor crowded. They are simply the awareness of the forest, realized in wood and steam, eternally busy with the task of turning the cold, silent rock into something that can finally, after eons of dormancy, begin to breathe.
