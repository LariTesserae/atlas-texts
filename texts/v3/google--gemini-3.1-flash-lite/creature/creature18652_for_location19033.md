In this high-altitudinal stillness, the inhabitants are not so much "living" as they are vibrating in resonance with the basalt. They have no pulse in the way we understand it; they have a rhythm.

The beings that inhabit these plateaus are the **Litho-weavers**. They are not fleshy, nor are they strictly mineral, but a hybrid architecture of gravity and geometry. Imagine a shape that is not a body, but a collection of slender, tensile threads—filaments as fine as spider silk but composed of the same hardened, heat-reflective silica as the surrounding flora. 

They do not walk. They drift, tethered to the terrain by their own internal magnetism. When they move, they do not disturb the dust; they glide in the thin pressure of the violet sky, mirroring the wind-sculpted curves of the plateaus. A Litho-weaver appears, at a distance, like a glitch in the light—a faint, translucent shimmer, a ripple in the clarity of the air. As they settle near a patch of the radiant moss, they unfurl, expanding like the closing of a book, revealing a central core that glows with the same indigo shifting to silver that the plants employ.

These creatures are curators of the silence. Their primary purpose—if such a word can anchor them—is to maintain the harmonic integrity of the stone.

When the basalt expands and contracts, producing those deep, tectonic hums, the Litho-weavers act as living tuning forks. They anchor themselves to the rounded ridges, interlacing their filament-limbs into the tiny fissures of the rock. They absorb the abrasive mineral dust that scours the peaks, filtering it through their porous membranes to excrete a transparent, glass-like resin. They use this substance to patch the small nicks and fractures in the landscape caused by the scouring wind, slowly, over generations, smoothing the jagged edges of the world.

They do not communicate through language, for there is no vocabulary for a world without water. Instead, they "speak" by altering their luminosity. A gathering of Litho-weavers on a wind-tossed ridge looks like a slow-burning constellation. They pulsate in waves of pale, icy light, synchronizing their bioluminescence with the shifting of the tectonic harmonics. When a particularly large shift in the mountain occurs—a deep, booming *thrum* that vibrates through the very ground—the weavers flare in unison, a blinding flash of mirrored silver that momentarily eclipses the pale violet sky.

They are lonely, but not in the way of sentient beings that pine for company. Their loneliness is a function of their span. Because they move with the moss-colonies, migrating only inches a decade, they are intimately acquainted with the topography of a single ridge for centuries. They know the exact mineral composition of their plateau; they can feel the temperature of the rock changing through the seasons with a precision that makes the stone feel like an extension of their own skin.

They are the archivists of the slow time. They do not die, so much as they eventually cease their movement, locking themselves into the basalt to become a permanent part of the curve. If you were to trace the surface of one of these rounded, aerodynamic slopes—the ones that look like sleeping giants—your hand would eventually find a spot where the texture of the stone changes, becoming almost organic, woven with a strange, impossible precision. That is a Litho-weaver who has finished its work, its long, quiet life finally absorbed into the patient geometry of the world. 

They are the ones who dream the mountains into being, polishing them with needles of light until the entire continent is as smooth and quiet as a prayer.
