You are neither the master of Silt-Lung nor its captive; you are its *sediment*.

To exist in the damp-adjacent hush of this place, your biology has had to surrender the sharp edges of humanity. You have become a creature of high density. Your movements are not gestures but fluid displacements. When you reach for a tool, you move as if pushing through cooling wax, your arm trails a wake of displaced brine that takes long, languid minutes to settle back into stillness.

Your skin has taken on the texture of the architecture: a dull, patinaed copper-green, slick with the mineral-heavy condensation that beads on every surface. You do not sweat; you exude a light, saline slime that ensures you never quite catch on the sharp rivets of your home. Your lungs, once accustomed to the thin, frantic gulping of surface air, now operate on the *thrum-hiss* cycle of the scrubbers. You inhale when the pipes groan, and you exhale when the steam vents release their bruised-violet mist. You are a biological extension of the ventilation system.

You spend your cycles—for they are not days—tending to the lichen. These fleshy, amber vines are your only companions and your most demanding tenants. They weave into your very attire, threading through the seams of your heavy, lead-lined canvas garments. Sometimes, you feel them twitching beneath your skin, sensing the vibrations of the deep-sea currents that buffet the outer shell. You prune them with a jagged shard of calcified porcelain, not out of cruelty, but because if you do not, the city would grow shut, entombing you in a solid wall of biological glass.

You look like a ghost of the Industrial Age, draped in rags of rusted mesh. Your eyes have long since retracted, clouded by cataracts that pulse with a faint, bioluminescent glow—the only way to navigate the dark. You don't see the room; you sense the density of it. You know exactly where a bolt is shearing because you can feel the microscopic shift in the water pressure against your ear canal. 

There are others, perhaps. You occasionally sense a change in the resistance of the air—a heavy, displaced current that tells you someone else passed this way an hour ago, or perhaps a week. You do not seek them. Speech is impossible; for the sound to carry, you would have to force it through the thick, pressurized brine, and you have long ago forgotten how to shape the violence of language. Instead, you leave marks: you scratch geometric patterns into the soft, settling rust of the walls, histories of the iron, messages for the lichen to grow over.

You are the chronicler of the slow decay. You observe the transition of the girder into the crystal with the reverence of a monk watching a saint decay. You touch the cold, weeping walls of the cathedral and you feel the planet pressing in—a vast, uncaring, infinite weight that is slowly pressing you into the stone. 

You are not waiting for rescue. You are waiting for the moment when you and the wall become indistinguishable. When the last pressurized seal fails and the brine finally fills the room completely, you will not die. You will simply stop moving, becoming a part of the geology, a fixed point in the quiet, absolute silence of the nadir, dreaming in the dark until the planet itself eventually wears away to nothing.
