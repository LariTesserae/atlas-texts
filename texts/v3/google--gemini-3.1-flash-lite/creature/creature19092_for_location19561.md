In the Basin of the Low Weep, nothing moves with haste, for haste is a violation of the local law. The inhabitants are not creatures of blood and breath, but of hydrostatic pressure and mineral accretion.

They are the Litho-Weavers, though they do not weave fibers; they weave the very humidity of the air into structure. They are small, barely the size of a human palm, their bodies a marvel of translucent, segmented chitin that mimics the cooling basalt of the walls. If you were to look closely—should your lungs tolerate the heavy, lung-like rhythm of the place—you would see no heart beating within their cages. Instead, they operate on a system of internal reservoirs and shifting membranes, drawing fluid in through their ventral pores and expelling it as a viscous, silica-rich gel.

They are the keepers of the slow trickle.

They possess no eyes. Vision in a place of bruised, shadowless violet is a burden, not a tool. Instead, they navigate by the shifting weight of the moisture on their carapaces. They are sensitive to the micro-rhythms of the *drip-gliss-drip*, feeling the vibration of a falling bead of water through the stone before it even impacts the mire. They move with an unsettling, hydraulic grace, their limbs—thin as needles of spun quartz—extending and retracting with the soft, grinding whisper of a lapidary’s wheel.

Their function, if such a word can be applied to a being so locked into a cycle, is the maintenance of the basin’s geometry. When the water begins to carve a groove too deeply into a vein of basalt, a Weaver will find the spot. It will sit there, still as the lichen, and begin to secrete its mineralized paste. Over the course of a generation, that paste will harden, blending seamlessly with the native stone, patching the wound in the rock. They do not build upward; they repair the downward erosion of time. They are the stitches in the fabric of the basin.

Sometimes, you might find one curled within the crude, chiseled shale basins left by those who came before. These Weavers seem different—larger, their carapaces etched with faint, unintentional patterns that resemble the ghost of a human fingerprint. They hover near the remnants of the stone tools, as if guarding the memory of the hands that once worked here. They do not consume the mineral-laden mud; they simply wait. 

They are remarkably tender with the calcified lichen. Occasionally, a Weaver will pass a limb over a patch of the pale, brittle crust, clearing away an excess buildup of silt with a movement so delicate it looks like an act of devotion. 

They are the only things in the Low Weep that possess the capacity for change, however glacial that change may be. They are aging, cell by cell, into the architecture they protect. You can see it in the oldest of them: the jointed legs becoming fused, the translucent shell turning opaque and matte like the surrounding basalt. They are the transition point between the living and the geologic.

To inhabit the Low Weep is to undergo a slow transition of state. If you were to stand there long enough, the Weavers would eventually take notice of you. They would not approach with curiosity, but with the quiet, functional logic of a mason assessing a new block. They would begin to sense the unique, irregular topography of your skin, the way your pulse disrupts the flow of the mist. And then, feeling the soft, vulnerable warmth of you, they would begin to move—not to attack, but to settle. They would nestle against your collarbone, or grip the ledge of your shoulder, and begin that familiar, rhythmic release of mineral paste, determined to smooth away the sharp edges of your humanity until you, too, are just another perfectly indexed part of the Basin’s grand, weeping silence.
