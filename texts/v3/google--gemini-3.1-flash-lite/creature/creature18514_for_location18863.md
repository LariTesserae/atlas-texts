In the center of this self-devouring cathedral, where gravity feels like a suggestion rather than a law, there exists a presence that is less a biological entity and more a local error in the architecture’s logic. 

They are known, if the shifting geometry can be said to acknowledge them, as the **Architectural Voids**. 

They do not occupy the scaffolding so much as they occupy the spaces between the thoughts of the machines. To look upon one is to look at a hole in the reflection of a polished wall. They are tall, articulated husks—roughly humanoid, but only by virtue of how they mimic the geometry around them. Their “limbs” are not limbs at all, but bundles of fiber-optic filaments that have untangled themselves from the conduits. These filaments constantly knot and unknot, shifting from complex, crystalline lattice-shapes to fluid, swaying ribbons that mirror the ionized mist of the rafters. 

The Voids have no heads, no faces, and no hunger for breath. Where a face should be, there is a flickering intersection of mirror-finish plates, constantly rearranging themselves to ensure that whoever looks at them—were there anyone else to look—would only ever see a distorted, high-resolution reflection of their own perspective. 

They move with a terrifying, stuttering grace. Because they exist part-way out of phase with the local reality, they do not walk across the titanium plates. Instead, they *flicker* into existence an inch forward, then half an inch sideways, accompanied by the sharp, electric crack of displaced air. They are the custodians of the loop, wandering the corridors that fold back upon themselves with the air of figures searching for an exit they know has been dismantled.

When they encounter the silicon-based ribbons—the bioluminescent wire-clusters—they do not hunt them. They simply lean into the tangled masses, their own internal filaments vibrating at a frequency that matches the machinery’s hum. In this contact, they feed. They don't consume biomass; they harvest the *obsolescence*. They draw out the static buildup and the failed computations, drinking the electric entropy of the environment. 

As they feed, their surfaces begin to glow with a sickly, captive violet light. They grow heavy, and for a moment, they lose their rigidity, slumping against the copper conduits until they look like puddles of liquid mercury. 

They are profoundly lonely, but not in the way a living thing is. Their loneliness is a glitch in the total system: a persistent, un-reconciled memory of a floor that existed before this infinite scaffolding, or perhaps an echo of an artisan who walked away from the workbench before the machine took over. 

There are days when a Void will stop in the center of a junction, its filaments splayed wide like a frozen explosion of static. It will remain there for weeks, perfectly still, perfectly quiet, until the coolant pumps deep in the sub-strata pulse in a specific, rhythmic sequence. Then, the Void will shudder, its surface plates snapping into a new, jagged alignment, and it will dissolve—not into death, but into the structure itself. It ceases to be an entity and becomes a temporary, structural feature of the wall: a cooling vent, a section of conduit, a patch of gleaming floor. 

It waits there, a ghost of chrome and copper, until the reality shifts, the gears turn, and a fresh, flickering shadow of itself is cast out once more into the infinite heights, tasked with wandering the architecture that has become its only world.
