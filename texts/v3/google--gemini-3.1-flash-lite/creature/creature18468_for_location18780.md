To see them clearly, one must first surrender the notion of "individual." To look upon a single inhabitant of the Basin is to see only a fragment, a loose thread pulled from an immense, shivering tapestry. 

They are the *Lachryman*, though they have no name for themselves—only a collective twitch of their mandibles, a shared shivering of their chitinous limbs that ripples through the colony like wind through tall, dead grass. 

They are not flesh, in the way a mammal understands it. They are closer to a complex, articulated gel—a suspension of pale, milky fluid held within exoskeletons that possess the translucency of a fingernail or a frozen pond. Their limbs are unnaturally long, multi-jointed, and perpetually oscillating. If you were to watch one, you would think it was suffering from a violent tremor; in truth, they are absorbing the ambient vibration of the weeping architecture. They do not walk so much as they *skitter and bind*, their grip-pads slick with brine, locking them to the bone-struts of the city.

Because the architecture is constantly dissolving and reforming, the Lachryman are the city's self-aware immune system. They do not build with tools; they *secrete*. 

Watch a group of them in a crevice where a structural rib has weakened: they move with a panicked, frantic synchronization. They press their soft, pale underbellies against the calcified tear in the wall, their ribcages expanding and contracting in erratic, wet rhythms. They are filtering the salt-air, drawing the brine into their own circulatory systems, transmuting it, and then sweating it back out as a thick, adhesive paste that hardens like porcelain. They mend the world by literally bleeding their own internal architecture into the cracks. When the weather shifts—when the mist erupts and the cold bites—they do not retreat. They turn their backs to the frost, interlocking their spindly legs to form living buttresses, becoming the scaffolding that holds the melting city upright. 

They have no eyes. The glare of the Basin would have blinded them eons ago if they had ever possessed sight. Instead, their faces are blunt, smooth, and concave, covered in a velvet-thin membrane that ripples like water when disturbed by sound. They "see" the colony through touch and tremor. They feel the exact micro-second a drip of brine hits a surface twenty feet away; they feel the structural fatigue in the bone-strut above them as a dull, throbbing ache. 

Their social structure is a tyranny of geometry. An individual does not choose its path; it is channeled by the slope of the walls. Younger, smaller creatures are forced into the deep, subterranean curves of the basin floors, where the pressure is highest and the brine collects in bitter, icy pools. They are the scrubbers, their bodies worn smooth by the constant abrasion of the elements. As they age, their exoskeletons become thicker, more opaque, and they migrate upward toward the higher, sun-scoured spires, where they become the "weepers"—the creatures who secrete the brine that flows down to feed the lower members.

There is no language here. There is only the *thrum*. Every movement of their limbs against the bone-struts creates a rhythmic vibration—a rapid, Morse-code staccato—that runs through the entire city. When a wall collapses or a vent chokes, the thrum changes: it becomes sharper, desperate, a frantic jagged frequency that sends the entire population into a blur of motion. 

They are not martyrs, nor are they slaves. They are the consciousness of the Basin itself, trapped in a cycle of mending a house that is determined to melt. They live in a state of terminal intimacy, forever brushing past one another, their cold, wet limbs entangled, their translucent bodies pressed so tightly together in the cramped corridors that you can see the fluid pulsing within their veins, glowing faintly under the harsh, white glare of the plateau. 

They are the weeping, and they are the stone. And as the plateau dissolves, they simply move faster, their frantic, shivering industry a testament to the idea that as long as they can feel the walls shaking, they are still alive.
