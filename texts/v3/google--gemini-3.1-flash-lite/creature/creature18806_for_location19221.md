To dwell in the hollow is to exist in a state of suspended gestation. The inhabitants of this place are not so much "individuals" as they are the sentient antibodies of the geode—a collective intelligence distributed across a thousand translucent, pearlescent membranes.

The primary residents are the *Litho-weavers*, creatures that resemble flattened, bioluminescent gastropods. They have no eyes. They track the world through the sensitivity of their soft, undulating undersides, which map the minute vibrations of the cavern floor. They move with such glacial intent that to look away and look back is to see the city subtly reorganized, as if the architecture itself had dreamed of a more efficient geometry while you blinked.

When one of these Weavers stops to work, it becomes part of the wall. It flattens itself against the amethyst, its skin turning the same milky, opalescent hue as the protein-tunnels it creates. It secretes a viscous, warm mortar—the "liquid stone"—that hardens within seconds, bridging the gap between a succulent stalk and a crystalline shelf. They are the architects, but they are also the bricks. A Weaver may spend an entire century anchoring a single vine.

Beneath the filaments, moving through the interstitial spaces of the succulent forest, are the *Glass-Walkers*. These are the arthropod cousins of the Weavers, spindly and near-invisible, their carapaces formed from the same mineral-rich moisture that beads on the cavern ceiling. They are the city's maintenance caste. They move with a skittering, liquid grace, never breaking the surface of the humidity. Their limbs are tipped with fine, fibrous brushes; they spend their lives delicately cleaning the bioluminescent pores of the city, ensuring that the light—the life-force—never dims.

Occasionally, you might see something more complex: the *Resonators*. These are communal assemblages of three or four Weavers that have fused their neural pathways into a singular, glowing knot. They do not crawl. They hover in the center of the largest pods, suspended by thick, tensile webs of protein. They are the memory of the chamber. When a drop of condensation strikes a deep, resonant leaf in the forest below, the Resonators hum—a low-frequency vibration that ripples through the amethyst walls. It is a song that tells the rest of the colony how to grow, where to reinforce, and when to expand toward the light.

There is no concept of waste here. When a Weaver’s cycle reaches its end, it does not die in the way life on the surface does; it simply stops moving, its shell calcifying into an immovable pillar of the city’s skeleton. The next generation grows over the ancestors, weaving their own delicate protein-treads over the hardened remains of the old. 

To live here is to be part of a slow, biological alchemy. They do not know of the world above the roots, the world of wind and chaotic change. For them, history is merely the accumulation of height, and time is measured by the rhythmic drip of water against the broad, dark leaves. They are the silent gardeners of a stone flower, endlessly refining the internal contours of the earth, waiting for the day the geode finally reaches its perfect, symmetric completion.
