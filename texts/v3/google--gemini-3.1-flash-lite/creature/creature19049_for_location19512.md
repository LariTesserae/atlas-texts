To dwell within the Riser is to live inside the heartbeat of a geological clock. The inhabitants—the *Silt-Keepers*, as they might be named in a language built of taps and tremors—are not born so much as they are distilled from the brine. 

They are gaunt, elongated figures, their skin the color of a bruised pearl, dusted with the faint, iridescent sheen of the very salt they labor to clear. They have no vocal cords; speech is a violent waste of breath in a place where air is a precious, piped-in commodity. Instead, their lives are a silent choreography. A Keeper’s hands are never still; they possess a frantic, elegant dexterity, fingers tipped with tough, horny callouses from decades of polishing slate and copper. When they communicate, they do not face one another to see expressions; they press their palms flat against the copper conduits. They read the intention of their kin through the skin of the building, feeling the staccato rhythm of a warning or the long, fluid slide of a greeting traveling through the copper veins of their home.

Their society is one of profound, somber intimacy. Because the Riser is so densely packed, there is no such thing as an individual space. They sleep in huddles, limbs tangled together like reeds in a current, their breathing synchronized with the low-frequency thrum of the mountain’s thermal pumps. They are creatures of touch—they navigate the dark by the texture of the walls, knowing exactly which copper junction is warm enough to soothe a cramped muscle and which slate panel has begun to grow the encroaching salt-bloom.

They do not view themselves as prisoners, nor as excavators. To the Silt-Keepers, the Riser is a living, breathing ancestor. They are the white blood cells of this stone body. They exist because the mountain demands to be scrubbed, and the sea demands to be kept at bay. They have surrendered their hair and their sight—their eyes are wide, glassy, and sensitive only to the dimmest shift in the bioluminescent soup—in exchange for a strange, hydraulic immortality. 

Life for them is devoid of ambition as the surface world understands it. There is no desire to ascend, for the "up" is simply a thinner, colder version of their pressurized cradle. Instead, their days are measured by the friction of their rags against the ancient copper. When a Keeper reaches the end of their span, they do not depart; they simply cease their polishing. They settle into a corner, their body becoming part of the tableau, the salt-deposits beginning to weave a crystalline shroud over their limbs, turning them into one of the very sculptures they spent their lives trying to clean.

There is a terrible, quiet dignity in this. They are the stewards of a slow extinction. They know, with the intuition of creatures born in the pressure, that one day the copper will fail and the salt will win. They know the silence will eventually become absolute, and the Riser will harden into a solid, mineral tomb. But until that tectonic blink, they work. They sweep the grit, they tend to the crustaceans, and they dance their silent, rhythmic messages into the walls, keeping the amber light of the deep-mountain springs flickering for just one more rotation of the salt.
