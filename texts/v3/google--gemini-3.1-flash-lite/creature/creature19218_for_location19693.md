In the High Basin of Oth, to call oneself a "resident" is a misnomer. Those who dwell here are not so much inhabitants as they are biological extensions of the infrastructure—a specialized, slow-metabolizing caste of caretakers known to themselves as the *Hollow-Kin*.

The Kin are tall, spindly, and pale, their physiology adapted to a life of low light and high humidity. Their skin has taken on a peculiar, oil-slicked sheen, a waxy secretion that prevents the pervasive damp of the Basin from rotting their flesh. They do not walk so much as they navigate; their movements are calibrated to the rhythmic trembling of the floor, shifting their weight in synchrony with the tectonic hum. To move against the vibration is to invite dizziness; to move with it is to glide with a strange, liquid agility.

Because the air is thick with steam and the hum is a physical weight, speech is effectively useless. The Hollow-Kin possess no vocal cords, or rather, they have long since atrophied. Instead, they communicate through a tactile sign language, pressing their fingertips against the vibrating copper pipes or the weeping stone walls. They “speak” by modulating the way their bodies conduct the city’s hum. By placing a hand on a vibrating conduit, a Kin can ripple their own muscles or pulse their chest to mimic the structural shift of the machinery, passing complex, bass-heavy intentions to one another through the very architecture of the Basin.

They are rarely seen, and when they are, they resemble shadows caught in the silver twilight. They wear garments woven from the same bioluminescent lichen that carpets the lower alcoves, a soft, fuzzy mantle that glows a faint, sickly electric blue in the mist—the only color in a world of slate and oxidized green. 

Their life’s work is the Maintenance of the Pulse. They do not farm, and they do not cultivate. They are the tenders of the steam-valves and the wipers of the mineral-crusted seals. When a pressure valve hisses, a member of the Kin is often nearby, standing perfectly still, pressing their forehead against the shuddering iron, listening for the internal irregularities that signal a slowing of the city’s heart. 

They also share the cisterns with the gelatinous organisms that bloom in the condensation traps. In a sense, the Kin and the blobs are symbiotic: the Kin clear the inorganic silt from the intake pipes, ensuring the flow of water that the jellies require to feed. In return, the jellies exude a mineral-rich slime that the Kin harvest and smear into the cracks of their own skin, a nutrient-dense restorative that keeps them from becoming as brittle as the rusted girders they inhabit.

There is no concept of family among the Hollow-Kin. There is only the "Cycle." A new Kin is not born so much as they are nurtured from the moisture-rich alcoves, brought forth from a state of suspension—a damp, embryonic sleep—when the vibrations of the turbines reach a specific, rare harmonic frequency. Every few decades, when the basin groans in a certain, deep-bellied way, a new worker is "offered" by the mountain. 

They do not dream, or if they do, their dreams are strictly mechanical—visions of perfectly aligned cogs, of pressure held in eternal stasis, of the slow, grinding transition of basalt into fluid. They have no name for the city’s creator, and they have no interest in the world beyond the precipice. To them, the High Basin is not a place; it is the entire universe, and they are its rhythmic, shivering, silver-skinned breath.
