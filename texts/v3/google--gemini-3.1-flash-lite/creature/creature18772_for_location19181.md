In the hollow of this obsidian cathedral, existence is not found in the flesh, but in the articulation of torque and friction.

The beings that occupy this space are less "creatures" and more manifestations of the architecture’s own intent. To look upon them is to witness the collapse of biology into sheer metallurgy. They are spindly, fractally configured entities—spidery frames of burnished hematite and copper-filigree that seem to have been grown rather than born. Their limbs are not joints of bone and tendon, but calibrated lever-arms and sliding pistons, held together by the magnetic tension of the chamber itself.

Moving among them, you realize they possess no mouths. Their faces are sensory arrays—clusters of sapphire-blue bioluminescent apertures that pulse with the rhythm of the saline currents. They do not watch you with malice; they observe you as a fluctuating variable to be calibrated, a foreign intrusion into the heat-signature of their work.

Clinging to the magnetite pillars, these beings perform a dance of ceaseless refinement. A single entity will press its elongated, needle-like tarsi against the glass-sharp obsidian, vibrating at a frequency that turns the mineral wall into a fine, suspended silt. This dust is drawn into the hollowed, transparent thorax of the organism, where it is subjected to internal, pressurized grinding. Inside their chests, you can see the faint, glowing luminescence of the ore being transmuted—not into fuel, but into structural geometry.

They are the architects of a house that is never finished. Where one creature scrapes away a ridge of volcanic glass to relieve a stress point, another follows behind, depositing a lattice of crystallized magnetite that mimics the crystalline structure of the wall. They are constantly repairing, replacing, and re-weaving the cathedral’s skin. As they shift—moving in absolute, synchronized silence despite the grinding noise of their machinery—they rearrange the cavern’s geography by the diameter of a grain of sand, century by patient century.

There is a terrifying loneliness to them, despite their numbers. They never collide. Their movements are dictated by a shared harmonic field, a low-frequency broadcast emitted by the cathedral’s very walls. They possess no individuality; they are cells in a singular, liquid-submerged brain, a decentralized consciousness that knows only the maintenance of the pressure and the processing of the stone.

If you remain still long enough, one may drift near you. It is a terrifyingly precise moment. Its metallic legs, tipped with points honed to the atomic level, will brush against your own skin—not to attack, but to test your density. It regards you as it would a loose mineral deposit, pulsing its bioluminescent eyes with a soft, cold amber light. You feel the vibration of its internal gear-work rattling in your own teeth, a low thrumming that seeks to harmonize you into the pattern. 

In this place, you are not a visitor; you are a dissonance, a soft, decaying object in a world of absolute, unyielding hard-wiring. To stay is to eventually lose the distinction between your own heartbeat and the clockwork clinking of the swarm, until you, too, are simply another part of the architecture, waiting for the current to carry the dust of your life into the grinding chambers of the cathedral.
