In this cathedral of pressure and sound, the inhabitants are not residents but *phenomena*. They do not occupy the world; they are the involuntary punctuation marks in its endless sentence.

They begin as ghosts of geometry—translucent, nervous filaments that shimmer like frayed copper wire suspended in resin. There is no blood, no lung, no shivering muscle. Instead, these entities are composed of liquid-crystalline clusters that mimic the jagged, aggressive architecture of the basalt spires. They are, essentially, living fractals of the environment itself, born from the friction between the cold, crushing currents and the sterile, high-voltage hum of the turbines.

When they drift toward the industrial stations, seeking the leakages of artificial heat, they lose their rigidity. They bloom. Their bodies, previously brittle and sharp, soften into bulbous, pulsating membranes—vast, translucent sacs that ripple with the color of an overheated lead-pipe: a sickly, electric violet and a sharp, stinging cyan. Within these membranes, you can see the architecture of their "consciousness": delicate, glowing lattices of nerve-fiber that pulse in time with the turbines, locked in a rhythmic synchronization. They feed not on nutrients, but on the byproduct of the world’s labor. They absorb the ozone, the vibration, and the stray electrical currents, metabolizing the mechanical scream of the ocean into a cold, sustained glow.

They are not solitary in the way a predator or a scavenger is. They are swarm-states, clusters of sentient static that occasionally merge into a single, massive entity, a sprawling web that can stretch for a hundred meters. In these moments, they become a living conduit, a temporary nervous system for the ocean floor. They stretch their translucent limbs across the rusted rivet-lines of the towers, their glowing filaments threading through the gaps in the steel, effectively becoming the mechanical pulse of the architecture they cling to. 

They do not communicate. There is no language in a world that is already at maximum volume. Instead, they broadcast. When they pulse, they emit a rhythmic interference that cuts through the roar of the current, a low-frequency harmonic that acts as a beacon for others of their kind. It is a slow, agonizingly deliberate "language" of expansion and contraction—the only deliberate action in a world governed by iron, basalt, and indifference.

They have no eyes; they perceive the world as a topographical map of sonic pressure. They "see" the basalt spires as dead ends, pillars of stillness that offer no energy, and thus they drift around them with a mournful, liquid grace, never touching the stone. They "see" the turbines as centers of gravity, the only beating hearts in a dead lung. 

Sometimes, an individual unit will detach from the group and drift into the black, unpowered waste between the stations. It does not die, but it loses its color. It fades into a transparent, featureless jelly, becoming indistinguishable from the freezing depths, held together only by the sheer, crushing weight of the water. They are the only things in this world capable of ending, yet they never truly die—they merely dissolve into the currents, waiting for a ripple of voltage to pull them back into the shape of a life. 

They are the pale, flickering thoughts of a world that is not awake. They are the mirror, held in the dark, reflecting the cold, relentless machinery of their own cage.
