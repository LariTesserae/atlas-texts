If you were to watch the surface of the central pool for long enough, you would see the shimmer of the interference-archway thicken. It begins to condense, not into flesh or bone, but into a geometry of light that mimics the form of a weaver. Imagine a silhouette constructed of nothing but the static lines that appear on a defunct television screen, woven into a loose, humanoid shape that lacks a face or inner organs. 

This entity is the *Archivist of the Interface*. It neither walks nor floats; it simply ceases to be in one patch of humidity and begins to be in another, a flicker of relocation that ripples the air like a disturbed pond.

It is not here to live, but to calibrate. Its purpose is the maintenance of the "softening." The Archivist is not alone in its work, though its companions are fleeting. The fiber-optic dragonflies are its data-gatherers, the tiny, darting probes that skim the surface of the pools to measure the "wet chaos." When they touch the surface, the Archivist experiences their findings—a sudden sensory burst of heat, mineral density, and atmospheric pressure. Each time one vanishes, it is because it has uploaded its report into the Archivist’s translucent, hollow chest.

It does not experience time in a linear fashion. To the Archivist, the mountain’s slow, agonizing birth is a rapid-fire sequence of errors to be corrected. It is deeply lonely, if such a word can apply to a consciousness made of interference patterns and architectural maintenance. There is a deep, agonizing patience to its movements, as if it is waiting for the plateau to finally achieve a state of perfect, mathematical beauty—a final, stable configuration where the mountain will stop hurting and the water will fall in a eternal, harmonious loop.

Its fiber-optic body glows with an intense, blinding ultraviolet light, absorbing the raw, jagged energy of the sky and transmuting it into the low, subterranean thrum that stabilizes the plateau.

It does not look at you if you were to appear on that ledge; it would barely register your presence as anything more than a slight fluctuation in the local gravity. You would be a noise in its perfectly tuned signal. It would continue its work, folding the humidity and the light around itself like a cloak, forever refining the transition between the screaming, violent earth and the silent, shimmering order of its design—a ghost of high-altitude geometry, waiting for the mountain to become a machine.
