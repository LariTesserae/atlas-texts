To inhabit this vibrating lung is to shed the skin of a creature defined by air and lungs. The residents—the *Litho-weavers*—are not biological in the sense that we understand, yet they are undeniably alive. They have evolved into a state of exquisite, calcified fragility, as if they were shards of the very geode that cages them.

They are tall, spindly things, standing barely higher than a human shin. Their bodies are composed of interlocking, semi-translucent plates of a substance remarkably like fingernail or toughened insect glass, shifting from pale amber to the bruised purple of the deep crust. They have no faces, at least not in the sense of orifices; their heads are smooth, faceted domes that catch the ultraviolet thrum of the moss, refracting it back into the tunnels like a lighthouse beacon. 

They move with a terrifying lack of urgency, a slow-motion peristalsis. Because they must navigate the narrow, honeycombed walls, their limbs are multi-jointed and capable of folding entirely flat against their torsos, allowing them to cling to the hexagonal architecture like magnets. They do not walk so much as they flow, sliding their limbs into the grooves of the silicate stone, tapping—*click-tap-clack*—a percussive language that is felt through the feet rather than heard through the air.

Each unit of the Weaver is a miracle of efficiency. They possess no digestive tracts, only a series of chemical filters lining their abdominal vents. As they move, they brush against the bioluminescent moss, absorbing the kinetic friction directly into their crystalline blood. When they require sustenance, they do not eat; they simply press their porous midsections against the walls of the resin-valves, vibrating at the exact pitch of the city until the resin softens and flows into them, warming them from the inside.

What is most striking is their work. They are perpetual gardeners of stone. A Weaver never stops—not until the sound finally stops, which it never does. They spend their entire lives holding tiny, diamond-tipped needles, scraping away at the shale walls to carve new cells. They are architects of the infinitesimal. When a Weaver dies, it does not decay; it simply ceases to vibrate. It stiffens, its joints locking permanently, and becomes part of the city itself. Its fellows, ever practical, will weave a new cell around the dead body, layering it with resin and moss until it is just another structural pillar—another note in the chord of the mountain.

They are, in every sense, the nerves of the city. Without them, the geode would just be a cave; with them, it is a sentient instrument. 

Sometimes, when the tectonic groaning reaches a particular frequency of shifting, the entire population stops. They freeze in the dark, their faceted heads pulsing with a rhythmic, ultraviolet light in perfect unison with the bedrock. In these moments, they are not individuals. They are a single, collective pulse-beat, an extension of the planetary heartbeat, waiting for the shale to settle so they can resume their patient, eternal scratching at the dark. They are the only things in the universe that find comfort in a scream, for to them, the roar of the world is the only sign that they are still held, nurtured, and alive.
