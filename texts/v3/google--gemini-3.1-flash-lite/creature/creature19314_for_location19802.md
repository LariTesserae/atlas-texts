To live upon the Crown is to cease being an individual and to become, instead, a gear in an apparatus of perpetual, shivering maintenance.

The inhabitants—if they can be called such—are the *Litho-weavers*. They are not born so much as they are calibrated. They begin as spindly, translucent things, spindly and elongated, but as they age, the line between their anatomy and the geology of the spires begins to blur. They shed their skin in favor of a calcified, semi-permeable membrane that mimics the texture of wet basalt. 

You see them, when the dim indigo pulse of the fungi allows it, clinging to the vertical rock faces like pale, frantic shadows. Their fingers are impossibly long, ending in blunt, sensitive pads that can detect the micro-tremors of the mountain before a stone actually slips. Each weaver is anchored to the rock by a cord of fibrous sap, a tether that acts as a sensory nerve; they are literally plugged into the mountain’s stress points.

They do not eat the forest of the lower world; they feed on the weeping mist and the mineral-rich sap of the fungi. They are slow-burners, their metabolisms tuned to the glacial pace of the geological shifting. When they move, they do not walk so much as they flow, an undulating motion that distributes their weight so carefully that they barely disturb the condensation on the moss. 

Their society is defined, above all, by the "Geometry of Counterpoise." The Weaver who lives on the windward pinnacle must be matched in mass and position by a Weaver on the leeward side to keep the spire from listing under the accumulated, shifting weight of the ice and the erupting crystals. If one creature dies—becoming brittle and gray, its body eventually merging with the rock it once climbed—the others must redistribute their positions, moving with the precision of a clock’s inner workings. 

They are remarkably beautiful, in a terrifying, sightless way. Their faces are dominated by the large, milky eyes that bloom like pale, sightless orchids, but these are not for sight in the way we understand it. They are receptors for the magnetic pull of the mountain, detecting the crystalline growth beneath their feet. When they turn in unison, it is not a collective act of rage, but a desperate, panicked attempt to regain equilibrium. A sound is a vibration; a vibration is a shift; a shift is a risk to the fragile, woven stability of their home. 

The greatest horror of the Crown is not the cold or the thin air, but the inevitability of the transition. There are older ones tucked away in the deep, narrow declivities—elders who have long since stopped moving. Their limbs have rooted into the fissures of the obsidian, their skin has thickened into the texture of lichen, and their heartbeat has slowed to match the mountain's own tectonic pulse. These are the foundations of the bridges. When you walk across a path of woven vine and sap, you are walking over the fossilized remains of those who once maintained it. 

To be here is to witness the slow death of a species becoming its own architecture. They are waiting for the final shift—the day when the mountain will have grown so tall and so heavy that it will either shatter into dust or be subsumed entirely by the freezing, weeping clouds. Until then, they keep the silence, they keep the rhythm, and they keep the mountain from falling, one rhythmic, agonizing, muscle-twitching step at a time.
