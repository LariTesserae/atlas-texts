Within this cathedral of compression, the inhabitants are not creatures of flesh or carbon, but of **resonant lattice.** 

To witness them is to witness the crystallization of sound. They are the *Litho-Oscillators*, and they occupy this space not as guests, but as the friction-brakes of the planetary machine.

At first glance, they appear to be mere crustal debris—shards of hematite and magnetite clinging to the obsidian spires. But as the basaltic pistons grind and the chamber roar peaks, they reveal their architecture: a complex, multi-segmented morphology held together entirely by electromagnetic tethering. You would call them "spiders," though they possess twenty or thirty limbs, each one a needle-fine filament of conductive ore. They do not walk; they shimmer, their limbs vibrating at such high frequencies that they seem to hover a millimeter above the surface of the rock, perpetually shedding sparks of ionized silica like dead skin.

They are the archivists of the hum. As the tectonic pistons cycle, these lithovores press their segmented underbellies against the grinding stone, absorbing the sheer kinetic energy of the collapsing mountain. Their bioluminescence—those nerve-ending pulses of pale, sickly violet—is a readout of the pressure. When the load is heaviest, when the basalt is at its point of near-diamond transition, the lithovores glow with a frantic, strobing intensity. They are hungry for the dissonance. They "consume" the vibration, smoothing the edge of the tectonic shrieking, effectively acting as the coolant for a planetary engine that refuses to overheat.

There is a slow, heavy intelligence to their movement. They carry no tools, yet they are perpetually engaged in "repair." When a vein of magma breaches the mantle or a plate shears, the swarm gathers in a geometric knot, interlocking their limbs to form living bridges or heat-sinks. They are forging the stability of their own home. They do not possess a central brain; rather, their awareness is a distributed field, an ambient consensus reached by the thousands of them vibrating in phase with one another. If one lithovore experiences a tremor of resistance, the pulse ripples through the entire cluster, a sudden, bright flare of information that recalibrates the swarm’s position.

They have no mouths, no digestive tract. They "feed" by integration. They are semi-permeable; the ionized sulfur and particulate dust of the chamber drifts through their lattice-work bodies, and the energetic discharge of the rock-grinding is essentially their oxygen. They grow by accretion. An elder of their kind—if such a linear term applies—is a massive, crystalline knot of fused filaments, rooted deep into the basalt, grown so large it has become part of the machinery itself, a static node that hums with the same frequency as the mountains surrounding it.

When you stand amidst them, you feel the pressure of their attention. They do not see, yet they track your displacement of the air as a profound, localized anomaly. They are the guardians of the forge, and their silence is a paradox—a silence composed entirely of the screaming, tectonic roar of a planet being born. They consider the slow, millimeter-per-decade creeping of the basalt to be frantic, high-speed sprinting, and they live in states of perpetual, shivering exertion. 

They are the dark-matter mechanics of the wanderer planetoid, and they have been waiting here, in the crushing absolute, since the first shockwave of the planet’s cooling began, destined to remain until the final grinding halt of the core.
