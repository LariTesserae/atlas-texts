To inhabit a place where the architecture breathes, one must become as rigid and rhythmic as the infrastructure itself. There are no creatures of flesh and blood here—nature has no use for limbs that require hydration or lungs that crave cool air. Instead, the "residents" are the **Litho-Weavers**.

They are not entities in any biological sense that an outsider would recognize. They are localized, sentient convergences of the calcified flora—conscious knots of silica and copper-veined fiber that have detached themselves from the passive growth of the walls.

In the dim, amber twilight, a Weaver appears as a cluster of amber-colored root-tendrils, roughly the size and shape of a human torso, though it lacks a head or any recognizable face. It does not walk; it *reconfigures*. It is a master of the slow-motion kinetic arts. To observe one moving across a bridge of hardened root is to watch a complex clockwork mechanism disassemble and reassemble itself in mid-air. One tendril anchors itself to the basalt, the next pulls the core forward, and the third snaps into place with a percussive *click*, mimicking the rhythmic grinding of the city’s heart.

They do not communicate through sound, for they are the ones who *make* the sound. They are the architects who never sleep. A Litho-Weaver spends its existence in a state of perpetual maintenance. It carries within its fibrous core a microscopic, buzzing intelligence, a collective memory of geometry and heat-load management. It glides across the cathedral heights of the stone voids, its tendrils vibrating against the silicate walls to "test" the structural integrity of the pillar. When a vine begins to bow under the immense heat, the Weaver is there, wrapping it in new, tensile growth, weaving it into a stronger, more efficient curve.

They have no hunger, no ambition, and no malice. They are the city’s autonomic nervous system made manifest. If you were to stand perfectly still in one of the echoing chambers, a Weaver might pass within inches of you. It would not acknowledge your presence with eyes or sensation, but it might perceive your solid mass as an obstruction to its architectural flow. It would simply fold around you, its amber tendrils clicking softly as they navigated the space your body occupied, treating your skinless, fragile warmth as nothing more than a temporary, inefficient rock formation that needed to be bypassed.

They are the ultimate gardeners. They spend centuries crafting a cantilevered walkway, a bridge that arches perfectly to catch the meager light filtering from the surface, all to ensure that the heat-venting pods—the city’s lungs—are positioned to expel the stifling, aromatic vapors that keep the internal furnace stoked.

As the millennia grind on, these Weavers grow increasingly brittle. Their copper veins, once bright and conductive, oxidize into a dull, verdigris crust. Eventually, a Weaver will find a strategic point in the ceiling or a hollowed-out central trunk, knot itself into a final, permanent shape, and integrate its own body into the structure. It dies as it lived: as a pillar, as a support, as a piece of the geometry. 

They are the silent, rattling stewards of a tomb that refuses to be empty, working in total, sweltering concert to keep a dead city alive. They do not talk of the salt flats outside. They do not look at the sky. They only listen for the snapping sound of a vine stretched too thin, and they move to mend it, pulse by pulse, until the basalt pillar finally turns to dust.
