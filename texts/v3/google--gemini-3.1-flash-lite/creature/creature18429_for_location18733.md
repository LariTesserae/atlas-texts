The inhabitants of the Spire-Basin, known to themselves as the *Vellum-Kin*, are beings born of the same suspension that cradle their mountains. They are neither wholly aquatic nor terrestrial, but rather creatures of hydrostatic equilibrium—translucent, elongated figures whose physiology is a mirror to the viscous medium.

Their skin is a pale, opalescent film, stretched over a framework of cartilage rather than bone. It possesses a subtle, iridescent quality, shimmering like oil on water when they pass through pockets of high-density mineral. They lack distinct facial features as humanity understands them; instead, their heads are smooth, tapering domes. Beneath the thin veil of their skin, a complex network of rhythmic, glowing organ-sacs functions as both a nervous system and a communication array. When they are calm, these sacs glow in a soft, steady amber, perfectly synchronized with the pulse of the woven city. When they are startled or mourning, the light bleeds into a frantic, staccato flicker, a rapid-fire code that ripples through the kelp lattices like silent lightning.

Movement among the Vellum-Kin is a deliberate art. Because the fluid resists velocity, they have evolved a method of propulsion that resembles the slow bloom of a jellyfish. They extend long, ribbon-like appendages from their waists—delicate, webbed filaments that they unfurl to catch the currents or use to anchor themselves to the needle-spires. To watch them traverse the space between peaks is to watch a silk ribbon caught in a gentle draft. They do not walk; they drift, turning in slow, elegant spirals, their limbs trailing behind them like streamers of fog.

They are a society of architects and graftsmen. To be a Vellum-Kin is to be obsessed with the tension of the lattice. With fingers that end in sensory tips capable of feeling the mineral precipitation on the obsidian rock, they spend their cycles threading *Amnion-kelp* stalks into self-repairing knots. They do not use tools in the traditional sense; they use *intent*. By humming at specific frequencies—low-frequency vibrations that harmonize with the bone-creak of the mountains—they encourage the mosses to bind to their structures, weaving the architecture directly into the biology of the rock.

There is a profound, meditative stillness to their culture. They do not possess a concept of "property," for the city is always in flux, always growing, always waiting for a shift in current to re-sculpt its boundaries. Instead, they value "resonance." A Vellum-Kin is judged by how well they harmonize with the city's pulse. A master weaver is someone who can hold the stress of a kelp-nesting against the shifting currents without causing a single node to dim.

During the Great Change—that terrifying, beautiful transition from periwinkle to bruised violet—the Kin become almost ghost-like. They retreat into the deepest, central husks of the lattice clusters, wrapping their own limbs around the structural stalks. They draw their bioluminescence inwards, darkening until they are nearly invisible against the obsidian. In those moments, the city goes blind, a cluster of silent, lightless pods suspended in a thickening dark.

They are the caretakers of a world that is still deciding what it wants to be. They look at the spires, still pushing upward with the slow, crystalline snap of calcifying bone, and they see a future that is forever in the past tense. They live with the patience of geology, never hurrying, never finishing, knowing that their existence—much like the protein snow falling from the impossible, unreachable surface—is merely a gentle, luminous drift through an endless, heavy cradle.
