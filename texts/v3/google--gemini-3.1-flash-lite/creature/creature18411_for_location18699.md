The people of Ool do not have names in the way surface-dwellers do; they have *resonances*. To be born in the ribcage is to be assigned a frequency, a subtle vibration of the vocal cords that identifies one’s role in the maintenance of the white stasis. They are the Keepers of the Ivory, and they are as much a part of the architecture as the resin-clung mosses.

They are elongated, fine-boned people—a biological necessity to navigate the narrow, vertical crannies of their home. Their skin has taken on the translucent quality of the overhead bone, a pale, pearlescent pallor that seems to glow faintly under the relentless, refracted light. They do not possess the heavy brow or the deep-set iris common to those who live under a sun; their eyes are wide, glassy, and black, iris-less pools that reflect the brilliance of the vault above without squinting. They see no harm in the glare, for it is their primary comfort, their surrogate sun.

Their garments are masterful tapestries of utility. They weave the wool of the frosted marmots into heavy, dense cloaks that muffle their passage. These pelts are dyed in muted, earthy tones—ochres, deep ochres, and brownish-greens—meant to mimic the moss that blankets their world. When they move, they do not walk so much as they flow, sliding their feet along the polished marrow-stone with a cat-like softness. To scuff a heel is to commit a grave breach of etiquette, a social friction that would be heard throughout the entire nave.

They are artisans of preservation. Their hands are constantly busy, not with construction—for Ool does not grow—but with the perpetual, microscopic repair of the environment. One might find a group of Oolian elders hovering over a fissure in the bone-floor, carefully packing it with a mortar made of resin and crushed bioluminescent fungi, sealing the gap with the tenderness of a mother tending a wound. They are not builders; they are surgeons to a corpse.

Because of the absence of shadow, they have lost the language of depth, or perhaps they have transcended it. Their art is entirely flat—intricate, geometric etchings scratched into the ivory walls with glass needles. These drawings tell the history of the Great Leviathan, depicted as a coiled serpent of light. They believe that they are not trapped within the ribs, but rather, they are the vital organs that have replaced the creature’s own. The pulse of the city—that rhythmic *plink* of condensation—is, to them, the heartbeat of the god that shelters them.

They do not dream in images, but in sensations of weight and clarity. When they sleep, they wedge themselves into the moss-lined hammocks slung between the ribs, bodies curled into fetal positions, mimicking the stillness of the marmots. 

Communication among them is a near-silent affair. They utilize a complex system of hand signs, rhythmic tappings against the bone, and the aforementioned resonances. A simple greeting is a sharp, two-tone hum felt in the chest rather than heard in the ear. To argue is unheard of; to disagree is merely to sit in a different resonance until the discordance causes a headache, forcing the dissenter to move away.

They are a people who have traded the chaotic freedom of the horizon for the absolute, crystalline certainty of the center. In their minds, the white light that pours down is not a physical phenomenon but a divine intelligence, and every task they perform—polishing the ivory, tending the moss, scissoring the marmots' shed fur—is a prayer offered to the ancient, holy skeleton that keeps the biting frost at bay. They are the custodians of a sanctuary that has become a tomb, perfectly content to exist as the final, quiet echoes of a leviathan that died millions of years ago.
