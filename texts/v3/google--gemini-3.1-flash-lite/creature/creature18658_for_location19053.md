To dwell upon this swaying loom is to exist as a flicker in the periphery of a titan’s inhale. Those who cling to the High Verdant Void—the *Sutured People*—are not quite terrestrial, nor are they truly aerial. They are elongated, thin-boned, and hushed. Their skin is the color of bruised plums or deep-sea shadows, a pigment evolved to mirror the indigo dimness of the phosphorescent fungi. Their eyes are vast, obsidian orbs, lidless and wet, designed to catch the faintest glimmer of a pulsing spore or the shifting geometry of the vine-mesh.

They move with a terrifying, liquid economy. A dweller does not walk; they undulate. Their joints are double-hinged, allowing them to wrap their limbs around the calcified vines like mountain climbers made of mercury. Because the ground beneath them is always shifting—rising and falling with the Great Breath—they possess a preternatural sense of balance, a vestibular system so finely tuned that they can sleep suspended over the abyss, their fingers hooked into the fibrous bark, never waking even as the vine shudders beneath them.

They wear little. Why should they? Fabric would only snag on the thorns. Instead, they paint themselves with the waxy, translucent secretions of the nectar-sacs, a protective glaze that holds in their body heat and repels the biting, freezing moisture that wicks from the dark. 

Their lives are measured in the *Tear*. A Tear is the time it takes for a bloom to unfold, release its spores, and wither back into the lattice. They do not build houses in a way a surface-dweller would understand; they graft themselves. When a child reaches maturity, they choose a depression in a primary vine and begin the "Binding." They use silver-threaded moss—a parasite they cultivate—to weave a hollow nest directly into the living tissue. As the vine grows, it envelops the reed-work, pulling it tight against its own hardened hull until the dwelling is indistinguishable from the plant itself. It is a home that breathes with the occupant, a room that heats when the occupant grows cold.

Their culture is one of rhythmic obedience. They are silent people, for to scream or shout would be to waste the atmosphere needed for survival. Instead, they communicate through a complex sign language rendered in the dark with the help of small, bioluminescent crystals harvested from the ceiling-shadows—brief, strobing flickers of blue and white that dance across the abyss like silent lightning.

They worship the Skiff-Gliders with a reverence that borders on terror. To them, these city-block-sized silhouettes are the lungs’ immune system, harbingers of the harvest. When a Glider passes, the people grow perfectly still, clinging to the underside of the vine until the creature’s shadow has vanished. They believe if they move, they will be mistaken for a parasite and pruned away by the world’s colossal, unconscious reach.

There is a profound, aching melancholy in their existence. They know nothing of metal, nothing of fire, nothing of horizons. They are survivors on a vertical tightrope, harvesting the blood of a sleeping god to keep their own hearts beating. They do not hope for an escape; they know no such concept exists. There is only the vine, the chill, the rhythmic thrum of the moist air, and the absolute need to be as light, as silent, and as fleeting as the pollen that sustains them. 

When a dweller dies, they do not bury their dead; they release the grip. They let the body fall into the lower, darker depths of the abyss, a plummet that lasts for hours, their remains becoming mulch for the deep roots, returning the borrowed calcium back to the skeleton of the world. They are the only thing in the Void that is not truly a part of the plant, and yet, in the end, they are the only thing that completes it.
