The inhabitants of the Hanging Vernal, known to themselves as the *Latticewalkers*, do not inhabit their world so much as they are an extension of its nervous system. 

They are gaunt, elongated figures, their skin the texture of a peeled lotus root—pale, almost pearlescent, and faintly damp to the touch. Their limbs are unsettlingly long, possessing extra articulations that allow them to coil around the thicker structural vines like tendrils. They do not have eyes in the conventional sense; instead, their faces are dominated by elaborate, feathered sensory pits that ripple with soft, iridescent color. These pits track the atmospheric pressure and the minute, electric thrum of the bioluminescent pulp that flows through the lattice beneath their feet.

Because the Vernal is a world of eternal, weeping moisture, the Latticewalkers are essentially hydroponic. They do not eat in the way earth-bound creatures do. Instead, they press their broad, porous palms against the saturated bark of their dwellings, absorbing nutrient-rich condensation through their skin. Their metabolism is glacial, mirroring the slow, rhythmic *plip-plop* of the falling droplets. A conversation between two residents might take three hours, punctuated by long, meditative intervals as they wait for the condensation to shift or for a flux-pocket to relocate the ground beneath their weightless heels.

Their society is devoid of haste, industry, or ambition. They are the gardeners of a continental-sized sculpture, and their lives are dedicated to the "Great Shaping." A Latticewalker may spend their entire maturity coaxing a single vine to bridge a chasm, guiding its growth through secret, artisanal incisions, singing songs of pitch and resonance that dictate how the plant-fiber hardens. They view themselves not as masters of their environment, but as the friction that keeps reality from drifting away entirely.

When the gravity shifts—when the floor tilts and the abyss yawns beside them instead of beneath them—the Latticewalkers do not panic. They are elegant in their reaction. They retrieve the polished stone weights that hang from their waist-sashes, swinging them like pendulums to create opposing momentum. They step into the flux with the grace of dancers, letting their bodies swing out over the void, anchored only by the silk-cords they have woven into their own garments. In those moments, they appear less like sentient beings and more like the airborne, drifting jellyfish that colonize the humid air around them.

The most elderly among them—those who have survived the shifting of the roots for centuries—become nearly stationary. They find a nexus point where the architecture is densest, a place of convergence where the sap-silk is thickest. There, they anchor themselves, weaving their own limbs into the growth until they are indistinguishable from the structure itself. Their skin thickens into bark, their sensory pits dim, and they become the living pylons upon which the younger generations walk.

There is no malice in this existence, nor is there joy in the human sense. There is only a profound, damp resonance. They are the tenders of a suspended garden, waiting out the slow history of the planet below, content to be the weavers of a tapestry that is perpetually unraveling and being painstakingly stitched back together, one drop of water at a time. They are the living silence of the Vernal, as calm and hollow and ageless as the mist that birthed them.
