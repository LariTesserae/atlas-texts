The inhabitants of Orem-Vahl are not biological in the way a visitor from the shifting, decaying world might expect. They are beings of temperate stasis, possessed of a physiology that seems to have been calcified by the very atmosphere they breathe. 

They are tall, slender figures, their skin possessing the matte, porous quality of unglazed porcelain or sun-bleached bone. They do not possess lungs that work in cycles of intake and exhalation; instead, they exist in a state of suspended respiration. If one were to place an ear against the chest of an Orem-Vahl artisan, they would hear no heartbeat—only the faint, crystalline hum of trapped energy, like a tuning fork vibrating at the edge of human perception.

Their attire is a testament to their eternal occupation. They wear garments of stiff, heavy weave, the fabric stiffened by the same perpetual dampness that coats the basalt. These clothes do not move with the wearers, but rather shift alongside them, as if they were part of a singular, articulated sculptural form. They do not walk so much as they transition; one moment they are standing by a lathe, and the next they are occupying a space three feet away, without the intervening blur of motion.

Their eyes are their most startling feature: horizontal slits of pale, luminescent iris, lacking pupils. They do not blink, for there is no grit in this air, no dust to irritate, and no moisture that needs to be spread. They observe the world with an intensity that is not curious, but rather proprietary. They look at the carvings they began eons ago with an expression of quiet, absolute satisfaction, as if the fact that the work remains unfinished is not a failure, but a perfection in itself.

Linguistically, they are silent. Communication, to them, is a matter of alignment. They sit together on the obsidian terraces, their shoulders almost, but never quite, touching. In this proximity, they exchange thoughts that look like ripples on the surface of a deep pool—a subtle angling of the head, a shift in the way a hand rests upon the knee. These are not conversations, but mutual confirmations of the world’s current state.

They do not dream, for dreams require a subconscious that drifts. Instead, they recount the "Great Arrival"—the moment Orem-Vahl was caught in the glass bell of the mountain. They remember the mountain while it was cooling, when the world still had the capacity to change. They are the custodians of that last, frantic energy, now preserved in the stillness of their own veins. 

When they sit at their tables, their bowls filled with a grain that never softens and never rots, they do not eat for sustenance. They pick up a handful of the grain and let it sift through their long, needle-thin fingers, watching the individual kernels hang against the white light like suspended dust motes. They are not waiting for a meal, nor for death, nor for the future. They are the curators of the present, the only beings capable of holding a single, solitary second in their palms until it becomes a permanent monument.

They look like statues of themselves, cast in the moment before a final decision was made, eternally poised to finish a carving or take a bite, but choosing, in the sweet, breathless, frozen logic of Orem-Vahl, to remain exactly as they are.
