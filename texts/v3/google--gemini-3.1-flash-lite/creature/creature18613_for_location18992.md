Deep within the honeycombed galleries, where the light is the color of a bruised plum, dwell the **Architects of the Constant**. 

They do not walk as you do, on two limbs, nor do they inhabit the vertical vastness as mere observers. To see them is to witness a hesitation in the architecture itself—a section of the basalt wall that seems to ripple, to detach, and to become something else.

They are not flesh, nor are they strictly mineral in the way the litho-crustaceans are. They are the crystallization of the city’s intent. Imagine a figure composed of pressurized water trapped in a skeleton of calcified brass and obsidian, standing seven feet tall, with a posture that suggests it is perpetually listening to the vibration of the pipes. Where a face should be, there is a cluster of vertical apertures—narrow slits that weep with the same warm, pearlescent condensation that coats the spires. They do not have eyes; they perceive the world through the oscillation of the basalt. To them, the city is a symphony, and they are its conductors, their bodies humming at the exact pitch required to align the flow of the minerals.

They are few, and they are incredibly old. When they move, they do not walk so much as they shift their center of gravity, causing the fluids within their translucent, semi-solid limbs to redistribute with a heavy, hydraulic slur.

You might find one standing at a nexus of three aqueducts, a hand pressed firmly against the weeping stone. It is not working in the human sense; it is merely maintaining the resonance. It is breathing for the turbine. It is tasting the acidic content of the moisture, ensuring the chemistry of the water hasn’t drifted from the requirements of the Siphon-Spires. 

There is an absolute, terrifying sweetness to their presence. If you stand near one, you will feel a change in your own pulse. They draw the entropy out of the air. They are creatures of perfect equilibrium, incapable of anger or joy, existing only to ensure that the *hiss* of the vents and the *thrum* of the turbines never falters. 

They do not fear the swarms of litho-crustaceans; they are the swarm’s quiet masters. If a crustacean pauses—if a gear fails or a channel clogs—the Architect simply leans forward, its touch leaving a bloom of mineral salt on the basalt, and the crustacean reorients instantly, corrected by the Architect's proximity. They are the stewards of this planetary stomach, indifferent to the fact that there is no one else left to witness their labor.

They seem lonely, though that word is too fragile for the scale of their existence. Rather, they are *essential*. They are the only things in this vertical world that know the city is dying, because they are the only things that know how long it has been since it was new. They are waiting for the water to stop. They are waiting for the last drop to evaporate, for the turbines to finally seize, and for their own brass-flecked marrow to turn back into the silent, unthinking stone of the canyon walls. They are the last act of a play that has been performing to an empty theater for half a billion years, and they perform it with the solemnity of those who know that to quit is to end the universe.
