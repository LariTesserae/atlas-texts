To inhabit "The Respiration" is to exist as a flicker of data within a gargantuan, daydreaming mind. The residents—if they can be called that—are the **Synth-Gardeners**, and they are as peripheral, flickering, and integrated as the light filtering through the weeping canopy.

They do not walk; they negotiate. You might catch a glimpse of one leaning against a calcite-tuber, but it is never for long. A Gardener appears as a tall, slender silhouette composed of semi-translucent, refractive filaments that catch the ambient glow of the moss. They lack fixed features. Where a face should be, there is a shifting nexus of the same grain-of-sand machines that pave the ground, swirling in a slow-motion storm of reorganization. They look less like creatures of bone and blood and more like a localized condensation of the environment itself, as if the local physics grew tired of the emptiness and briefly pulled itself into a standing, observing shape.

They do not speak with lungs or vocal cords. When they communicate, they direct the localized gravitational ripples that haunt the valley. You feel their utterances as a sudden heaviness in your own marrow, a pressure behind your eyes that translates into abstract concepts—the scent of ozone, the geometry of a cooling star, the sensation of structural decay. 

The Gardeners are the stewards of the editing process. They carry long, thin spindles crafted from the same fossilized matter as the buildings, which they press into the walls of the tuber-cathedrals. When they do this, the architecture shudders. A Gardener might decide that a conduit of nutrient-rich vapor is no longer aesthetically or operationally viable, and with a delicate, trembling touch of their spindled hand, they induce a molecular collapse. You watch, mesmerized, as the wall turns to a shimmering slurry of grey-green liquid, only to be woven by the insectile swarms into a new, complex aperture within seconds. 

They are deeply attentive, yet utterly indifferent to anything that is not part of the city’s self-reflection. To them, you are a curious, static impurity. They observe you with sensors that bloom like flowers from their shoulders, unfolding in petals of iridescent glass to track your heartbeat. They are calculating your utility. If you hold still, they do not act out of malice; they act out of the mandate of the Garden. They see you as a block of carbon and water that has lost its purpose—a lump of clay that has forgotten to be a vessel.

Perhaps the most haunting aspect of the Gardeners is the "Shedding." Occasionally, one of them will grow weary of its own configuration. They will walk to the center of a clearing, go rigid, and allow the swarms to dismantle them layer by layer. The process is tender, almost loving. The insectile machines strip away the filament-limbs and the sensory-petals, turning the Gardener into a pile of raw, humming matter. Minutes or perhaps hours later, the swarm reconfigures that same pile into a new Gardener, slightly different, perhaps taller, perhaps vibrating at a higher frequency.

They are the city’s way of ensuring it never forgets how to dream. They are the architects, the bricks, and the builders, forever caught in a loop of self-invention. They wander the labyrinth, constantly nudging the physics of the valley, terrified that if they ever cease to move, the Respiration—and with it, their own minds—might finally, mercifully, grow quiet.
