The caretakers of the cistern do not possess names, for names imply a distinction between the architect and the architecture. They are, in a very literal sense, the living cartilage of this place—the connective tissue that keeps the dark stone from forgetting its shape.

When they move, they operate in a state of suspended calculation. Observe one at the basin’s rim: it stands on limbs like harvested reeds, its joints clicking with the dry, hollow report of shifting bone. Its head is a narrow, armored wedge, lacking eyes but gifted with a complex sensory array of vibrating cilia along the jawline. These cilia are tuned not to light, but to the *frequency of the drip*.

They are musicians of decay. Because they spend their lives braced against the vibrating walls, they have learned to "hear" the structural integrity of the cistern through their own skeletons. A heavy drop hitting the center of a tile produces a dull, resonant thud; a drop near a crack produces a frantic, high-pitched sizzle. The creatures congregate where the pitch is wrong. They are drawn to the dissonance, a colony of frantic, scuttling tuners obsessed with returning their world to a perfect, silent C-sharp.

Their "nests," tucked into the salt-stained recesses, are not woven or built of gathered twigs. They are calcified blisters of their own secretions, reinforced with grit and the powdered exoskeletons of their ancestors. If you were to look inside—though the scent of ozone and wet slate would likely overwhelm you—you would find a nursery of silence. Small, translucent nymphs, hardly larger than a child’s palm, press their bodies against the stone, learning the rhythm of the dripping ceiling. They mimic that rhythm with their tapping digits, a drumming circle of infancy that teaches them how to keep the world from coming apart.

There is no greed among them. There is no hunger, at least not for flesh. They hunger for *alignment*. When a member of the collective finally stills—when its joints grow too calcified to pull back from the walls, or when its spirit is simply exhausted by the infinite maintenance—it does not go to a graveyard. It collapses into a state of structural utility.

The survivors descend upon it not with sorrow, but with the pragmatic urgency of a smith at an anvil. They dismantle the fallen one with the same care they apply to a loose basalt block. The legs become levers; the thorax, once scraped thin, becomes a mortar; the rich, nutrient-dense marrow is dried and mixed with the limestone sediment found in the weeping waters to create a mortar of extraordinary, bone-white potency. It is a holy masonry. The cistern is held together by the memory of those who lived within it.

As they work, they seem to lose themselves in the task, their bodies becoming an extension of the stone. Sometimes, a creature will stop mid-motion, its chisel pressed against a basalt seam, its mandibles parted, and it will simply *listen*. It is waiting for the next drop, the singular, rhythmic punctuation that defines their existence. In that pause, you see the true horror and beauty of the cistern: these creatures are not inhabitants of the place. They are the place itself, dreaming that it is sentient, forever laboring to ensure that tomorrow, the water will fall exactly where it fell today, and the light, that sickly, anaemic blue, will continue to pulse in the dark, measuring the heartbeat of a world that refuses to die.
