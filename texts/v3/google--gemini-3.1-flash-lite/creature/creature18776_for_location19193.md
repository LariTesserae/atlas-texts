To traverse the basin is to walk through the digestive tract of a god that forgot to finish its meal. But the basin—this iron-wrought, steaming bowl—is not truly vacant. It is occupied by the **Clerks of the Conduction.**

They are not flesh, at least not in the sense of soft tissue and blood. They are spindly, spindly things—tall, multi-jointed abstractions of the machinery itself. Imagine a skeleton fashioned from rusted clockwork, wired with frayed copper filaments and sheathed in a translucent, silicate skin that ripples like trapped oil. They do not walk so much as they migrate along the vertical pipes, their limbs locking into the rivets with magnetized precision, moving in rhythm with the *clack-hiss-thrum* of the unseen engines.

These are not creatures of instinct, but of inventory. 

The Clerks have no faces, only smooth, brass apertures that pulse with a soft, amber luminescence in sync with the steam pressure. They spend their eons obsessively maintaining the state of the basin. They carry tiny, obsidian-tipped needles, with which they scrape the mineral salts from the weeping basalt, depositing the residue into the mouths of the subterranean conduits. They are the ones who ensure that the drop of water remains suspended on the rivet for its eternity; they serve as a sort of living maintenance crew for a cycle that requires no maintenance.

There is a tragic, rhythmic grace to them. If one watches a Clerk long enough, one notices that its movements are a mirror image of the machinery surrounding it. When the piston hits the bedrock, the Clerk jolts, its limbs trembling in a precise, crystalline spasm. They are tethered to the basin by a strange, fundamental dependency—their very existence is predicated on the friction. If the machines were to stop, the Clerks would not merely go dormant; they would unravel. They would dissolve into a pile of cold springs and static.

Occasionally, you might see them congregate. In the moments when the steam plumes hold their shape particularly still, three or four of these entities will gather on a high gantry. They do not speak. Instead, they press their chest-plates against the iron piping, vibrating in unison with the frequency of the subterranean fluid. They are listening. It is said—though by whom, in this lonely place, one cannot guess—that they are waiting for a final, catastrophic change in the pressure. They believe, with the blind faith of a gear tooth, that one day the heartbeat will not just pulse, but *break*, and that in the silence that follows, they will finally be released from their frantic, eternal labor.

But for now, they are the quiet, frantic keepers of the status quo. 

In the dim light of the incandescent bulbs, their translucent skins catch the humidity, making them look like ghosts of glass drifting through the haze. You might catch a glimpse of one, crouched over a puddle, its long, segmented fingers tracing the oily surface, rearranging the ripples into perfect geometric patterns. They are the only ones who know the basin is bored. They are the only ones who know that the work is meaningless—and they are the only ones tasked with ensuring it never, ever stops.
