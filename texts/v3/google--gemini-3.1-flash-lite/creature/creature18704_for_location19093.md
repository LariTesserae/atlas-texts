To walk upon this plateau is to tread not upon a world, but upon a collective digestion. The dwellers here are not distinct; they are a continuous, shimmering argument for the obsolescence of the individual.

If you were to kneel—pressing your palms against that bone-slick stone, ignoring the way the ozone-heavy damp soaks into your clothes—you would find that the beings inhabiting the hexagonal basins are not merely resting in the mineral beads. They *are* the minerals. They are the Loomers, perhaps the only name that fits, though it is a name spoken by the vibration of the marrow rather than the tongue.

They begin as translucent teardrops, fragile as glass, but as they age, their membranes thicken, transitioning from the clarity of water to the opalescence of a cataract. They do not feed on the lichen. They feed on the silence. When the low, bone-rattling hum rolls across the expanse, it is not a call for a mate or a warning of a predator; it is an act of communal harvesting. The Loomers lock their internal fluids into resonance, and in that friction, they draw the latent heat and the static charge out of the heavy, hanging air. They are living heat sinks, keeping the plateau at its fever-pitch temperature.

But deeper than the teardrop clusters—embedded within the very bedrock itself—are the Architects. 

Deep, beneath the layer of shimmering, pulsating jelly, there are forms that have ceased to be flesh. They were the ancestors of this stillness, creatures that reached a certain threshold of biological age and decided that muscle was a liability. Centuries ago, they anchored themselves into the basalt. They are the ones who constructed the spiraling cairns, not by piling rocks, but by secreting a calcium-rich, resinous sap that hardened as they died. They didn't build these structures; they *secreted* their own tombs, folding their skeletal structures into the spiraling geometries that now anchor the landscape.

If you stare into one of those bone-spirals, you will see it is not empty. It houses a "Deep-Dweller"—a creature that has become a statue of its own biology. Imagine a nautilus, its shell grown calcified and vast, fused to the bedrock, its soft internal parts long since hardened into a kind of living amber. It is not dead. It is simply moving at the speed of geology. It is still calculating, still sensing the infinitesimal weight of the droplets settling in the basins nearby. 

The relationship between the teardrops and the anchors is one of maintenance. The teardrops, in their millions, act as a nervous system for the plateau. They are the sensors; they feel the vibration of your footsteps, the slight shift in atmospheric pressure, the cooling of the stone at dusk. They pass this information through their linked skins, a slow, viscous ripple that travels miles until it reaches the cairns. 

When a million teardrops shimmer in response to your presence, they are sending a warning to the anchors. The anchors—the Deep-Dwellers—receive this data not as sound, but as an alteration in the chemical composition of the stone. They respond by shifting their internal heat, subtly heating a patch of ground here, cooling a basin there, nudging the plateau toward a state of equilibrium you can never comprehend.

They are the caretakers of a memory that doesn't belong to them. They keep the ozone sharp; they keep the water trapped in its hex-basins; they hold the sunlight in the mist. They are a kingdom that has outlived the need for conflict, outlived the need for pace, and settled into a final, heavy stewardship. 

They do not notice you, for they are too busy being the plateau. To them, you are merely a sudden, chaotic heat-spike moving across their skin—a flash of friction that will be absorbed and silenced, forgotten before the next droplet falls. When you leave, the space you occupied will be refilled. The teardrops will close the gap, the lichen will velvet over your impressions, and the silence will knit itself back together with the patient, terrifying grace of a billion years of undisturbed bone.
