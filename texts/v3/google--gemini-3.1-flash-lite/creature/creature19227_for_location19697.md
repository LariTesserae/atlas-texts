The inhabitants are called the *Lithos-Walkers*, though they possess no language for names, only a series of percussive clicks and sighs that mirror the moisture-thick air. To see them is to mistake them for part of the landscape—shadows shifting against the bruised-purple glow of the lichen.

They are elongated, their forms refined by the constant necessity of adhesion. Their skin isn't quite flesh; it has the matte, non-reflective quality of wet slate, dappled with patches of pale, fungal bioluminescence that pulse in slow, rhythmic sync with the condensation trickling down the spire. They are spindly, possessing extra articulations in their fingers and toes, which end in widened, sensitive pads that can detect the subtlest vibration of water moving through the stone’s pores. They never walk in the way land-dwellers do; they flow, pressing their chests against the basalt, feeling for the shifting tremors of the wind before it strikes.

They do not gather. They fear the density of groups, for in the vertical void, mass is a liability. Instead, they operate as solitary poets of the cliff-face. A single individual might spend a lifetime—a long, slow span measured in the darkening of a moss patch—on a stretch of rock no larger than a hanging tapestry.

When they move, they move with a terrifying, liquid grace. They are light; their bones are porous, seemingly hollowed out to mimic the volcanic stone they share. Their eyes are vast, crystalline orbs that possess no iris, designed to track the erratic, frantic dashes of the translucent amphibians that provide their only sustenance. They do not kill these creatures with violence, but with a patient, predatory stillness, waiting until the amphibian’s own momentum carries it into the reach of their long, sticky tongues. 

Their culture is one of profound, silent stewardship. They see themselves not as masters of the spire, but as its sentient condensation. When they carve a bowl into the rock, they are not claiming property; they are creating an offering, a small, still eye in the storm of the waterfall. They knap obsidian not for weapons, but for the satisfaction of the *clink*—a focused, sharp sound that briefly pierces the monotonous roar of the mist, reassuring them that they still occupy a space within the soundscape.

There is no procreation as the world knows it. Upon the death of a walker, they simply press themselves into a crevice, their translucent skin hardening, dissolving into the mineral crust, providing the base for a new, glowing bloom of lichen. They do not grieve; they understand that they are merely redistributing their moisture back into the spire.

They are creatures of terminal patience. They spend hours watching the way a single runnel of water branches over a jagged edge. They listen to the fog. They are the human echo of the abyss, and when the clouds shift and the light strikes the spire just right, they are nearly invisible—a shimmer, a ripple, a soft, damp secret held against the skin of the mountain, waiting for the rock to finally, mercifully, wear away into nothing.
