The inhabitants of the Basin are not merely dwellers; they are the antibody-folk, the constant pruners of a god that refuses to stop dreaming into existence. 

They are small, slender beings, their skin the color of river-slicked shale, often patterned with faint, mottled lichen-like patches that shift in hue depending on the local pH of the sap they spend their days handling. They possess no hair, for hair is too easily snagged by the aggressive tendrils of the Lattice; instead, their heads are smooth and cold to the touch, often adorned with minute, weeping pores that mirror the atmosphere of the ravine.

Because gravity here is a suggestion rather than a law—given the erratic, sagging nature of the bridges—they move with an unsettling, fluid grace. Their limbs are long, with dual-jointed fingers that have evolved to exert precise, surgical pressure. They are always touching the city. They walk with one hand pressed against the "walls," a constant, tactile conversation with the biology of their home. To lose contact with the pulse of the bridge or the vibration of the vine is to risk being disoriented by the shifting geometry of the architecture.

They carry no packs, for packs are heavy and slow. Instead, they wear intricate webbing made of cured pith. Attached to these webs are their obsidian blades—deeply black, volcanic glass, chipped to an edge so fine it can sever a vein of vascular tissue before the plant can react with its clotting sap. 

They do not speak in sentences. Their language is a rhythmic, percussive affair—sharp clicks of the tongue and hums produced by vibrating their own chests against the hollow shells of their pods. These sounds are designed to carry through the dense, wet air, cutting through the omnipresent *thrum-thrum* of falling water. It is a language of maintenance: instructions for redirection, warnings of a branch that has grown too thick, or the mourning of a pod that has been swallowed by the encroaching moss because its inhabitant grew too idle.

What is most startling is their eyes. They are large, dark, and lidless, reflecting the emerald shafts of light in a way that suggests they are constantly scanning for the next intrusion. They do not sleep in the way we understand it. They enter a state of "rooting," where they press their bodies against the warm, metabolic core of their pods, hooking their web-harnesses into the plant's own circulatory system. In this state, they become sluggish, their heartbeat syncing with the slow, internal rhythm of the Basin. They dream alongside the forest; they feel the sap move, they feel the swell of new buds, and they dream of the geometry they must carve into existence come dawn.

They harbor no illusions of mastery. They know they are not the masters of the Basin, nor are they its parasites. They are the friction that keeps the organism from choking itself. They are the essential pain that tells the city where it ends and where the void begins. 

When they die, they do not bury their own. They lay the body within a particularly hungry cluster of vines, allowing the lattice to knit through the bones, until the inhabitant becomes a structural pillar, a piece of the furniture they spent their lives trying to keep at bay. To die is to stop pushing back; to die is to finally be subsumed, becoming the soft, green foundation for a stranger’s nursery pods. They are a cycle that never breaks, a people who work themselves into the very grain of their home, until they cease to be visitors and become, quite literally, the walls.
