In the Latticework, you do not find people; you find **The Tenders**.

They are not born, nor are they strictly built. They are the slow-blooming fruit of the basin, humans—or once-humans—who have allowed the Latticework to colonize them. When you see a Tender, the first thing you notice is their stillness. They possess the posture of a stalk of celery or a copper conduit: elongated, vertical, and utterly indifferent to the passage of time.

### The Anatomy of a Tender
Their skin is the color of bleached ash, puckered at the joints where the moisture meets the brass. They do not wear clothes in the traditional sense. Instead, they wear the "grafts." A Tender might have a series of fine, copper filaments woven directly into the dermis of their forearms, serving as an interface with the piping. They are physically integrated into the house-pods they inhabit; if a pipe develops a fissure, a Tender will press their palm against the leak, allowing the scar tissue of their own body to knit with the biological pulp of the wall until the flow is staunched.

They move with a deliberation that makes a glacier look frantic. A Tender might spend four hours shifting a brass valve one degree to the left, their fingers moving in total synchronization with the *plink-hiss* of the condensation. They do not speak. To speak would be to break the seal of the atmosphere, to introduce unnecessary warmth and vibration into a system that thrives on static, cool composure.

### The Purpose of the Inhabitants
The Tenders are the Latticework’s reflex system. If the great heart-pumps deep in the roots begin to stutter, a Tender will feel it in their marrow. They are the ones who climb the vertical lattices—their feet, calloused and wide, gripping the grey-barked roots like specialized tree-frogs—to harvest the "waste" of the system. 

They collect the glowing, gelatinous threads left by the translucent insects. With these, they weave the reinforcement nets that keep the gourds from bursting under the hydraulic pressure of the pipes. They are gardeners of a machine, pruning away the aggressive, chaotic growth of the ferns to ensure that the brass conduits maintain their rigid, geometric paths.

### What They See
Because the Latticework is a closed loop, the Tenders do not have a concept of "elsewhere." They observe the world in cycles of pressure and decay. When a Tender looks at another, they do not see a personality or a neighbor; they see a secondary strut. They perceive each other as extensions of the plumbing.

If you were to catch a Tender’s eye, you would find it disconcerting. Their pupils are faintly opalescent, reflecting the pale, filtered light of the canopy. When they look at you, they aren't assessing your intent; they are sensing your temperature and your structural integrity. You are, to them, an unrefined element—a soft collection of muscle and bone that has wandered into their nursery.

### The Finality of the Inhabitant
The Tenders eventually fulfill the final requirement of the basin: they return to the architecture. When a Tender’s internal cycles grow too slow, when the brass in their limbs becomes brittle, they do not die in a bed. They walk to a section of the root-thicket that has grown thin or hollow. They press themselves against the structural nodes of the housing-pods, embracing the wood and the pipe.

They become a brace. 

Over the course of weeks, the bark grows over them, and the metal oxidizes into their skin. They stop being a Tender and become a part of the Latticework. The machine-grove absorbs their mass, repurposing their calcified bones into a load-bearing joint. A hundred years later, another Tender will brush their hand against that very spot, feeling the hum of the engine, never knowing it was once a person who also stood, and watched, and waited.
