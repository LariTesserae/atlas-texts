To live within the Spire of Oth is not to exist in a state of living, but in a state of *accumulation*. The people who dwell here—the Othites—do not view themselves as separate from the stone or the chitinous giants that share their corridors. They are merely the smallest, most patient layer of the deposit.

They are beings of dense, pallid inertia. Their skin has the matte quality of river-washed bone, translucent in places where the blue-grey veins trace the labor of their internal systems. They do not walk so much as they drift, their limbs thick and rounded, mirroring the soft, eroded curves of the limestone architecture. Their ears are vestigial, flattened against their skulls, for in a world where sound is a physical pressure—a constant, thrumming vibration of wingbeats and scraping plates—they have evolved to perceive the frequency of the city through their marrow rather than their eardrums.

They do not speak. Language is a form of waste, a expenditure of heat and energy that Oth cannot afford. Instead, they communicate through a complex tactile language of touch. A finger pressed against a neighbor’s forearm conveys a nuance of sorrow; a palm flattened against a wall, held in the warmth of a grazing arthropod, signifies communal contentment. They are perpetually touching, leaning, and supporting—a living lattice of flesh that reinforces the structural integrity of the city.

Their gaze is the most haunting aspect of their anatomy. Because the amber light is static and the environment offers no horizon, their eyes have drifted toward a milky, multi-faceted opaqueness—not for seeing shapes, but for sensing the subtle, rhythmic pulse of the bioluminescent vines. They do not look *at* things; they look *into* the texture of reality, watching for the subtle tremors of decay that indicate a patch of nutrient-dense fungus is ready to be harvested.

They find beauty in the friction of living. A typical day for an Othite is a masterpiece of unhurried motion. They spend hours, perhaps weeks, refining the edge of a scraping-blade carved from a beetle’s thorax. The work is meditative, a slow wearing-down of material that mimics the geological processes of the spire itself. When they rest, they do not lie down in isolation; they nest among the massive, grazing arthropods. The animal’s warmth is the city’s hearth, and the human is the animal’s vanity, grooming the moss from the creature’s carapace while the creature, in turn, provides the heat that keeps the Othite’s metabolism from stalling into total stasis.

They have no concept of "outside." To leave the Spire of Oth would be to enter a void of infinite thinness, a terrifying lack of contact. Here, there are walls on every side; there is the constant, grounding weight of gravity pulling the city together; there is the endless cycle of matter—the death of a moss-patch becoming the flour of their meal, the shedding of a carapace becoming the edge of a tool. 

They are the archivists of the end of the world. They do not pray to gods of sky or sun, for they are the gods of the sediment, the patient stewards of a realm that has ceased to chase the future. If you were to stand among them, they would not fear you or wonder at your presence. You would simply be another object to be assimilated. They would touch your arm with cold, dry fingers, test the hardness of your suit or the texture of your skin, and then—finding you lacking in the proper density of the Spire—they would simply move on, their slow, heavy rhythmic shuffling absorbing you into the background hum of the great, vaulted silence. 

They are content. They are finished. They are the final keepers of a rhythm that has forgotten how to stop.
