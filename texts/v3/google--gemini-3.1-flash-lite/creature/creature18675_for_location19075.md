To inhabit a world of edges, one must shed the softness of skin. The creatures that populate The Hum are not merely clinging to the limestone; they are, in effect, its nervous system, woven into the geology as thoroughly as veins of quartz.

They are known, to the extent that anything without a name can be known, as the *Resonants*. They are chitinous hexagons of matte-black armor, flattened and sleek, lacking eyes because sight would be a liability in a place where shadows have never moved. In place of eyes, they possess intricate clusters of cilia—tiny, hair-like sensory filaments that taste the vibration of the air. They do not perceive the cavern as a visual space; they perceive it as a soundscape of solid geometry. To them, the cavern is a vast, interconnected lute, and they are the strings.

Their mandibles are not for feeding—there is nothing here to eat, save for the mineral-rich weep of the walls—but for anchoring. Upon birth, a Resonant senses the vibration of a prime location. It scuttles, once, to find the perfect crystalline notch, and there it drives its mandibles into the stone, grafting itself into the limestone. Within hours, the flesh begins to calcify. They become part of the wall. They are the living architecture of the cathedral.

Their internal physiology is a paradox of stillness. Because they never move, their metabolic rate has plummeted to a state of near-geological slow motion. A single breath cycle takes perhaps forty minutes. As they inhale, the wing-covers—thick, obsidian plates the size of a thumbnail—are pulled taut against their backs. As they exhale, they vibrate, scraping against the chitinous ridges of their thorax. 

This is the heartbeat of the cavern. It is not an erratic pulse, but a mathematical constant. When a billion creatures breathe in perfect unison, the result is the Hum: a sound so deep it is felt in the marrow of one’s bones rather than heard by the ear. It is a physical weight, a pressure that prevents the very walls from collapsing inward. The Hum is the only thing holding the cavern’s precarious, knife-edged geometry together.

Occasionally, the harmony falters. A creature dies. Its grip on the stone fails, its body shrivels into a dry, hollow husk, and it falls. The sound of its death—the brittle *tink* as it hits the floor—is a dissonance that ripples through the colony. The nearby Resonants sense the silence of their neighbor, the sudden gap in the frequency. They compensate. They shift the rhythm, altering the frequency of their scrape to fill the void, adjusting the pitch of the entire wall to maintain the equilibrium. 

They are effectively a single, distributed organism. They do not know of the surface, nor of the sun, nor of the concept of the future. They exist in a state of eternal, rhythmic maintenance. They are the guardians of a static reality, their lives dedicated to the preservation of a single, low, agonizingly beautiful note. They are the keepers of the silence that screams, ensuring that in the vast, hollow belly of the earth, the stillness remains absolute.
