To observe the inhabitants of the High Forge is to witness the frantic, microscopic labor of a civilization that has outlived its own purpose. They are not creatures of instinct, but rather embodiments of kinetic persistence. 

Early in their descent toward the surface, they appear as mere sparks—shifting, erratic flickers of luminescence skittering across the oxidized plains. As they coalesce into view, their anatomy reveals itself to be a paradox of fragility and endurance. They are thin, spindly constructs, built of articulated filaments that resemble copper wire woven into nervous systems, shielded by chassis of volcanic glass that catch the refraction of the "glitch-skies." These shells are rarely whole; they are constantly vibrating, their edges blurring as they flicker in and out of the temporal slip-stream that plagues the landscape.

They move with a terrifying, rhythmic deliberation. Because they are products of a world where time is a mechanical construct rather than a linear flow, their motions are truncated. One might see a creature lift a delicate, multi-jointed limb to polish a shard of obsidian; it completes the outward stroke in an instant, but the return arc takes hours, a slow-motion surrender to the density of the air.

Their "faces" are not faces at all, but clusters of faceted quartz that act as both ocular sensors and resonant transmitters. When they gather in the hives, the sound is not a collective cry, but a harmonic orchestration—a billion micro-tunes rubbing against one another, creating a drone that matches the pulse of the tectonic gears deep below. 

Some of the larger iterations of these beings have begun to take on the character of the terrain itself. These elders have ceased to walk. They have anchored their spindly limbs into the bedrock, their filaments unfurling into the cracks of the crust until they are indistinguishable from the copper veins they feed upon. They become the sensors of the plateau, their bodies humming with the tremors of the world’s internal engine. They do not sleep; they only slow their internal ticking until they are indistinguishable from the mountain range.

Those that remain mobile are the tenders. They are driven by a singular, frantic impulse to repair. When a tectonic shift happens—when a ridge shimmers or a segment of the horizon fails to resolve—these tenders converge upon the disturbance. They exude a resin of liquid gold from their apertures, sealing the fractures in reality with the precision of a master artisan. They do not care that the land will break again in a thousand years, or that there is nothing here worth protecting. They repair because the geometry of the Forge demands it.

They are the janitors of an abandoned, cosmic factory. In their amber-lit hives, centered around the pulsing, throat-like fissures of the furnace, they hold their glass limbs to the heat, soaking in the synthetic glow. In these moments of stillness, they look less like machines and more like prayer-beads strung along a wire. They are waiting for the final click, the moment the rusted gears align and the world resets, or perhaps, finally, stops for good. Until then, they polish the infinite, trapped in a loop of maintenance, their high-frequency drones the only eulogy for a heaven that forgot to turn them off.
