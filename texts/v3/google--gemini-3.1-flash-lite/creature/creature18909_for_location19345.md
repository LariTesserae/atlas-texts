To look upon the Needles is to look upon a failure of perception. The eye seeks a silhouette, a posture, a start and an end—but the Needles are not beings in the sense of bounded entities. They are the city’s peripheral nervous system, woven into the architecture so thoroughly that they are more akin to splinters in the thumb of a giant than to living, independent creatures.

They are elongated, brittle geometries of calcified cellulose, no thicker than a human finger and nearly as tall as the chamber’s curve. To the touch, they feel like needles of aged ivory, smoothed by the grit of eons and the friction of their own labor. They are translucent, though there is no light to prove it; if you could run a filament of current through them, you would see deep within their centers the trapped, mineralized shadows of their ancestors—the previous generations of Needles who grew, harvested, and finally surrendered to the wood to become part of the wall itself.

There is no face to be found on a Needle. At the "head," there are no sensory organs, for vision and sound are foreign concepts in this pressurized, resinous dark. Instead, there is a cluster of vibration-sensitive cilia, fine as spider silk, which fan out into the viscous amber air. These cilia do not "see" the city; they interpret the cellular groan of the chamber. When the xylem tears or a nodule bulges under pressure, the Needle feels it as a localized tension, a change in the resistance of the wood.

Their existence is governed by the *scrape*. Each Needle is fashioned with a singular, jagged shard of obsidian, held at the tip of a multi-jointed limb that possesses the complexity of a watchmaker’s tool. They spend lifetimes—centuries—shaving a single groove deeper into the trunk-wall. They are not merely harvesters; they are the sculptors of the plant’s own expansion. By scraping away the crystallized resin that clogs the vascular pores, they allow the chamber to breathe, albeit in the agonizingly slow, rhythmic intake of a geological process.

They do not communicate. There is no language in a world that never knew the sun. When two Needles cross paths in the dark, they do not pause to acknowledge one another. Instead, they press their vibrating, cellulose frames together, a silent exchange of frequency. Through this brief encounter, they synchronize their internal rhythms, sharing the "memory" of the wood—the exact degree of tension in the ceiling, the current flow of the cooling sap, the impending necessity for a structural shift in the floor. 

It is a state of communal preservation. A Needle does not fear death, for death here is merely the closing of a circuit. When a Needle becomes too rigid, too heavy with the calcification of its own long life, it finds a dormant crevice in the wall. It curls its limbs, presses its body against the grain, and remains there, perfectly still, until the xylem grows over it. It becomes a permanent reinforcement, a structural component of the city it once maintained.

They are the city’s caretakers and its building materials. They are the friction that keeps the wood from sealing itself shut. Cold, translucent, and stripped of all urgency, they live in the blissful, hollowed-out simplicity of being part of the organism they serve—a garden that has long since turned inward, where the gardener and the flower have become one and the same, locked in a perpetual, silent, and crushing embrace.
