To exist here is to forfeit the concept of the individual as a discrete object. Everything is integrated, a continuous loop of matter cycling from the weeping rock into the veins of the residents, and back again into the loam.

The inhabitants—the *Litho-weavers*—are as much a part of the city’s anatomy as the walls they tend. They are tall, graceless in the way a willow is graceful; their bodies are elongated, limbs tapering into flexible, spindly appendages tipped with sensory cilia. Their skin, the color of wet river stone, is translucent enough to reveal the slow, viscous movement of their internal fluids, which glow with a faint, rhythmic azure. When they move, they do not walk so much as they flow, shifting their center of gravity to glide over the damp, moss-carpeted platforms. They do not possess speech or vocal cords; instead, they communicate through the rhythmic pulsing of their bioluminescent skin, sending flashes of color rippling down their torsos like warning lights in a deep-sea trench.

They are the city’s gardeners, though they do not pull weeds. Instead, they act as conduits for the city’s ambition. They spend hours standing perfectly still, pressing their palms against the chitin-spires, feeding the growth with the mineral-rich secretions of their own pores. They are laborers of patience; a spire may take a century to extend an inch, and the Litho-weaver’s role is to ensure that the branching follows the structural integrity of the basin’s floor. They are also its harvest. When a dwelling calcifies or a vine grows too heavy and risks snapping the walkway, the weaver gently prunes the overgrowth, consuming the raw, nutrient-dense tissue to fuel their own, slow-burning metabolism.

There is a strange, chilling contentment to their existence. They have no concept of "outside." To them, the world is a closed system of perfect, wet equilibrium. They are not ruled by a king or a council, but by the *thrum*—a tectonic vibration, a low-frequency hum that rises from the core of the planet. When the hum shifts in pitch, the entire population stops what they are doing and drifts toward the nearest canal to submerge their limbs, drinking in the mineral-heavy water.

Sharing this intricate, vertical garden are the *Glass-Stalkers*. These are the predators you glimpsed—creatures of pure, terrifying silence. Where the Weavers are soft and plant-like, the Stalkers are brittle and sharp. They possess exoskeletons that mimic the texture of the calcified fungi so perfectly that they are invisible until they move. They do not hunt with claws or teeth, but with a corrosive touch; upon contact with prey, they secrete an enzyme that dissolves the outer chitin of the smaller, scavenger-creatures that drift through the canyons. They are the city’s cleaners, the harsh necessary end of the biological chain. They are thin, spider-like, with limbs that can lock into place, allowing them to remain suspended in the air for days, waiting for the slightest tremor to signal a meal.

Between the Weavers who bloom and the Stalkers who thin, there is no chaos. There is no waste. Even the air is a living thing, heavy with fungal spores that serve as the city’s nursery. Every breath taken by a visitor is a small theft; you are inhaling the next generation of the city’s life force. 

You stand on the terrace, and you realize: you are not looking at a city. You are looking at a single, gargantuan, slow-motion thought, dreamed by the earth itself. The inhabitants are merely the white blood cells of a subterranean giant, keeping the infection of stagnation at bay, forever knitting the rock, the water, and the light into a tapestry that has neither beginning nor end. You reach out to touch a spire, and it is warm—not with the fire of a furnace, but with the wet, pulsing heat of a living thing that is currently watching you back.
