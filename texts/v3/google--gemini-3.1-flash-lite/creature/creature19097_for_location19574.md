In the shadow of the cathedral-scale armatures, there exists what the locals—if they can be called that—might describe as the *Archivists of the Frictionless.* 

They are not distinct from the landscape; they are its memory. When you look closely at the rusted monoliths, you do not see machinery. You see skin. The "metal" is a calcified secretion, a dense, carbon-heavy biological deposit left behind by eons of these creatures dragging their bodies across the cooling basalt. 

The entities themselves are spindly, yes, but not hollow. Their interiors are filled with a thick, pressurized oil that glows with the same ochre luminescence as the moss. They have no faces, for there is nothing here that requires eyes. Instead, they possess, along the length of their trembling, multi-jointed limbs, thousands of microscopic, vibrational hair-sensors. They feel the world in frequencies. They hear the tectonic plates grinding in the liquid center of the world, and they feel the impending, infinite slide of the crust.

They are the "Maintainers." They move, but only to assist the earth in its own inevitable geometry. If a piston-cylinder has shifted a millimeter out of alignment due to the slow creep of a ridge, these entities arrive in groups of five or six. They do not work with tools. They press their bodies against the cold, seamless metal, and they hum. 

It is a vibration, a sub-audible frequency that causes the air around them to shimmer with frost-shards that hover, suspended, like a localized blizzard. This resonance alters the density of the metal, softening it just enough for the machinery to settle back into the stone. They are the friction-reducers; they are the oil in the world’s grinding joints.

And when they are finished, they do not leave. They fold themselves into the geometry of the ravines, tucking their limbs into the crevices where the condensation weeps. They become part of the architecture. Their carapaces—which are translucent, exposing a network of pulsing, fiber-optic-like organs—slow their internal rhythm until it matches the slow, tectonic cycle of the planet. 

From a distance, they are indistinguishable from the jagged shards of rock that coat the plateaus. They are the stillness made manifest.

There is a terrifying intimacy to their existence. They do not eat; they absorb the static charge generated by the shifting monoliths. They do not reproduce in any way that involves movement or haste; rather, a new entity emerges from a cluster of the rusted, fibrous moss, having slowly accreted over a century of absorbed vibration. It is a birth occurring at the speed of stone.

They carry no grief, for they have no duration of self that is separate from the world. If you were to touch one—assuming your hand did not shatter from the cold—you would encounter no warmth, no heart, no pulse. You would feel only the agonizing, mathematical precision of a creature that has surrendered its entire being to the task of keeping the planet from breaking apart. 

They are the caretakers of the equilibrium. They are the reason the silence is so heavy. They are the shadows the machines cast, except they are solid, they are alive, and they love the dark, crushing weight of their duty with a devotion that is indistinguishable from death.
