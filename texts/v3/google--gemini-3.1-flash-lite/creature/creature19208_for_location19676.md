The people of Ouros-Thal are as lean and angular as the basalt pillars they inhabit. They are a race of people whose silhouettes have been smoothed by the necessity of tight spaces; they walk with a characteristic, crab-like hunch, their shoulders perpetually braced against a gravity that seems to pull sideways as often as down. 

To look at them is to see a history of the cold. Their skin has the matte, monochromatic quality of shale—dull grays and muted bone-whites, broken only by the intricate patterns of deep-etched scarring caused by accidental contact with the obsidian edges of their home. They dress not in layers of cloth, but in dense, overlapping plates of hide, cured until it is stiff as armor, the seams stitched with the heavy, waxy sinew of the mountain goats they tend. 

They move with a terrifying, rhythmic composure. There is no running in Ouros-Thal; to run is to risk a stumble that, in this vertical sprawl, would be met with an eternity of falling. They perform their tasks—the scraping of lichen, the milking of the six-limbed herds, the rhythmic tapping of stone-chisels—with the deliberate slowness of shadows lengthening at sunset. 

Their language is not a thing of vowels or long, melodic sentences. It is a tongue of clicks, rasps, and sudden intakes of breath, perfectly calibrated to occupy the stillness of the mountain without shattering it. To a stranger, a conversation between two weavers of Ouros-Thal might sound like the stone itself settling—a dry friction of gravel against gravel. 

They are a people defined by their relationship to the condensation. They do not drink greedily; they sip, and they monitor the rhythmic drip of the stone with the devotion of monks. When the mist rolls in, they stop entirely. They do not fear the damp; they lean into it, pressing their foreheads against the weeping cracks in the rock, letting the fine rime-frost form on their eyelashes. It is their liturgy, a shared communion with the mountain’s slow, cold breath. 

There is an eerie lack of ego in their expressions. Because the city deepens rather than expands, a householder today lives in the same hollowed-out chamber their great-grandparents carved; they are not owners of their history, but stewards of a long, dark tunnel. They view their lives as a singular, prolonged act of anchoring. When they die, there is no ceremony of fire or sky-burial; they are committed back to the architecture. A loved one passes, and the family spends the next generation carefully carving them into the wall of their own dwelling, sealing them behind a slab of slate, turning the ancestor into the very foundation that holds the next generation against the void.

To live in Ouros-Thal is to belong to the mountain more than to one’s own body. You are a nerve ending in a basalt hand, shivering in the thin, sharp air, waiting for the next drop of water to fall, held in place by the knowledge that the mountain is the only thing that remembers you, and that the only way to endure is to become as hard, as silent, and as unmoving as the stone itself.
