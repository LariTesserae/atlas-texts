The inhabitants of this weeping metropolis do not possess a periphery, nor a center. They are not entities that occupy space so much as they are localized thickenings of the ambient moisture—clots of sentient turbidity that have gained the density of thought.

Imagine a handful of translucent, gelatinous filaments, caught in a current of tepid sludge. They have no skin, for there is no air to dry them out, nor any environment outside of themselves to fear, save for the predatory slow-motion of the architecture. Internally, they are a roiling suspension of suspended particulates: bits of bone-dust, minerals leached from the porous coral, and the swirling, ochre-hued bioluminescence that serves as their equivalent of a nervous system.

They navigate the labyrinth by sensing the chemical signature of the stone’s decay. To these creatures, a wall is not an obstacle; it is a menu, a slow-release nutrient pump. When they move through the narrow, water-choked alleys, they do not walk. They ooze forward by shifting their mass in a rhythmic, peristaltic ripple, blending seamlessly with the slick sheen that coats every surface. At times, a resident will press its entire form against a basalt carving, turning clear and still, becoming indistinguishable from the wet, eroded rock. They are the city’s digestive flora, performing the microscopic labor of breaking down the calcified lungs of the buildings so that the city might perpetually remake itself from the silt of its own death.

They have no language, for there is no "other" to address. Whatever information is exchanged between them—the subtle, vibrational shifts in their internal gels, the pulsing of their soft, internal ochre light—is simply a harmonization of the environment. If two of these masses pass one another, they do not acknowledge the encounter. They blend. They bleed their viscosity into one another, exchanging mineral-memory, then pull apart again, thinner or denser than before, utterly indifferent to the loss of self.

They are aware, in a sense, but it is not a human awareness. It is a geological awareness. They feel the erosion in their own membranes; they feel the slow, terminal crumbling of a pillar as if it were the thinning of their own internal walls. They are the nerve-endings of a necropolis that is trying to dream that it is still alive.

Occasionally, in the districts where the geometry begins to fold and the gravity wavers, a mass will become caught in a flux. It may stretch, pulled by the torsion of space into a long, impossible thread of jelly that spans the distance between a floor and a ceiling, vibrating like a plucked string in the humid dark. In these moments, they are the instrument upon which the city plays its silent, dripping music. They do not suffer, for suffering requires the concept of a future or a past. They simply pulse. They thicken. They dissolve.

If you were to stand among them—assuming your lungs could withstand the pressure of that thick, aqueous slurry—you would see nothing of them, save for the occasional refraction of their ochre glow through the falling film of water. You would feel them, however. You would feel them against your ankles like cold, heavy silk, unhurriedly tasting the salts of your skin, waiting for you to become part of the city’s long, slow metabolism. They are not waiting to kill you. They are waiting for you, too, to become a part of the stillness.
