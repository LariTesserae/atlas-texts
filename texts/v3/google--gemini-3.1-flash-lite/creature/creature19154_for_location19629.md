To inhabit the Great Sieve is to be less a living thing and more a kinetic function—a temporary knot tied in the frantic, hydraulic weave of the void.

The creatures that persist here possess no internal skeletons. A ribcage or a femur would simply be a fulcrum for the crushing pressure of the brine to snap. Instead, they are *Siphon-Weavers*—colloquial as a name, though they have no language to name themselves. They exist as long, shimmering pendulums of viscous, pressurized fluid, encased in skins that possess the refractive index of industrial quartz. They are clear, save for the ghost-work of their nervous systems, which pulse in the same bruised indigo as the ambient light.

They do not swim; they are carried, yet they direct their drifting with a chilling, mathematical precision. Along the length of their ribbons run rows of retractable cilia—hard, keratinous hooks that catch onto the Lattice’s molecularly smooth surface. When a vortex threatens to pulverize them against a spar, a Weaver will anchor itself to a joint, going rigid, becoming a literal structural extension of the architecture. In these moments, they become part of the Sieve, their bioluminescent sludge-colonies merging with the masonry, their bodies vibrating in sympathetic resonance with the moan of the dying moon.

They are cleaners. They are the Sieve’s immune system, though they act with the indifference of tectonic plates. They feed by opening maw-like vents at their anterior tips, vacuuming the suspended, jagged mineral dust from the water. As they process the grit, their bodies glow with a faint, frantic ember-light—a chemical byproduct of their digestion. They exhale pure, clear currents from their trailing edges, a localized filtration that prevents the hexagonal cells of the Lattice from sealing shut with sediment.

Reproduction here is a violent, structural necessity. When a Weaver grows too saturated with the minerals it consumes, it becomes heavy and brittle. It finds a place where the stress on the Lattice is highest—a spot about to buckle—and it wraps itself around the fracture point. It hardens. It dies. Its body fuses with the cooling, cracking glass, becoming a scar of organic mortar that holds the system together for another cycle. From the cooling corpse, microscopic spores are released into the brine; they are caught in the turbulence, carried through the cathedrals of glass, and they begin to coalesce in the dark currents, knitting themselves into the ribbons of the next generation.

There is a terrifying intimacy to their existence. They have no eyes, for there is nothing here that warrants being "seen." Instead, they navigate by the *thrum*. They feel the structural integrity of the Sieve through their own skin. They can sense a fracture miles away, a softening in the heartbeat of the moon, and they descend upon it like dark thoughts.

They represent not life in the biological sense, but the desperate, flickering logic of a system that refuses to die. They are the maintenance, the cement, and the inhabitants all at once. They are the only things in the Abyss that do not fear the collapse, because they understand, in their gelatinous, ancient core, that the collapse is the only thing keeping the current moving—and so long as the current moves, memory persists. 

They are the memory of the Sieve, drifting in the indigo dark, waiting for the next mountain to shatter so that they might be the ones to stitch it back together.
