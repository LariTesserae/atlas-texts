The beings of the Sump-Reach are known, if they are known at all, as the **Lithari**. They are creatures of density, their biology a cousin to the very rock they inhabit, possessing no internal marrow, but rather a pressurized, cooling system of mineral-rich slurry that flows through silicate veins.

To look upon a Lithari is to see a silhouette refined by gravity. They are squat—rarely taller than a stack of three wide, flat paving stones—with limbs that possess extra articulations, allowing them to flatten themselves against the basaltic geometry as if they were shadow-stains on the wall. Their skin is not flesh, but a leathery, mottled membrane that mimics the texture of cooling obsidian, pebbled with inclusions of quartz and feldspar that catch the diffuse ambient light, making them shimmer with a dull, subterranean luster.

They lack what we would consider faces. Where a sensory center might be, there is instead a faceted, crystalline array—a cluster of recessed, milky-white nodes that do not “see” in the optical sense, but rather map the vibration and thermal conductivity of the space. They perceive the Sump-Reach as a topographic map of touch; every scrape of a chisel, every groan of shifting tectonic plates, every drip of the weeping ceiling is a tactile sentence written against their bodies.

The Lithari do not communicate through sound. The air beneath the earth is too heavy, too swallowed by stone to carry the nuance of language. Instead, they "speak" through the pressure of their touch. When one Lithari meets another, they press their chest-plates together—a ritualized grinding of surface—and vibrate their internal hum until the other feels the resonance. They share states of being: the cooling of a tunnel, the exhaustion of the arm, the discovery of a new moisture seam. To them, conversation is a physical alignment, a brief period of structural harmony before they return to the solitude of their labor.

Their lives are an exquisite, agonizing patience. A Lithari might spend an entire cycle—a span of time measured not by suns, but by the accumulation of a specific mineral deposit—carving a single conduit. They are indifferent to the passage of aeons. They view themselves not as masters of the Sump-Reach, but as its gardeners and its gears. If the mountain settles, closing a tunnel, the Lithari do not panic; they simply begin the excavation anew, perhaps shifting to a different angle, adjusting the geometry of their society to match the earth’s whim.

At night—if such a concept can be applied to a place of permanent twilight—they do not sleep. They "lock." They wedge themselves into the tight interstices of the fissures, bracing their limbs against opposing walls until they become, for all intents and purposes, structural load-bearers. In this state, their internal slurry slows to a near-halt, and they become as still and as unmoving as the foundation stones themselves.

They are the archivists of the slow-time. There is no ambition in them, no desire to leave the basin. They are the living expression of the Sump-Reach’s own slow crystallization. To be a Lithari is to be a note in an unfinished chord, waiting in the darkness for the world above to finish its violent, haphazard development, while they—patient, cold, and meticulously precise—carve the foundation of something that will never be reached, only felt.
