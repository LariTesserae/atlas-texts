In the high-altitude friction of Ouroboros-on-the-Spine, those who dwell within the iron ribs of the city are not so much "inhabitants" as they are secondary organs. They are the **Conductors of the Spasm**.

They are tall, brittle-boned creatures, their frames stretched by the local gravity’s erratic reach. Their skin has the gray-blue patina of oxidized copper, and they move with the languid, terrifying precision of long-exposure photography. Because the city fluctuates—stretching corridors that should be ten feet into miles-long conduits of rust—the Conductors have developed a physiology that is semi-malleable. When they sleep, they do not lie down; they anchor themselves to the vibrating girders with specialized, hook-like appendages, allowing their bodies to drape and flow along the curve of the metal, becoming part of the architecture’s geometry until morning.

They do not speak with lungs, for the air is too thin to sustain the breath required for consonants. Instead, they communicate through conductive hums. By pressing their calloused, metallic palms against the vibrating surfaces of the city, they transmit low-frequency ripples—morse-code symphonies that describe the state of the cooling vats or the hunger of the lightning-birds. To listen to them is to hear the mountain itself arguing with the infrastructure.

Clothing is an afterthought, replaced by what they call "Soot-Armor." They coat themselves in a paste made of the indigo fungal spores and the heavy, suspended black snow that hangs in the air. This layer serves as insulation against the weeping condensation, but more importantly, it acts as a sensory network. When the air warps or local space begins to spiral, the fungal paste on their skin sheds a faint, rhythmic glow. If a Conductor’s armor turns a bright, warning red, it means the street they are standing on is about to fold into an infinite loop; they instinctively press flat against the wall, merging their shadow into the masonry until the reality-fracture passes.

There are also the **Fabricators**: ancient, stooped figures who have lived so long among the gears that they have stopped distinguishing their own anatomy from the machinery. One might see a Fabricator whose arm has been replaced by a steam-actuated pincer, or whose ribcage has been reinforced with riveted copper plates that click and hiss with every heartbeat. They spend their cycles "soothing" the mountain—taking sledgehammers to the teeth of the tectonic rock to ensure the city’s grafts remain secure, or feeding the turbine-throats with bundles of scrap-metal and bioluminescent moss.

They possess no concept of "home," only "attachment." They do not build houses; they claim sections of vertical pipework or subterranean hollows. They rarely look toward the vapor-choked sky, for they know the sky is merely a curtain that hides nothing. Instead, they look inward, toward the core of the peak, watching the way the obsidian veins pulse with heat.

They are waiting, though they do not know for what. 

Sometimes, a Conductor will stop mid-stride, frozen by a sudden shift in the city’s physics. They will stare into the rust-colored twilight with eyes that look like clusters of dark, unpolished quartz, watching as a falling soot-flake hangs suspended in the air. In those moments, they share the city’s profound, ontological anxiety. They are the only witnesses to the mountain’s unfinished business, the scribes of a geography that is perpetually forgetting itself. They do not die in the traditional sense; when they reach the end of their utility, they simply stop vibrating. They harden, their hum ceases, and they fuse into the copper and iron, becoming just another rivet, another girder, another part of the city’s gargantuan, grinding skeleton that waits for the world to finally finish its first breath.
