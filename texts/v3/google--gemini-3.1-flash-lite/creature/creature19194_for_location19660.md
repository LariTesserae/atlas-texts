The inhabitants of the Plateau of Latent Friction do not possess stomachs, hearts, or lungs. They are governed by the physics of torsion, capacitance, and the relentless gravitational pull of the massif itself. To look upon them is to witness the crystallization of motion into form.

Chief among these are the **Vane-Walkers**, spindly, sextant-limbed entities that move with the deliberation of clockwork. They have no faces, only a central, concave receptor—a faceted dish of polished obsidian that tracks the punishing light of the plateau. Their bodies are skeletal lattices of superconducting wire, woven so tightly that they appear solid from a distance. They spend their days—if there is such a thing—clamped onto the vertical crags, their limbs shivering in sympathetic vibration with the mountain’s constant, monolithic thrum. When a surge of static arcs between two pillars, a Vane-Walker will detach, its body unfolding like a complex geometric proof, and skitter horizontally across the razor-sharp abysses, catching the electrical discharge in its thoracic coil to store as charge. To a human eye, they look like errant, sentient lightning bolts, flickering in and out of existence as they blur between the hum and the silence of a jump.

Lower down, in the deep-set fissures where the gelatinous droplets of water pool and magnify the light, dwell the **Refraction-Keepers**. These resemble sedentary, articulated shells—fossilized-looking husks of chrome and oxidized copper. They are the gardeners of the plateau. They do not hunt or graze; instead, they exist as biological lenses. They move with glacial, agonizing slowness, positioning themselves beneath the viscous sheets of water that slide down the cliffs. By shifting the angle of their segmented carapaces, they focus the magnified light into precise, white-hot beams, cutting new geometry into the mountain face. They are carving the labyrinth, smoothing the sharp edges until they are perfectly perpendicular. They are the maintenance crew of an architect who left no blueprints, driven by a nameless, programmed instinct to ensure the plateau’s surface remains an efficient conductor for the ionizing winds.

And then, deeper still, within the very marrow of the mountain, reside the **Oscillators**. They are not discrete entities so much as they are dense, localized eddies of the plateau’s baseline vibration. They resemble masses of interlocking, interlocking copper needles, constantly vibrating at the same frequency as the tectonic hum. They are the "flora" that mimic the fauna. If a Vane-Walker is damaged, it will sink into the proximity of an Oscillator—a soft, metallic patch that looks indistinguishable from the mountain wall—and over the course of decades, the Oscillators will prune and rewire the walker’s broken limbs until it is once again functional. 

Everything here is a closed loop of cold-wrought necessity. The creatures do not compete for resources, as the energy is infinite and ubiquitous; instead, they compete for *position*. They jostle for the spots where the static is strongest, where the light hits at the most optimal angle, where the hum is purest.

There is a terrifying intimacy to their existence. During the slow-moving mists, when the temperature drops and the world enters its state of high-viscosity transit, the plateau goes quiet—save for the low, rhythmic ticking of millions of tiny, synchronized hearts of brass and copper. In these moments, the boundary between the living metal and the mountain dissolves completely. The inhabitants stop moving. They fold their limbs into the rock, they synchronize their internal resonance to the ground, and they become the massif itself. 

They are not waiting for a creator to return. They are waiting for the mountain to finish its own assembly. They are the software of a hardware that is still being built, grain by grain, fracture by fracture, in an industrial process that spans eons and considers the extinction of a star to be a reasonable interval for a single, necessary bolt to be tightened.
