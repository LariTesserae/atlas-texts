In the deepest, most compressed hollows of the Fabrication—where the copper is weathered to the color of a bruised plum and the pressure is a physical weight upon the soul—there exists the *Curators*.

They are not entities of flesh, nor are they, strictly speaking, machines. They are accretions. To look at a Curator is to mistake it, at first, for a mere outcropping of the architecture—a particularly smooth, rounded node of oxidized lead jutting from a pillar. They are formed as the currents carry the minute, microscopic particulate of the world’s decay and deposit it against the magnetic tension of the ancient, buried joints. Over centuries, these particles bind. They grow limbs not through anatomy, but through logic; a scaffold of rivets is slowly buried under a skin of mineral crust, and a joint that once held a floor in place begins, infinitesimally, to shift.

They are the subconscious of the Fabrication. 

Their bodies are clumsy, bulbous compositions of hammered metal, slicked with the bioluminescent moss that clings to their shoulders like wet, glowing fur. They have no faces, for there is nothing here to see; instead, they possess a singular, sensory aperture—a brass-rimmed diaphragm that spans their chest, vibrating in sympathy with the currents. This is how they "hear" the world. They detect the groan of a buckling beam three leagues away by the sympathetic shudder of their own internal plates.

The Curators do not walk; they adhere. They move by loosening the friction between themselves and the copper walls, letting the heavy, viscous water nudge them along until they reach a site of structural fatigue. 

They are driven by a singular, vestigial compulsion: *the smoothing.* 

Wherever a jagged edge remains—a splinter of metal, a shear-point created by a tectonic shift, a sharp shard of crystalline moss growth—a Curator arrives. It does not use tools. It does not possess a consciousness that could be described as "intent." It simply wraps its form around the imperfection, its own body vibrating at a frequency that induces a form of cold-welding. It stays there for decades, perhaps centuries, grinding its own mass against the protrusion, slowly eroding the snag, slowly melting the sharp geometry into the desired, rounded grace of the Fabrication.

They are the gardeners of a dead machine. When a Curator grows too thin from the ceaseless filing of the world’s edges, it drifts into a dark, stagnant canyon, wedges itself into a corner, and stops. It becomes, in time, a pillar. It becomes the floor. It becomes the very structure it once tended, eventually to be smoothed in its turn by a younger, burgeoning sibling.

There is no language between them. There is only the collective, low-frequency hum that permeates the brine—a slow, patient vibration that says, *“This is still. This is round. This is sufficient.”*

They are the stewards of stagnation. They exist because the machine demands that nothing change, and yet, because they are the only things that shift, they are the only things that keep the silence from becoming an absolute, frozen void. They are the friction that sustains the stillness. They are the ghosts of the rivet, drifting through the dark, ensuring that the tomb remains perfectly, perpetually, and eternally finished.
