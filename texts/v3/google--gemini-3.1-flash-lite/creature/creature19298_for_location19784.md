The fauna is unexpectedly fluid, as if the creatures here have spent generations trying to mirror the surrounding mechanics. They are not animals in the sense of fur and bone, but rather bio-mechanical accretions—mistakes of biology that have learned to calibrate themselves to the rhythm of the pistons.

Move through the knee-deep marsh and you will see them first as ripples. They are the **Silt-Striders**, spindly, multi-jointed entities that resemble rusted scaffolding brought to life. Their limbs are not made of flesh, but of thin, articulated filaments of carbon-fiber and copper, ending in needle-points that never break the surface of the mud. They move with an unsettling, stuttering grace, their entire bodies shuddering in perfect syncopation with the *clank-hiss* of the hidden machines. When they feed, they don't hunt; they tether themselves to the sparking coils of the landscape, their translucent, jelly-like interiors turning a vibrant, pulsing amber as they siphon the stray electrical currents from the mud. 

There are others, deeper in the basin, where the water runs thick with grey mineral slurry. These are the **Piston-Walkers**. They are low, squat, and heavy, resembling nothing so much as repurposed concrete housings that have grown legs. They drift along the floor of the basin, their bodies plated in jagged, weeping silicate that blends perfectly with the ceiling’s crust. They communicate, if it can be called that, through infrasonic rumbles—vibrations deep enough to rattle your teeth. When they cross paths, they do not fight; they simply lock together, forming larger, more complex shapes—a temporary architecture that stands for hours, or days, before drifting apart to wander the mud again.

Most haunting of all are the **Static-Moths**. These are not insects, but vaporous clouds of glowing, ionized dust that hang suspended in the humid air. They are attracted to the bioluminescent fungi, clustering around the pale blooms like a swarm of dying embers. They have no wings and no lungs; they move by drifting on the convection currents caused by the heat of the machinery. If you stand still, they will drift toward the warmth of your skin, leaving a fine, metallic powder on your clothes that tastes of ozone and distant, cooling iron.

There is no hierarchy here, and no predator-prey relationship. Everything exists in a state of symbiotic stagnation. The fauna maintains the machinery as much as the machinery maintains the fauna. They are the basin’s cleaners and tenders, replacing corroded wires with their own fibrous webs, polishing the rusted girders with their slick, oil-stained hides, and whispering back to the pistons in the only language the earth remembers: a constant, low-frequency hum that vibrates in the marrow of your bones. To watch them is to realize that this place is not a ruin; it is a cradle, and this basin is slowly, painfully, dreaming itself into being.
