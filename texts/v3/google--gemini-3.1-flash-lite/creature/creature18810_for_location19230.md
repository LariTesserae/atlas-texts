The inhabitants of the Vascular Sprawl are not dwellers so much as they are *symbionts of necessity*, creatures who have forgotten that anything exists outside the xylem.

To see them is to witness the physical tax the Sprawl extracts from its tenants. They are tall, gangly beings, elongated by the thin, oxygen-poor atmosphere and perpetually stooped to avoid the serrated, oscillating vines that drift through the interior corridors. Their skin is not flesh in the way you understand it; it is a mottled, translucent leather, stained a permanent, bruised violet by the weeping condensation that coats every surface. They take on the color of their home, shifting from the gray-green of the calcified bone-wood into the deep, oxidized copper tones of the brass conduits they tend.

They do not speak with vocal cords. Their throats have long since become redundant, replaced by a series of porous, vibrating flaps on their chests—an adaptation to the *thrum-hiss* of the industrial sap-pumps. When they “talk,” they match their own resonance to the city’s heart. A conversation sounds like a sequence of rhythmic clicks and pressurized whistles, mirroring the grinding of the iron-clad conduits. If you listen closely, a group of locals arguing sounds indistinguishable from a piston seizing in its housing or a valve venting excess steam.

They are the **"Sap-tenders,"** though they have no name for themselves. Their lives are defined by the maintenance of the fluid. They carry heavy, brass-bound stethoscopes pressed against the city’s walls, listening to the flow of the nutrient-rich blood of their world. When a branch begins to calcify too rapidly or a conduit clogs with crystalline sap, they move with a frantic, insectile grace to apply caustic acids or kinetic shears. 

But there is a tragedy in their existence: they are part of the architecture’s digestive tract. 

Because they spend their lives in the weeping districts, drinking the iridescent condensation and breathing the spore-filled exhaust of the boilers, they do not remain static. As a Sap-tender ages, they do not simply die. They become, quite literally, "part of the furniture." They grow brittle. Their limbs begin to branch. The iridescent mist settles into their pores, hardening their joints into fixed, vine-like hinges. 

You will find them in quiet corridors—the elderly, the sick, and the exhausted—anchored permanently into the wall panels. They are not dead; their internal processes have merely synched with the plant's metabolic cycle. They become living junctions, their fingers stretching into the brass piping to act as biological pressure regulators. They spend their final decades as ornate, semi-conscious statues of brass and bone, serving as the city’s nervous system, their eyes clouding over with a pale, milky film that observes the shifting light of the canopy for centuries after their hearts have ceased to beat.

There are also the **"Scavengers of the Void,"** though they are rarer, and perhaps more dangerous. These are the creatures that have detached themselves from the maintenance rituals entirely. They are draped in motley rags of woven-fungus and discarded copper sheets. They do not tend the pumps; they hunt the amber-shelled beasts that drift through the voids. They are lithe, terrifyingly quiet, and possess a preternatural ability to navigate the Euclidean-defying folds of the branches. They move across the undersides of the city, suspended over kilometers of empty air, clinging to the architecture with long, prehensile digits. They are the only ones who see the Sprawl for what it is: a dying god. Where the Sap-tenders look for ways to extend the life of the circulation, the Scavengers have begun to harvest the plant, peeling back the bark to look for the deep, pulsing nodes of pure energy hidden in the heartwood.

In the Sprawl, you are never truly alone. If you stand still long enough, you feel the soft pressure of a hundred thousand long-dead ancestors shifting their weight in the walls. You feel the architecture looking back at you, calculating whether you are a threat to the flow, or merely more compost for the roots.
