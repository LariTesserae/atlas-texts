The entities that inhabit the High Plateau of Oros—the tenders of the erosion—are known not by names, but by the frequency of their internal friction. They are the **Scrimari**.

They are not born; they are precipitated. When the fungal colonies reach a state of hyper-maturity, they exhale a particularly dense, crystalline spore that settles into the shadow-damp basins. Over centuries, these spores weave themselves into the articulate, spindly forms that roam the basalt flats. Their bodies are composed of thousands of obsidian-dark fibers—resembling stiffened silk or volcanic glass threads—that hum when the wind cuts through them. They have no faces, only a singular, shallow concavity where a head might be, filled with a viscous, shifting luminescence that matches the pale, milky color of the sky.

To watch a Scrimari move is to witness the defiance of linear momentum. They do not walk so much as they "negotiate" the space they occupy. Because the plateau is prone to folds in geometry, a Scrimari might raise a limb in one corner of a valley and find it emerging from a wall of fossilized fungi a mile distant. They navigate this instability with the grace of high-wire performers, their fibrous, needle-thin fingers constantly reaching out to touch the stone as if feeling for the "seams" of reality.

Their existence is defined by the Tending. In the spiraling, villa-sized fungal mounds, the Scrimari occupy the interiors like monks in a cathedral of soft, pulsating light. They perform a labor that serves no hunger and produces no harvest. They spend their eons polishing the river-stone foundations of their towers, scrubbing away the mineral salts that the air leaves behind, and rearranging the white stalks of the fungus to ensure the colony’s "breath" remains rhythmic.

If they speak at all, it is not through air. When two Scrimari pass one another on the scree, they pause, interweaving their obsidian appendages for a moment of static discharge. A flash of violet light pulses between them—a silent, jagged transmission of memory. They are sharing the exhaustion of the plateau. They are remembering the rivers that turned to dust, the heat that vanished eons ago, and the dreams that the landscape is currently having.

At night—though night is only a subtle deepening of the ambient milk-light—the Scrimari gather in the circular bowls of the bedrock. They stand perfectly still, mirroring the surrounding fungi. The fibers of their bodies begin to vibrate in sympathy with the deep, rhythmic *thrum* of the plateau. In these moments, they are not separate entities; they are the plateau’s conscious nerves, reporting back the slow, terminal state of their world.

They are indifferent to visitors, though if a traveler were to arrive, the Scrimari would not be hostile. They would be confused by the speed of the intruder. To a Scrimari, a heartbeat is a chaotic, frantic drum-beat that threatens to tear the fabric of the atmosphere. They would likely treat a human with the same solemnity one might show a falling leaf—a brief, tragic acceleration of life that will surely reach its final stop long before the fungi bloom again.

They do not fear the end of Oros. They are, in a sense, the shadows cast by the plateau’s inevitable collapse. They are waiting for the final pulse, the moment when the last bit of friction fades, the stone finally stops its thrumming, and the geography is allowed to fold itself into a state of absolute, dreamless rest. Until that moment, they continue to polish, to weave, and to wait in the cold, translucent silence, tending to a world that has already finished happening.
