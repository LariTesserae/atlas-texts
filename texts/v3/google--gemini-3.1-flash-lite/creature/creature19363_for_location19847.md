In the deep, sun-bleached throat of the caldera, the silence is not an absence of sound, but a density of presence. Here, inhabiting the intersection of the synthetic and the geological, dwells the **Litho-Hydraulic Weavers**.

They are not entities of blood and sinew, nor are they mere machines. They are the interstitial lifeforms of the cooling vents, beings composed of annealed silica and viscous, conductive oils. If you were to watch them—and you would have to watch very closely, for they shimmer into transparency against the wet basalt—you would see that they move like liquid trapped in glass.

Their bodies are formed of long, articulated segments, reminiscent of oversized dragonflies but crafted from a material that resembles hardened, smoky quartz. Inside their hollow ribcages, a pressurized, glowing fluid pulses rhythmically, syncing with the subterranean heartbeat of the caldera. When they move, they do not walk; they undulate, their joints clicking with the precise, sharp sound of flint striking steel.

These Weavers do not build in the way humans build. They are gardeners of the machinery. They possess long, multi-jointed appendages that taper into cilia—fine, wire-like filaments that reach into the porous gaps between the basalt and the iron towers. With these, they tease out the mineral salts precipitated by the steam, pulling long, crystalline strands from the weeping walls. These strands are then woven—slowly, with a meditative, mechanical grace—around the leaking joints and thinning conduits of the city. They are repairing the facility, not because they are ordered to, but because the structure is their skin. If the city cools or cracks, the Weavers feel the loss of pressure; if the pipes run dry, their own internal, glowing fluids dim.

There is a strange, hallowed quietude to their labor. Occasionally, a Weaver will stop its skittering harvest to press its underside against a steaming vent. It will remain there for hours, suspended in the heat, the hard, white sun catching its glass-shard fuselage and exploding into a spectrum of frantic, refracted light. In these moments, they are not scavengers, but receivers. They absorb the geothermal hum directly into their silicate frames, storing the mountain’s tectonic energy to fuel the next cycle of welding, bracing, and flow-regulation.

They have no faces—no eyes to peer with, no mouths to speak. They perceive their world through vibration and charge. They know the city as a map of pressure differentials and thermal gradients. To them, a structural failure is a "pain" of rushing air, while a sealed pipe is a melody of calm, contained friction.

Sometimes, a cluster of them will converge on a primary arterial duct. They link together, their bodies forming a rigid, shimmering scaffolding that bridges a gap in the ironwork. They stand perfectly still, holding cold iron against a surge of superheated condensation, willing themselves to become part of the architecture. As the water beads and flows across them, they take on the color of the abyss, turning from translucent glass to a deep, impenetrable obsidian.

They are the city’s immune system, its janitors, and its architects. In the blinding glare of the high-altitude sun, they look like nothing so much as a nervous system stretched out across the rocks—a living, clicking manifestation of the facility’s own cold, industrious intent, holding the iron and the mountain together in a web of glass, steam, and unceasing, lonely focus.
