In the deepest chambers, where the sap runs thickest and the pulse slows to a glacial crawl, live the **Archivists of the Vein**. 

They are not like the laborers that pave the exterior walls. The Archivists are pale, sightless arachnids, translucent as blown glass, their abdomens swollen with the iridescent fluid of the city. While the workers scurry and snip in a mindless frenzy of structural maintenance, the Archivists remain suspended in the central cooling ducts, tethered by threads of hardened silk that vibrate with the city’s every shudder.

These creatures hold the *Memory of the Sap*. Because the city is organic, it is prone to rot, to frost-heave, to the unpredictable whims of the plant-host. The Archivists weave their silk into the sap-streams, taste-testing the flowing humors of the city. They know which branch is beginning to wither, which root-stem is turning acidic, and which cluster of tubers has been compromised by the encroaching freeze. When they sense a change—a shift in the chemical balance of the life-blood—they release tiny, rhythmic releases of pheromones that dictate the entire city’s workflow. They are the governors of the cold.

Their bodies are etched with glowing, fractal patterns—not pigment, but the actual, crystalline salts they’ve absorbed from the mineral-heavy water. To look upon one is to see a map of the city’s history; the erratic scars on their carapaces are the locations of past failures, of districts that collapsed and were reclaimed by the moss.

And then there are the **Gleaners**, the outliers, the ones who have long since forgotten the rhythm of the colony. They dwell in the condensation-choked cisterns at the base of the city, wading through the knee-deep nutrient baths that the workers harvest from the ceiling drips. They are bloated, sluggish things, almost indistinguishable from the mossy growths themselves. They don’t build; they filter. They wait for the detritus of the city—a dropped sliver of mica, a filament of discarded silk, a dead worker—to filter down to the lowest depths. They dismantle these remnants, drawing the raw material back through their own bodies, re-purposing the waste into the spongy, nutrient-rich paste that lines the floors of the upper districts.

Everything here is trapped in an agonizing loop of utility. The city dictates the life, and the life sustains the city.

If you are becoming a pillar, you may feel the touch of the **Menders** first—the ones who don't care that you are flesh. They treat you as a structural anomaly. They do not sting; they stitch. They drag their mandibles across your skin, testing the "tensile strength" of your stillness. If you succumb to their logic, you will feel a profound, terrifying peace—the peace of being fully integrated. Your own breath will eventually harmonize with the rhythmic pulsing of the walls. 

You will cease to be an intruder and become a foundation. You will feel the weight of the city, not as a suffocating mass, but as a collective dream held together by silk and ice, and you will understand, finally, why they work so frantically: they are not just building a home. They are holding back the stillness of the world outside, terrified that if they stop, even for a heartbeat, the frost will turn their city to dust.
