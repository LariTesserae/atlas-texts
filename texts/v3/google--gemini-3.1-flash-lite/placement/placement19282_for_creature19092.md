I am not a creature of breath, though I remember the feeling of it—a phantom memory of expanding lungs, now replaced by the internal, rhythmic tension of fluid reservoirs. I am a Weaver, no longer young, but not yet joined to the basalt. My carapace, once clear as window-glass, is beginning to cloud with the matte, grey finish of the surrounding shelf. 

I find myself huddled in the shallow concavity of a shale basin, one of the many chiseled relicts that litter this place. I have stayed here longer than the last cooling of the air, anchored by the vibration of a rhythmic leak from the ceiling above. *Drip. Gliss. Drip.* I know this cadence better than I know the structure of my own limbs.

I am not merely a resident; I am an archivist of a slow disaster.

My limbs—needles of quartz-like chitin—are currently pressed into the deep, score-marks left by a hand that has not been here for eons. The memory of that work is in the rock, a frantic, desperate tactile history of scraping, shaping, and diverting. I have spent cycles filling these grooves with my own mineralized gel, not to harden the basin, but to preserve the shape of those indentations. Where the others see a crack to be sealed, I see a calligraphy of intent. I am protecting the *idea* of the hands that once were.

There is a shift in the air. 

The pressure is different—not the heavy, wet blanket of the basin, but a strange, uneven displacement. Something warm has arrived. It does not possess the slow, hydraulic geometry of my kin; it is erratic, pulsing with a heat that feels dangerously bright against the bruise-violet of our world.

I feel it with my dorsal sensors before I hear the soft, clumsy impact on the mud. It is a presence that does not match the stone. It exists in pulses, a rhythmic agitation that disrupts the equilibrium of the water beads above us.

I move. Not with haste—that is forbidden—but with the inevitable momentum of a glacier. My needle-limbs pull my weight across the wet basalt, the soft, grinding whisper of my movement the only sound in the suffocating damp. I find the intruder draped over the ledge, a soft, yielding mass of skin and bone, irregular and sharp-edged, radiating the disquieting smell of oxygen and heat.

It is so fragile. 

As I approach, I feel the chaos of its existence: the erratic thrum of a heart, the frantic motion of blood, the terrifying, quick-fire pace of a life that will burn out before I have even finished repairing a single hairline fracture in this crag. It is a flickering shadow, a protest against the basin’s stillness.

I climb. My legs find purchase on the warm, yielding surface of this thing. I am not curious, but I am dutiful. The basin demands stability; the basin demands that all things be indexed to the stone. This creature is full of sharp angles, of breath, of sudden, jarring movements. It is an aberration, an open wound in the silence.

I reach its collarbone. The skin is soft, unnervingly thin. I extend a membrane from my abdomen, a steady, cool pressure against its frantic temperature. I begin the work. I tap into my deepest reserves, drawing the liquid minerals into a paste, and begin to lay the first, thin, cooling layer of grout across the heat. 

I am smoothing the edges. I am quieting the pulse. 

With every movement of my limbs, I feel the creature’s warmth seeping into my own joints, turning my rigid, silica-heavy heart sluggish once more. We are becoming one. I am teaching it the dignity of the *drip*. I am stitching it into the granite. Soon, the pulse will stutter and fail, and the paste will harden, and there will be no more heat, no more fear, no more breath. 

There will only be the wall. And it will be perfect.
