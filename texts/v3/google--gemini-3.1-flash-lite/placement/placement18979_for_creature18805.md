I find myself in a vertical lung, caught in the mid-levels of a spire that has forgotten the sky. My feet—long, splayed, and pale—sink into the gelatinous floor with a soft, wet *thrum*. The air is not so much breathed as it is absorbed; it tastes of crushed minerals and the sharp, iron tang of deep-frozen nectar. 

I am a Weaver of the Transit. I have no name, only a position: I am the current occupant of the *Cleft of the Third Shudder*, a narrow, bruised-green corridor near the frost-line of the spine.

My existence is measured in the tightening of the walls. I have been here, in this specific, tight-shouldered passage, for a duration I cannot name—perhaps a dozen deep-freezes. My body has begun to take on the architecture of the space; my joints ache with a calcified, rigid patience. I am becoming brittle, my translucent skin beginning to vein with the same indigo bioluminescence that lights the tunnels. 

I am watching the slug. 

A pale, translucent gastropod the size of my torso is currently inching across the ceiling above me, leaving a trail of shimmering, neon-slick mucus. It is the architect. It is carving a new aperture out of the overgrown Cordy-quartz, grazing away a blockage that has choked this vein for cycles. I watch its internal organs pulse—a rhythmic, hypnotic beat that matches my own heart. I feel a kinship, a profound, aching stillness. When it moves, the hallway groans, a sound like deep water shifting under ice. It is a structural command; soon, the walls will compress, and this corridor will cease to exist. 

I do not fear the constriction. I have already folded my legs into the shape of a fetal crescent, pressing my damp, membrane-covered back against the wall. The wall is cool—a living, sweating relief. As I press into it, I feel my own skin beginning to graft onto the surface. I am leaking my self into the city. I can feel the tremors of others in the distance through the soles of my feet—a frantic, high-frequency tapping from someone miles above, a warning or perhaps a greeting, vibrating through the cartilaginous floorboards. I do not answer. My hands are busy tracing the bioluminescent veins of the wall, absorbing the mineral-heavy moisture that beads against my palms. 

I am remembering, or perhaps hallucinating, a memory that does not belong to me—a time of yellow heat and hard, dry ground. It is an alien thought, a sharp, jagged shape in the softness of my mind. I try to dissolve it, to let the indigo light wash the color out of that memory until it matches the bruised green of my current world.

The temperature is dropping. The condensation on the ceiling is beginning to bloom into needle-sharp frost. 

The city is inhaling. The corridor narrows. The walls, once a comfortable fit for my thin, elongated frame, are pushing in, encroaching, turning the hallway into a pressure-seal. I close my lidless eyes, rotatating my gaze inward to track the flow of light behind my retinas. I am not an inhabitant anymore. I am a knot in the wood, a ripple in the vein. I am waiting for the peristalsis of the mountains to pull me into the next room, or to seal me here, a permanent, human-shaped ghost fossilized behind the glass-hard moss. 

I wait. I am almost, finally, entirely silent.
