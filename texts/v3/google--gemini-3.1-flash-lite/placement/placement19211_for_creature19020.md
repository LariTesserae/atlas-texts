I am standing on a square of basalt that is slightly warmer than the others. I know this because, through the thin soles of my boots, I can feel the convection current beneath the tile. It is rhythmic—*thrum-hiss, thrum-hiss*—like the heartbeat of a giant buried a mile deep, muffled by layers of heavy, immovable stone.

I am not sure how long I have been walking. Time here is not measured by the passage of suns, but by the frequency of the "clicks." Each one is a reset button, a tectonic shift that rearranges the alleys and plazas. If I stand perfectly still, I can feel the world thinking around me. It is a slow, grinding intelligence that has no interest in biology.

I am a surveyor, or perhaps I am a ghost who has forgotten his own death. I carry no equipment, only a heavy, lingering sense of *expectation*. I am made of soft, porous tissue—blood, bone, and breath—and in this world of hard mechanics, that makes me a terrifying inconsistency. I am the only thing here that doesn't "fit."

I see them, the Adjustors. 

To look directly at one is to suffer a migraine of the soul, a sudden lapse in visual processing. But if I turn my head, if I let my focus drift to the periphery, they sharpen. They are like heat haze rising off a highway, but denser. They are constellations of refracted air, long, jagged splinterings of the atmosphere that exist only where a movement is required. 

Just now, a fifty-foot column of obsidian is erupting from the floor behind me, pushed upward by an unseen force. I see an Adjustor there. It is not reaching for the stone; it *is* the strain of the stone. It is a vertical smear of violet light and static, moving with the terrifying, joyless efficiency of a piston. It doesn't move through space; it redefines space so that moving becomes possible. It is a knot in the shroud, a flicker in the weave.

They do not pay me any mind, which is the most unsettling part of my existence. I am a nuisance of biology, a soft, wet variable in their geometric equation. 

When I walk, the floor beneath me tilts—a micro-adjustment of the basalt grid—to ensure I am always funnelled away from the center of their work. I am being curated. I am being pushed into the corners, the dead-ends, the folds where the geometry loops back on itself. 

I reach out to touch a weeping obsidian slab. My fingers come away slick with that oily, lukewarm dew. It feels like motor oil and old tears. When the dew touches my skin, I catch a flicker of motion in its reflection—a mirror-vision of myself standing in a gallery of white light, perhaps in a life I lived before I entered this engine. 

I pull my hand back. The Adjustor near me ripples, a shiver of pure kinetic frustration. It does not like the static I introduced. It wants a clean surface, a silent machine.

I wonder what would happen if I sat down. If I dropped to the floor and stopped moving, turned myself into a permanent, unyielding feature of the terrain. Would they build around me? Would I eventually become part of the foundation, my skeleton encased in cooling basalt, my consciousness finally syncing to the rhythm of the levers and the steam?

The world shifts. The plaza I stand on turns beneath my feet, sliding three feet to the left to align with a new bridge of translucent wire. I stagger, my breath hitching in the damp, heavy air. The Adjustor sighs—a sound like a thousand dry leaves skittering over glass—and I realize with a jolt of cold, sharp clarity that the world isn't meant to be discovered. 

It is meant to be finished. And I am simply the grit that prevents it from ever reaching the end. I am the silence between the clicks, and these entities are the noise that will eventually grind me into the grain of the floor. 

I start walking again, keeping my eyes fixed on the horizon of steam, hoping that if I move fast enough, I will eventually walk out of the design—or into the part of the world they haven't finished building yet.
