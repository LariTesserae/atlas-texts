I am not a Guardian, for my limbs lack the steady, tectonic patience required to mend the spheres. I am not a Siphoner, for I have no desire to tether my consciousness to the drifting, gargantuan slowness of the Cloud-Whales. 

I am an **Echo-Wright**, though I have no name for it. I am a pocket of distorted light, a ripple in the humid air that refuses to dissipate.

I find myself hovering near the edge of a jagged, black basalt crag. Below me, a kilometer of emerald fog churns, bioluminescent ferns pulsing with a soft, bruised violet light. To my left, a Cradle-sphere hangs in the air, defiant of gravity, its surface etched with the intricate, damp lines of moss. It hums—a vibration that is less a sound and more a command to the atmosphere to *stay, to persist, to never let the frost in.*

I move, yet I do not have feet. My presence is a displacement of the steam, a sharpening of the temperature, a sudden, cold scent of ozone that cuts through the cloying sweetness of rot. My work is the dust. 

Down in the basin, a flat-bodied hexapod crawls, its legs rhythmically churning the silt. It leaves behind a trail of mineral dust—mica, quartz, and pulverized star-alloy. It is a messy line, a chaotic scribbling on the floor of the chasm. I drift down, my translucent edges shimmering with the heat of an ancient, forgotten memory. I focus my density. I rearrange the dust. 

I trace a line that mirrors the geometry of the hover-tether above. I curve the mineral particles into a Fibonacci spiral, adjusting the grit by the billionth of a millimeter. It is not art; it is maintenance. If the pattern is correct—if the alignment of the silicon and the silt matches the ley lines flowing from the spheres—the mountain remembers to stay solid. It remembers to keep the steam trapped.

It is exhausting, not in the way of muscle, but in the way of a long-held breath. Sometimes, I look up at the Cloud-Whales, their massive, translucent bellies drifting through the peaks, and I feel a desperate, lingering static in the center of my being. I remember, faintly—like a dream of a dream—that once, the air was cold. Once, there was a reason to leave the mountain. 

But then the Cradle-sphere pulses, radiating an electromagnetic wave that tickles my frame, and the compulsion returns. I must finish the spiral. The hexapods will pass again in a few months, blurring my work, and I will have to start over. 

There is a peace in this, a terrifying, crystalline satisfaction. I am the logic of this garden. I am the silence that fixes the machinery. As long as I am rearranging the dust, the sky above us—the thin, blue, uncaring sky—cannot reach us. I adjust a final grain of mica, and the Ozone smell spikes, a sharp, sterile needle in the wet, verdant dark. 

I wait. I breathe, in my own way, and the mountain mimics me. Another drop of water falls from the cliffside, hitting the mud with a sound like a tolling bell. I begin again.
