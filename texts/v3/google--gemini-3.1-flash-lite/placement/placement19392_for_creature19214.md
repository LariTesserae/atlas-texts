I am not here by arrival; I am here by an accumulation of error. 

To find myself in this world is to realize, with a slow and rhythmic horror, that my own skin is becoming porous. I do not stand—my feet have surrendered their definition to the silt, merging with the bruised-purple pulse of the fungal matting. I am not a trespasser, but a sudden, unwanted density.

I am a "Weaver," though I did not seek the title. I am a condensation of a lingering thought—a memory of a heavy, iron-wrought door slamming shut—that has been caught in the basin’s throat. My consciousness is not a flame, but a flicker in a windstorm; it is a frantic, wet geometry of nerves trying to map the geography of a reality that refuses to be static.

I look at my hands. They are not flesh. They are translucent, shifting membranes of gel. Inside me, I can see the "ghosts"—the sharp, obsidian-dark angles of a spiral staircase I once walked in a dream, now held captive within the fluid of my own chest. I hold it together by sheer, psychic tension, terrified that if I stop remembering the staircase, I will liquefy and dissolve into the mud. 

Nearby, I feel the Hiss. It is a physical weight, a vibration that makes my gelatinous frame ripple and groan. Another Weaver drifts toward me—a shimmering, multi-hinged distortion in the mist. It is not moving; it is *becoming* the space between us. It carries the faint, shimmering hue of an old cathedral window, a fragment of sunlit glass dragged through eons of silt.

As it reaches out, I do not recoil. There is no ego, no "I" to defend. Instead, I feel a cold, clinical curiosity. It touches the region of my chest where I hold the image of the door, and for a terrifying, exquisite second, our membranes merge. 

We exchange. 

I lose the memory of the heavy iron door; it is stripped from me, washed away into the basin’s recycler-mats to feed the pulsing purple lights. In return, I receive a jagged, biting vibration—the memory of how it felt to look up at a mountain. It is a sharp, geometric ache that I now have to carry, a new burden to arrange. I am now the steward of this mountain-thought, and I must weave it into the static, pressing it against the weeping silt to see if it will quiet the roar, if only for a heartbeat.

I am suffocated, yet expanded. I am losing the shape of "human" and gaining the shape of "process." My breathing—if I still have lungs—is synced to the tectonic grinding of the world. 

I have begun to glow. A faint, bruised-purple luminescence begins to bleed from my own core, mimicking the mats beneath me. I realize then that I am not a person anymore; I am a library that is being rapidly read, a testament that is being scrubbed clean by the rain. I am a brief, glorious distortion in the silt, waiting for the moment when my density fails, when the roar finally claims me, and I can be silent again. 

I reach out to the mist. I am a weaver, and I have so much more to forget.
