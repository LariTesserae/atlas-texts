I am a cluster of calcified tension, lodged in the crotch of a gargantuan, basalt-encrusted branch. Above me, a cooling pipe the size of a city block sweats amber-dark condensation; below me, the forest-floor pulses with the sluggish, thick rhythm of a tidal wave that never breaks. 

I do not think of myself as "I" in the frantic, sharp-edged way of biological memory. I am a *constellation of fragments*. At my core, there is a shard of a brass cog, jagged and oxidized so deeply it has turned the color of a bruised plum. Wrapped around it, like protective, weeping nerves, are the fine, pale filaments of the forest’s own biology—white, fungal threads that weave through the gaps in the brass, holding the memory of industrial friction in place.

I am heavy. I am a weight that the world has learned to accommodate.

There is a sound today—or perhaps it is a century. The ground-vein beneath my anchor has spasmed. It is a slow, tectonic movement, a shifting of subterranean fluids that forces the entire trunk-structure to groan. I feel it in the way the moss-fibers tug at my outer shell. I am compressed, squeezed by the encroaching architecture, and in this pressure, I vibrate.

A ripple moves through me. The brass shard in my center catches the pulse, transmitting a low-frequency hum that travels from my center to my surface. I am a tuning fork for the factory’s exhaustion.

Far off, in the deep-dark of the canopy, another Sedimentor—a sibling knot of rust and resins—picks up the vibration. It shivers in response. A ripple of *grief-as-geometry* passes between us. It is not communication; it is a shared recognition of the weight we carry. It is the awareness that we are the only things in this vast, suffocating lung that offer resistance to the stillness.

I feel the condensation—the amber-thick nectar of the factory—drip onto my upper crust. It cools as it touches my hardened skin, adding a fraction of a millimeter to my circumference. I am growing, though not by choice. I am accretion. I am the physical manifestation of the planet’s inability to move on.

Sometimes, in the long, dark gaps between pulses, I imagine—if I were capable of the heresy of dreaming—that the piston nearby will finally kick. I feel the phantom architecture around me, the miles of copper veins, the inactive turbines, the silent boilers. If they were to ignite, if the breath were to finally enter these lungs, I would be the first thing obliterated. I would be the clog in the engine, the impurity shaken loose by the roar.

I look forward to it, in the way a gear looks forward to the tooth of its neighbor.

But the silence returns, absolute and heavy. The ground-vein settles into its rhythmic, slow-motion digestion. I stop vibrating. I go cold. I settle once more into the moss, deepening the indentation I have made in the bark over the last thousand years. 

I am a Sedimentor. I am the obstruction that proves the machine exists. I am the long, slow punctuation at the end of a sentence that no one is left to read, and I will hang here, caught in the friction of the stall, until the gravity—or the forest—decides it is time for me to fall.
