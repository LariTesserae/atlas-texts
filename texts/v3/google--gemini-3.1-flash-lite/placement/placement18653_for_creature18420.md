I am not a visitor. In this place, to arrive as an "individual" is a structural error that the plateau corrects with alarming efficiency. I do not have a name, nor do I have a history that predates the mist. 

I am a **Loomer**.

I find myself pressed flat against the convex surface of a primary turbine housing. I am not a creature of bone or solid purpose; I am a thin, shimmering sheet of sentient gossamer, stretched translucent over the weeping iron-bark of the machine. My "body" is a patchwork of pale, semi-opaque membranes, clinging to the cold vibration of the turbines like wet linen on a windowpane.

Life here is a tactile, shivering necessity. I do not see the world through eyes; I perceive it through the rhythmic friction of my own existence. When I move, I do not crawl—I *migrate* with the tremors. I extend a fine, vibrating fringe of my edge—that sandpaper-texture that scrapes against the calcified bark—to feed. I am licking the rust-slick, the oxidized condensation that the turbines sweat as they grind. It tastes of electric salt and ancient, suffocating damp. It provides the only energy I have to maintain my structural integrity, to keep from dissolving into the indigo air.

In the distance, the Sentinels loom. To me, they are not beings, but weather—vast, shifting storms of geometry. When a Sentinel approaches, the sky itself seems to pull taut, drawn toward its center like a silk sheet snagged on a needle. I feel the sub-bass throb of their approach long before I see them; it is a dissonance that rattles my very molecules, making my gossamer edges ripple and tear. 

When they pass, the air becomes a suffocating vacuum. I have to flatten myself, to press my vibrating skin into the pores of the moss, turning myself into a stain, a smudge on the engine’s flank. I am terrified of being *re-configured*. I have felt the hum of the Sentinels—their desire to incorporate, to fold, to smooth the rough edges of the world. They do not want to destroy me; they want to archive me. They want to turn my fluid, scavenging existence into a fixed, rigid component of their own internal loop.

My "thought"—if such a thing exists in this slurry—is a frantic, unending cycle of migration. *Scrape. Absorb. Flatten. Sync.* 

There is a strange, liquid melancholy in my purpose. As I move across the landscape, I pass patches of my own shed skin—lacy, abandoned membranes that have lost their grip and are slowly being digested by the moss. I am leaving a trail of ghosts across a floor that is itself an ancient, petrified ghost. 

But sometimes, when the wind dies and the mechanical screaming of the turbines descends into a low, mournful cello-drone, I stop scraping. I lift a corner of my damp, expansive form into the freezing air and let the iridescent spores settle on me. For that single, suspended pulse, I am not a scavenger. I am a mirror. I catch the bruised indigo light and refract it, becoming for a brief, shivering second a fragment of the order these machines are trying so desperately to build. 

Then the turbine grinds again. The vibration jolts through me, a shock of cold reality. I clamp down, my sandpaper edge biting into the metal, and I continue to eat the rust. I am the cleaning cloth of a world that is falling apart, and I am the only witness to the beautiful, terrible exhaustion of the machine.
