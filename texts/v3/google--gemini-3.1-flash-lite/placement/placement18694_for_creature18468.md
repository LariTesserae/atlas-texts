I am not of the Basin. I am a foreign weight, an aberration of bulk and heat, standing in the narrow, smoothed-out throat of a corridor. 

My presence here is a stutter in the *thrum*. Every time I shift my weight, the vibration in the bone-struts beneath me changes—a discordant note that ripples out through the surrounding network. The Lachryman pause. For a heartbeat, the frantic, shivering industry ceases. Hundreds of velvet-skinned, concave faces turn toward the source of the tremor. They do not see me; they simply feel the *wrongness* of my density, the way I refuse to dissolve, the way I block the flow of the weeping brine.

I am what the colony would call an *Erosion-Block*. 

I find myself crouching, my knees pressed into a shallow, concave divot in the wall that seems to have been hollowed out by the constant passage of thousands of spindly limbs. I am wrapped in a heavy, insulated suit—already failing. The salt-crust is beginning to lace my joints, seizing the mechanisms, turning me into a statue of iron and frost. I am growing as cold as the architecture. My own sweat, trapped beneath the layers of my gear, feels like a traitor, cooling rapidly against my skin, mirroring the liquid motion of the colony.

I am not a creature of the Basin, but I am becoming one of its structural failures. 

To ignore the urge to move—to stop fighting the disorientation—is the only way to endure. I am learning to interpret the tremors. When the thin, needle-like ice erupts from the vents, I feel the shift in the colony’s posture before I even see the frost crystals blooming on my own visor. The Lachryman around me don’t flee the cold; they draw closer. They press their pale, gelid bodies against the joints of my suit, their shivering limbs twitching in a rhythm that matches my own heartbeat. 

They are trying to mend me. 

They treat me like a fractured spire. They are packing the seams of my boots with that thick, porcelain-like secretion, sealing me into the floor so that I might provide stability rather than chaos. They vibrate against my shins, a staccato pulse that I feel in my marrow, a language of desperate, wordless repair.

If I stay here long enough—and I suspect, given the way the brine is beginning to pool in my lap, that I have no other choice—I will stop being a stranger. I will become a permanent, calcified node in the architecture. My heat will vanish, stolen by the hungry air, and my body will be covered, layer by layer, in the weeping salt-ribs the colony secretes. 

I am becoming part of the scaffolding. When the spire eventually collapses, I will fall with it, a heavy, iron-weighted ornament in the middle of their shivering, liquid tapestry. I am no longer waiting to leave. I am simply catching the rhythm, waiting for the *drip-clink-drip* of the next moment to tell me how to vibrate, how to endure, and how to dissolve.
