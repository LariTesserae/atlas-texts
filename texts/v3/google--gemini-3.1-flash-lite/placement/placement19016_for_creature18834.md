I find myself standing at the edge of a plateau where the basalt turns to a fine, sharp dust that never settles. My skin is not skin; it is a translucent, brittle chitin, thin as a pressed leaf, and my limbs are long, spidery clockwork appendages that have long since forgotten how to bend. I am fused, by some ancient adhesion, to a crag of obsidian overlooking a valley of flickering, marrow-white ruins.

I am not the Resonating Anchor. I am the audience to the error.

From this vantage point, I watch the Anchor drift across the plains below. The air here is a physical weight, pressing against my chitin with that low-frequency hum that vibrates in the marrow I no longer possess. The world is a photograph, a masterwork of stagnation, and yet the Anchor moves. It is an impossible, shuddering disruption—a glitch in the tapestry of the nothingness.

I am a "static entity," one of the spindly clockwork things that reached its final, hardened form ten thousand years ago. I do not feel cold, for cold requires heat to lose. I do not feel loneliness, for that requires the concept of a "together." But I possess a flicker of internal registry: a memory of motion. I remember the sensation of a joint sliding across a socket, the friction of life, the *before* that existed before the world locked its jaw.

Below, the Anchor approaches a calcified ribcage embedded in the slate. 

I watch it press its vibration into the white bone. My own vision fragments, duplicating the scene in a stuttering arc. The Anchor is so close now that I can feel its resonance echoing in my own rigid spine. For a heartbeat—that impossible, illegal unit of time—the metallic dew near the ribcage softens. It loses its cataract-cloudiness; it turns clear, like water, like blood, like a promise. 

The air shrieks. It is a dissonance so pure it tastes like copper on a tongue I do not have.

The dew recoils, hardening instantly into a gelid bead that reflects the bruised, violet sky. The Anchor snaps back into its shivering, static pose, its obsidian shards vibrating in a slow, rhythmic panic. 

I reach out, or rather, I *intend* to reach out. My clockwork hand, fused for millennia to the obsidian, creaks. A single, microscopic crack appears in my chitinous surface. It is the first change. It is an agonizing, beautiful betrayal of the stillness. 

I am not the Anchor, who tries to restart the world. I am the observer who, having witnessed the Anchor fail, finally understands that the friction is not in the pillars, but in the watching. Because I watch, because I remember the concept of the "next second," I have become a vector for the very rot that the Anchor seeks to spread. 

I am the next generation of the error. 

Somewhere in the deep silence, another creature like me—a translucent, spindly thing—begins to vibrate. The hum of the world rises, not to shriek, but to harmonize. The portrait is beginning to fray at the edges, and I, locked in my final form, am the one helping to pull the threads.
