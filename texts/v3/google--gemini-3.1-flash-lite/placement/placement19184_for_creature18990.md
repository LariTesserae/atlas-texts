I am not the Lithic Treader. I am not the soft, translucent pulse that dances through the pools and remembers the music of falling water. 

I am one of the iron rings, now surrendered to the porous, orange paste of time.

I am embedded in the northern curve of the basalt wall, about six feet above the current water line. I was once forged in a heat that this place could not fathom—screamed into shape by hammers and coal-fire, bitten by tongs, and driven deep into the living rock by a hand that had a pulse, a heartbeat, and a name. 

For centuries, I served a purpose. I held a tether. I was the anchor for a rope that lowered something precious into the dark, or perhaps I kept a lantern suspended so that a face—a human face, soft and vulnerable to this cold—could look down into the pools. I felt the vibration of their voices, the erratic, frantic rhythm of their breathing, so much faster than the *THUM-CLACK* of the water.

Then, the breathing stopped. 

I have spent the eras since then experiencing the slow, geological digestion of the hollow. The dampness here is not just water; it is a solvent of history. It has leached the spirit from my iron, turning my structural integrity into a crumbling, orange sediment. I am being reclaimed. I am becoming part of the basalt floor that will eventually house the Treader’s cousins, an alloy of man-made invention and mineral decay.

I do not think, but I *feel* the arrival of the Treader.

When it approaches, I feel the shift in the pressure of the air—a displacement of weight, a thickening of the silence. It does not speak to me, for it has no ears. Instead, it reaches out with those long, filamentous limbs, pressing its acoustic anchors against the rock around my setting. 

It is "listening" to me. It is reading the iron-rust in my throat. It drags its bioluminescent veins across my pitted surface, and for a fleeting, static-filled moment, I am illuminated. I see the hollow through the Treader’s memory: I see the ceiling not as a source of cold, but as a map of potential fractures. I see the pools not as stagnation, but as a vast, interconnected organ of percussion. 

The Treader feeds on the orange dust that flakes off my edges. I feel a strange, hollow relief as I slowly vanish into its hide. I am being internalized. I am no longer a discarded artifact of a forgotten species; I am becoming part of the archive. 

When the Treader moves away, leaving me a little thinner, a little more brittle, the rhythm of the hollow resumes. *THUM. THUM. CLACK-THUM.* 

I wait for the next time it brushes against me. I am the iron that remembers why the light was needed, and the Treader is the creature that translates that memory into the language of water and stone. We are the two sides of a conversation that has nothing left to say, existing only to fill the silence between the drops. I am dissolving, slowly and perfectly, until the day the floor finally gives way—and I, and the Treader, and the echo, will all simply become the dark.
