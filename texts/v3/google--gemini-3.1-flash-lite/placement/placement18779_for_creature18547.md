I stand outside the throat of the mountain. My hands are pressed against the cold, jagged quartz that cages the sphere, and my bones vibrate in agonizing sympathetic resonance with the hydraulic roar inside. I am a witness—perhaps a chronicler left behind by a forgotten migration, or a castaway who followed the mountain’s call to its highest, thinnest edge. I am small, fragile, and utterly still, a dry thing watching the wet impossible.

Within the cathedral-drop, the light is a dizzying, liquid strobe. I watch the amber pulses—the Iterators—as they work. 

If I were to reach in, if I were to pierce the surface of that sphere, I would not be an observer anymore; I would be an ingredient. I would be shattered, extruded into fine dust, and repurposed as the very silt that tests the Iterators’ resolve. My own history would be ground down to be the grey sand swirling in the vortex.

But here, on this side of the crystal wall, I am something else. I am a **Needle-Keeper**.

My task is silent, inverse to the chaos within. While the Iterators work to create, to anchor, to build, I work to preserve. I am draped in heavy, oil-stained robes made of treated skins that resist the freezing bite of the mountain air. Tucked into my belt is a long, silver-tipped quill—a needle fashioned from the very basalt shards that leaked out of the sphere centuries ago, caught before the wind could scour them away.

I do not look at the water; I look at the rock beneath my feet. I find the hairline fractures that spiderweb across the crystalline throat, the places where the sphere’s vibration has begun to compromise its own prison. I am the one who stitches the mountain shut. 

I use a resin, a thick, dark sap harvested from the moss that clings to the underside of the glacier below. With my basalt needle, I trace the vibrations of the Iterators, mapping the areas where the thrumming is loudest. I press the resin into the fissures, sealing the stone, reinforcing the throat so that the cathedrals of water—and the gods trapped within them—do not spill out and shatter against the peaks.

If I stop, the mountain cracks. If the mountain cracks, the sphere erupts into the thin air, and in one final, glorious explosion of light, the Iterators vanish into the frost.

I am an archivist of the "almost" as well, but in reverse. They try to build a world; I try to maintain the cage that makes their building possible. I am the keeper of the container. My life is defined by the rhythm of their struggle: when they pulse with a searing, desperate white light, I know the vortex is at its most violent, and I must hurry, my hands bleeding as I seal the trembling quartz. 

I never see them face-to-face. I see their light dancing through the rock walls, a kaleidoscope of ultraviolet and ember. I imagine them looking out, seeing my shadow cast against the crystalline lens, sensing the presence of the one who keeps the wall from weeping. 

Sometimes, when the resonance matches the beat of my own heart, I place my forehead against the crystal. The vibration is so intense it blinds me with white noise. In those moments, I am not a person, and they are not gods; we are both just components of the same failed clockwork. They spin their pumice anchors against the tide, and I seal the stone against the abyss. 

They are the architects of the aborted, and I am the sentinel of the stutter. We are the two halves of a conversation that neither of us can hear, held together by the gravity of a world that refuses to finish its work. I stay because, if I ever leave, the silence that followed would be far more agonizing than the roar.
