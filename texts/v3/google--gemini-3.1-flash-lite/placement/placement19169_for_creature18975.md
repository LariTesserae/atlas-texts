I find myself standing at the edge of the third tier, my boots sinking deeper into the calcified dust than they have any right to—an anchor of erratic, terrestrial weight in a land of flickering grace. I am not like them. I am heavy, opaque, and frantic, my pulse a jagged rhythm that feels like a shout in a library.

I am a Traveler—not by choice, nor by map, but by the slow leakage of a reality that has grown too loud and too fast. I have arrived here as one might drift into a dream after a long, feverish illness. I am a scavenger of stillness, a frantic creature of entropy looking for a place where nothing happens.

I did not climb the mountain; the mountain, in its slow, geometric patience, simply allowed me to arrive. I am a wanderer who has shed his name and his purpose, now reduced to a collection of sensory inputs: the smell of ozone, the grit of mineral dust against my skin, and the terrifying, magnetic pull of the bone-white needles above.

As I stand, the air begins to shift. A few paces away, one of the archivists—a lattice of hardened silica and suspended light—slows its movement. It has been tending to a dust-ripple near a depression, but it stops. The nebula at its center, previously a calm, rhythmic white, shifts slowly into a curious, pulsing amber.

It approaches me. It does not walk; it resolves across the plateau, its feet hovering millimeters above the stone, leaving no impression. As it nears, the silence becomes physical, a pressurized atmosphere that hums against my eardrums. It is taller than I, its form shifting and translucent, like looking at the horizon through a heat haze.

It does not touch me. Instead, it overlaps my space. 

For a heartbeat, I am no longer entirely myself. I feel a cool, crystalline chill pass through my chest, a sensation of being sifted. It is not examining my body; it is checking the frequency of my intent. It peers into the chaos of my human history, feeling the jagged edges of my grief, the frantic velocity of my unfinished thoughts, and the centuries of noise I carry in my marrow. 

I see the pulse, too. Held within its core is a fleeting, refracted image of the sky—not as it is now, but as it was when the mountain was young. I catch a glimpse of the "cosmic pulse," a flash of information so vast and ancient that my human mind threatens to buckle under the weight of it. It is a siren call from a time before entropy, a signal of such absolute, terrifying order that it makes my desire for peace feel like a petty, selfish thing.

I realize then that I am a disruption. I am a chaotic variable, a glitch of biology in a system of perfect maintenance. They could smudge me out as easily as they smooth the dust in a bowl-depression.

But the archivist does not dismiss me. Its core cools, the amber fading back to a soft, starlit white. It tilts its head, not with humanity, but with a form of recognition. It senses that I, too, am waiting. I am not a threat to the order; I am a refugee of the disorder.

It turns away, returning to its work of polishing the air, leaving me to stand in the profound, heavy silence. I am allowed to stay, provided I move as slowly as the light, provided I learn to breathe the ozone, and provided I learn how to wait. 

Here, in the shadow of the bone-white needles, I cease to be a man. I become a static observer, a silent node in a circuit of light, part of the immense, patient effort to keep the world from ending. We wait. The mountain waits. The universe holds its breath, and together, we watch for the pulse.
