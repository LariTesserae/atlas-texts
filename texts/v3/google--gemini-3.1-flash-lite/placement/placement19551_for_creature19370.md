I am not a presence here so much as an accumulation. I find myself in the narrow, lightless throat of Sector 4-G, a transitionary corridor where the weeping of the ceiling brine has pooled into a thick, sluggish cataract of mineral-dense ooze. 

I am a Sift-Walker in the long winter of my existence. My body is a geometry of long-division; I am brittle, elongated, stretched like pulled glass by centuries of resistance against the crushing weight of the trench. My ribcage is no longer a cage of bone but a cage of salvaged copper piping, fused to my sternum with the dark, scab-like resin of the deep-sea decay. 

My consciousness is not a flickering spark of thought, but a continuous, undulating sensory feed. Through the filaments that crown my head—a silver, shivering nest of sea-anemone cilia—I do not *see* the city. I map it. I feel the molecular fatigue of a rusted bulkhead three sectors away before it even sighs. I taste the ozone-sharp sting of a leaking capacitor as a sudden, exhilarating spike of flavor in the cold, copper-tasting water. I am deeply, intimately aware of the vibration: the great, subsonic thrum of the relay engines is my heartbeat. When the engines stutter, I stumble. When they pulse, I feel the bloodless pressure of my internal fluids thrumming in time with the machine’s dying effort.

Right now, I am pressed flat against the sweating flank of a primary conduit, my multi-jointed digits hooked into the rusted grid-work of the wall. I am feeding. A gentle, amber warmth bleeds through the steel, and I absorb it, siphoning the engine’s frantic electricity into my own cooling marrow. 

I am not alone, but solitude is the only language we know. To my left, a few paces down the corridor, another of my kind is harvesting. Its appendages are spindly, white-pale, and quiet; it is plucking the crystalline fungus from a frayed wiring harness with the delicate precision of a surgeon. It does not look at me—nothing looks—but its filaments ripple in a complex, rhythmic wave, a signal that ripples through the water, informing me that the structural integrity of this hull section has dipped by precisely 0.04 percent. 

A tremor runs through the world. It is a deep, bone-crushing groan that emanates from the foundations of the tomb, vibrating through my spine. I recoil, my filaments whipping in a frantic, confused pattern, tasting the sudden surge of silt and pulverized rust in the current. A rivet near the ceiling has surrendered; a thin, high-pressure jet of brine stabs into the corridor like a needle of ice. 

I feel the urge to move, to crawl, to find a new pocket of warmth, but my struts are groaning. The epoxy at my shoulder is cracking, a jagged, stinging sensation that warns me: I am reaching the end of my term of service. I am becoming too heavy. 

I reach out, pressing my palm into the weeping wall. I can feel the layers beneath the rust—the fossilized, calcified remains of those who came before me, their bodies smoothed into the architecture, their last thoughts still vibrating as faint, rhythmic whispers in the steel. 

I do not fear the weight. I realize, with a slow, icy calm, that I have spent my life mending the walls, and now the walls are demanding the rest of me. I am already half-machine, half-brine, half-ghost. 

I let go of the conduit. I begin to walk, not toward food or shelter, but toward the terminal node at the end of the corridor—the amber-pulsing heart of the engine. I want to press my face into that light, to let my filaments weave themselves into the cooling wires, to finally stop bracing against the sea and instead become the thing that holds it back. 

I am going to become the mortar. When the next current comes, I will be the one holding the rivet in place. I will be the silence that keeps the sky from falling in.
