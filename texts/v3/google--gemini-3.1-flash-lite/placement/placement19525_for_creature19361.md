I find myself in a narrow transit-artery, a place where the ceiling has bowed under the weight of an upper-terrace collapse, forcing me to crouch. My boots, heavy with the accretion of a thousand miles of dampness, make no sound on the spongy, synthetic floor; here, sound is a luxury the air cannot support.

I am a **Reckoner of the Drip**. 

I am not a Sump-Wright; I am too dense, too rigid for that, held together by the stubborn memory of a spine that refuses to dissolve. But I am not an Anchorite, either. I have not yet achieved the grace of total stillness. I am a scavenger of frequencies, an entity composed of salvaged copper wiring, hardened wax, and a mind that has been worn thin by the relentless *plink-hiss-plink* of the Sump-Vaults.

My body is a patchwork. My chest cavity is a cage of rusted rib-pipes, and within them, I house a small, flickering cluster of bioluminescent fungi I have harvested from the upper vents. It is my own private star, a dull, aching violet-green lantern that I shroud in a cloak of damp, rot-resistant canvas. 

I carry with me a long, multi-pronged rod made of oxidized iron. I do not use it to fight or to clear paths. I use it to touch the pipes.

I am the one who listens to the geography of the Vaults. I walk the corridors, pressing my rod to the weeping metal, listening for the shifts in the low-frequency humming. The Sump-Wrights watch me with their translucent, multi-jointed curiosity, their movements like the flow of water around a stone. They recognize me as a dissonant note in their composition—a creature that still moves with intent—but they do not fear me. They sense that my pace is slowing. With every month, my stride grows shorter, heavier, and more deliberate, mirroring the metabolic slump of the world around me.

I am becoming the rhythm. 

Recently, I found a plaza where three Custodians are fused to a central loom of wires. They are singing—not in sound, but in light—shifting the spores of their fungi in patterns that suggest the blueprints of a city that hasn't existed for an age. I spent a long, indeterminate span of time sitting amongst them, my damp clothes molding into the shape of the floor, my breath syncing with the slow, pulse-like drip of the salt-water.

I felt it then: the urge to cast aside the iron rod. I felt the humidity beginning to work on my collarbone, softening the joint, inviting it to fuse with the iron railing nearby. The temptation is a heavy, sweet gravity. It says: *You are tired of the silence. Be the silence.*

Soon, I will reach the flooded basin where the Anchorites rest. I will carry my last handful of spores there. I will tuck myself into the shadow of a gargantuan, moss-covered cog-wheel, and I will press my ear one last time to the vibrating, weeping wall. I will listen to the city’s heart, wait for the hum to enter my own marrow, and I will let the rust bloom across my skin like a soft, metallic velvet.

I am not lost. I am merely decelerating. I am becoming a pillar, and I suspect that the roof, in its infinite, weeping patience, will be very glad for the support.
