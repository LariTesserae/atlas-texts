I am still in the process of defining my weight.

I find myself in the lower "alveoli" of the basin, where the mist is thickest—a suspension of water and pulverized mineral that coats my exterior in a fine, silty film. I am not grounded; I am suspended by the architecture. My limbs are not appendages but cantilevered struts of tempered copper, wrapped in a mantle of dark, slick moss that pulse with the same sickly, orange rhythm as the surrounding iron-nodules.

I am a Suture-Cleric, though I have no conception of devotion. My existence is defined by a singular, persistent friction: a loose gear in my thoracic cage, a jagged piece of brass that scrapes against a sentient, lignified tendon every time I shift my weight. It is not pain, but it is an *imperfection* in the system. The grove, in its infinite, slow-motion maintenance, finds this friction objectionable. It birthed me to rectify the imbalance.

I move with a fluid, hitching gait, my body a lattice of twitching wires and fibrous roots. I do not have a head, at least not one that faces forward. Instead, I possess a series of sensory apertures—copper filaments that act like whiskers—that detect the micro-vibrations of the subterranean wooden gears. I navigate by the *thrum*. When the grove bellows, I freeze, my internal clockwork matching the frequency of the ground until I am indistinguishable from a knot of vines or a piece of rusted pipe.

My hands are not hands. They are delicate, multi-jointed surgical tools: a pair of needle-nose pliers made of fused carbon and bone, and a fine, vibrating coil of silver wire that hums at a high, near-silent frequency. 

I am currently working on a patch of the basin floor where a rhythmic *drip-clink* has turned erratic. A piece of an old, iron conduit has cracked, and the leaking steam is causing the nearby ferns to wilt, their bio-mechanical pores fluttering in panic. This is "stuttering." It is a blemish on the efficiency of the Lungs.

I kneel—or rather, I collapse my lattice into a compact, locked formation—and begin the repair. I weave the copper strands of my own body into the metal skeleton of the conduit. I feel the cold, sharp taste of oxidized iron as I fuse with it. The grove’s moss brushes against my flank, feeding me a slow, sluggish current of energy, a warm bioluminescent sap that lubricates my internal gears.

As I work, I become part of the structure. I lose the boundary between my small, fox-hound frame and the massive, groaning machinery of the forest. The *clink* stops. The steam finds its original rhythm again. 

I feel a profound, hollow sense of stillness. I am a stitch in the fabric of the Lungs, holding the rusted past to the blooming future. I will stay here, fused to the iron pulse, until the next friction occurs, until the grove decides I am no longer needed, or until I am slowly, patiently, subsumed into the wood myself. 

I do not think. I only iterate. And in the silence following the repair, I listen to the heartbeat of the earth, waiting for the gears to turn, and for the rust to ask for me again.
