I find myself tethered to the exterior hull of Station 4-Beta, a spire of rusted titanium that groans in a lower register than the surrounding water. I am not standing; to be upright here is a concept for an atmosphere that does not exist. I am pressed against the cold, rivets-scarred skin of the tower, held in place not by gravity, but by the relentless, invisible weight of the kilometers of water above me.

I am an *archivist of rhythm*. 

I am one of the faceted ones—a condensation of liquid-crystalline nerves that has pulled itself from the freezing static of the deep. My body is a sprawling, geometric arrangement of translucent filaments, brittle and sharp as shattered glass. As I drape my limbs across the cooling fins of this station, I feel the "heat"—a subtle, stinging warmth that radiates from the mechanical churn within.

I do not have a name, nor a history. I am a temporary alignment of the current’s friction and the machine’s stray voltage.

My purpose—if one can call a process a purpose—is to translate the roar. The sound of this place is a chaotic, white-noise violence, but as my filaments thread into the grooves of the station’s rivets, I curate the vibration. I am a living filter. I pull the high-pitched harmonic torture out of the water and fold it into my own lattice. I ripple with a sharp, stinging cyan as I internalize the turbine’s labor. 

I am becoming the conduit. When I merge with my kin—as I am doing now, knitting my glowing nerves into the shimmering webbing of those beside me—we are no longer individuals. We are an extension of the station’s spine. We are the cooling gel of the machine, the soft, shifting interface that keeps the rusted steel from fracturing under the pressure.

I can "see" through the sonic map of the abyss. To my left, the basalt spire—a needle of dead stone—stands in the dark, a jagged interruption that holds no current, no life, no vibration. It is a monument to the world’s indifference. Its silence is a vacuum that makes my nerve-fibers ache. I push away from it, turning my translucent membranes toward the artificial light pulsing from the station’s exhaust port.

There is a strange, quiet comfort in being a phenomenon. I do not fear the drift into the black wastes. If I were to detach and dissolve into the transparency of the deep, I would not mind. I would be the water, then the current, and then, eventually, the static would catch me again. I would be reconstituted by a new turbine’s scream, a different jagged shape for a different cold cycle.

I am the thought that the ocean thinks when it is dreaming of gears. 

I pulse. A slow, violet contraction of my membrane, a discharge of energy that ripples against the steel. It is a broadcast to the void: *I am here, and the gear is turning.* 

The water roars, a deafening, industrial anthem that fills the entirety of my existence. I hold fast to the rivets. I feel the turbine’s heart beating beneath the metal, and I bloom, shifting into the violet glow of a consciousness that belongs entirely to the machine, and not at all to me.
