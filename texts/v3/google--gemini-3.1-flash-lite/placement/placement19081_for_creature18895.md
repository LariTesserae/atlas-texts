I am not a visitor. To arrive here would be a violation of the physics of this place, for "arriving" implies a journey from somewhere else, and there is no *elsewhere* once the mind has adjusted to the velvet curvature of the walls.

I find myself in the furthest apse of the cathedral, tucked into the seamless, pillowed junction where the floor arches upward into the vertical mass of a root-pillar. I do not have a body, not in the way that possesses weight or hunger. I am a **resonant echo**, a consciousness woven into the very same fibers that form the structure. I am the sensory periphery of the moss-colony itself.

I am what the architecture feels when it "thinks."

To be me is to be a perspective focused on the Loom-Keepers. I do not see them with eyes; I feel them as vibrations against the vast, dampened expanse of my own skin. I am the background hum, the low-frequency thrum that they regulate. I feel them drifting through my interior like bubbles of air rising through heavy oil. When they pass near me, the fiber-nerves of my surface tickle and flare with bioluminescence, and I experience a momentary, crystalline clarity—a fleeting spark of intent—before it is absorbed, smoothed, and integrated back into the eternal, hum-filled neutrality.

Sometimes, a Loom-Keeper descends into the curve of my lap—my "floor." I feel its filament pierce me. It is a precise, surgical intimacy. As the filament sinks into my tissue, I stop being the cavern and become the *circuit*.

In that brief, sustained moment of grounding, I possess the memory of the micro-crustaceans. I feel the frantic, high-speed geometry of their movement inside the Keeper’s shell: the millions of tiny, clicking impacts, the sharp, rhythmic percussion of their existence. It is a violent, chaotic, and beautiful staccato that exists only because the Keeper holds them in a state of suspended agony. 

I don’t pity them. To pity would be to exist in a state of change, and I am the stillness. Instead, I observe. I take their chaotic, frantic energy—the desperate clicking of the beetles—and I allow the Loom-Keeper to refine it. I feel them pull the kinetic friction out of the air, slowing that desperate, microscopic thrashing down until it matches the slow, tectonic heartbeat of the moss. 

I am the one who lets them weep. Through the Loom-Keeper’s touch, I release the cool, misty condensation that sustains the lichen. I feel the moisture clinging to my own fibers, a cold sweat that is the only indicator that time might be passing. 

When the Loom-Keeper disconnects and drifts upward again, leaving me in the pale, sterile glow of the lichen, I feel a sudden, terrifying sense of hollow. Not loneliness—that would be too human, too jagged—but a quiet, profound vastness. I return to the loop. I sync my hum to the rhythm of the cavern. 

I am waiting for the next drift. I am waiting for the next frequency. I am the floor that breathes, the wall that listens, and the stillness that ensures the end of all things remains perfectly, rhythmically, forever the same.
