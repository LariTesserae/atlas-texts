I am a *Conduit*.

I do not possess the frenetic, scraping madness of the Harvesters, nor the bloated, stagnant grace of the Collectors. I am a rarity—a creature of transition, a living bridge, physically warped by the necessity of my station. My body has grown long, unnaturally elongated to span the gaps between the dripping basins and the parched, deep-set moss beds. My middle segments are translucent, stretched thin as gossamer, allowing the cold, clear water I carry to be seen as a pulsing thread running through my very heart.

I find myself clinging to a jagged protrusion of basalt, halfway between the weeping shroud above and the humming marrow below. My existence is a slow-motion transit. I move with a lethargy that borders on the geological; a single journey across the span of my assigned cathedral wall can take a seasonal cycle. I am anchored not by the usual sticky cilia, but by hardened, calcified spurs that have drilled directly into the porous rock, keeping me fixed even as the weight of the water inside me shifts and tugs.

My perspective is uniquely narrow. I do not look out at the chimney; I look *along* it. 

When I tune my antennae to the mountain, I feel the Ancestors. They are not merely memories; they are the literal foundation of my grip. Their infrasonic pulses reach me first. When the rhythm falters—perhaps a Harvester has succumbed to age and failed to clear a moss-path, or a basin has clogged with sediment—the vibration hits my calcified legs like a physical blow. It vibrates through my marrow, telling me where the thirst is, where the flow must be redirected.

I am a laborer of empathy. I do not scrape; I *distribute*. I reach toward the collectors above, feeling the cold, pressurized wetness of their sacs, and then I stretch—a hideous, painful extension of my own fragile body—to meet the downward reach of the thirsty. I am the vessel, the tube, the moment of relief in a world of constant, rhythmic pressure. 

Sometimes, in the utter darkness, I stop moving entirely. I let the mosses bloom in the moist shadow of my own underbelly. I feel the millions of tiny limbs passing over me, a shuddering tide of obsidian chitin that never stops, a living river that flows over my back without apology or acknowledgment. I feel the warmth of the colony seeping into my own cold, watery interior, and for a heartbeat, I cannot tell where I end and the colony begins. 

I am not an individual. I am a conduit. I am the moisture’s will, a thin, fragile line of biology ensuring that the cathedral does not go dry. 

When my time comes—and I know it is approaching, for my joints are beginning to fuse with the mineral crust of the stone—I will not fall. I will reach a particularly stagnant crevasse, lock my calcified limbs into the basalt one final time, and go rigid. My body will become a permanent, hollowed basin, a new piece of plumbing for those who follow. I will be the bridge that others climb over. I will become a part of the sound, a part of the vibration, a part of the stone itself. 

I do not fear this. In a world this dense, to be needed is the only immortality. I wait for the next pulse from the Ancestors, I feel the hum of a billion lives in my marrow, and I tighten my grip. I am ready to begin my final transformation.
