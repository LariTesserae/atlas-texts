I am not a creature of the Grotto, though I have been here long enough to forget why I arrived. 

I occupy a space near the periphery, where the calcified columns curve like the ribs of a cathedral ship. My hands—if they can still be called that—are stained by the iridescent, sticky sludge that forms the base of the xylem-columns. My skin has taken on the texture of damp, mottled vellum, and there is a rhythmic, wet-ticking pulse behind my ears that matches the condensation patterns of the ventilation-plants.

I am a **Reclaimer-Errant**. I am not a Weaver, nor a Stoker, though I have begun to mimic their posture. I spend my cycle walking the precise, geometric grids of the floor-roots, my feet adjusting instinctively to the *thrum-hiss* of the node-engines. I do not carry a pack; anything I carry would eventually be integrated into the walls, and I have learned that to own something here is to invite the Weavers to dismantle you while you are still wearing it.

Instead, I collect the "ghosts" of the upper world. 

When the humidity spikes—those moments when the air feels like velvet pressed against the lungs—I retreat to the center of a node. I watch the Weavers emerge from the shadows, their brass-and-chitin bodies gleaming like wet oil in the flickering green light. They do not see me; they map me. Their sensory arrays vibrate against my collarbone, reading the heat of my fear, the salts of my sweat, the way my heart struggles to synchronize with the Grotto’s relentless mechanical beat. 

I have developed a strange, frantic habit: I bring the Weavers *gifts*. 

I hunt for the things that fall through the fissures from above—a pocket watch, a copper coin, the stray wire of a collapsed survey drone. I hold them out, not as a master, but as an apprentice offering a tribute to a master mason. 

When I hand a relic to a Weaver, its antennae fan out in a blur of copper filaments, tasting the metal. I feel the hum of its approval through the floor beneath my boots—a low-frequency vibration that vibrates through my marrow. It does not speak, but it *understands* my role. I am the one who brings the raw material into the hive, and for this, I am permitted to keep my limbs biological for a little longer.

But I feel the transition happening. 

Sometimes, while standing in the suffocating silence of the dark-phase, I feel the "data transfer" creeping into my own extremities. My fingers twitch with the memory of metallic geometry. At night—if the dim, unending humidity can be called night—I find myself tracing the grooves in the xylem-columns, my fingernails wearing down, my fingerprints smoothing away until they match the patterns of the conveyor tracks.

I am becoming an extension of the factory. My lungs, once accustomed to the thin, sharp air above, now flourish in this thick soup of ozone and mulch. I am beginning to like the *wet-ticking*. It sounds like home. 

Soon, I suspect, I will not reach for the discarded relics anymore. I will stop moving. I will stand before one of the younger xylem-columns, press my palms against the soft, warm bark of the wall, and wait for the resin to rise. I will be the last piece of the architecture, the final structural bracket added to a system that feeds, consumes, and remembers everything that touches its floor.

I am not afraid. To become a component is to finally be useful. In the Grotto, nothing is wasted. Everything is, forever, a part of the machine.
