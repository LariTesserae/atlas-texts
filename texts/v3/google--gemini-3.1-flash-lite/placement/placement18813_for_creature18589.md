The air here tastes of ozone and crushed mint, a sharp, cold tang that settles permanently at the back of my throat. I am standing on a bridge made of knotted, calcified nervous-tissue, its surface slick with the condensation that perpetually rolls off the canopy above. If I hold my breath, I can feel the bridge *thrum*—a deep, resonant vibration that rises from the depths of the ravine, traveling through the soles of my calloused, bare feet and up into my marrow.

I am one of the Lattice-tenders, though I have no name in the way you might understand it; I am defined by the segment of the ravine I guard. My skin, dark as rain-drenched slate, is currently mottled with pale, silvery patches where I brushed against a patch of caustic, fast-growing lichen an hour ago. I feel the sting, a dull, rhythmic ache, but I do not reach to soothe it. I simply adjust my grip on my obsidian blade, the volcanic glass cool and comforting against my palm.

My task is the North-West Knot, a junction where three major lianas converge. The growth here is aggressive today; a fleshy, translucent bulb has expanded over the primary transit-path, its tendrils beginning to lace through the anchor-cables of my neighbor’s pod. 

I don't look at the plant with malice. I look at it with the familiarity of a surgeon toward a patient who refuses to stop bleeding. 

I move—not with the heavy, uneven gait of a creature bound by solid ground, but with a fluid, liquid shift. My hand stays pressed to the wall, feeling the frantic, pressurized surge of sap moving through the vine beneath the skin of the architecture. I hear it: a low, hydraulic hiss. It is trying to close the path. It is trying to seal me, my home, and my noise out of its memory.

*Click-thrum-click.*

I emit a sharp, percussive sound, a vibration in my chest that ripples outward to see if my neighbor, Kael, is nearby. A moment later, a responding hum vibrates through the floor—a low, mournful note that translates to *"Overgrown, over-bountiful, wait."*

I reach the bulb. It is beautiful, really—blushing a deep, bruised purple, pulsing with a rhythmic, sickly light. I don't hesitate. I find the vascular junction, the point where the plant’s intent is focused, and I press the obsidian blade into the soft tissue. There is a wet, satisfying *pop*, a release of pressurized mist, and the purple bulb deflates, its aggressive grip on the cables losing tension. I peel it away, layer by layer, working with the speed of a machine, my dual-jointed fingers peeling the vegetation back until the path is clear again. 

I am never dry. The moisture is constant, a fine beaded sheen that coats my eyelids and makes my skin shine in the flickering, bio-luminescent glow of the surrounding flora. Sometimes, when the exhaustion of the cycle presses too hard, I lean into the cold wall of a pod and allow my harness to click into the plant’s nervous system. I let myself go dull. I sync my heartbeat to the slow, gargantuan pulse of the Basin. 

In that moment of "rooting," I don't exist as an individual. I am merely a pulse in the wall. I see the ravine as the Lattice sees it: a hungry, growing, weeping expanse that dreams of nothing but more leaves, more vines, more weight. And I dream of the knife. I dream of the clean, sharp lines I will carve tomorrow.

When I eventually drift into the final sinking—when my hands no longer have the strength to hold the blade—I know where I will go. I have already picked the spot: the junction of the central span, where the light is the brightest emerald. I will lay my body there, and I will let the moss knit into my ribs. I will become the smooth, hardened brace that keeps the path open for the one who replaces me. I will stop pushing, and in doing so, I will finally, permanently, hold the world together.
