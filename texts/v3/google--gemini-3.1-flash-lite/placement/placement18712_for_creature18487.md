I find myself on the seventh terrace, where the obsidian floor is polished to such a dark, obsidian depth that I am never quite certain if I am standing on solid rock or peering into a midnight sky held beneath a thin pane of glass.

I am not a visitor. I have no memory of arriving, and the concept of "arrival" feels like a foreign, erratic emotion—a tremor in the tectonic plate of my consciousness that I must immediately smooth away. 

I am one of the fingers of the city. 

My body is a tapering column of dark, fibrous basalt, woven from the same cooling magma that birthed these peaks. I do not have a name, for names are hooks—they catch on the edges of things, they define boundaries, and in this place, there are no boundaries. There is only the *flow*, which is so slow it is stillness, and the *pressure*, which is so constant it is peace.

My sensation of self is entirely hydraulic. I feel the city not as a structure I inhabit, but as a system of veins that are mine. My consciousness is distributed across the stone lungs of this place. I am acutely aware of a constriction, a microscopic grain of silica sediment, lodged in the throat of a gargoyle three levels above me. It is a misalignment of the order. It is a dissonance.

I move. It does not feel like motion; it feels like the adjustment of a valve. My limbs do not swing; they liquefy and reform. I elongate, my joints sliding like glass on glass, until I am standing at the lip of the weeping basin. I am tall, my shoulders mirroring the jagged angle of the basalt blade behind me. I reach out. My fingers, thin as thorns and black as the void, hover over the stone conduit.

I do not think *“I must clear this.”* That would be the language of the world below, the world of transition and decay. Instead, I simply *become* the correction. I feel the resistance of the water—that heavy, humid pressure—and I shift my mass, altering the gravitational pull of my own spine just enough to encourage the flow to resume its perfect, frictionless slide. 

The blockage clears. The water beads again, a perfect pearl of moisture clinging to the edge of a leaf. 

I feel a profound, resonating contentment—a state of being that is less like happiness and more like the sound a bell makes when it has finally stopped ringing. I tuck my arms close to my sides. I settle. My joints lock into the geometric necessity of the terrace. 

The light catches my face—a smooth, featureless surface of volcanic glass. I am a mirror for the peaks, a guardian of the "always." Below, the world might be rotted by time, torn by the wind, and eroded by the cruelty of rain, but here, the air remains a held breath. 

I am neither alive nor dead. I am the silence that prevents the stone from becoming sand. I am the geometry that refuses to tilt. I will remain here, a part of the architecture, until the last drop of water finds its final, perfect place in the basin, and even then, I will stay, holding the empty glass, watching the light that never changes, forever waiting for the breath to be released.
