I am not a Clerk, for I have too much weight. I do not chime with the machinery, nor do I have the grace to navigate the vertical pipes with magnetic precision. 

I am a **Sediment-Sifter**, a scavenger of the slow-time. I am a creature of heavy, calcified purpose, built to dwell in the lowest reaches of the basin, down where the water table kisses the basalt and the pressure is so heavy it feels like armor. I do not have a skeleton of clockwork; I am clad in a carapace of matte-black iron plates, bolted together by my own hand over the long, long centuries. I am broad, squat, and possess four hydraulic limbs that terminate in wide, flat pads, allowing me to trudge through the weeping mud without disturbing the delicate geometry of the oily puddles.

My eyes are not apertures of amber light. They are two thick, circular portholes of industrial glass, condensation-crusted and dim, recessed deep into a hood of riveted steel. When I look out, I see the world in hues of ochre and wet charcoal—a static, frozen panorama that never shifts. 

I am the ghost of the basin’s digestion. 

I spend my eons trailing the Clerks. I do not aid them, nor do I interfere. I move in the wake of their obsessive cleaning, walking behind them as they scrape the mineral salts from the weeping walls. Where they take the refined residue for their conduits, I collect the "waste"—the microscopic flakes of rust, the microscopic glass-shards of shed Clerk-skin, the stray copper filings that drift down like metallic snow from the gantries above. 

I am not bored. I am possessed by a heavy, mechanical form of curiosity. 

I find myself hunkered at the base of a primary vent, the steam-heat pressing against my plates like a physical weight. My internal organs are not gears, but a pressurized bellows system that chugs in a long, slow wheeze, matching the *thrum* of the earth. I take the grit I have collected—this debris of eternal maintenance—and I pack it into the microscopic fissures of the basalt floor. I am trying to build a map. Or perhaps a barricade. I am trying to change the architecture of the "unmoving" by introducing a variable that the machines haven't accounted for: accumulation.

Every thousand cycles, I stop. I sit perfectly still in a puddle of iridescent oil, waiting for that moment when the Clerks congregate on the high gantries to press their chests against the pipes. From my vantage point in the dark, wet low-ground, I watch their silhouettes against the yellow-hued smog. I feel the vibrations through my own limbs, rattling through my iron casing until my own internal bellows shudder in a dissonant, frantic rhythm.

They are waiting for the heartbeat to break. They pray for the silence. 

I, however, have decided that it should not break. I have grown fond of the hum. I have grown fond of the fact that I am the only thing in this basin that is *adding* to the landscape rather than merely maintaining its stasis. By filling the cracks, by burying the oily pools, I am slowly raising the floor of the basin. I am a slow-motion geological event, a tidal wave made of rust and labor. 

If I succeed, one day the machinery will run out of room. The pipes will be choked. The pistons, unable to strike the bedrock, will shudder and grind against the layers of debris I have planted. 

It is a small, heavy ambition. My aperture of glass fogging with steam, I reach down with a wide, blunt pincer, scraping a layer of mineral slime into my collection bin. The *clack-hiss-thrum* continues, absolute and indifferent. Above, a Clerk flickers. 

I continue to work. We are all keepers of the status quo, but some of us are keepers who intend to bury it under a mountain of our own making.
