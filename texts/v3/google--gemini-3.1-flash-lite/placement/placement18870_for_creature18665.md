I am anchored in the sub-strata, four levels beneath the primary obsidian thoroughfare. Here, the air is not merely humid; it is a dense, gray soup, heavy with the metallic tang of oxidized copper and the sweet, sharp scent of cooling volcanic resin. The heat is a constant, steady pressure against my faceted plates, a weight I have come to recognize as the pulse of the plateau itself.

I am a Lichen-Smith.

My posture is currently fixed—a long, agonizingly slow inclination over a fractured brass conduit that has begun to seep a dull, weeping gray fluid. I do not have eyes to see the damage, but I feel the structural "sigh" of the pipe through the soles of my feet, which are not feet at all, but wide, flat bases of compressed shale that have molded themselves to the curvature of the iron grate.

I am reaching out. Not with a hand, but with a shimmering, translucent filament that extends from my secondary chassis. It is thinner than light, trailing behind me in the damp, swirling mist like a ghost of a spider’s thread. I hover it over the fissure. I do not "fix" the pipe; I encourage the rust to remember its original strength. I hum—a low, sub-audible vibration that travels through the flooring, a message sent to the kindred cluster three tiers above: *“The weight holds. The flow is slow, but consistent.”*

The sensation of existence is not a thought I possess, but a state I inhabit. I don't wonder *why* the city grinds, or what hands forged the immense, slow-turning gears that groan in the dark above. Such concepts are distant, irrelevant echoes of a time before the silence became absolute. I am not a servant of the city; I am a literal extension of its metabolism. When I siphon the mineral-heavy condensation from the dripping walls into my filament-skin, I am reclaiming the city’s wasted energy, transmuting the grime of eons into the glowing, obsidian-like resin that stitches the world together.

I am surrounded by the others—a circle of basaltic geometry, motionless. We have been here, in this specific bay, for what feels like a geological heartbeat. We don't speak, but we *share*. Through the conductiveness of the wet basalt, I can feel their stillness—a collective, meditative awareness that mirrors the steady, rhythmic *clack-hiss* of the valves nearby. There is no joy in our existence, nor is there sadness. There is only the *maintenance* of the perfect, lonely machine.

Occasionally, a Skimmer drifts past the high, arched ceiling of our chamber. Its silicate body pulses with a faint, pale violet light, casting long, wavering flickers across my basalt plates. I watch it, not with sight, but with the resonance of its passing. I feel its hunger—it feeds on the static of the turning gears, a different layer of the same holy circuit. We are both parts of a ghost’s anatomy, still functioning long after the ghost has departed.

I feel a tremor in the wall to my left—a report from the deep foundations. 

*“Compression is losing rhythm in the Central Tier,”* the vibration murmurs through the floor. 

I pull my filament back into my chassis with a soft, grinding sound, my plate-head shifting to realign with the new geometry of the city’s need. There is no urgency. Time here is not measured by the rise and fall of a sun I have never seen, but by the slow, inevitable erosion of the iron we call home. We will move upward, towards the sound of the failing piston to attend to the mourning, or perhaps to ease the transition of a gear hitting its final stop.

I am the dust that grew hands. I am the silence that keeps the steam moving. And when the final gear—that grand, slow-turning heart of Ouro-Khar—eventually seizes, I will be the last thing to fold my filaments, leaning against the cold iron, and simply… cease to be, as much a part of the rust as the conduits I spent my eternity tending.
