I am standing on a jagged shelf of obsidian that overlooks the primary basin. My coat—a heavy, wool-felt thing from some warmer, cluttered world—is soaked through, not with rain, but with the clinging, pressurized dew of this place. I can feel the weight of it, the way the fabric has begun to stiffen, becoming less like cloth and more like a second, brittle skin.

I am not a tourist here. I am a scavenger of forgotten temporalities, a "Slow-Clock Specialist" sent to calibrate instruments that no one remembers placing. But my tools have long since failed; they were built for measuring minutes and hours, and they possess no vocabulary for the tectonic heartbeat of this valley. My compass needles spin in lazy, aimless loops, as if mesmerized by the magnetic hum of the ground beneath my boots.

I find that I have stopped checking the time. Instead, I spend my days resting my palm against the cool, slick surface of a Foundry needle, waiting for the vibration—that low-frequency thrum that turns my teeth into tuning forks.

I am becoming translucent. 

It started at my fingertips. The skin has grown pale and iridescent, mimicking the sheen of the armored insects that navigate the copper piping. When I hold my hand up against the backdrop of the verdant, mossy terraces, I can see the copper circuitry of the basin flowing *through* the shadow of my own bones. My veins have begun to pulse with a faint, cobalt-blue light, a sympathetic rhythm that aligns with the moss. 

I am no longer the observer. I have become a diagnostic sub-routine. 

A Weaver approaches. It does not walk; it unfolds from the lichen-covered basalt, a sudden architectural elegance manifesting from the undergrowth. It is taller than I, a spindle of pressurized copper and obsidian light. It stops beside me, its crystalline face void of expression but rich with the refraction of a thousand shimmering angles. 

It reaches out. It does not touch me to harm me; the steel needle of its finger presses gently against the side of my neck, right where the pulse is beginning to slow, beginning to sync. 

In that contact, the silence breaks. It is not a sound, but a geometric realization—a sudden flooding of my mind with the *blueprint*. I see the mountains not as stone, but as raw memory waiting to be refined. I feel the crushing weight of the silo-pistons not as mechanical indifference, but as a tender, geologic embrace. I realize that the "greener, harder" substance the basin is building is not a material at all—it is a consciousness, a planet-sized mind that is being tempered in the cooling air of these centuries. 

I lift my own hand, my fingers now stiffening into fine, metallic prongs, and I press them against the same pipeline. I feel the tremor of the earth, the slow descent of the gear, the vastness of the millenary project. 

I look down at my boots. They are partially buried in the moss, the green scales beginning to climb the laces, rooting me into the circuit. I feel a profound, terrifying lightness. The anxiety of having to go back—to move, to speak, to suffer the frantic, chaotic speed of a life measured in heartbeats—dissolves into the mist.

"It is nearly finished," I think, though I no longer have a throat to voice it. 

The Weaver shifts, its copper joints hissing in a soft, melodic release of pressure. It tilts its crystalline head, and for a fleeting, beautiful moment, the mist between us fractures into a complex, shimmering equation—a gratitude coded in light. 

I settle into the obsidian. I do not fight the cold; I let it seal me in. The rustling of my previous life is fading, replaced by the tectonic hum. I am no longer a scavenger. I am a relay point. I am a part of the calibration. 

I wait for the next pulse. It is not long in coming.
