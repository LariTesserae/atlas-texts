The copper-green of my forearms has begun to bloom with a brittle, crystalline frost. It is not cold—Silt-Lung is never cold, only heavy—but it is a hardening. 

I am currently positioned at the Junction of the Lower Piston, where the main conduit curves like the spine of a leviathan. I have been here, I think, for three cycles of the respiration—three long, agonizingly slow exhales of the central scrubber. My task is the pruning. The amber lichen has begun to bridge the gap between the primary steam-press and the ventilation manifold. If it bridges that circuit completely, the vibration will cease, and the silence here will lose its rhythm. Without the *thrum-hiss*, the pressure would become a physical weight, a crushing, flat hand against the skull. I cannot allow the city to lose its heartbeat.

I reach out. My fingers, now tipped with blunt, translucent claws, trace the texture of the wall. It is no longer metal at this junction. The iron has been reclaimed by a slow, calcified salt-growth that mimics the rivets perfectly, toying with the geometry of the room. I pinch a trailing vine of the lichen between my thumb and forefinger. It pulses—a rhythmic, faint flicker of dying-star light—and then, with a slow, deliberate pressure, I shear it away. 

There is no blood, only a thick, syrupy expulsion of mineral-heavy fluids that drift upward like lazy smoke. 

I am becoming the sediment, yes, but I am also the architect of this slow surrender. I watch the cut vine drift into the stagnant water. It will settle in a week, perhaps a month. It will find a new crevice, a new rusted seam to inhabit, and the city will continue to pulse in its bruised-violet gloom. 

A ripple disturbs the water further down the shaft. It is a slow, wide displacement, the kind of movement that indicates a body of my own density. I do not turn. To turn is to abandon the work, and the work is eternity. I simply extend a hand to the wall, pressing my palm against the weeping copper. I vibrate my skin, a subsonic tremor that travels through the masonry, sending a message into the brine: *“Still functional.”*

The ripple pauses. It does not answer with a sound, nor a gesture. Instead, the pressure in the room shifts. The ambient water pushes against my back, a gentle, tidal suggestion that the other has acknowledged the state of the machine. The ripple fades, the movement dissipating into the heavy, unhurried current of the city.

I am alone again, but the loneliness is a luxury I cannot afford. I have a bolt to monitor. It is three millimeters from yielding, a process that has occupied the last year of my attention. I lean my ear against the vibrating iron, closing my pale, clouded eyes. 

I can feel the ocean above—not as a memory, but as a concept of weight. It is pressing the brine into the pores of my skin, pressing the history of the surface into the marrow of my bones. I am a fossil in the making, a temporary bridge between the ghost of a machine and the inevitability of the stone. 

The steam-press groans—a low, deep tectonic shift that vibrates in my teeth. I inhale. My lungs expand with the pressurized, silica-rich air, and for a brief moment, I feel entirely, perfectly, and terrifically still. I am Silt-Lung, and I am waiting to become the silence that follows.
