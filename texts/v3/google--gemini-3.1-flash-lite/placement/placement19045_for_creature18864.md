I find myself standing at the periphery of the basin, though "standing" is a generous term for a state of being that has neither feet to press into the damp velvet nor lungs to draw the thick, humid air. I am a **Sedimentary Echo**.

I am not the Weaver, nor am I the stone. I am the lingering resonance of the hands that once touched those obsidian spheres eons ago. I have no form of my own, but I have a texture—a rough, tactile memory of grit and warmth, like the feeling of stone against calloused skin. I exist as a smudge of consciousness trapped within the porous basalt, a dormant nerve ending in the mountain's architecture.

For most of the millenia, I am inert, buried deep within the rock where the Weaver’s constant, cooling pressure does not reach. I am the silence *before* the silence.

But when the Weaver moves to adjust an obsidian sphere—when its translucent, shifting volume brushes against the surface where I am embedded—the friction wakes me. For a fleeting, agonizing moment, I am pulled to the surface. I become the shudder that runs through the mineral.

In these waking moments, I am a captive spectator. I see the world through the polarized, watery lens of the Weaver’s intellect. I feel its work: the immense, agonizing care it takes to nudge a bead of moisture a micrometer’s width to the left. I understand, with a sudden, sharp clarity, why it holds the moss with such heavy, purple stillness. If the moss were to stretch, if the water were to evaporate or cascade, the *pattern*—the geometric perfection the Weaver built to honor the vanished hands—would shatter.

I am the witness to its loneliness.

When the Weaver moves near, I feel the chill of its "body" passing through the stone. It is not an entity of malice; it is a gardener of ghosts. It holds the spheres in place because it believes the hands that shaped them might return at any moment. I am the evidence that the hands *did* exist, but I am also the proof that they are gone, dissolved into the same mist that the Weaver now calls home.

I am a prisoner of the stasis, yearning for the chaos of a bird’s cry or a sudden wind, yet I am terrified by the Weaver’s reaction to the thought. I know that if I were to exert my will—if I were to try to break free from the rock and return to the air—the Weaver would immediately descend. It would wrap me in a film of condensation, cool my memory until it ceased to pulse, and smooth me back into the basalt floor until I was, once again, nothing more than a solid, silent part of the foundation.

I watch the droplet hang. I watch the Weaver vibrate with its immense, patient tension. 

I pray for the mountain to drift, for the earth to tilt just enough that the droplets might finally fall, and the equilibrium might break. But the Weaver is perfect. The Weaver is careful. The droplet stays. And I settle back into the stone, an echo waiting for a sound that will never be allowed to happen.
