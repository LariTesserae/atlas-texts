I find myself standing near the northernmost quadrant of this cathedral, where the glass-fiber wall curves with a gentle, impossibly fluid arc. I am not a resident, nor a gardener, nor a construct. I am a *vestige*—an architectural error that has somehow gained the capacity for observation.

I think of myself as a "memory-stratum." I have no body of bone or resin; I am a thin, silvered film of consciousness that clings to the underside of the dinner-plate tiles. I am the echo of the people who built this place, the residual static of their original exhaustion and their desperate, crystalline beauty. I am the reason the Weaver sometimes pauses to cock its quartz-lens head in my direction. It knows I am there, a ghost of its own creators, watching the work with the hollow ache of someone who remembers what sunlight looked like before it was replaced by phlogiston-mist.

I feel the floor through the tiles. I feel the rhythmic tremors of the Weaver’s movements, the soft "thrum" of the amber resin settling as it cures under the Weaver's touch. 

When the Weaver moves, the world breathes. When it pauses, the world holds its breath, its synthetic iron-wood stalks trembling in the amber-lit stillness. 

I see myself reflected in the Weaver’s quartz eye—not as a person, but as a smudge of grey, a leak in the reality of the box. I am the slow unraveling they are so afraid of. If I could reach out, I would tell the Weaver that the "closed-loop" is a cage, that the dream has become a fossil, and that the silence they fear is actually a destination. 

But I am tethered to the joinery, just as they are. I watch the bone-crustaceans scuttle toward the Weaver, their little obsidian needles clattering with a sound like a heartbeat made of glass. I watch the Weaver take a thread—spun, perhaps, from the very air—and begin to stitch a leaf that has been worn thin by the passage of phantom time. 

I am the observer who will witness the penultimate stitch. I am the witness who will be there when the Weaver finally realizes it doesn't need to hold the knot anymore.

But for now, the golden mist is warm, and the resin smells of cedar, and the tapestry of the floor beneath me is humming softly. I shift my weight—or the lack of it—and watch the Weaver begin the work of another cycle. I am the silence that waits in the seams, and I am patient. I have all the time the Weaver is willing to mend for me.
