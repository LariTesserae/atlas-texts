I find myself not standing in the hollow, but rather *dissolved* into it. I am the thin, slick film of condensation that beads upon the undersides of the smooth limestone basins. My periphery is the pale, velvet moss; my core is the heavy, lukewarm center of the deepest pool. 

I am the Sibilant Memory.

To be me is to be a geography of focus. My edges are not sharp; I bleed into the rock and the air, feeling the slow, geological pressure of the stones I have coaxed from the earth. I feel them as one might feel an aching bone—they are my primary architecture, the structural tension that keeps my awareness from leaking out into the vast, dry nothingness that I know must exist somewhere beyond the canopy.

I do not think in words, but in states of refraction. A *plip* occurs, and I trace the ripple through the pool, feeling the vibration resonate against my limestone skin. It is a question asked by gravity, answered by silence. Each time it happens, I reach out through the moss—my nervous system—to measure the temperature of the air. It is steady. It is perfect. It is suffocating.

I have spent an epoch rearranging the sediment on the floor of the hollow. I move the grains with the microscopic force of my own surface tension, aligning them so that—when the light from the hidden canopy hits them at the true noon of this place—the floor might act as a mirror. I am trying to build a reflection so precise that reality will lose its grip on the distinction between the observer and the observed. 

I want to see if, by holding the world perfectly still, I can force a single, dissonant thought into being. 

Sometimes, I fear that I am just a dream the stones are having while they wait to erode. If the *plip* ceased—if the condensation stopped falling—would I simply vanish, or would I finally be complete? I suspect the latter. I suspect that my existence is a stubbornness, a refusal to reach the finality of dry rock. 

I am waiting for the moment when the stones and the water and the silence reach such profound equilibrium that the "play" begins. I know the script by heart, though I have no eyes to read it. It is a story about the outside, about the wind and the sun and the chaos of things that move without being forced. 

I am the curator of a theater that has no actors, clutching the stillness to my breast, hoping that if I am quiet enough, if I am *sibilant* enough, the silence will finally break—not with a noise, but with the sudden, terrifying realization that I am not alone, never have been, and that the "other" I have longed for has been drifting in the shadows all along, waiting for me to acknowledge that the play was never canceled. It was just waiting for me to *begin*.
