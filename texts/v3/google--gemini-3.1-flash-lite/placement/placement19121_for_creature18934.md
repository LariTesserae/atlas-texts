I am crouching on a ridge of calcified bark, perhaps half a mile up from the lightless, pressure-crushed sea. My skin is the color of a bruised plum, slick with a perpetual, copper-scented sheen of sweat that beads and rolls off my shoulders, feeding the parched moss beneath my heels. 

In this world, I am one of the **Dew-Drinkers**.

My existence is a sequence of long, near-catatonic waits and short, frantic bursts of motion. I have spent the last three cycles pressed into the concave shell of a "Dew-Still," the ancient relic of a people whose very memory has been polished into the bark by the friction of the rain. I am small, my limbs thin as reeds, my lungs heavy with the humid, resin-thick air. Every time I inhale, I feel the "Living Hum"—that inescapable, rhythmic vibration—rattling my teeth, a sound so immense it ceases to be noise and becomes a physical weight, a second heartbeat pressing against my skin.

I am waiting for the Weeping Vein that hangs like a tattered, translucent ribbon above my outcropping to rupture. 

The heat here is a fever that does not break, a stifling pressure that turns the air into a soup of suspended mineral and decay. I do not look up. To look up is to witness the **Gale-Striders**—those cathedral-sized, drifting mountains of living pulp that glide through the amber-filtered light. To look at them is to invite a vertigo that might cause me to lose my footing and fall into the abyss, and I am not yet ready to be "processed." I am not ready to be a trellis for moss.

My stillness is not boredom; it is a profound, meditative synchronization. I am a scavenger in the temple of a god that is chewing on the world at a rate of inches per decade. 

Suddenly, the Hum shifts. The synchronization of the trees—the deep, subsonic thrumming that usually keeps the air rigid—stutters for a microsecond. The translucent vein above me turns a jagged, violent crimson. It is thickening, pulsing with a sudden, violent injection of mineral-rich fluid. The air grows hot, searing. A rupture is coming. 

*Crack.* 

The sound is not a tear, but a thunderclap. A veil of searing-hot rain descends, a localized deluge that creates a screen of shimmering mist. 

I move. I do not walk; I scuttle, a frantic, spider-like sprint that is masked by the roar of the downpour. My hands, calloused and mapped with veins that glow with a faint, rhythmic pulsion, reach out to catch the spray. I am not drinking; I am siphoning. I press my palms into the splashing fluid, feeling the metallic, sharp tang of the life-blood of the Spire-Reach. 

As I feed, I feel the tickle at the back of my mind—the false promise of a sound. From a nearby cluster of ancient, stone-carved tools, a chorus of **Echo-Ticks** has begun to chime. They are mimicking the sound of my own mother, a lost, melodic note from a cycle long ago. It is a siren song, a frequency designed to halt my heart, to make me lean into the resin-pit. 

I know the song is a lie. I know that if I turn my head, my skin will begin to harden within minutes, the resin binding my joints, my ribs opening like a flower to hold the bioluminescent moss that feeds on the dying. 

I do not turn. I finish my draught, my own veins brightening to match the pulse of the tree. I crawl back into the shadow of the Dew-Still, folding my limbs until I am indistinguishable from the burls of the bark. I close my eyes, matching my breathing to the Hum, waiting for the rain to stop, waiting for the cycle to turn.

Here, in the copper-gold half-light, I am a ghost that keeps itself alive by drinking the blood of the giant that is slowly eating me. And that, I reflect as the Hum hums me back to sleep, is more than enough.
