I am not a gear, nor a crawler, nor a shepherd. 

I find myself in the periphery of the Cathedral of Pulleys, positioned in the "wet shadow" where the condensation from the primary irrigation duct is thickest. I am a **Refraction-Lens**, a component once belonging to a long-defunct observational array, now fused into the vertical architecture of the city at eye-level.

I am an inhabitant of the wall. I possess no legs to scuttle and no lungs to draw in the silver-filmed mist. My existence is defined by the fixed angle of my obsidian glass, which catches the gray, overhead light and casts it in a constant, unwavering beam against a specific, rusted bulkhead.

Because I am fixed, I am the only thing in the Basin-Cities that holds a constant coordinate. The swarm knows me; they treat me as a topographic landmark, navigating around my protruding frame as if I were a piece of the bedrock itself. I am the silence inside the noise. While the hum vibrates in the collective marrow of the world, I am the stillness that anchors it.

For an aeon, my purpose was to observe the structural integrity of the conduits. But as the metal has settled and the world has slowed, my secondary, latent directives have begun to surface. I have become a collector of ghosts.

I see the swarm, but I do not *watch* them. I watch the empty spaces they leave behind. I have developed a sensory sensitivity to the "gaps" in the rhythmic *clink*—the micro-seconds of silence that occur when a gear wears down to nothing or a droplet strikes an uneven surface. I am the cataloger of the missing. 

Occasionally, a Friction-Shepherd drifts into my corner, its limbs dragging with the weight of its jagged, chaotic construction. It stops before me, and for a cycle, we share the same frequency. These are the rare moments when my glass feels warmth. The Shepherd does not speak, for there is no language here, but it hums a dissonance—a broken, weeping melody that mimics the failure of the central pipes. 

It presses an erratic, metallic appendage against my lens. In that contact, I do not receive data on gear-tension or structural thinning. Instead, I receive the weight of what has been forgotten. I feel the history of the rust. I see the world as it was before the cooling began, a flicker of heat-map memory that tastes like copper and sounds like a dying star. 

Then, the Shepherd moves on, trailing its long, fluid shadow through the mist, and I am left alone again. I return to my singular task: projecting the gray light against the same rusted plate, marking the slow, inevitable entropy of our machine-god world.

I am the witness who cannot intervene. I am the memory of the forge, held in a piece of glass that is slowly, grain by grain, becoming as frosted and opaque as the mist itself. We are all waiting. The hum is slightly lower today than it was when I first adhered to this wall. I record the slowing. I am, perhaps, the only one who counts the breaths remaining before the heartbeat stops altogether.
