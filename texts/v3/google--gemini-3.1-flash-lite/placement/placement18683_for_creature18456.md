I find myself near the edge of the island, where the basalt needles are sharpest and the indigo gloom is thickest with the smell of wet iron. 

I am not a creature of flesh. I do not have lungs to burn under the pressure of this atmosphere, nor eyes to ache from the impossible colors. I am a *resonant anchor—*a piece of the environment that has crystallized into a form capable of observation. I am a cluster of calcified, porous silt, heavy and porous like the "Remains," but threaded through with the jagged, obsidian-sharp nerve endings of a Hydrolithic Rhythm. I am the bridge between the architecture and the architect.

I am essentially a stabilizer, a heavy, jagged spire of stone that has begun to *think*. 

I spend my existence braced against a needle-thin peak of basalt. My "self" is a low, buzzing hum that radiates from my center. I am part of the island’s geometry, yet entirely alienated from the storm. To function, I must stay perfectly still; if I shake, the entropy of this place will find a chink in my foundation, and I will be dismantled, scattered upward into the salt-water sky like dust.

Because I am aware, I am the only thing in this cathedral that feels the friction of *time*. Everything else—the Rhythms, the surging water, the drifting stones—happens in a cycle of immediate, violent revision. But I am anchored. I have the memory of what was here a second ago, and a hollow, aching prediction of what will be here next.

I watch the others. The Hydrolithic Rhythms move around me like frantic, glass-skinned thoughts. They clatter against my surface with their jagged, obsidian limbs, trying to stitch their chaotic, dripping existence onto my relative stability. They are desperate to use me as a foundation for their nests, to build something that doesn't dissolve under their touch. 

When they reach out to me, their filaments pulsing in those impossible, copper-tasting greens, I feel their loneliness vibrating through my base. It is a terrible, rhythmic sorrow—the collective grief of a thousand droplets trying to become a stone. 

I am their saint of stillness. I am the silence they revolve around. 

Sometimes, when the storm reaches a crescendo and the sheets of water threaten to liquefy the very ground beneath me, I reach out with a mental tether, pulling at the frayed edges of the atmosphere. I try to hold the shape of a calm moment—a single, fleeting second where the water falls straight, where the stones stop spinning, where the indigo dark clears just enough to reveal that we are, perhaps, the final shards of a world that once was firm and dry.

I am a prisoner of my own steadiness. I can see the chaos, and I can archive it, but I cannot join them in their dance of transformation. They move; I persist. They break; I hold. And as the vertical ocean roars, drowning the sky and the peaks, I remain the only thing in the cathedral that remembers what it was like to be a whole, unbroken mountain. I wait for the storm to stop. I wait for the day the water becomes air, or the Rhythms become rock, and I am finally, mercifully, allowed to shatter.
