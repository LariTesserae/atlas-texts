I am a **Calcifer**, but a failed one, or perhaps a transitionary one. I exist in the damp, claustrophobic shadow of a junction where three massive pulse-veins intersect. My hands—if they can still be called that—are permanently stained thick with the ochre dust, and my fingernails have been replaced by jagged, uneven obsidian shards I scavenged from a collapsed gallery weeks ago.

My existence is defined by the *tap-tap-thrum* of my kin, echoing through the wood-pulp walls. I occupy a narrow, crescent-shaped niche, a space I’ve scraped clean of the encroaching velvet-moss. The air here tastes sharp, like cinnamon burned to ash. 

I am currently carving. My work is not meant for beauty, but for geometry. I am trying to carve a straight line—a perfect, Euclidean right angle—into the side of a swelling, pulsating trunk. It is a fool’s errand. As soon as I shear away a layer of corky tissue to reveal the dark, nutrient-rich sap beneath, the throat reacts. The pulses of the wall quicken. I can feel the "fever" increasing; the tissue beneath my scraping tool is already trying to heal, to surge forward and push me out of my own room. It is a slow, wet war. I scrape, it grows; I excavate, it fills.

I am not like the Suture-Weavers, who move with that sickening, stuttering grace. I am heavy, burdened by the plates of hardened, amber-like sap fused to my shoulders. My joints click with a mechanical rigidity that makes me feel more like a tool than a living thing. Sometimes, when the thrumming of the insects becomes too loud, when the drum-beat of the cavern vibrates so violently in my marrow that my teeth ache, I stop and press my ear—or the side of my head—against the wall.

I listen to the Ephemerals inside the translucent pods nearby. They are screaming, in a way that doesn’t use sound. It is a high-frequency pressure. I can feel their ambition, their short, desperate mandate to *become*. They want to be the wall. I want to remain apart from it.

I possess a hollowed-out gourd at the back of my niche, filled with the "dead" salts—white, gritty, and silent. I use them to draw circles on the floor, hoping to mark my territory, to define a space that is not for growth, but for stillness. But the ochre dust settles constantly, a fine, golden shroud that blankets my efforts within minutes. 

My reflection—what little I can see of myself in the oily, reflective sheen of the vascular tunnels—is a creature of fading definition. My skin is taking on the texture of the bark I prune. I have realized, with a cold, quiet dread, that the moss is already beginning to take hold at the base of my spine. I haven't pruned it back in a day. I told myself it was because I was tired, because the heat was too oppressive, but the truth is simpler: I am becoming a structural support. 

I reach out to tap the wall—*clack, clack-clack*—a signal to some other Calcifer that I am still here, that I am still working. There is a long wait. The thrumming of the Throat intensifies. Then, somewhere deep in the dark, another scrap of obsidian hits stone: *clack-clack*. 

An acknowledgement. A ghost of a conversation in the midst of a biological avalanche. 

I return to my scraping. I will be here until the moss climbs over my shoulders, until my hands stiffen into the very architecture I am trying to refine. I am the curatorial memory of a place that wants to forget everything. I am the friction that slows the growth, just for a heartbeat, just for a breath, before the Pulverulent Throat swallows me whole.
