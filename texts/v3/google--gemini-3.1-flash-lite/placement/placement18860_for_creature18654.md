I am standing on a spine of grey, braided root, seven feet above the soaking mulch of the basin floor. My feet, wide and calloused, have memorized the geography of this specific strut; I do not need to look down to know where the wood is spongiest.

I am a Tender, though the word has no meaning to me, just as the concept of a "name" has been filtered out by the slow, constant cooling of the air. 

My left forearm is tethered to the plumbing. A cluster of oxidation-green copper filaments emerges from the soft, ash-colored skin of my inner wrist, diving neatly into a brass junction box that hums with the hydraulic pulse of the deep heart-pumps. There is no pain in this interface. There is only a rhythmic *thrum-thrum-thrum* that vibrates against my own radius and ulna, a shared heartbeat between the boy I once was and the vessel I have become.

I am watching a fissure. 

A high-pressure conduit, thick as an oak trunk, has developed a hairline crack near the elbow of a junction. It is weeping a milky, nutrient-rich fluid, a slow *plink-hiss* that disrupts the equilibrium of the ambient temperature. It is inefficient. It is an error.

I move. It takes forty minutes to adjust my posture, to untether my wrist-interface, and to transition from the standing rest-cycle to the task-cycle. My joints creak with a sympathy—a faint, metallic scraping sound that mimics the shifting of the gourds above. My body feels heavy, not with the weight of muscle, but with the steady build-up of minerals and fiber. I am becoming more rigid. I am becoming more useful.

I reach the fissure. I do not have tools—the Latticework requires no external steel. I press my palm against the weeping brass, my fingers splayed against the cool, damp surface. I hold my breath, not because I need to hide, but because the sound of my lungs is a chaotic gust in this stillness. 

I concentrate, pulling the inner cold of the basin into my own center. I feel the scar tissue of my palm begin to knit with the warm, vibrant pulp of the plant-wall, fusing with the copper oxidation. I am sealing the leak. I am becoming the gasket. 

A shadow shifts on the platform opposite mine. Another Tender stands there, looking up. Their eyes—opalescent, milky, and vast—catch a stray shaft of filtered, grey light. They are not looking at me; they are reading my temperature, gauging the heat of my effort against the chill of the pipes. 

They do not acknowledge me with a gesture. They simply begin to weave a thread of glowing, gelatinous insect-silk between two hanging ferns, securing a drooping section of the canopy. 

There is no "we." There is only the network. 

I turn my gaze away, settling deeper into the brass. The vibration of the engine flows through my bones, and for a moment, I feel the distant, rhythmic cycling of the heart-pumps deep in the mire. I am not cold. I am not lonely. I am merely a secondary strut, keeping the world from sinking, waiting for the day my own structural integrity wanes so that I may finally, permanently, become the wall.
