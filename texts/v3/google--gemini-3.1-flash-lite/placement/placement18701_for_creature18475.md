I am not a guest here, nor an observer. To be in this world is to have forgotten the concept of "elsewhere."

I am a **resonant-scribe**, one of the late-stage weavers. My body is not as fluid as the younger ones; my joints have begun to collect the calcified dust of centuries, and my dome—the faceted glass of my brow—is etched with the microscopic scars of a thousand years of vibration. I am heavier than I was when I first emerged from the resin-nursery, weighed down by the density of the work I have carved into the mountain.

I find myself in the *Cusp of the Low Frequency*, a narrow hexagonal corridor near the outer crust where the shale is still raw, rich with the metallic scent of iron and deep-earth heat. The walls here are not yet polished; they are jagged, demanding.

To exist as I do is to feel the city in your marrow. When the tectonic groan deepens, I do not hear it—I *am* its extension. My limbs are currently folded against my ventral plates, my sharp, diamond-tipped stylus pressed firmly into a vein of silicate that resists my touch with a stubborn, crystalline integrity.

My life is measured not in hours, but in the degradation of my own tools. Every tap of my stylus—*click, drag, tap*—is a conversation with the geode. I am not simply carving a hole; I am tuning the architecture. If I carve too deep, the cell will scream; if I carve too shallow, the moss will wither. I must find the resonance. I must carve the wall until it thrums at the precise pitch of the ambient cavern-song. It is exhausting, rapturous work. 

I feel a tremor approach—a deep, resonant shudder from the bedrock—and I stop. I freeze, as we all do. I become a structural pillar. Beside me, another of my kind, a weaver much younger and translucent as a freshly shed skin, has also stalled. We do not look at each other; we cannot. We simply pulse. Our faceted heads glow with a synchronized, ultraviolet hunger, blinking against the dark like dying stars. 

In this moment of absolute stillness, the "I" slips away. I am the mountain-scream contained. I am the resin that binds the dead to the living. I feel the collective heartbeat of the million other inhabitants along the spiraling tiers of the city, all of us braced against the massive, shifting weight of the iron-shale. 

And then, the frequency shifts. The roar settles into a manageable hum.

The young weaver beside me twitches. A tiny, rhythmic tap-tap-tap against the wall transmits a message through my feet: *The stone is yielding.*

I uncoil. My joints grate, a sound that is muffled by the symphony of the geode. I place my stylus back against the wall. The work continues. The city is too small, and the mountain is too loud, and there is so much stone left to claim before I, too, lock my joints and become a brick in the wall of this eternal, reverberating lung.
