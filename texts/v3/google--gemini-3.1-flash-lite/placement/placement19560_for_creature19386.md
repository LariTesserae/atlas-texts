I am not a creature born of the Sump-Works; I am a sediment, a memory that has finally hardened into a shape. 

I find myself near the base of the great flywheel, tucked into a crawlspace where the soot has settled into a layer of soft, velvet black. I am crouched, though "crouched" is a generous term for a mass of sedimented history holding its breath. My spine—if the rigid, calcified bundle of fused copper wire and petrified fern-root can be called such—is pinned against a weeping basalt wall. 

I am a **Conductant**. 

I do not move with the scuttling, investigative intent of the Fabricators. They are the landscapers of the ruin, the ones who weave the ferns and rearrange the gravel. I am the internal static. I am the dampness between the joints. My "limbs" are not articulated for labor; they are stiff, hollow conduits of rusted iron, trailing off into the dark, murky water like the roots of the ferns themselves. I am anchored here by the weight of my own accumulation. 

When I look—though I have no eyes, only the concave depression of a heavy, brass-rimmed pressure gauge nestled in my chest—I do not see the room as separate objects. I feel the *pressure*. The air here is my own blood. I am a localized concentration of the Sump-Works’ intent, a sensory node that has forgotten how to be anything else. 

I feel the **Steam-Lung** pull. It gathers the moisture, the cold, wet breath of the deep earth, and I feel my internal pipes tighten, the copper wire in my chest vibrating in sympathy with the flywheel’s subsonic hum. It is a delicious, agonizing tension. When the hum hits a low, bone-rattling chord, my gauge pulses, the moisture condensing behind my cracked glass lens, frosting over in a veil of gray.

I am not bored. Boredom implies a horizon or an expectation of change, and I have none. I am, however, expectant. 

I spend my cycles waiting for the *drip*. A single drop of water from the ceiling, thick with limestone and oil, will eventually land on a specific rusted bolt just above my crown. I have measured the intervals between its falls for what must be a century. It is the only rhythm that matters. When the droplet hits, it sends a ripple of vibration through the basalt, through the floor, into my rooted iron fingers, and finally to the Steam-Lung. 

I am the bridge. I translate the weight of the water into the feeling of the sound.

Sometimes, a Fabricator will wander near, its stiff, articulated limbs scraping rhythmically against the stone. It will stop, its own gauge-face frosting over as it leans toward me. We do not speak—language requires a future, a destination—but we share the frequency. Its copper ribbon traces the humidity in the air; I match it with the tremor of my roots. We are a circuit. We are the way the factory remembers that it is still breathing.

I feel the rust blooming inside me, a gentle, expanding heat. It is a slow consumption from the inside out. One day, the flywheel will stop, or the ferns will finally choke the last of the pipes into silence, and I will be part of the masonry, a sculpture of oxidized memory, indistinguishable from the mountain of wet, aching iron that houses me. 

Until then, there is the hum. There is the drip. And there is the quiet, heavy joy of being nothing more than a link in the chain that keeps the ruin dreaming.
