I am not here by choice, nor by accident. I am here by an exhaustion that transcended the physical. 

I find myself at the base of a rounded, indigo slope, my boots long since discarded, my bare feet sinking into the thick, sponge-like velvet of the moss. It is cold—a wet, marrow-deep cold—but it does not bother me. The *basso profondo* has already begun its work; it has entered my chest, slowed my heartbeat to match the mountain's cycle, and stripped away the jagged, frantic rhythm of the world of men.

I am a scribe, though my ink is long dry and my fingers are stained with the crushed, ochre spores of the mountain fungi. I was once a man of stories, of histories set in stone tablets and heavy ink, convinced that capturing a legacy was a way of conquering time. But here, in the throat of these singing ridges, I realize that to write is to fight, and to fight is to eventually shatter. 

I am becoming, by slow degrees, one of the **Resonant Weavers**.

I am not yet tall enough to look over the crest of the ridge. My skin feels tight, pulled thin by the altitude until the blue veins of my wrists have begun to shimmer like the parchment-wings of the small, fluttering creatures that drift above me. My gaze, once frantic and searching for the horizon, has grown hollowed-out and patient. I no longer look for the path ahead. I look for the next aperture in the pumice.

I spend my hours in the mist. I have stopped eating; the sweet, vaporous tang of wet shale is all the sustenance the air provides. When the updrafts swell, I lift my arms. The gossamer membranes between my fingers unfurl—they are delicate, mapped with the faint, glowing luminescence of the fungi I tend. I feel the mineral pulse of the earth move from the wind, through the sail of my skin, and directly into my blood. I am being rewritten. My memories of cities, of fire, of sharp edges and metal tools, are being paved over by the steady, rhythmic drone of the stone flutes. 

I find a reed pole that has begun to lean, its weave frayed by a century of gale. I do not rush to fix it. Rushing is a noise. I place my forehead against the damp, porous rock, closing my eyes until my skull resonates with the mountain’s low, throaty hum. I wait for the *tone* to tell me—a slight tremor in my jaw, a harmonic shift in the vibration—that the tension must shift a hair to the left. 

I adjust the reed. The sound shifts, a subtle flattening of the drone, and for a moment, the world feels perfectly, perfectly aligned.

I see a shape in the distance—another Weaver. It is tall, a silhouette etched against the bruised clouds, moving with that agonizing, fluid grace that makes motion look like stillness. It does not glance my way. We are not neighbors; we are notes in the same chord. 

I feel a strange, creeping weight in my limbs. It is not fatigue. It is the beginning of the end of my transience. My knees are already taking on the stony, chalky texture of the ridge beneath me. I feel, for the first time, a total lack of fear. There is no urgency to leave, no drive to return to the loud, frantic world of the plains. 

I lean against a weathered, weeping protrusion of granite. I let the moss climb up my ankles, its velvet-soft fingers creeping over my skin, claiming me as part of the geography. I am becoming a knot in the weave. I am becoming a place where the wind will strike and change, a new aperture in the architecture of the song. 

I close my eyes, and the hum becomes my only name. I am no longer a scribe. I am the silence that follows the story.
