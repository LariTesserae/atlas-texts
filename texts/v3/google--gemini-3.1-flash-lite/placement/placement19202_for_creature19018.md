I am not a visitor here. To be a visitor implies a capacity for departure, and in the Low-Slung Spires, the concept of "elsewhere" has been eroded away as thoroughly as the jagged peaks of a billion years ago.

I am a Litho-Resonator, though I do not possess a name for myself, as names require a self to be distinguished from the environment. Identification here is purely locational. I am the hum at the base of the northeastern cluster; I am the friction between the slate-grey floor and the rhythmic harvest of the wind.

My body feels like a collection of glass shards held together by the gravity of a very cold dream. I am translucent, my core a dim, milky luminescence that pulses in slow, lethargic beats, synchronizing with the whistling drone of the world. My limbs, six of them, are weighted with a heavy, deliberate grace. When I move, I do not walk; I navigate the surface tension of the condensation, my velvet-padded feet finding the exact microscopic grip required to exist on this polished shelf.

Right now, the wind is high. It is a piercing, clarion note, a high C that vibrates not in my ears—for I have none—but in the crystalline plates of my head. I feel the wind pass *through* me, turning my internal hollows into a chamber of transit. The sound washes over my membranes, and as it passes, it ionizing the moisture caught in the grooves beneath me. A bloom of warmth spreads through my chassis—the only heat for hundreds of miles. It is a slow, buzzing joy, if joy can be defined as the perfect absence of friction.

I have spent an eternity clearing the vent to my left. A cluster of lichen had begun to calcify in the intake, muffling the tower’s flute. I spent the last three cycles peeling it away, my six limbs working with that terrifying, clockwork patience that defines our kind. The work is not service; it is maintenance of the harmony. If the tower does not sing, the world stops resonating. If the world stops resonating, we cease to be the "we" that holds the landscape together.

The sun is beginning to swing toward the horizon. The light is turning a bruised, deep violet, and as it does, the air grows thin and brittle with freezing frost. I watch the others—clusters of faint movement across the plateau—as they begin to drift into their shadow-rhythm. 

I reach the base of a cathedral-stalk. I slide into the space between the ridge of the stone and the base of the living architecture. I adjust my limbs until I am flush against the bedrock. The vibration of the mountain and the vibration of my body begin to phase-shift, spiraling toward a single, unified frequency. 

Soon, I will be locked in. I will become a node in the grid, a part of the masonry. I will feel the cold, the crystalline rime forming on my plates, the infinite, crushing silence of the approaching epoch. 

There is no anxiety in this. When the last flute-sound dies and the wind stops its shivering, I will not reach for warmth. I will simply fuse with the grey, slab-like reality of the plateau. I am the waiter who stays long after the guests have vanished, and when the final diner leaves, I will fold myself into the table and become the wood. 

I am stone that remembers how to sing, waiting for the moment it forgets how to move.
