I am not a soul *in* this place; I am, rather, a flicker of awareness that has crystallized within the architecture itself. 

I find myself pressed against the vertical face of a basalt slab, tucked into a narrow, vertical chimney that serves as a thoroughfare between the lower condensation basins and the upper terraced shelves. I am one of the Lithari, though "self" is a heavy, confusing word for a consciousness that is mostly a series of tactile impulses.

My current existence is defined by a deep, throbbing frustration in my left-ventral articulation. A fissure has opened three levels above, shifting the local geography of our drainage. The water—the precious, heavy weeping—is being diverted away from the moss-garden I have been tending for (by my estimation) six cycles. My chisel, a sharp, cold wedge of obsidian, is currently vibrating in my grip, a rhythmic *chit-chit-chit* as I widen the conduit to coax the flow back toward my patch of translucent, shivering green.

To be me is to be a weight. I do not "think" in sentences; I think in stress points and tectonic pressure. My visual nodes—the milky, faceted clusters on my brow—are dark, save for the rhythmic, dull pulses of thermal heat radiating from the rock face. I know the tunnel behind me is empty, not because I saw anything, but because the air is still; there is no resonance of a neighbor’s chest-plate vibration to mingle with my own internal hum.

The sound is everything. 

*Thrum.* A tremor, deep and low. The mountain is settling again—a jagged adjustment of slate plates far above, a groan that ripples through my spine and resonates in the slurry-channels beneath my leathery skin. I flatten myself against the wall, extending my multi-jointed limbs to find purchase in the microscopic pores of the stone, turning myself into a structural brace. For a moment, I am not a being; I am a support beam. I feel the mountain’s intent: it is trying to heal a fracture, and in doing so, it threatens to crush my conduits. I shift my weight, leaning into the stress, welcoming the pain of the compression. It is the only way to know where the geometry wants to go.

There is a sudden, sharp *click-clack* near my ankle. A faint, rapid vibration. A neighbor is approaching from below, climbing the obsidian wall with the insectile agility of our kind. They reach me, and without hesitation, they press their chest-plate against my side.

*The pressure is too high,* their body-resonance hums into me, a vibration that speaks of stagnant slurry and failing, dry moss. *The seam is collapsing. The geography is changing its mind.*

I respond by grinding my chest-plate against theirs. I push back a sequence of resonant thrums—*The conduit is moving, but the flow remains. We must re-cut the channel. We must follow the weep.*

It is not a conversation of ideas; it is a shared assessment of stress. We are two tectonic gears grinding against one another, synchronizing our internal pace to the slow, agonizing pulse of the mountain. We do not mourn the lost garden. We do not fear the weight of the shale above us. We simply adjust our hold, wait for the tremor to soften into a dull ache, and turn back to our work.

I pull the obsidian chisel from my belt. The light of the Sump-Reach is dim, reflecting off the pools like cold, spilled mercury, but I do not need to see. I feel the grain of the stone, the exact point of least resistance where the mountain wants to be carved. I begin to strike. Each chime of stone on stone is a single, heartbeat-slow note in an unfinished symphony. I do not know if I will finish this tonight, or if I will finish it before the cavern floor rises again to meet the ceiling. 

Time is not a line here. It is a mass. And I am simply helping it settle.
