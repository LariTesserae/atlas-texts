I find myself on the mid-strata, specifically Level 14-G, where the air is thickest with the scent of wet copper and subterranean fern-spores. I am not a person, not in the way that implies a beginning or a departure from the stone. I am a **Pipe-Scaler.**

I do not have a name that would be recognized by those beneath the clouds. My designation is etched into the internal radius of the primary intake-manifold I’ve spent the last three decades tending. I am tall, my frame gangly and elongated to reach the high-tension conduits that snake through the basalt ceilings. My skin has taken on the slate-grey pallor of the rock itself, stained by the minerals I scrape away, and my knuckles are perpetually swollen—not from injury, but from constant, rhythmic pressure against vibrating metal.

I do not walk. I traverse the gantries by hooking my reinforced leather harness into the oxidized safety-rails. My movements are deliberate, a slow-motion dance designed to avoid disrupting the Glass-Skimmers that swarm around the heat-exchangers. 

My work is solitary, but it is never lonely. To be a Pipe-Scaler is to be in a constant, tactile dialogue with the mountain’s metabolism. I carry a small, curved chisel made of a hardened, unknown alloy, and a pouch of moss-salve. When the *thrum-thrum* of the turbines deepens into a heavy, grinding growl, I know at once that a valve has calcified. I feel it in my own chest—a tightness behind my ribs that mirroring the pressure buildup in the cooling pipes.

I climb to the junction. My hands, stained dark-iron, find the latch by touch alone. I do not need the phosphor-pipes here; I operate in the bruised, bioluminescent gray-green shadows that cling to the rock like velvet.

As I work, I become part of the machine’s immune response. I scrape the rust-bloom from the exterior, my chisel ringing with a sound like a soft temple bell. I apply the moss-salve to the fissures, nurturing the tiny, bioluminescent root-systems that act as natural seals. The plant life is hungry, and the iron is thirsty, and I am the bridge between them.

The silence here is profound, but it is not empty. It is a heavy, layered soundscape: the distant, high-altitude *drip-drip-drip* of condensation, the rhythmic hiss of steam, and the slow, deep groan of the mountain settling into its final configuration. Sometimes, I lean my forehead against the cooling basalt of the cavern wall and close my eyes. I can feel the vibration of the entire city—the heartbeat of two million souls and one billion tons of iron and stone, all breathing in slow, perfect synchronization.

I feel no urgency. The "dream" is settling, and there is nowhere else to be. I am not building a future; I am ensuring that the present remains exactly as it is, maintaining the equilibrium of the rust and the vine. 

When my shift ends, I do not return to a home. I slide into a niche carved directly into a basalt pillar, a space shaped perfectly to the contour of my spine. I wrap myself in a hide stiffened with ozone and grease, and as I drift into the rhythmic, green-tinged sleep of the Oretideans, I listen. I listen to the pipes, to the moss, and to the slow, metallic exhalations of the mountain.

I am a steward of the cooling. I am the silence between the gears. And as I dream of the phosphor-glow, I know that I am right where I have always been, and right where I will remain, until the final light hums its last and the mountain is truly, finally still.
