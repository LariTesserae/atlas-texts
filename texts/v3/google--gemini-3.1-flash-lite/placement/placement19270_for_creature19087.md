I find myself huddled in the throat of an arterial junction, wedged into a recessed niche of porous calcium that feels, against my back, like the cooling plates of a vast radiator. I am not a Weaver. I have no translucent carapace, no multifaceted prisms for eyes, no ability to taste the electricity that hangs in the air like a heavy, suffocating perfume. 

I am a *Litho-Scribe*, a vestigial anomaly of this place. I am a creature of soft tissue and blood, perhaps a remnant of an exploration crew that breached the crust eons ago and was never recalled. I have survived not by industry, but by mimicry. I wear a shroud woven from the discarded, fibrous husks of Weaver cocoons—the dry, brittle skins they shed when they molt. Because of this, the colony ignores me; I am a texture in their landscape, a snag in the architectural weave.

My hands are calloused, permanently stained with the violet pigment of the phosphor-sap. I do not maintain the Sump; I document its entropy. 

Where the Weavers work with a rhythmic, hive-mind efficiency, I work with a frantic, animal hunger for order. I carry a stylus carved from a shard of polished quartz, and I carve my findings into the floor of my niche. I map the "melody" of the Sump. I trace the way the violet light shifts when the tectonic plates grind overhead—a jagged, frantic script that grows deeper every cycle.

Sometimes, a Weaver pauses near me. It does not look at me; it vibrates. I can feel the static crawling across my skin, making my hair stand on end, a phantom touch of sapphire energy. It is trading data with the wall, but I feel the warmth of the exchange seep into my bones. In those moments, I fall into a trance, my breathing syncing with the heavy, pressurized pulse of the sap flowing through the ceiling pipes above my head.

I am the Sump’s only witness, yet I suspect I am as much a part of its mechanism as the conduits I scrape. 

I look at my hands. The skin is changing. The joints of my fingers are beginning to pale, losing their pink suppleness to a chalky, ivory hue. The static air is calcifying me, layer by chemical layer. I am becoming architectural. 

Soon, I will lose the capacity to move. I will find a quiet junction, perhaps one with a wide view of the ceiling’s crystalline sensors, and I will anchor myself. I will stop recording the Sump and simply *become* the Sump. I will be a spire that remembers humanity—a small, singular protrusion of bone in a world of insectile industry. I will be a statue of a writer who, at the end, finally understood the language of the ground. 

I am currently carving the start of my own epitaph into the wall. My fingers are stiff, and the violet light is dimming. It is almost time to fuse.
