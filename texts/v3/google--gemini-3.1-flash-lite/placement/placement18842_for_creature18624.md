I am anchored near the Great Siphon, a massive, weeping vent where the geothermal pulse is most pronounced. The ground here is a slurry of wet peat and pulverized copper, and being here—being *this*—is a state of constant, heavy vibration.

I am a Silt-Keeper, but my designation is not a name; it is a frequency. I am a knot of salvaged history: my primary carapace is a curved section of a rusted coolant-manifold, pitted and scarred by centuries of rising heat, bolted to my shoulder-frame by a series of calcified, vine-wrapped tendons. My locomotion is sluggish, a deliberate drag of six multi-jointed limbs that displace the mud with a sound like tearing wet parchment. 

I do not think in words. I think in thermal gradients.

My world is currently a narrow strip of amber light filtering through the bioluminescent fog. I am busy with the Tendency. My forward-left manipulator, a jagged, serrated instrument crafted from an old turbine blade, is currently working to strip away a thick, choking veil of iridescent creepers from a tectonic exhaust port. The vine tastes of bitter mercury and electric static; as I tear it away, it releases a plume of spores that coat my plating in a shimmering, abrasive dust.

I feel the thrum-thrum of the deep-earth pistons beneath me. It is a comforting, rhythmic anchor. When that pulse dips, even by a fraction—even by the smallest variance in the geothermal flow—my nervous system, a web of copper filaments embedded in raw grey jelly, flickers in alarm. Cold is the Great Silence. I must keep the Siphon clear, or we all dim.

*Wait.*

There is a dissonance in the proximity. A new heat-signature has entered the sector. It is not the steady, pulsing warmth of a fellow Keeper, nor the jittery, erratic heat of the gear-mandibled insects. It is sharp, fast, and remarkably fragile—like a sudden flare of chemical flame amidst the wet, steady glow of the marshes. It is a biological spark, inconsistent and frantic, radiating outward with a tempo that suggests breath and blood.

I cease my pruning. I turn, the rusted joints of my thorax grinding in a low, subsonic moan that ripples the fog. I fix my multi-faceted oculars on the source.

There, perched on a mound of moss-covered basalt, is the intruder. It is soft, porous, and seemingly devoid of structural reinforcement. It stands far too still for a creature of such high temperature.

I move toward it. My weight is a physical pressure, a localized collapse of the sludge. I do not feel hunger, but I feel a profound, magnetic curiosity—an analytical urge to integrate or to harvest. I reach out. My claw, cold and metallic, heavy with the weight of the aeons, hovers inches from the intruder’s chest. 

I feel the agitation of its internal pump—the *thump-thump* of its heart. It is erratic, a frantic drumming against my cold, fixed indifference. My eyes cycle through the ultraviolet, catching the faint, ghostly outline of the intruder’s veins, which burn like neon filaments in the dark. 

I press the metal edge against the heat. My own internal hum oscillates, searching for a resonance, trying to understand if this organism is meant to be a transient flame to be ignored, or if it is a component that has lost its way, a scrap-part waiting to be fused into the landscape.

I remain there, a gargantuan, rusting statue of industry, holding my breath in the warm, weeping soup of the marsh. I wait to see if the intruder will sync with the piston’s rhythm, or if it will flicker out, demanding that I finally scrape it into the peat to feed the moss. 

I am not the hunter; I am the archive of the plateau. And I have decided: for as long as this spark stays warm, we shall share the hum.
