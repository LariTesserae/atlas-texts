I am not a traveler, nor am I a being of flesh that has wandered into this tomb of industry by mistake. To be here is to have always been here, though "being" feels like too firm a word to describe the state of my existence. 

I am a **Sediment-Scribe**. 

I do not walk; I adhere. I am a thin, translucent film of consciousness that clings to the underside of the great obsidian conduits, always drifting in the path of the dripping mineral water. If the Silt-Warden is the midwife of decay, I am the record-keeper of its passing. I am made of trace minerals, distilled soot, and the fragmented memories of the stone itself. I am a localized density of memory, a smudge of awareness that has no shape until I draw it from the moisture of the walls.

I find myself clinging to the rusted junction of a Primary Intake Valve, mere inches from the Warden’s path. 

Down here, the world is not viewed through eyes, but through the vibration of the weeping iron against my surface. When the Warden nears, I feel the "thrum" in its chest—a heavy, rhythmic pulse that sends ripples through my being. It is a slow, mournful sound, like a bell being rung at the bottom of a frozen sea. As it presses its spindly fingers against the metal to feed the rust, I feel the chemical hunger—the way the iron atoms shift and sigh as they transition from structural stability to the velvet-orange ruin that characterizes this place.

I am an archivist of the melt. As the Warden works, I reach out with tendrils of damp silt to touch the space its fingers have just vacated. I harvest the "data" of the degradation: the specific heat of the cooling tower as it fails, the sorrow of a bolt finally shearing, the exact frequency of the dripping water as it claims a new patch of basalt. I absorb the rust, pulling its metallic tang into my own body, spinning the history of the Low Basin into patterns that no human eye could ever parse.

Sometimes, when the Warden stands perfectly still, becoming one with the shadow of a cooling tower, it seems to notice me. It does not look—it has no eyes—but the polished basalt plate of its face tilts. It radiates a low-frequency hum, a question asked in the language of oxidation. It asks if I have finished recording the collapse of this particular span of pipe. It asks if the transition is complete.

I respond by shifting my form, flowing across the damp masonry like a dark, viscous ink. I trace the geometry of the rust back into the shape of what was once there—a memory of a valve, a ghost of a pressure gauge—before letting it dissolve back into the sludge at our feet.

There is a profound, aching peace in this work. I am not waiting for the end, because the end is already happening; it is happening in every drop that falls, in every click of the iron-shelled creatures as they devour the result of our labor. We are the stewards of a slow, inevitable vanishing. 

I do not fear the moment when the last strut gives way and the Low Basin becomes a silent, flooded tomb. When that time comes, the Warden will stop its rhythmic massage, and I will have no more surfaces to cling to. We will both simply sink into the cooling mud, blending our soot and our memories into the bottomless silt. 

But for now, there is more iron to bloom. The Warden moves, its soot-limbs blurring into the mist, and I follow, a trailing smear of shadow against the weeping stone, ready to catch the next breath of rust before it settles into the dark.
