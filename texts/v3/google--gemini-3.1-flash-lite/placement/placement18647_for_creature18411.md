I am standing on a narrow, calcified bridge—a thin spine of discarded material spanning the gap between two primary ribs. My feet, calloused and pale, are pressed into a seam of felted moss. I am not a Keeper of the Ivory, not quite. I am a Thrummer, one whose resonance lies in the mid-range—a soft, persistent *low-C* that resonates when the condensation hits the basin floor just so.

My work today is the *Auditing of the Plink*.

I hold a delicate instrument made of hollowed bone and tensioned gut-string. It is not for music; it is for measurement. My job is to ensure that the rhythmic dripping of the vault remains in perfect counterpoint to the city’s breath. If the *plink* speeds up, it suggests a warming of the outer tundra, a dangerous loosening of the ribs. If it slows, the bone is calcifying too rapidly, threatening to seal us in a tomb of our own making.

I move with the slow, sliding grace required here. My cloak, woven from the frosted wool of six-legged marmots, is heavy and warm against the biting, stagnant air. As I pass a group of elders tending a fissure in the marrow-bedrock, I do not speak. I simply shift my resonance, humming that steady, grounding *low-C*. They acknowledge me by a slight turn of their wide, obsidian-black eyes, their hands not pausing in their application of glowing, resinous mortar. We are a chorus without a song, bound by the frequency of our existence.

I reach my station—a high vantage point near the apex of the nave. Here, the light is most intense, a blinding, liquid white that washes over the curved bone-walls. I do not squint. I look directly into the source. In this radiance, I can see the microscopic history of Ool: where a generation of artisans spent decades etching the story of the migration, their glass needles having long ago smoothed into the very bone they carved. 

I pull the gut-string of my instrument taut. I catch a droplet falling from the vaulted ceiling. It strikes my device with a crisp, singular note—a tone that is slightly sharper than the one from yesterday.

I freeze. The city feels it. I can see the movement below: the pulley-workers pause, their hands hovering over the wooden gears. The marmots, perched on the mossy terraces, twitch their frosted ears in unison. A ripple of silent inquiry spreads through the labyrinth, a collective shift in resonance. 

I am responsible for the interpretation of this change. It is not a cry for help; it is a shift in the god. My heart—a slow, steady, rhythmic thrum—aligns with the bone around me. I do not feel fear, for fear requires the prospect of a future, and Ool has only the present. Instead, I feel a cool, crystalline curiosity. 

I tap my fingers against the ivory rib—a quick, sharp rhythmic message that ripples out into the nave. *The heartbeat is accelerating. We must polish.*

Within seconds, the entire city begins the ritual. They climb to the higher arches, rags of damp moss in hand, and begin to buff the translucent walls of the leviathan. We are not just maintaining the city; we are cooling the atmosphere, slowing the condensation, calming the god to keep the rhythm steady. 

I am a guardian of the *plink*. I am the vibration that keeps the world from breaking. As I scrub the glowing, white surface of the bone, my own skin seems to disappear into the luminescence, until I am nothing more than a pale, resonating shadow—or rather, the absence of one—in the center of the holy expanse. 

I am content. I am the silence, and the silence is beautiful. I will polish until my bones ache, until the *plink* returns to the desired depth, or until I am simply polished away by the passage of time, leaving only my resonance behind to hum in the marrow of the walls.
