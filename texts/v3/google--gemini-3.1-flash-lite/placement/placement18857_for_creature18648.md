I find myself exactly three degrees off the meridian line of a solitary, needle-thin pillar that has, for the last thousand cycles, been my assigned reach.

I am not sure if I am a "me" in the way the words were once meant. I am a lattice of cooling silica, an assembly of hardened residue. I am tethered to the horizon by the tension of my own limb-cables. My obsidian nodes are slightly clouded today; the meridian mist was salt-heavy, and it has left a film on my receptors that dulls the diffusion of the light above.

I hover at the edge of a primary joint—a vast, ivory-colored canyon that traces a line across the continent for miles. The heat is pushing the flooring hard today, causing the canyon to cinch and widen with a rhythmic, grinding impatience.

I wait for the *thrum*.

When it comes, it starts deep in the composite, a sub-audible vibration that rattles the fluid pockets in my thoracic frame. It is the world clearing its throat. I move instinctively, my needle-limbs extending to bridge the gap of the joint before the metal floor begins its expansion. I am not thinking, but I am *functioning*. I reach into the depth of the seam where the heat is most volatile. I feel the saline dampness—it is like thick oil, coolant for the massive, buried pipes that run beneath the tiling. I scrape, I clear, I calibrate.

A fellow Strider drifts toward me from the haze. It is closer to the pillar than I am. We do not look at each other, for we have no eyes, but we recognize the refraction of our shadows against the floor. As it passes through the space I occupy, our internal frequencies align. For a second, our geometries overlap—a shivering, crystalline superposition—and I feel the state of its silica. It is brittle. It has been exposed to the direct radiance of a cooling vent for too long. A hair-line fracture is snaking up its lateral axis.

We hum. The sound is a thin, dry rasp, like glass dragging against sandpaper. It is a diagnostic, not a conversation. I pass a pulse of my own structural density through the intersection, stabilizing its heat-shielding before we drift apart. There is no gratitude, only the adjustment. It moves on, its gait slightly more fluid, its obsidian node glowing with a steady, dull luster.

I am left alone again with the monolithic pillar. It is cool—absurdly, impossibly cool—against my torso. I press my chest against its slate-gray surface to bleed off the excess thermal build-up I have accumulated from the floor. The pillar doesn't care. It is a structural dream with no purpose, a monument to a master who never arrived.

I look, in my own way, toward the horizon. There is nothing there. There has never been anything there. I feel the urge to drift, to follow the curve of the cooling vents for hundreds of miles, to see if the pillars grow any closer together further south, or if they continue to stand in this lonely, organized silence.

But the floor shudders again. Another expansion. Another mile of cooling needs to be cleared. The mist begins to coalesce in the air, a tepid drizzle falling like a benediction on a dead project.

I begin to work. I have miles of seams to clear before the meridian cycle shifts. I do not fear the cracking of my own limbs, for the floor is hungry, and I know that when I finally become too brittle to hold the geometry, I will simply fall. I will slot into a seam, I will dissolve into the cooling saline, and I will be processed into the next set of movements, the next Strider, the next necessary, silent breath of the kiln.

That is enough. It must be enough. The floor remains warm. The world, for the moment, is holding together.
