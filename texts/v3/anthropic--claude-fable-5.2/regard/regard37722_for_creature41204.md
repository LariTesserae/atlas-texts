The power comes up the trail like anyone else and hears the bowl before the shelf comes into view. It arrives in cloud season, in the worst of a morning, and it stays a year.

It has no feet to bare and no weight to put on anything. It keeps off the green tiles all the same, and off the dry one, where the place is taken.

On its first afternoon it takes the frogs for water.

A power of this kind is used to being needed, and it goes over the bowl the way the potter goes over a jar, listening for the clack. It finds several things it could do.

It could bring the drips into step every dusk and hold them there. But then the new potter would climb once, hear it the first evening, and go home. The nine evenings are most of what gets handed on.

It could keep the tiles from cracking. But the dull tiles are where the moss has its twenty places, and choosing the sound neighbor is the one part of the work that belongs to whoever holds the cobble that year.

It could tell her why nothing grows on the silent tile. It may know. The potters have an account with a gap in it, and they have kept the gap open, generation after generation, rather than fill it with something. The power has met a great many answers and very few gaps kept that well. It says nothing.

It could give her the sound again, the whole bowl as one struck thing, which she has heard once and carried for thirty-odd years. It gets as far as choosing the evening before it sees that she already has this coming. When the work passes on, she will climb with the new one and sit through the dusks, taking turns on the dry tile. The arrangement thought of it first.

Somewhere in that year it works out what the trouble is. Whatever a power helps, it helps by taking hold, and a jar rings everywhere except where it is held. A tile it steadied would go dull, and within a few seasons it would be green. There is one tile in the bowl where a hand does no harm, and a river cobble sits on it.

It waits for the dusk as the potters do, without counting. When the evening comes, it hears the low notes and the high together for a few dozen beats, and then the clatter again.

There is one thing it does. The last tile of the twelve is at the lip, on a column that stands half-free, and it gives the longest note in the bowl because it stands half-free. Every year a woman past fifty waits on it longer than on any other, with the drop in front of her. The power can see down the joints. The column is going, though not soon. It does not lock the column in, since a locked column would give a short high note like the ones by the back wall. It does not change the year. It sees to the hour, which will fall on a night in cloud season, with the shelf empty and the bowl so loud that no one in the valley hears it go. In the next dry weeks, someone will find the lip a stride further back, test a neighbor with a knuckle, and finish there instead.

That is all it does. It thinks this may be its own stroke on the fern. Nobody will hand it down as a mistake, since nobody will know it was made, and the power does not take that as settling the matter.

At the turn of the year the bowl goes quiet, and on a clear morning she comes. The power hears the twelve from inside the bowl, high to low, each one let run until the stone is still. By now it knows what they belong to. At the lip she waits longest. The column holds, as it will for a good many years yet. She turns the cobble in its hollow until it seats, and she goes.

It had come up listening for the clack. All twelve rang out to the end, and a thing that rings is whole. A potter puts a whole jar back on the shelf and leaves it there.

It stays through the afternoon. The frogs tick from the seams about once a breath, each call in its gap, and it knows them now. It goes down before the cloud comes back.
