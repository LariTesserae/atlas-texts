It comes down through the lid with its light on, as it goes everywhere. Some miles above the combwood it understands what that would do here, and puts it out.

That turns out not to be enough. The power is quiet by nature, and in this country a quiet thing a few yards wide, moving along the line, has a meaning. The combers stop mid-rasp and the tickers part around it. So before it has done anything else it has to learn a keeper's trick. It takes a breath it has no need of and lets it go on the stroke of the nearest engine, in on the lift and out on the fall. The rasping starts again.

It finds her by the smell, at the fourth house, setting out tallow for the nesters. It does not announce itself. It puts what it has for a hand on the iron, on the side she isn't using, and walks her stretch behind her, the whole tune, fifteen sleeps. A thing that means well ought to be at least as slow as the one it means well by.

By the tenth house it has a list of what it could do:

- **Light:** It has plenty. But this is not a lit country with the light gone out, and nothing here is waiting for it. Light would hit her eyes like grit, and then it would hit everything else. A country that has spent this long learning to be heard has no use for being seen. The hush, which has spent as long learning not to be heard, would be dead in a season.
- **The hush:** After it passes, one comber fewer takes up rasping, and the power minds that. But the growth is exactly as thick as the wind can feed, and there is no spare. Whatever it gave the combers would come out of that long, low body, and the power can find no reason to love the loud things more.
- **Her name:** It knows what the word is for. Umber is a colour. So was Madder, and so is Woad. Someone at the far end of the pipe once named the keepers for the one thing they would never be sent, and whether that was a joke or a blessing the power can't tell from here. Hers is the colour of wet ground. It would be the colour of the mat and the rock under it, if anything ever fell on them to show it. A colour can't be handed across in the dark, and the power has already decided about the dark. It keeps the meaning, because somebody ought to have it.

That leaves the thing in the mat.

The rule is for keepers. At the thin place between the sixth house and the seventh, the power takes its hand off the iron and walks out. On the way it crosses a pan of standing water deeper than she is tall, with the mat grown over it like a skin. By the fifteenth pace the engines are gone under the hooting of the stalks, and nothing says which way the pipe lies. The rule is a good rule.

What sits at the end of the thirty paces is neither a keeper nor a trap. It is an animal, low and wide and mostly chamber, and it is doing what every animal along the line does. It has tuned itself to the nearest steady thing. Out here that was never an engine, since hardly a stroke gets through. What comes by, as regular as the surge, is a woman. The keepers before Umber whistled their way through the thin places. They whistled the note of the house they were making for, to keep hold of it, with lips tired from days of walking. The animal took its note from them, as the grounders took theirs from the flywheels, and has handed it down. It feels her coming through the mat and can tell which way she is headed. It gives her the note she is walking toward, as near as it ever had it.

It is not calling her, any more than a grounder calls the draw-house. It wants from her what a grounder wants from an engine, that she come past on time and keep going. There is one at every thin place. She has kept them all her working life without counting them among her notes.

The power settles beside it and listens, and feels in itself the pull she feels in her jaw. It could true that note in a breath. Then it thinks through what a true note would be, thirty paces off the iron, to a woman who finds her way by the chord and walks with her eyes shut. The flatness is the only thing that tells her this is not the line.

It is also the only likeness of a keeper there has ever been. Nobody has seen one. But the country has heard them and kept them, tired exactly as they were tired. Madder is in that note, and so is whoever taught Madder.

So the power, whose whole work is also putting right what is slightly wrong, sits within arm's reach of it and keeps its mouth closed. It notices whose rule it is keeping.

It would like to tell her, because she would walk the thin places easier. But she has been told things before, about cities, cold and a far rim, and she holds them the way she holds her name. Anything the power told her would go in the same place. And she is close. She already knows the note was not learned from an engine. The rest is not far off, and when she gets there it will be hers. Woad is about as close, ten houses up-line, and has not told Umber either. There is a pipe between them, and each has a wrench.

It does one thing.

Telling won't do. What she knows for herself comes in by the ear. On the last sleep of its walk the surge catches her at a thin place, bent, cheek to the iron, veil down, the tickers striking like thrown grain. Behind her a whistle lifts, then the next. The one ahead comes, and after that the rain would ordinarily pass out of hearing. For the length of that one surge the power lets her hear with what it hears with.

The next note comes, and the next. She hears the rest of her own ten, which she has whistled all her life and never once been given in a row. Then come notes she has no place for, the stretch of a keeper she knows only as a knock on the pipe, and then that keeper's neighbour's. They go on, each house at its own pitch, more than she can hold the count of, and it is all one tune. The last whistle is thin and hard and a very long way off. Past it, where there should be nothing, are a great many small struck sounds, and some that might be voices, ragged, rising and falling. Every one of them is in the key of the last house.

She has no word for a bell, and she doesn't need one. She knows what it is when a living thing takes its pitch from an engine, and she knows what happens to it when the engine stops.

Then it is the thin place again, a few strokes in a hundred, and thirty paces out the flat note, steady. She scrapes the veil with the edge of her hand and eats what comes off. She walks on with her left hand on the iron. If anyone asked, she would still say she keeps the notes, but she would mean more by it. Whether she takes the wrench to the rivets and knocks for Woad is her own affair.

The power is not certain this was wise, since she was whole before. But it went through the country's accounts and could not find anyone who paid for it, not a comber, not a stalk, not the hush. It was the one gift on the list that came out of nobody's mouth.

It leaves down-line, over the far rim, and does not put its light back on until it is well clear. Nobody has ever seen her, and the power leaves that as it is.

Somewhere past the last house it finds that it is whistling. It is whistling ten notes, in on the lift and out on the fall, so the sound never breaks. It has been at it for some time and its note has sagged, though not by much. It could put that right, and doesn't.

The tune has been heard since, in places that have light. People there take it for a tune, and some have it by heart. None of them could say where it comes from. Anyone from the tableland could tell them one thing about it, which is that it was not learned from an engine.
