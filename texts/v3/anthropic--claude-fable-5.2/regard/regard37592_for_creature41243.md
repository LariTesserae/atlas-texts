It comes down the ladder between shifts, late in a cycle, when everything bare is furred and there is the most to read. Crossing the room it turns sideways and ducks three times. The third is for a header the fitters have not chalked yet.

The first difficulty is where to stand. The tea can has shown what becomes of things set down on floor that is spoken for, and the visitor sees further ahead than the chalk does, so more of the floor is taken than a fitter would know. It finds a place at last by the new wall, among the props. Even a finished room keeps a little floor for nothing.

Then it does what powers do, and goes through the list.

It could hurry things. The four-inch line is on trestles in the yard, the pump is on a wagon, and wagons can be made to arrive. But nothing here is waiting. Being early is not a hardship. The interval between the cold and the iron is the only time this thing can be seen, and it is being seen by people who rub out their own chalk to agree with it. The visitor has watched a great deal of building, and it mostly goes the other way.

It could hold things back by leaving one plate blank and keeping one band of wall bare for good. It considers this longer, because of the fitters' account, the creature with the lifespan of a building programme. If that were right, it would be the one thing in the room that called for mercy. So the visitor checks. It attends to the moment a pipe is offered up and the iron goes white before the hangers are tight. Nothing goes out of the room then. It is what happens when a hand goes into a glove. On the day the last plate comes off, what lives here will not be over. It will be here in the ordinary way, as things are that need no frost to show them. So the visitor holds nothing back. To keep a thing from arriving so that you can go on watching it arrive is a collector's kindness.

It counts the oats. There are a dozen, with no leaf, all bent toward the lamp. It could give them a sun. It stays by them a while instead, and does not pretend that this is rescue. There is more seed in the weave than came up, and in a week the blankets will go up the ladder and be shaken out in the yard. That is no one's doing.

Then it comes to the warm part. It could find the keeper, who is somewhere tonight with his hands in his pockets or around a cup. It does not look. Looking would be a kind of drawing, and the warm part has no drawing, which the visitor judges to be correct. The pipes have bore sizes and a tolerance of one chalk line. A man should not. He is the only part of the room that will come down the ladder of his own accord, and he may come a little to one side of where he is expected. He may lean a foot left of the dry patch, or take the wheel at a different quarter. If so, the warm places ought to move. The chalk did.

It does not touch the wheel. Of everything it leaves undone, this is the hardest. The bare arc is a hand wide, with cold on either side of it, and the visitor has a hand, or what would serve. But that place is taken.

Standing on its floor for nothing, the visitor sees that the room is already doing the thing it came to consider doing. The room is keeping a place a degree warmer than it has any reason to be, ahead of anyone, and making no claim. It is how the visitor would have gone about it. There is not much to add, and the visitor is old enough to know what not much comes to.

It comes to three things.

The first is grease on the four bolts of the plinth, worked into the threads over the orange. The pump should go down on clean steel. It is fitter's work, and any fitter might have done it.

The second waits for the thaw. Something overhead reverses, the mains run warm, and the sleeves fall with their sound of wet sugar. The band on the wall fades from its edges inward. When the floor lets go of the tea can, the visitor picks it up and keeps hold of it, there being nowhere down here to put it.

The third is the glove, which it leaves. More than that, it sees to it that the glove will be left. When the fitters clear out with their trestles and their lamp, one of them will come up a glove short and not go back down for it. So whoever descends on the first watch will find a wheel that has never been turned, with a glove laid on its rim. He can make of that what he likes. Whether it was put there to cover the place or to keep it, the visitor does not settle either.

The dripping stops. The sump skins over with a new lens. The band on the wall whitens first. A minute or two later the rectangle returns to the plinth, between bolts that are dark now and shining. Then the fainter lines come, and then the first needles on real iron. Last, the white takes the hub of the wheel and the spokes, and closes along the rim from both sides. On one side it stops at the glove. On the other it stops at nothing.

The visitor watches it stop. Then it goes up the ladder with the can, and leaves the hatch open, so there will be damp enough to read by morning.
