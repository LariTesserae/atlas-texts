**The Attention**

It arrives the way the lightning does, inward and without noise, and for the first time something sees the tableland whole for longer than half a second.

It sees what the flash shows: the pavement, the pale threads, the loaves of the tors, the eyes. It also hears the bats, which nothing here that is not a bat has ever done. It stays through a whole tapping for that alone, since a thing that has gone unheard for so long ought to be heard once all the way through. They turn out to be saying *moth, moth, wall, you, moth*.

Then it considers what to do. It could do anything, which is the difficulty.

The first thought is the one anyone would have, which is to lift the lid. It does not need to guess what follows, because the place rehearses it twice a year. The power likes sunlight. It has noticed this about itself, and it has learned to notice when it is about to hand someone the thing it likes. The vixen's pupils close to slits. Her festival would be a sky she cannot look at over pans that do not fill. It leaves the lid where it is.

The second thought is the water. It looks into her and finds the works there, in the bone and the liver, as it finds them in everything on the plateau. In most litters there is one kit that fails and need not have. The power does not pretend that this is nothing. But the number of foxes up here is set by the fall, not by the water. A kit saved at the eastern tor becomes a yearling starving in the west, where the holdings are thin, and it cannot say that this is the better death. It leaves the water. That is not the same as being at peace about the water.

The third thought is smaller: give her something. A bat on the ground with a broken wing would mean more to her than any sky. But its attention, now that it is here, covers the bat as well, and the jerboa, the nightjar on its flat stone, and the beetles ticking in the floor of her den. There is no gift in the currency of this place that is not taken from someone it regards equally. It gives her nothing.

It looks over the rim, with rather more attention than she does. There are beings down there too, keeping the same three shifts, and what should be done for them is a separate question with its own long answer. It notes only that they do not know they are feeding anything. It will not ask them to burn one day longer for a fox's sake, or to stop one day sooner for a sky she does not want.

They will stop, though. It can see the day. It is not in her life, and probably not in her kits'. The furnaces are banked, and the lid thins and tears as it always does. The cushions flower. Five days pass, then six. No whistle sounds, and the hum does not come back into the stone. Every animal on the tableland, at the proper hour, turns its eyes upward and waits for a fall that is not coming. They will be on time, as they have always been.

So it looks for what might carry across that day, and finds that most of it is already here. The hares eat almost all of the seed, and the almost is the plateau's whole savings, lodged in the joints of the pavement where a hare's mouth does not fit. The hares themselves have never needed the works. And there is a vixen who walks to the rim on the days the light hurts her, and takes leverets with her eyes half shut, and whose kits pounce on their own shadows instead of running from them. She knows the relining as her famine. It is also a school, and her line is the one that attends.

There is the other thing too, which is why the power noticed her at all. In a country where every creature has learned that all good comes from above and on schedule, she is the one who stopped looking up.

It sees then what it must chiefly do, which is refuse to be the ceiling. It could be a better one than the smoke. It could rain down a cleaner grain at gentler hours, and every eye on the plain would turn to it and be fed. That would make the place its own, and the one level head on the plateau would have no more reason to stay level. It declines.

It does two things.

The first is for the day it saw. Between the last quench and the first real weather there is a gap. The pall is slow to clear, the pans are a finger deep, and nothing up here has ever waited on a cloud. So it sets one rain aside. It will fall in the second week, when the waiting has gone on longer than any relining and some granddaughter of hers, many times over, is standing at the third pan north and has begun to do what her line does, which is stop waiting and look across. It will be plain water. It will taste of nothing, which will alarm her. It will run black off the stone for an hour and then run clear. After that the sky keeps its own schedule and so must they. The power does not know that one rain is enough. It knows the gap is the only moment when the place will have no provider of any kind, and it is willing to be that once.

The second it cannot really defend. The brass pin is in the lee of the eastern tor, with the surveyor's number half worn off. It does not restore the number, and it does not finish the job for her. It leaves on the brass, beside her mark, something of its own, in the only script she reads, that says *another was here; no threat; gone*. That is the entire record of its interest in the place.

Before it goes it comes down to the height of her head and stands out on the pavement, on the horizon, where in other countries trouble and dinner come from. It wants to be seen level if it is seen at all.

A sheet goes off inside the pall. Across the plain, in every direction, the paired green points turn upward, except for one pair. She counts. Among the jerboas and geckos is something at no height she has a use for. She gives it one ear for as long as that takes, then gives the ear back to the sky, where the owls are. When the dark returns she is already moving.

She comes home at the quench with a jerboa. At the pin she stops, reads it, and rubs her jaw on the brass somewhat longer than usual, until it says only what it said before. Then she goes in to the kits, and later north, to the third pan, to wait for it to sweat.
