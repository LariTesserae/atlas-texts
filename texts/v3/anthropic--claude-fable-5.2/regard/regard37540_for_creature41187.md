It comes the way the swift comes, because the slope is bright. It knows this about itself, and stops at the saddle.

There is the row of pegs. It hangs up what it has that might work loose: its haste, its size, its wish to be thanked. It rinses at the trough. Then it waits for the hour before dawn, when the water shows what is in it instead of sky, and goes down into the mist with the others, crouching at every pan.

It looks into the face, and then behind it, where none of them can look. The young ones have the sum right. The seams run closer. A long way in, further than anyone living will cut but not further than the works will reach, the rock is more water than rock. A wedge set there would bring a sound like someone shouting.

It looks under the cloud. The bed is still there, three days long. The top of it is pale and bare. A day down, rain has taken the white off everything but the undersides of ledges, and a grey fleshy weed grows in the gravel. Two days down there is sedge. At the bottom, where side gullies bring in sweet water, there are willows thick as a wrist, and something skates on the pools. Swifts nest in the cliff just under the cloud line. Once there was nothing in front of that cliff to eat.

Then it reads the rims, all of them, in the order the dots were made. The first lifetime of the works would not fill one rim. The moths and the goat flies were always there. The swifts are newer, and so is the hawk. The tally is what the people say it is. It is also a count of how much there is, again, to be drawn. It does not think they have read it that way.

After that it considers what it could do.

It could take the bitterness out of the water. It stays on this longest. It would not refuse out of fondness for the works. It does not believe a wound should be kept for the sake of the dressing, and if the salt could be lifted clean it would lift it and let forty people sleep through a dawn. But the salt is an old sea folded into the rock, and lifted, it would have to be set down. It knows of no place called away. The people found that out before it came. Everything it could do with the bitterness is what they are already doing, done faster, and faster here is a pour.

It could dull the pans so that nothing takes them for sky. But the brightness is the water going up. They have done that sum too, and pecked the remainder where a foot has to pass. It cannot better that.

It could tell them about the valley. But a voice from above is a pour, and a comfort handed down is a softening, and nobody here softens anything. They do not take things on report. They crouch and look. A fact is not a comfort, though, and a fact can be laid where a hand will find it.

On the question of where the next mark goes when a rim is full, it has a view. It leaves the view on the peg.

So it does two things, both by threads.

It goes in behind the face to where the seams gather and does what any of them does on finding a thread thickened: it fits a stone. This takes it several tries. The old ones do it finer. The water still comes. But next season the young will cut their row and find the face weeping no harder than it did the season before. They will do the sum again, and it will come out as the next row, and only that.

A plug is not a cure. It has to be looked at. So the seams behind the face are its row now, to go along each dawn, listening. Nobody sent it. There are about forty-one of them, and you never see forty-one.

Then it goes down under the cloud to the first willow and takes one leaf. The tree will not miss it. It does not pretend it took nothing. It carries the leaf up in a fold, the way they carry the small dead the other way, and lays it in the last row, on bare clay, where a palm will come.

---

She comes down before the mist has lifted. She passes her hand over the crust of her own pan, feeling for a ridge the wind did not make. Then she steps down to the last row and lays her hand flat.

There is something under it that is not clay. It gives beneath her palm and comes back. Nothing in the works does that. Here a thing holds or it splits.

She lifts it with two fingers, as she would a crane fly. It is narrow, the length of her finger, with a fine saw along its edge and a stalk. It is not barley, and not anything from over the saddle. It is damp. She touches it to her lip. It is not bitter, and as it dries it leaves no white.

Her other palm is still on the floor of the pan.

"Dry," she says, not loudly, because it is, and that is owed first. Somewhere above her someone hears it and goes on working.

She does not go on. She folds the leaf into her cap, since there is no other place for a thing that has been found. She stands at the bottom wall with her face toward the drop, though the direction makes no difference to her eyes. She is listening downward, which no one here does.

"Something's come up," she says.

Above her, a figure that should have risen and stepped along the wall has not. The gap is noticed the way a stopped thread is noticed. Then there is someone on the stair, coming down to find out what, and someone else at the foot of it, waiting for the stair to clear.

Behind that one, a third is waiting without impatience for its turn to climb to the face.
