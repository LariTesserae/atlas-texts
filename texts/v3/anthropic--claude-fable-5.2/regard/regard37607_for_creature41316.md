It comes the way everything comes, with the dawn tide, and it does not come up.

It stops out in the cloud past the lip, at what it takes to be its own length from the last flag. It is wrong about its length. The next morning the snails have left the flags along the lip, the whole four hundred paces, and at dusk the moths come in a little short of the edge. Nothing looks toward the cloud. A keeper cutting heather near the lip sets her flint down, stands a moment with the skin of her back, and moves her work one stack in.

It moves off. The following morning the outer flags are still bare, and it moves off again. It does this for nine days. On the tenth there is a snail on the last flag below the first stack, rasping, and it stays where it is. It had not known how far its looking reached ahead of it. The snails took that measure, as the salamanders take the measure of a stack.

Where it comes to rest there is a hollow in the cloud that it did not make. It fits.

It is a thing that can do a great deal, and for a while it stands with what it could do, the way a keeper stands with a slab before choosing where it goes.

It could open a seam in the back wall and let a stream down. The stream would cross the shelf and go over the lip, and along a stream there is room for everything to drink a body-length apart. The stacks would not be relaid. The clay would crack and not be relined, and the hare would not stand against the fox again.

It could round every arris under the moss in one night. Then nothing would put a foot wrong, and the fox would have to do its own choosing, and the hares would learn to bolt. Hurry would come onto the shelf and travel it end to end.

It could hold the back wall still. But the household that carries its bedding out from under a block is moved by the same thing that clears the snails from a doorstep before a birth, and it cannot find where it would cut the one from the other.

It could put one clear note in one child's throat. It knows whether they can sing. It keeps that where it is, as a gap is kept at a door.

Each of these is a thing set down where something is already standing. The town has far more gap than wall. It looks for a long time and does not find a gap that is empty.

Twice since it came they have carried someone to the lip, set the body on the last flag, and stepped back. The dawn fog came over. It did not step forward, and when the fog cleared the flag was bare. It has let that be.

The one space it does not look into is the round one. It could. On the first dusk it looked as far as the edge, where the copper pinheads stop, and saw the lane open through the packed bodies at the first stack, and the place one drinker wide. Then it did what the oldest woman does.

What it can see without looking is the path. The path was laid straight and bows now, and the flags have been lifted three times and set further out. The round grows as a child's space grows. On the stack side there are hares to slide ahead of it and keepers to lift the flags. On the other side each lifting has brought its edge nearer the lip, and past the lip there has never been anything to give way.

It has seen that it is standing there.

So it does what a neighbor does, and nothing else.

At dawn the fog climbs the outer cliff through the place where it stands. The builders are right about spent fog. It keeps itself no colder than the air, takes nothing, and the tide comes over the lip as wet as it ever was. A good morning still gives two hands' depth. That water has been through it, and at dusk everything on the shelf kneels to that water flank to shoulder. This is as near as it comes, and the permission was not its to make.

When a hare moves, the adjustment travels the length of the shelf like a hand smoothing cloth. It used to run out to the lip and stop. Now it runs to the lip, and something beyond the lip shifts by the right amount, and the shifting comes back in across the flags, very slight, and has settled before it reaches the cliff. The deer still stamp on moss that returns nothing. This is returned.

The creatures on the outermost flags, who held their pockets of air against nothing on one side, now hold them against something. They do not turn to look.

On the shelf almost nothing is different. The stacks tick. The oldest woman kneels at the first stack's basin with the place at her left hand, and drinks, and leans a little to the right, and the lane closes from the far end forward. The children ask her what it is, and she gives them the account. But asked who lives here, she sweeps her arm from the cliff to the lip and the arm no longer stops at the lip. It goes on a little way over the last flag, into the cloud, and only then comes back to touch her chest.

It was not here on the morning the rock was bare. It cannot say what the animals were facing. It can say the place was kept, and that it was the right size.

The round will reach the last flag in some keeper's lifetime. On that morning it will do the one thing it has learned here, and step back.
