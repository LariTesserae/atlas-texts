**What Comes After Her**

It is known, if at all, as an interval. She leaves the pool in the last of the dark and goes up the chute, and her smell hangs there and slowly goes flat. Then the bank stays empty a little longer than it should. Nothing comes down. The paste at the edge takes no print, and the ticking in the leaves does not pause.

It has been in the basin some nights by then. It came over the eastern ridge, the way the burnt smell comes, and stopped at the last levee, where her paths turn back. From there it learned the place the way the place requires: by smell, by touch, by the small sounds. It learned the manners at the water first. It waits for her as she waits for the pigs.

It could do a great deal. That is the first thing it has to hold, the way she holds the burnt smell on her lip, and then let go.

It could bring the storm. It stood on the sandbar above the pool at the bend, over the fish packed back to back in water as warm as blood, and considered this. It did not tell itself the pool was anything but hard. Some of those fish will not see the river. But one storm without a season behind it would call the frogs up out of their chambers into a week of wet sand, and then the clay would close over them. The fig is fruiting on the dry clock, and the bats came for the fig. The leaves fell because the rain stopped, and the leaves are what is being carried down to make the ground. Everything here is spoken for, including the thirst.

It could answer her. It could put a thin rising whistle upstream on any night it chose. She would stop chewing, and the trunk would lift and test the air and find nothing. There is already one thing in her life that comes as a pressure in the chest and never arrives. It will not be a second.

So inside the basin it does nothing, and it takes some nights to be sure that this is not laziness. It reads the stain at the head of the chute and leaves none. It finds a newer print at the cut bank, three blunt toes and the nick of a fourth, smaller than hers: the last calf knows which pool goes last. It finds the clay still under the sand at the bend. If that pool goes to mud she will dig, and the deer and the pigs will drink after her. It recognizes this as what it would have done. Forefeet did it here before it came.

It permits itself one thing. When she walks into the pool and lets herself down until only the trunk is out, it is there for that hour. It takes none of her weight, because the water does that. It changes nothing, but it is there.

Then it goes upstream, past the end of her paths, along the sand. A night and a day on, there is another chain of pools, another chute, and another scuffed place in the litter, which says male, here last night. So the answer exists. Between the two stains the riverbed runs unbroken, with no post, no cut stump, and no track wider than a pig. That is what it needed to know. Nothing is missing here. What she waits for is upstream, and the river will carry it. The only work is to see that the way stays open, and that work is not in the basin.

It goes back over the ridge to the country where things are made by hands. It is more at home there than it would like, for that is the country of things that can do a great deal. What it does there is small and slow and done through hands, because nothing over there is done any other way.

The gas at the flare burns because no one has found it worth catching. A figure in a column comes out the other way, a fitting is ordered, and one night the stain on the haze is not there. The moths at the top of the canopy stay in the canopy. The air that slides down the ridge toward morning brings only the ridge. She curls her lip to it and finds nothing to hold, and she does not mark the lack. It has no need for her to.

On a map in a cooled room, the lowland is a blank between two ridges. It does not make the blank precious, because precious things get visited. It sees that what is true gets read: the ridge is steep, the gas lies east, and the ground between floods. The basin is not worth the trouble, and that protects it longer than admiration would.

It leaves the train alone. The train carries what it carries to people who are also waiting for a season to turn. She will stop for it every night of her life and then go on, and that is hers. Its only care is that the thing that has never arrived goes on not arriving.

Then it returns to the levee and waits with everything else. The pools lose a finger's width a night. One evening she lifts her trunk to the west and holds it there a long time, and it watches her know.

When the sand is river again she walks into it, goes under, and walks along the bottom. The frogs dig out. For a few nights the basin is loud. She whistles, thin and rising, like something much smaller, and after an interval she is answered from upstream.

It hears both from the levee. Then it leaves by the east. In the morning the mud at the chute shows deer, then pigs, then three blunt toes pressed deeper than anything else on the bank, and nothing after.
