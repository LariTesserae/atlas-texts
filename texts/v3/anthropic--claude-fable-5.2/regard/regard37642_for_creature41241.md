Late in the year, when the air gap is at its widest, the hall is noticed.

What notices it could lift the cairn off like a lid. It could come down the seam with the light, or up the throat with the spring. It does neither. It is in the water the way the warmth is, and the test of its arrival is the silt, which does not lift. The shrimp would climb it if there were anything to climb.

It starts at the foot of the stair and goes inward by the lanes. It could cross the stacks, but nothing here crosses the stacks.

It reads the walls first, because they are going soonest. Under the mineral the pecked marks are still whole. They are tallies. Each row is two counts side by side: what was carried up the stair, and what was left to breed. The second count is always the larger. That is all the wall says, and it says it row under row for a very long time, in deep pecking and shallow, many hands. The last row is low down by the stair. It has one mark in it, on the side of what was left.

It has come with gifts, as such things do, and it goes through them.

Light is the first gift anyone thinks of. A second seam, a hand wide, would be a kindness to an eye. Within a year there would be green under it. Within ten the felt would be losing, and everything here stands on the felt.

It could stop the stone. At a paper's thickness a generation, the jar mouths will close against the animals that now use them, in about as long again as the hall has stood. But the same slow stone holds the stacks against the settling of the roof, and it made a seal out of a ring of puddled clay. And a mouth that narrows that slowly is a hand closing on the widest. The hall has made animals to fit it once already, with no one meaning to. The line will come in narrower. It has the time.

It stays one day at the mouth of the oldest salamander's jar, which is one movement. It looks for what a line implies, a leaning toward the next place. Whatever she has, it is not that. She is where the water finishes. The whole day of the hall comes to her last and complete: a moult, a hatching, a lid's worth of silt from six stacks off. Waiting needs a next jar, and for her kind there has never been one. She is not behind anyone. She is at the end of the row, and the end is a place, the warmest one open to her.

It comes to the bowl last. It could know what is under the lid without lifting it, and it does not look. This is not delicacy. The hall asks one question of a jar and has answered it here, by going round, for longer than the walls have been smooth. To look would be to ask a different question and bring the answer into a room that has no use for it.

For the one inside, it carries what it always carries for someone found alone in the dark: warmth, a place kept, company, a name in the count. It finds each one already here. The warmth comes up an arm's length away. The place has been kept by everything that walks. There is company on every side, the nearest of it a chin on a rim, facing this way. And the count is on the wall.

The people without metal did all of it, with cobbles and clay and their hands. Then they did the part it finds hardest, which was to go up the steps and shut the door.

One thing is wanting, and it is small. The wall is filling. In a few more generations of salamanders the last row will be smooth, and the hall will go on being right about the jar with nothing anywhere that says so. So it takes the tally. It does not take it off the wall, which keeps what it has for as long as it has it. It takes it the way a count is taken over, by agreeing to it. The rows say what was carried up and what was left, and at the bottom there is the one, left and held. It will keep that where the mineral does not reach, at the hall's reckoning and not its own.

It adds no mark. It took nothing and leaves nothing, and a row with nothing on either side is not a row.

Then it does what they did, which is easier for it, since the door is already shut. It goes out by the lanes. The silt does not lift.

On the surface under the seam the grey lies an arm's length deep. In the gap a drop gathers and falls. A shrimp starts across the oldest salamander's rim and does not reach the other side.

A long while later, another drop.
