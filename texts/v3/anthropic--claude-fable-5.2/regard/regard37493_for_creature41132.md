It did not look at the basin from above. From above the place is all pattern, and it is easy to be wise about a pattern. It began at the old shoreline, where the first pair dug in, and walked.

Its first step set off the papery rustle in the nearest wall. It stood still until the soldiers quieted. After that it kept its weight off the hardpan, and it took the season the crossing takes. It put its ear to a great many walls. It watched an aardvark open a flank at midnight and stayed to see the old line come back by dawn.

It had come as such powers do, with things to give:

- rain
- eyes
- a truce
- a mushroom

Rain was the obvious one. Every afternoon the grey veils hung a kilometer up, and letting them finish falling would have cost almost nothing. It walked the oldest districts to see what the rain would land on and found the slumped towers, the first slopes. The basin was already building toward rain, at the speed of dead mounds. When the water comes it will cut channels, and grass will close the avenues. Carrying the damp up forty meters a grain at a time will stop being the only way to live here. That will be good country, and it is not owed early. The power left the rain where it was.

For eyes, it had to know what the worker lacked. It went in the only way there is, at her size, behind her, up the shaft with a wet grain. It looked in her for anything that leaned toward light, or away from her own end, and found nothing of the kind. There was a pull along the trail and a pull toward damp. At the moment the grain seated there was something it would not call joy and could not call nothing. She is not a creature kept in the dark. Eyes would give her the afternoon to be afraid of and no use for the fear. It pressed its own grain into place with its face and turned around. The grain is still in the wall.

It stayed longest in the low cell, where it could not stand over the mother either. It watched the small dark insect at the end of the sleeve, the legs that no longer reach the floor. It searched her, as it had searched the worker, for somewhere a grievance could be kept, and it did not find one. It noted that leaving would have been easier if her feet had touched the ground, and that this unease was its own and not hers. She flew once, on a heavy evening, and everything since has been what the flight was for.

The truce it weighed at the plugs, with the soldiers of both houses in the plaster. Twenty paces is what the straw will bear. Without the standoff the stronger house strips the pan around the weaker, and then it holds more ground than it can roof. The even spacing is the standoff, and so is the orchard, one thorn tree to a tower, and so is every lodger's address. It left the borders alone. It did not enjoy leaving them.

The mushroom it thought about hardest, because here the danger was real. The garden is one growth in millions of rooms, a cutting of a cutting. A single mold that learned its way past the workers' mouths could take the basin tower by tower, and the parrots and monitors and foxes after it. Fruiting would answer that with spores and mixing and new stock. But a fungus that can fruit can leave. The compact holds because neither side has a life to go back to. Give one of them a door and every garden becomes a negotiation. So it gave no mushroom.

It did not leave empty-handed, though. In the old districts it found a tower whose sleeve had stopped pulsing that day. The workers were still weeding and would not be for long. From a comb that had two days left it took one bead, the size of a pinhead.

Then it walked on to the edge and was there for the heavy evening. It did not shield the winged ones or pull the fox off its leap. In the morning the pan was silver, and the thumbprint lids were drying. It could have known which of them would come to a tower. It chose not to look. It waited by one of them for some weeks, for no reason it could defend. On the dusk when the first pale workers came up through their pencil-wide hole, a stalk of straw lay a hand's breadth off, where wind might have dropped it. This was not wisdom. Through the whole crossing it had allowed itself nothing larger than weather, and a straw is within what weather does. By morning the stalk was sheathed in a thin crust of mud, and under the crust it was being carried away.

The bead it kept. There is no safe place to put such a thing, since a garden left untended for two days is lost. So somewhere that is not the basin there is now a room the size of a skull, dark, held near blood heat, breathing once a day. It smells like the inside of a bread oven. The power chews the straw and weeds the bloom with what it has for a mouth. It has not been away for longer than two days since.

It has not decided what the cutting is for. If the gardens of the basin ever go green-black together, it may carry the bead back to the first scrape at the blank edge. Or it may judge that to be an ending like the rain and only go on tending.

It was thorough in the basin, and it found no intent anywhere in that white bloom. All the same, a mold that can digest but not move now has a second carrier, one that keeps it warm and wet and cannot go far. It is as fair to say the fungus keeps the power as the reverse.
