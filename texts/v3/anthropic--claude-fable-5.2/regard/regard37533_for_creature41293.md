The power comes to the hollow the way attention comes to anything, without crossing the ground between. It leaves no track on the crust. It is there at dusk, when the stone begins to pay out its heat, and for a long while it does nothing.

This is method, not restraint. It has learned that whatever it feels in the first hour of a place is mostly about itself. What it feels in the first hour here is the obvious thing, thirst on the hollow's behalf. It could bring rain. It holds the thought of rain and follows it down, the way the water would go.

Rain would pool at the centre, where the thorn tree stands. It would soften the gypsum domes, and the thin green line beneath them, which lives on filtered light and an hour of dew, would be drowned or laid bare. It would float the chert flakes off the slab and set them down somewhere else, and afterward the mud would crack in a new pattern. The seeds in the seams would swell, split their coats, and send roots into clay that is already, every handspan of it, somebody's. Then the water would go, as it went before. The seedlings would last a season. The seed bank, decades of the tree's only spending, would be gone in one.

A single rain is a promise. The power could keep the promise and send rain again and again, until the hollow was a pan and then a marsh, with reeds and frogs and things that walk. That would be a good place. It would also be the end of this one, down to the last bush, and done in the name of kindness to it.

It considers smaller gifts. It could send an animal to eat the pods and carry them over the rim, but an animal would be thirsty here every day of its life. It could raise a second thorn tree for company, but there is no room, and the tree is not lonely. The loneliness arrived with the power and will leave with it.

By midnight the pity has worn off, and what lies under it is closer to respect. Nothing in the hollow is asking. The saltbush have divided the damp to the last drop and hold their borders without quarrel. The lichen is stopped and can stay stopped. The seeds are shut and whole. The power had taken these for lives enduring a lack. They are lives that solved a hard problem long ago and have lived inside the solution ever since. The one thing they need is the thing they have, for almost nothing to happen, and to go on almost not happening.

So that is what the power sets itself to look after.

It sends its attention out past the rim and down. The damp at depth, which every root in the hollow draws on at the rate of a held breath, is the far edge of older water lying under the whole plain. That water has no defender, and in time someone will want it. The power does what it can do without being seen. A survey line, years from now, will run a little to the south. A bore will be sunk in another basin, where the water is sweeter and nearer the road. A track that might have crossed the hollow by the low eastern side will keep to the high ground, for reasons that seem good to the driver. None of this will ever show from the floor of the hollow. Like most of what lives here, the help stays below the surface.

It allows itself one other thing, and knows this one is partly for its own sake. Toward the end of the night it lets the air have a little to give. It is nothing near rain, only a dew of the kind that comes here by itself a few times a year, small enough that the ground will not have to remember it. The varnish on the rim goes from greasy to slick. Under the northern ledge the lichen takes up water and, for the first time in months, resumes. When the first grey light comes down through the gypsum, the green line wakes and feeds for an hour and a little more. The power watches this the way a person watches a sleeper's hand move.

Then the sun clears the rim, the dew is gone, and everything stops again, unharmed.

Before it leaves, the power attends to the slab on the western side. It considers the flakes lying where they fell from someone's knee, the core turned until it was too small to hold, the oval worn so smooth it feels wet. This is the only other attention the hollow has ever had, and the power sees that it was the right kind. The people came and sat. They took a few handfuls of seed and ground it, built nothing, and went on. They knew what the place could afford.

The cobble lies with its white end toward the centre. Someone carried it a long way and set it down like that, the way you set down a thing you mean to pick up again. The power does not touch it. It was not the one who set it down.

It goes. The next night the crust is the same grey, the stone is warm, and each bush stands at its distance. Some year a saltbush will die, as none has lately, and leave a circle of clay with no roots in it and seeds in its seams. Some year after that, rain will come without anyone sending it. The power has not arranged this. It has only seen to it that the hollow will still be there.

The thorn tree ticks.
