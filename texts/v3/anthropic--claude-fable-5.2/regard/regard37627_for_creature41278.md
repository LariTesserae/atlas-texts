The attention comes the way everything comes here, up the escarpment from below, and for a long time it does nothing but look.

It looks first for harm, that being its work, and finds the ordinary amount. Netters' knuckles split on clear nights. Newcomers are sick for a week. The old ache at the hip, and a quarrel held at breath's distance can last twenty years. None of this is hidden, and none of it is what the place is made of.

Then it goes through what it could do.

It could lift the cloud. The mesh would dry in a day and the cisterns in a season.

It could stop the green, and roofs would stay done. But the rota would go with it, and the rota is the one week in eight when the works manager and the pit boy have the same hands. These people already hold that a roof is something you do. Elsewhere it takes a prophet and several centuries to get that across, and it rarely lasts. The power is not going to be the one to take it back.

It could give them an echo. They would stand further apart.

It could tell them why nothing is built on a tor, and it spends longest on this one. A reason can be argued with, improved on, and found no longer to apply. Give them one, and inside a few generations there would be a shed on Sixty-One. The habit is in better repair than any reason would be. For the same cause it will not show itself. A power that is known gets something built for it, and the only open ground is the tors.

So it does very little, and all of it in the grain of the place.

Some of it is done inside the weather, where nobody would look for a hand. On certain nights the rime lies lighter on the mesh than the cold warrants, and the netters' hands get a week to close. A clear hour runs long now and then. A child taken into a cold kiln for her first echo gets her voice back a little rounder than brick ought to manage. Nobody calls any of this a miracle. They call it a dry day.

It looks down the ropeway too, past the railhead and along the lowland wires to where the white bells end up, and it sees a year coming when the orders thin. It will not prevent that. It does not hold worlds still, and these are the last people it would insult by pretending that anything stays done. But it notes that no town anywhere knows better what to do with a thing that is worked out: leave it alone, let it close from the edges, and tell it afterwards by the nearest stone. It tests whether that will bear the weight, and judges that it will.

Then it does the one thing it wants to do, which is come up in a coal bucket, there being no other road.

It arrives black and is sick for a week. It could have excused itself from that and does not. It is given felt, nailed boots, and a room under Eighty-Eight. It learns the hand on the sleeve, and it learns to place its feet. Nobody asks much about below. They ask whether its coat has been baked.

It goes on the rota, one week in eight, with a hoe and a wire brush in the gutters behind the wire mill. It is not very good at it, and the green has closed over again before it comes back round. It had expected to mind this. But it has spent a very long time among peoples who beg it to fix things once and for all, and here it is among people who would find the request faintly careless. This is the first work it has been given that resembles its own.

What it can do about the quarrels, it does the way anyone does, standing close, with a hand on a sleeve.

In the clear hour it climbs Forty-One with the others, takes off its hat, and stands with its face up. It is used to being the thing looked toward. It does not know what they are looking at, any more than they do, and it looks too.

It leaves the painter alone, except once. One afternoon in the old ground, the bank that should have come up the cliff at three holds off for a quarter of an hour, and she reaches a stone further out than she has been. She scrapes. The shape is there, pale and waiting, and she fills it in.

The power did not put it there. In all its looking it has not found who made the first marks, only that something attended to this plateau before it did, went its round, and left every stone accounted for. It had taken itself for the first to notice the place. It is only the latest, which is a different office, and it thinks a better one.

There is a lamp in its back window, left unshuttered overnight. In the morning there are wings on the sill, and it sweeps them off. It has never looked to see what eats the moths, though it could. Asked why it keeps the lamp, it would consider the question honestly and say it was for the moths.

Then its week comes round, and it picks up the hoe.
