It came as a hand, because a hand is what the room knows.

Before it touched anything, it considered what it could do, since it could do a great deal.

It could lift the water off the meadow. But the flood had been there a year, things were living in it, and it was the only road the eel had ever been offered. It left the water.

It could undo the three rules. But the room takes nothing from anything. The stem does not hurt the rock, and the eel does not flinch from its own side. To clear the room, the power would have to push aside whatever stood in the way of its idea of a room, and that would be the first displacement the place had known. It left the rules.

It could swell the eye, silver the flank, and turn the blunt head toward the sea. But the eel had been put once already. It left the eye.

Then it reached across the middle, which it could do, and found the far wall and what had been set down there. It was every filling the room had kept, each one whole and in the same place. It took its hand back and left the middle as it was. Then it felt the clear patch and knew what a hand could not tell. The patch was not clear because of the spring alone, or the head alone. It was clear because something still arrived there new, and something still waited there to be given to. A spring-house is for drawing. When nobody draws, everything comes and nothing goes, and a room must learn to hold it all somehow. This room had learned.

So it went away and came back with the heel of a loaf and a jar.

It held its fingers still at the sill. The eel rose and mouthed them with the fine file, let go, and laid its chin across a knuckle. The power broke the crust and set a piece at the mouth, inside the patch, where bread is only bread.

The teeth closed. The piece was in the fingers and then it was not, and it was not anywhere else in the room either. It had gone into the eel, the way things used to go into things. The power gave another piece and then held the belly, lightly. It was no longer flat.

It did not know whether to feed the second length, because nothing settles that, and it saw that nothing needed to. It broke the heel in two. On two-length days it laid the other half in the groove of the sill, under the thread, and left it. On the next visit it checked the groove with the other hand, and the answer has differed.

Then it held the jar under the spring. The thread is weak and the jar took a long time, but the power had the time. It drew the full jar out through the gap, past the sharp edges, up through three metres of brown water to the bank, where it had laid a board. It set the jar down on the wood.

Below, the chin left the lip of the trough. The head turned and held. For the first time it pointed toward the gap.

The power drank. The water tasted of nothing but water, and it understood that this was no small thing. Someone had kept it so through a year when no one came, working a wall that gave nothing back, and the keeping had been owed a drinker all that time. It drank the whole jar.

It did nothing else. It had other places to attend to, and it attended to them, and it came back the next day with bread and the jar.

It has kept coming. The eel has not learned otherwise, and now it does not need to.

The patch around the sill was measured at the turn of the season and had not narrowed by anything a finger could find. The place on the belly where fingertips cannot get underneath is still two fingers wide. The power does not pry at it. The knock still comes from the middle, about as often as before, and the eel still turns to it. There is a second knock now, from above, and it comes after the hand. The eel turns to that one too, and afterwards it goes back to the lip more quickly.

After the crust each day, a fingertip rests on the eye. This is not done to hurry it. The eye is flush.

If the eye stands proud some autumn, the power will not carry the eel anywhere. It will lay its hand palm down across the broken edge of the slab, which will be as sharp as it is now, and the eel will go out over a hand. After that the jar will still be filled each day, for as long as the spring runs, so that the patch stays open for whatever lies in the trough.

If the eye never stands proud, there will be bread.
