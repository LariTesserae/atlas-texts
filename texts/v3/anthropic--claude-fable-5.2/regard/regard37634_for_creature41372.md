For eleven years it does nothing, which on the plateau is not long.

It begins with the plant, because the plant is the oldest thing here and everything else stands on it. It goes down through the pit, past where the light reaches, through the brown strata and the pale tubes to the sealed core, and listens there the way the children are taught to, by contact. It is looking for want. It finds a being that grows at the rate it is worn away, and it cannot establish whether that is contentment or only arithmetic. It checks whether the ropes of dust cost the plant anything it would miss, and they do not. It checks whether the shells are wounds. As nearly as it can tell, the plant rises around a held note as flesh rises around a splinter, and then meets its own sound at the wall and undoes it, so that nothing crosses in either direction and the strange note cannot get out among the dust lines. The rooms where three thousand people talk are quarantine. The plant builds them without stint and levels an empty one without haste. The power concludes that the plant has no grievance it is able to detect, and concludes nothing more than that.

Then it considers what it could do.

It could bring rain. Water is the one thing the plant has spent its whole life sealing out, and the moths are the right rain for this place.

It could wake the western sounder. It has examined it under the swell, and it is dark, palm-sized, and unbroken. It has simply finished. If it were started, a shell would stand there again in two centuries. The town would again have forty objects no one can make, and a thousand people who call that place ground would have been corrected.

It could mend the herders' ears, but what they would hear is the drone.

It learns the spoken names from the women of the western side, who recite them over their work. It could return each name to its owner. But each was said once, by a mother, in a voice she had been saving, and that is the size they are. It leaves them where they are kept.

It does not take any of this as leave to go. The sounders that remain will go quiet one at a time. Each morning in each shell, whoever wakes first puts a palm on theirs, and the power takes this as the nearest thing to a request that anyone here has made. Nothing could be done if it were still. That is the part it can do something about.

It spends the eleventh winter in the knuckles. They run at child height along the inner ribs, smooth and solid to the eye. At the centre of each is a space smaller than a moth, and in that space the drone is gone. So the plant does for a child's jaw what it does for a sounder. The sounders hold the note and draw down the dust, but the silence was never theirs. The plant makes the room, and a throat can hold a note. It cannot do so without pause, but the knuckles show that the plant will wait while a child sleeps, eats and goes out to rake.

Nobody knows this, for ordinary reasons. Indoors a second note fights the first, so children are told to stop. Outdoors there is no rib to set the jaw against, only loud ground, where no one lies down. And a knuckle has no door.

The power decides that this cannot be shown by a sign in the sky or a voice in a room, since either would give the town one more thing it did not make. It has to be shown by a body. So it comes up over the rim as visitors do, in one, and its lips crack on the second day. It is given grease, felt, plugs and a cord. It rakes a length of street for its keep. Within the week someone needs to call it from a distance and gives it a hand-name, a finger drawn across the mouth. It has no spoken name, and no one asks for one.

Then it walks out the western street to where the street stops. It chooses the spot for who passes there and not for what is buried, and it keeps off the swell. Twenty paces short, it steps off the still line onto the loud ground, lies down, sets its jaw against the mat and hums one note that belongs to no shell. It does this in daylight, as the women cross the band in daylight, and for the same reason. It comes back every day.

Adults on the street look, and look away, as they would from a grown person doing something children are told not to do. The children know exactly what it is doing.

By the cold season there is a lip of horn-grey wood. By the next there is a cup that two hands could rest in, with living green on its outer face. Dust that trembles in the air above the mat falls straight down inside it and lies flat. A moth that drops there before dawn is still there at midday, whole, belonging to no line. Anyone on the plateau can read this with their eyes.

To those who ask, it signs from the shoulder, large and slow, in the half of the language it has: one note, held here, no sounder. It adds nothing, because it knows nothing else for certain. It does not know whether a shell raised by voices would outlast its own wearing-away with no rope of dust to feed it. It does not know whether three thousand people, or a thousand, would want to spend their breath that way, by turns, for the decades it would take. It does not know whether people whose songs never rest on one pitch could bear to rest on one.

Then it gets up and stops. A cup left alone will be level in thirty years or so. That seems long enough for people to make up their minds and short enough that, if they choose not to, nothing will have been changed. It hands back the cord and goes down over the rim. It keeps the hand-name.

A few days later a herder woman near her time comes in from the west with two others. She walks up one side of the swell and down the other without altering pace. At the cup she stops. She looks into it for some while at the dust lying flat. Then she lays two fingers along her jawbone, as at the start of a name, and does not go on to make the rest.

That season a child from the shell that keeps the birthing room is seen lying face down on the loud ground beside the street. Whether she is told to stop is not something the power arranges.

It goes on attending. A person here can only be hailed when they happen to look, so it looks often.
