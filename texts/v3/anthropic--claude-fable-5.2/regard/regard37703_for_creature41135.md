You raise your arm.

That is the first decision, and most of the others follow from it. She has greeted you from the distance her people greet at, given you the news a walker needs, and gone back to her line without waiting to learn what you are. So you are a walker. You answer in her signs, slow and wide: *road good. from the east. no hurry.* She does not look up to read it. She had your weight through her soles some time ago and found it ordinary, because you have made it so.

You stay for one out-and-back of her nine masts. You could know the plateau in an instant, but that would be knowledge of some other place. This one happens at walking pace, the shadows, the front of the roar, the creep of the skin toward the southern rim.

So you walk. When the hiss comes up in the east you push plugs home, though nothing here can hurt you. In the shadows you stand still. A runner puts its nose to your ankle and you let it decide.

There is a great deal you could do. You go through it the way she goes along a road, sighting from each swell.

You could pin the skin to the rock. The lines would stay where her grandmother put them. Nobody would need to kneel on them again, and nobody on a far swell would have reason to raise an arm. You have seen by now that the road is mostly the recutting.

You could tell her what comes to the spile. You could tell her whose the ear-plugs were. Each would take one sentence. But she has never asked the dark what it is. She has given it water and a word, and has warmed the wax in her palms once a year so it will not crack. That is a way of knowing a thing, and it may be a better one than yours.

She was taught that the fell does not mind a clean line, and she has chosen to believe it. You know the answer. If it were the hard one you would owe it to her. It is not. So you ask for a stretch of road, kneel, and cut it yourself with a flake of chert, shallow, just through to the pink. That is all you say on the subject.

You could mend the left ear. You hold that one longest. Mended, she would hear the night voices better, and the quiet-house better, and that is not nothing. She would also be the one person her age on the plateau who could hear, by an act no one could account for, and she would know that something had singled her out. You leave it. You are not sure you are right, and you carry the not being sure with you.

You are wary of yourself in all this. Restraint is an easy thing for a power to admire in itself. So you look properly for the place where she is simply short of something.

You find it. A few hundred hours, in sixty years, of a face and its voice arriving together. And it is a thing a walker can give.

At the fifth mast you help her cut the skin back from the stilts. You warm rasp-wax in your hands and work it into the seams. Inside, she lights the lamp.

She speaks softly, formally, with long pauses. You do not fill them. It takes you most of the first evening to understand that they are not hesitations. They are the turn she is leaving for you, at full width, the way her signs are made wide to be read from far off. When you take one, you take it slowly. When you do not, she goes on, and what comes after an untaken pause is always the truer thing. She watches your mouth. You have given yourself an ordinary face, a little tired, one that moves when it talks, and you let her watch.

On the second night she tells you her spoken name. You do not know whether you count toward the twenty.

On the last evening you tell her one true thing about yourself. You say that at noon, in the full roar, you sing. You have done it every day of the walk, as loud as you can, and have not heard a note. It is true. It began on the second day without your deciding. The roar does that, it turns out, to anything with a throat.

She watches your mouth to the end of the sentence and a little past it. Then she laughs, and the laugh is too big for the house. She puts her fist against her breastbone.

"Here," she says. "Only here."

"Yes."

A pause. You leave it alone.

"I thought," she says, "it might be everyone."

You say you can only speak for one. She nods as if that were plenty.

That night something comes up through the thatch under the floor. The stilts take its weight. Three pulls and a rest. She lies with her palm flat on the boards and says nothing, and you understand that there is a word she is not saying because you are there. Even at this size, you cost something. It is time to go.

In the morning you each tie a knot. Yours is a plain one, a walker's. Under her fingers it will feel like any other.

By noon you are a swell apart and the roar is in your teeth. She raises her arm. *herd west, small. water, two hours.* Then she makes a shape that is not yet a sign: her fist to her breastbone, her mouth wide open, her other hand turned toward you, asking.

You give it back.

Then you both walk on, a kilometre apart, mouths open at the sky, hearing nothing.

You leave one knot. You leave a few hundred paces of straight line, already closing its lips. You leave a shape made with a fist, which may travel or may not. You could look ahead and see whether it reaches the rim. You would rather have it the way news comes here, from far off, on somebody's arm.

What you take with you is the spile. For nine years she has left water out for a thing she has never seen. She has asked nothing of it, kept herself from looking, and given it a name with no hands. You came to learn whether this place wanted watching over. It is already being done, by a woman of sixty, in the dark.

Some nights later she is alone in the house with the lamp unlit. She says her word, barely louder than breath. Below her, the drinking stops.

Much farther off than that, you stop too.

Then you go on.
