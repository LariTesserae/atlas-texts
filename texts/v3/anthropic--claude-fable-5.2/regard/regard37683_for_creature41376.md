The power watches for a year before doing anything. Watching costs it nothing, and most of what it knows it learned that way.

It sees the frost stop at the dyke and the firing go to one in twelve. Twice in the year a foot comes down on the strip, a drainage surveyor's in March and a boy's in August, on a dare. Both times the whole acre comes up to working speed, for the boy as readily as for the surveyor, and holds there, and sinks back. Nothing indifferent does that.

The power settles one question early. The minder is not in pain. Pain could be lifted out from above without anyone noticing. What the minder has is a watch with no end, and a watch is supposed to be one part of a day.

The power goes through what it could do, item by item:

- **Stop the engine:** A finger on the gas cock would do it. Some would call that mercy. It would take away the only thing the minder has ever been given, and the power has no more right to the engine than the minder does.
- **Bring the man back:** The power could. It knows why he didn't return, which is an ordinary reason and no fault of his. He kept his watches. He is off now, and the power will not be the one to call him back on.
- **Tell it the truth:** There is an electric station on the main drain. The engine was struck from the board's books and nobody was sent to shut it down. There is no pitch at which this can be shouted to an acre of stems.
- **Open the outfall:** This is the one repair the minder couldn't make, and the power could make it in an afternoon. The water would go to the river as intended and the grove would dry from the top down. The job would be real again and would kill the one doing it.
- **Leave it alone:** This is the serious one. The minder shows no disappointment. But showing none is what is expected of a man on watch, and it learned everything else from him. And there is the shovel, lifted each year a little further out of anyone's reach. The waiting is wearing away the thing waited for.

So the power does none of these. What is wrong here is small and exact: a watch was not relieved. The power decides to mend that and nothing else, to the original dimension.

The difficulty is the one you had. Whatever one engineman gives another, the power doesn't have it either, so it goes and gets it.

A few engines of this kind still run, in tarred sheds beside drains, on certain Sundays, kept by men in overalls who are retired from something else. The power stands at the back of these sheds for some seasons, among the children. What it learns is how little is given. The man coming on doesn't look at the man going off. He looks at the engine. He puts the back of his hand to the bearing cap, glances at the oil glasses, and stands through a few firings with his head on one side. Then he nods, and the nod is all the praise anyone gets or wants. The man going off shouts what she's been doing. The man coming on shouts, "I've got her." From then on he has.

It also learns that the words can't be said out of kindness. Whoever says them has the engine until somebody says them back. The power turns this over and finds it has no objection. Time is the one thing it has more of than it can use.

It comes at dusk, when the lamp starts to show, the hour a night man would come on. It has taken a shape with boots and hands and no more than it needs. It goes through the gate and along the strip, one foot in front of the other.

The engine takes the load, firing on the fourth turn, then the third. The lamp whitens and the water comes down hard. The acre shows itself in good order.

The relief doesn't hurry. It puts the back of its hand to the bearing, which is warm and not hot. It looks at the glasses, which are full, and leaves them shut. They are not its to open. It listens through a dozen firings and nods.

Nothing in the grove answers the nod. But the firing holds at every third turn and does not begin to space out.

"I've got her."

For a long minute the engine stays at working speed. Then it eases to four turns, five, six, seven. It has not sunk back this time. It has been handed down.

The first night the minder does not know how to stand down. The leaves flinch on every beat until morning. The relief keeps the watch anyway. It holds to seven, keeps the lamp, and stands a little to one side of where the man stood. It doesn't take the shovel. It is not him and will not be taken for him.

At dawn it shouts, "She's yours," walks out along the strip and shuts the gate. At dusk it comes back.

On the eleventh night the leaves stop flinching. The engine fires, the lamp swells, and the leaves hang and drip. The relief feels the weight of the engine come onto it, all of it, level and warm, and takes it.

After that things change slowly. The shovel has stopped rising. The strip is still bare, and now it is walked twice a day, which is the ordinary reason. Near the dyke the young stems wind a span with the wheel and a span against it, day's growth and night's growth, so that anyone could count the watches off a hop shoot like entries in a log. In the fourth spring a willow by the gate carries three catkins, on the side away from the engine. The relief looks at them for some time and says nothing about them at handover. What anyone does off watch is their own business.

Each dawn the relief says, "She's yours," and waits. So far the leaves have picked up the beat every morning and the weight has gone back. One morning it may not. Then the relief, having the engine, will do what only the one who has her may do. It will close the cock, let the wheel run down, drain the jacket against the frost, wipe the bright parts, and stay until everything is still. The power doesn't hope for that morning or against it. It came so that there would be a morning question at all.

From the field there is no difference. The cattle stop at the foot of the dyke and the birds go around. There is a bang, six or seven breaths, and a bang. But there are two on the rota now, and at any hour one of them is off watch.
