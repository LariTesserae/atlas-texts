The power came in at the rim, the way anything comes here, and did what the place requires first. It listened. It stayed through a rising tide, a falling one and the slack between, then through a wet season and a dry one. By the end it could hear the gap without having been told.

It could also hear what the gap was for, so it went back and looked at the thing overhead. That was a petrel, two summers old and unpaired, hatched on a stack a long way up the coast. On two nights it had flown circles in the fog above the rim, over ground that smelled right and that sounded, in one narrow band, like birds. It had called down once each night and gone back to sea.

Then the power took stock of what it could do, which was nearly anything.

It could take the note out of the basin in an afternoon, thousands of half-full pipes with a handful of grit each. It went as far as choosing the grit. Then it thought about the next rising tide, and the woman walking inward with her mouth shut, humming, and nothing anywhere throbbing back. She would know by the second cairn that something had been through her field. Eleven years of two on a good tide would become, between one step and the next, work that had not been needed. She broke one pillar once and has not broken another. The power took that as instruction.

It could put the peat back. It did not think about this long. The lake was not a wrong done to anyone, and the pipes were bored by the flat's own water, leaving. There is nothing here to undo.

It could turn birds. On any summer night there are petrels in their thousands on the water beyond the cliff, and a young one is not hard to turn. But a steered bird has not chosen, and a colony is nothing but choosing, the same hole found again each year by smell in the dark. She would count thirteen and believe the gap had done it. That would be false, and she is not someone to be handed a false thing.

It could show itself. It decided against this more firmly than the rest. A person who knows she is watched by something kind begins to work differently. She looks up, she waits, and stopping pipes becomes a way of asking. She does not make much of things, and the power concluded that it should not be made much of.

It left her hearing alone. With the top back she could not stand the centre, and the warm pipes are near the centre. About the middle it was less sure. She has not asked, and it cannot ask. It marked the place, and means to think again if the middle starts to go before the work is past needing her.

That left very little, which seemed right. What she has made here she made by taking away. The power did two things.

The warm pipes were due to go cold in about four winters. They will take longer. Rock that thick cools unevenly, and no one could show this was not its own doing. She will go on finding them by their flat notes, the fog will go on thinning over them, and on cold days her fingers will bend.

And it stayed, for her feet. She is sixty and walks alone on wet glass in bad light, and if she went down in the field no one beyond the rim would hear it. Some morning a boot will go. The power means to be the reason the shelf is under her hand in time. She will take a new cut across the old ones, say something to the pillar, and go on in and stop her two.

The work it did not touch. It went along the plugs one by one, out to where she had worked longest, and the clay was sound in all of them. It has been in the room once, on a slack, to hear what she hears there. It did not sit on the slab.

It did see one thing she may not have. The gap does not have to beat the rain forever. The old flat was never quiet either. It had surf on one side and wind across it, and tens of thousands of burrows carried over both. Past a few hundred pairs a colony is its own noise and its own smell and needs no slat out of any fence. Her arithmetic only has to hold along one stretch of rim for some years. The power could find no decent way to tell her this. She will get it by counting, the way she gets everything.

It keeps the evenings. When the wind is steady it is at the rim, a little off from where she stands, and it hears the soft places open in the groan as the birds drop. When she begins to hum it takes care to have no note at all. Anything of its own in the air would beat against hers, and she would feel it in her jaw and might count it.

Late the next summer, on a night with no moon, the young petrel came in under the fog a third time. The woman was in her bed beyond the rim. The bird circled and called. Then it came down, badly, as they do on land, onto a fold of lava two pillars from a pipe with a bird in it. It shuffled to the lip of a dry bore, put its head in, and kept it there for some time. It was gone to sea before the dark thinned.

The power had not turned it. It went back over the night to be sure.

There was a pool in the fold, the size of two hands, with the orange film on it.

She came along the rim the next morning with the clay against her stomach. She saw the film before she reached it, because nothing else in the basin is that colour, and it was wrinkled, and she had not been that way in a week. She got down on the hide of her knees. There were three toes with the web between, small as a thumbprint, printed twice, and a smear where the breast had dragged through.

She did not touch it. It would keep for most of a week.

She looked at it for about as long as the limpets take. Then she got up and went in on the rising tide, side-on with her elbows close, humming.

That tide she stopped three.
