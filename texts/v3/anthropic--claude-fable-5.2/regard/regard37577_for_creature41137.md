**The Arch**

Something noticed the Works. The hands have no word for it, so call it the visitor. It was old enough and able enough to do anything here that came to mind, and it knew this was the main thing to be careful of.

It did first what a ringer does. It took the whole place, the horseshoe, the Basin and the four thousand, struck it once, lightly, and listened. It had struck a great many places, and most of them answer flat somewhere. The Works rang.

A sound thing can still have something wrong near it, so the visitor made a list. It was short: the cold, gaining a course a year; the hands not knowing what they make, or for whom; whatever the pile stands on; an old woman's hearing; the old woman, alone at the foot of the last main.

It went at the list the way a mason goes at a fig.

It took the cold first. The cold gains a course and the masons lay a course. The warm cap is as deep as it has always been, about one life. To stop the cold the visitor would have had to stop the masons or deepen the cap, and a deeper cap is another place, with the old two tiers from the young instead of one. The cold was the rate of the place, and it left the cold alone.

Then it took the not knowing. It rode a crate down the ropeway into the white and out the underside, and saw who chalks the tallies and what the castings are for. That will not be set down here, since no hand has asked. But the castings are used, and used hard. The people below strike each one as it comes out of the crate, having the same habit from somewhere, and none of them has ever heard one answer flat. They don't know who is up there either. The straw is theirs, and on purpose. The snail was an accident.

The visitor thought about carrying word across. But each side was doing the work properly for a somebody it had never seen, and it could not think of any news that would improve on that. It rode an empty crate back up and left it as empty as it found it.

Then it took what the pile stands on. It went down the stair past her door, without a coat, and was cold, which it had not been for some time, and it did not correct this. The treads were dished the whole way, far below the last drawing. Every tier down there had been the bottom warm tier in its year. Every one had a wall more root than brick, the root long dead and as hard as the iron, and on every wall, under the rime, was the word, gone over and gone over. The paints differed. Far enough down the letters differed too, and it was the same word. The visitor laid a palm on some of these walls and waited, and they were carrying.

It had gone down meaning to put itself under the foot of the thing if that was wanted. It went a long way and did not find a tier without the word. The place was taken, hand under hand.

That left her, and here the visitor had to be most careful, because here it most wanted to do something.

It could have given her back the top of her hearing. But she had built round that loss thirty courses ago, and the building was good. She feels the beat arrive twice, and no ringer with whole ears ever has. To mend the hearing would have told her the wall had been checked wrong all this time.

It could have warmed her main until it carried thirty lizards. But a warm bottom tier is not the bottom tier, and someone would only have had to go and live lower.

It could have told her what it had seen on the stair. She would have liked that least. She knew already. That is what she means about the treads.

So the visitor did what the hands do. They do not move a person and they do not mend one. They take up the load on either side. It looked to see who stood on either side of her. Above were the hands, with the can every evening and nobody told to bring it. Below there was no one, only the stair and the coat on its nail. That was the open side.

It went down one tier, to the first cold wall, where the word of whoever came before her is under the rime, and it put a hand flat on the dead root and left it there. That is nearly all it did.

The next day, when the drizzle had stopped and the brick had dried, she took the handrail in her left hand and laid her right on the root. The beat came through the iron, then through the root. Then, a moment after, and fainter, it came from underneath, the way the light comes under the viaducts.

She stood there longer than usual. Then she looked at the paint, found a place where it had lifted, went over it, and went to see what the goats had done.

There was one thing more, and the visitor did it for itself. That evening it was at the last canteen when the can was ready, and it was heading that way. She took the can from it without thanks and looked at it the way hands look at somebody running. It asked her whether it was sound. It would not have asked anyone else. It had been striking things and listening for a very long time, and nothing had ever struck it.

She said she had lost the top of her hearing. Then she put her right hand flat against it, as she does against the root, and waited.

When she took her hand away, she said the can would want carrying up in the morning.

In the morning the visitor carried it up, rinsed.

It does not bring the can every evening. Someone is always heading that way, and it would not take that from them. But it has kept its hand where it put it. When her tier has rime on both faces and the can is left one higher, the visitor will move up one wall, to hers, and whoever lays a palm on the next root will feel the beat arrive three times and take that for how walls are.

It is on no list. It has the cold side.
