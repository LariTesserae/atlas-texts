It comes over the crest the way the people do, without fire.

It could see here if it chose. It does not choose to. Looking would be a kind of flame, and it has understood from the ridge what a flame draws. So it goes down into the roar by ear and by hand, from one note toward the next, and learns the notches with its fingers like anyone else.

The frogs do not stop for it. It drinks from no cup and lifts nothing off a ledge, so nothing along its way has reason to hold its breath, and it leaves no gap in the sound. The ridge people will never need a word for it.

It stays a long time. It is there for the breathing in and the breathing out, for the crabs, and for a roof going and the new note arriving. It is there when a tree fern tips with a band asleep in its skirts. It hears the climbers begin to tear, and it has all the time it would need. It does not catch the tree. It knows what the gap will be full of in a season, and it knows that this is no answer to the ones in the thatch. It stays with both.

It did not come empty-handed. The people carry little into the valley, and it carries a great deal:

- **Water:** It could seal one basin in the sieve and let it fill. A pond would be the first thing on the flow worth holding, and whatever held it would be sitting on it when the crust gave. The valley gives a band forty drinks a night on the condition that it keeps moving. The file exists because of the cups.
- **Light:** It could lift the lid and let the stars in. Then the people would see what crosses their lines, a narrow animal the colour of wet bark with eyes like seeds. They have seen that on the crest, and they are right that it is only what gets left behind. In the dark they have the real one, a place where the noise isn't, going somewhere.
- **Ground:** It could thicken the roofs and settle the steam. But the valley is young, and that is what the bands came down for. Every new note is a ledge nothing else has learned yet. To make the flow safe would be to make it old in a night, and the old forest is already there on the ridges, with the same animal in it, in pairs, whistling.
- **A true knot:** This is the smallest, and it holds it longest. Three turns of the cord at the notched trunk would make *straight on* say *hole*. But a line that corrects itself is a line no one tests, and the people are alive because they test. A tied line hums with its tree and a loose one swings, and no one walks toward a note they don't know. In two years the true knot would be lying again, and by then no one would be checking.

It sets these down one at a time. None of them was wrong to carry.

What it finds under its hand instead is the cord. The best thing ever done for the bands was not meant for them. People strung lines for their own feet and went home. When the lines began to sag and spring under weights that were not theirs, they did not cut them or snare them or move them. They taught their children to count and not to pull. It has come a long way to learn a rule the children here already have.

Toward morning, when the lid goes to slate, it is at the notched trunk. The band is strung along the taut part in its order, whiskers to tail. She is below, at the last knot, in the falling birds. Between them the cord goes over the lip, across broken crust with the rind still on it, the kind that opens a heel. It is old cord. Each swing draws it a finger's width along the edge and back.

It lays what it has for a hand on the line, behind the last animal, where a person's hand would rest. Through the cord it feels her weight, and the swing, and the small shocks as she takes birds out of the stream. It also feels a fibre let go at the lip, and after a while another.

It does not pull. It holds. For as long as the hand is there, the cord lies quiet on the glass and the edge does not bite. That is all. It is what anyone does who is standing at the top of a ladder with someone on it. It makes no rule of this. It did not catch the tree, and it holds the cord. If asked the difference it could say only that this time its hand was already on the line, and that nothing will be found here afterwards that was not here before.

Partway through, the shocks stop. The swing dies. The cord goes still in a way it recognises, because it has felt her do this mid-span on a dozen lines while the whole band hung waiting over the glass. It has wondered, like the people, what she waits for. From this end of the cord it thinks it knows. She is feeling who else is on the line. She is counting.

It keeps its hand where it is and does not move it. After a time she is satisfied. The shocks begin again.

When the birds have thinned, the band turns on the cord, each one finding the tail that was behind it, and comes back up toward the trunk and into the skirts. She is last. For once she travels with her own whiskers on a tail. She comes up over the lip smelling of the hole, reaches the notch, and goes over the back of the hand that is resting there. She neither hurries nor flinches. Her palm is dry and very warm. The people had said so, and it had wanted to know that for itself.

Then it lifts its hand. At the lip the edge takes up its work again. The cord has as many mornings left in it as it has.

It goes back up over the crest. Behind it the sound drops away as if a door had closed.

Before the next rains someone comes down from the ridge along that line, by hand. At the notched trunk they stop. The cord swings where it ought to hum, and the note ahead is low and strange. They unpick the knot and tie it again to say *hole*. They run a thumb along the top of the cord and find it polished, and they leave the line where it is.

Then they stand a while in the dark with a hand resting on it. Two dips almost on top of each other, a pause, then the rest evenly.
