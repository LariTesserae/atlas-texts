Something has noticed the plateau.

It has no body, and for a long while it takes none. It comes in at the outer rim, over raw rock, to where the oldest grinders are rasping glass under the thin end of the cloud. From there it goes at the pace of the place, a body-length a day. It stays for one turn of the wheel. Eleven years is not long to it, but it is the least time in which everything here happens once.

It could do nearly anything, and it goes through the list.

It could make room. No grinder has ever turned around when it wanted to. But room is where the wind gets in, and the green has rarely felt wind, and every animal taken out of the press takes its breath out of the cloud. The attention spends its nights inside a ring around a kiln, where rime grows on every edge and small arcs stitch flank to flank. Afterward it no longer thinks of room as something the grinders are short of.

It could make quiet. It wants to, because the roar is hard even on something with no ears. Then it notices whose want that is. Nothing on the plateau has heard silence, and nothing is listening for it. The calves are dropped into the roar in the same hour they are dropped into the steam.

It could take the flat feeders off the flanks. It looks at this longer than at anything else. The lichen needs eleven years to crust over, and the feeders are one of the reasons it gets them. A herd with nothing feeding on it would come back around to bare glass. The attention does not like this answer. It keeps it.

Then the pond.

There is one animal in the pond that it has come to know from the others. It is as long as the top joint of a thumb, with a bristle gone from the left row. Each dusk it rises under the freezing skin and pushes what bristles it has through.

The attention could deepen the basin so the water stayed. Water that stays is behind the trailing front within a season, with no cloud over it. It could stop the wheel, and then nothing would ever come back around to moss as deep as a mattress.

It could lift them. This is the nearest it comes. Lifting is easy, and they ask every evening. But there is nowhere to set one down. No horn grows a feather that would cover a body that size, and the air a hand above the ice is thirty degrees colder than anything the animal has known.

So it watches one whole night instead, closely, and sees what the bristles do now. Fog freezes along them. The freezing gives up its warmth, and the warmth goes down the glass into the body hanging underneath. In the morning the white fur drips into the water the animal lives in. They ask for what the small ones are given. What comes to them is something else, and it is theirs. The attention leaves the asking alone.

It could guard them. It works out how the pond might be lost: one night of wind, the cloud torn off, the lid going down to the floor. Then it works out the rest. The pond was not luck. It took a low place and a night under ice, and the teeth make the low place lower every turn. What is lost here will come again.

That is no comfort to the ones under the lid, and it does not pretend otherwise. But a hand held over them tonight would have to be held over them every night, and after enough nights they would be its animals and no longer the plateau's. Nothing here is made. It decides that nothing here will be kept, either.

It can see further than one turn. The fissures at the hub will stop breathing. Water will settle at last where to run, the ring will breach at its lowest scarp, and there will be a current. In the pond the big ones already row every day in one direction, because the water they live in is always leaving. No one arranged that. The attention cannot find a way to improve on it.

One thing is left. What the big ones do under the ice in place of dividing has not been seen. It could see, since ice is nothing to it. It does not look. An animal of this plateau has, for the first time, a night to itself and somewhere to spend it. That is most of what the pond has given them. To look would be to take it back.

So it does very little, and does it the way things are done here.

On its last evening it goes out ahead of the leading front, to column heads no horn has reached, and takes a shape at last. The shape is not much: a height, an edge, a point. It lets the charge crowd onto the point. As the air dries, the beads begin to arrive from the cups behind it. Each lands with a tick too small to hear, and it hears each one. It holds still. All night, breath that is not its own freezes along hairs it did not grow, and at the root of every feather a bead rides out the cold just under freezing. For one night it is a windward surface.

In the morning the sun reaches the beads through the ice. The feathers let go at the root and fall the height of a horn, and the drops run to the nearest cups.

It does one more thing. It has made itself enough of a body to breathe, and it breathes out, once, into the underside of the cloud. All the plateau's water has come out of lungs, and this is a cupful more. It will be rime by the next dawn and rain by mid-morning, and after that it will go around with the rest. In a few days the front will reach the place where it stood, and the grinders will eat the lichen there and take the top grain of rock with it.

It leaves across the herd. The next dawn finds it over the pond, with the lid still on. A dark shape hangs under a patch of white fur, and there is a gap in the fur where one bristle is missing. The shape faces the way the wheel turns. The attention looks down at it through the ice, from the height of a grinder's back, and comes no closer.
