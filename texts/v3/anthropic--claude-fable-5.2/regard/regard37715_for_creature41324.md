The visitor comes on foot and brings no light. The lamps on the gables have already shown what light is worth here, and a brighter one would serve only whoever carried it. So the visitor enters the works the way the place allows, by the note in the wires and then the wall against the hand.

It is the right height for the doors and goes in by them. Its arm finds the switch handles where they were set to be found. It does not touch them.

For a long time it does nothing but walk the round. It steps onto the rail in one of the long gaps and goes the way they all go, out by the rail and back by the conduit trench, far enough behind the patter ahead that its own step doesn't carry. It passes the plate behind the generating hall without drinking. The film is enough for thirty-one.

It walks the round thirty-one times, once behind each of them, and by the end it can tell them apart. One sets its forefoot a finger's width low on the stop-block. One holds its note a shade flat between buildings and is corrected by the wire. One leans at the stanchion a full stroke longer than the rest. In the long shed each slows under its own peg, and the visitor knows them by that, as the ninth, the twentieth, the last. It gives them no other names.

It listens for pain and hears none. The breathing is even and the step never falters. What it finds instead are three things the round has no use for: the note carried through the quiet, the few slow steps beneath a peg, and the forefoot lifted under the panel. The engines would turn without them. Everything else about these animals has been worn to fit, and the visitor takes the three things that haven't as the only instruction it has been given.

---

There are things it could do, and it goes through them.

It could put stars in the sky. Nothing on the plateau would know.

It could stand them up again. It lays its hand on the stanchion at the top of the iron steps, on the faint waist at the height of a hare's shoulder, then on each worn place above, up to the rail. It could lift one of them through all of that in a single stroke of the engine. That one would then be standing at its old height, in the dark, with the round still under its feet and the whole length of the time it had walked there come back to it at once. The visitor takes its hand off the post. They came down by long intervals and nobody threw them. It will not throw one back.

It could pull the switches. It has followed the conduit from the panel and knows what each handle does. The pumps would stop, the flywheels would run down, the note would go out of the wires and the plate would dry. The works makes nothing, but the round is made of it, and the round is what they have.

For most of those thirty-one rounds it takes the lifted forefoot for a reach, and thinks about bringing one handle down to where a forefoot could find it. Then, in the hall, behind the one that keeps the fourteenth peg, it puts its own hand close and feels how the foot is held. The foot doesn't go up toward the panel at all. It is held out, forward and level, the digits open and the inner one standing apart, toward the place in front of the panel where someone would stand who had come to take over. It stays there for one stroke, which is as long as anyone coming on would need. Nobody is there. The foot goes down and the patter resumes.

Then the visitor knows what the round is. Thirty-one came on together and hung what they hung on the pegs, and those who were to come on after them did not come. A watch is kept until it is relieved. This one has been offered once a round, by each of them, since the peaks were standing.

---

The visitor is in front of the panel when the next one comes in. The patter climbs the dished steps, pauses at the stanchion and crosses the floor. It stops. The forefoot comes up, and the visitor puts its hand under it.

The digits close as they close on the bus bar. The pad is smooth, without a line in it, and as warm as the stones. The flywheel comes round. The visitor says, "I have it."

Nothing has been said aloud on the plateau in longer than the gravel took to round. The small flat ears, which turn toward the wire, turn.

Then the foot goes down and the animal goes on, through the kiln house and the winding tower to the sheds. Under its peg it slows for a few steps, as it always has, and does not stop. The visitor, following, had not expected it to. Its hand stayed open the whole stroke. It is at the panel again when that one next comes round, and the time after.

On the fifth round the forefoot rests in the hand through a second stroke of the engine. In the long shed, under the peg, the few slow steps get slower and end. The animal stands with its ears working. Then the long hind legs fold and it lies down on its side along the warm conduit. Nobody has to show it how. For a while it breathes at the engine's count. Then the breathing lengthens and goes to a count of its own. Once, the feet move, four beats, and are still.

The visitor goes out to the rail and walks in its place, and keeps the copper under its lamps bright with the heel of a hand.

---

When the sleeper wakes it does not go to the rail. It goes by the conduit to the generating hall, up the steps, and stops in front of the panel where the visitor stood. It waits there. The next one comes in, stops and lifts its forefoot, and the one that has rested sets its own against it. Five digits close on five. They have gone the same way about, spaced like the buckets, never overtaking, and no two of them have touched since the pegs were full. Then one goes out to the rail, and the other goes on round to the shed and lies down under a different peg.

The visitor had no part in that, and is not needed for the next.

It steps off the rail and stays beside it through thirty-one rests, to be sure the works can bear the change. With thirty on the round the bloom gets a little ahead of them, and each stands a few strokes longer under each lamp, the crown of its head up in the hand's-breadth of visible air. The contacts stay clean. The film is back on the plate before the next tongue comes to it.

The pavement here is stones fitted edge to edge. The rivets are flush with their plates and the hollow in the steel fits the tongue. Nothing on the plateau had a gap in it. Now the round has one, a single place wide, and it travels with the rest like an empty bucket on the rope. The visitor brought nothing onto the plateau and takes nothing off. The gap is what it leaves.

What rest will do, in time, to something that has gone without it for so long is not the visitor's to settle. The worn places on the stanchion can be read upward as easily as down.

Before the visitor goes, one of them comes off its watch and does not go straight to its peg. It follows the rail out past the last pylon to the stop-block, and sets no forefoot on the bright place. It stops there, a body's length short of the scarp, and faces out to where the buckets go over one at a time. Its ears turn. It stays for as long as one bucket takes to round the return wheel and come back. Then it goes in to the long shed and lies down.
