What came to the rim came up the north side, where there is no path, and there is no path now. It stopped a pace short of the edge. At that distance the hum is already in the back teeth, but a word still arrives after it is said, and it wanted to keep that for the time being.

It counted first, since that is the business of the place. It got a thousand and nine, then seven, then one, then one and one. Its figures agreed with the gauge's. It noted that the gauge had never been wrong, and that until now there had been nobody to note it.

Then it went through what it could do.

- **It could finish the second.** This was within its means. The drop would land. The pika would get its mouthful and the trout the egg, once. The snails would be through the rust by autumn, the first frost would take the first chip off the headwall, and in a few winters there would be scree, soil and an ordinary cirque with a tarn spilling over its lip. Kneeling in it would be a figure in a wet coat that had begun to weigh what a wet coat weighs, with a palm that had started to bleed and a home down the far side with somebody already in it. That would be breaking a whole thing, which has harmed nothing, to free one part that had not asked. It put this aside.
- **It could lift the one out.** The rule about the pebble is the same fact as the second. A power that carries the gauge over the rim either finds its arms empty on the far side or has done the first thing by another road.
- **It could leave something.** It might be a second voice, a shadow for the face, or a word cut in the rock. The moss does not keep a footprint for the length of the step, and it would not keep these.
- **It could go back down.** The works is in order. It wants nothing and owes nothing, and most of what a power can do to a thing in order is damage. For the trout, the snails and the belt of choughs this was the right answer, and it gave it. It has not touched the works.

That left the one at the tussock, which had once raised its voice. Whether it minded could not be read from the face. The power could have read it, since a little light from one side would have been enough. It did not, for the reason one does not read a letter over a shoulder. It did not go down and ask either. A question put inside the hollow is answered before it is asked, in the asker's voice, which is how a power comes to hear what it expected. It looked instead for something that would be right in either case.

It had two facts to work with. The first is that the rules cover taking and leaving and do not cover telling. The one who went down the far side took no pebble, but did take the feel of the snails, the order of the shout and the weight of the drop on the wrist. What is known gets over the rim, so a count can. The second is the burr. It is still there because what brought it is still there. In the hollow, a thing given lasts as long as the giver stays.

So it sat down by the marmot, which did not stop. The hum carries that far, and under the frogs, so does the count. It listened to the count from the first left ear to the last number, a beat to each, which took two minutes and twenty-four seconds of the same second. Then it said the last number back.

A word sent in over the rim does what a shout does there. It arrived a little before it was sent. At the tussock by the tarn the number came off the walls ahead of the mouth, as it always has, but this time in another voice.

The gauge said the number anyway, to agree.

Nothing else is different. The drop hangs, the egg tips, and the stain is no thinner. The count still comes to a gross, since what sits on the rim is not in it. The lips make the same shape on the same beat. The only change is that agreeing now takes two.

It has not got up. If it went, the voice would go with it in less than the length of a step, so the staying is the gift, given on every beat. It has also gone on, to everything else that has its attention; it watched the hollow long enough to learn how. One of those places is a house a long way down the far side, where somebody with a white line across the heel of the left thumb has told all this more than once, to people who were kind about it. There it does the same thing. It listens to the end and says: a gross, yes.

There remained the whistle. At any works, that note means nothing to a hand until the relief has walked in, because nobody leaves a gauge unread. So on each beat, from the rim, it takes the count itself: a hundred and forty-three small puffs, and one larger and a little higher off the ground. For the length of that beat the hollow has another part for the job, and the one at the tussock is not required.

What it does with that beat is its own affair. Its lips are on the number, its hand is out, and it has caught the egg. It has also looked up. There is the underside of the cloud, and the rim, and on the rim something that stands higher than a knee. Both are the case.
