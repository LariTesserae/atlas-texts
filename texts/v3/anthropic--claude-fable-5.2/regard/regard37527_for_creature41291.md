It comes in by the causeway. A power could arrive at every door at once, and the plateau would permit it, the way it permits scree with no cliff. But the rule about doors is the only law the city has, and a guest keeps the law of the house. So it walks from dawn to dusk on the left, the right-hand track as empty beside it as it was found.

On the way it works out what it has been doing wrong.

It has been looking, for a long time and from far off, with what it took for tact. But the first rule covers every direction, including the direction of a turned head, and makes no exception for a head that is large or means well. Attention is a presence. Wherever its regard has rested, no one could be, which is why it has only ever had evidence: warm hearths, a moved mallet, plants that turn out to be plants. It should have recognised the arrangement, being used to it from the other side, as something generally inferred from its fences. And the place it has watched longest, and most tenderly, is a house by an unfinished square with a laid fire and a cup on the sill.

It cannot test this. Testing would be looking.

So it goes through what it could do, and declines most of it.

- **The first rule:** it could be lifted, and everyone stood in one square by evening. But the second rule goes further down. Nothing here is met for the first time. They would arrive already long acquainted, mallet and all, and what the squares ask for was never a crowd. It is one upright that stays what it is.
- **The kerb:** the frost has it in hand.
- **The mallet:** it has no opinion and sees that it must not acquire one. Left of the door, right of the door: that is two people writing to each other with the only pen in the house.
- **The cold hearth:** it is laid for a particular hand.
- **The question:** gone or yet to come. It could know. Either answer has an edge on it, and nothing here was made to take an edge. They have built a whole courtesy on the not-knowing, and they have not asked.

What is left is small.

It is a guest. At dusk it comes to a fire banked that morning by someone now a day away. It burns three bricks. It drinks fog from the cup, and its lip finds the thinned place without being told. In the morning it cuts peat, which rounds its corners on the way to the stack, and stacks it cut face inward because that is how the stack was found. It banks the fire. It takes up the half-twisted cord and lays in the second strand. Its knots, it is interested to find, are neither neat nor impatient. They hold just as well, and are frayed at the pins by the time it straightens up. It starts a new length, twists half, and leaves it on the sill.

At a square it stops at the kerb, on the stone worn smooth for stopping, and looks across fifty paces at the upright. It does not cross.

In mid-afternoon the fog comes up to the height of a roof. It could see through, and it lets the fog be fog. Somewhere along the fence a note is being held, a little to the right. It does not answer. A voice from the right is exactly what is waited for, and it is not that. Instead it finds the tone the wind draws from the cords and gets behind it, dead on, adding nothing but weight. Every held note on the plateau leans on that tone and now has more to lean on. The beat deepens. Each of them hears their own *here, still here* come back with something under it.

It does this for one fog. Nothing on the plateau can be given for the first time, so once is enough. By evening the cords have always sounded like that.

Dusk brings it to the last square. It stands at the unfinished part of the kerb, among the loose stones, and does not go in to the house. A threshold dished on the right should take its first foot from the foot it was worn for. The groove in the paving is filling with the last of the fog.

The taking up is never seen. That is not a secret the plateau keeps. It is a condition, like the cold. So the power gives the one thing a power finds hard to give, an hour that nobody is watching. It turns its head, and a turned head is a day away.

It will learn the rest the way anything is learned here. Some net, some day, may have a third kind of knot in it, or a mallet may lie where neither of the two would put it. Or there will be no such sign, and the fires will be banked regardless.

Behind it a slate lid ticks. They do that as they warm, and it is dusk. It keeps its head where it is.
