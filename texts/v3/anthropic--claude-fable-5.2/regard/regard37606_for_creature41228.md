It arrives in the dark, before the first tick, so as to hear the day from its beginning.

It does not come down the ramp. It is simply in the lanes, the way the warmth is, and it disturbs the dust no more than the warmth does. The dust is the record here, and it has no wish to be written into it.

Then it does what it would do first anywhere, which is nothing, for one whole day. It hears the bricks take up the heat. It hears the first pod go off down a lane, and the roof groan at noon. When the wind finds the funnels it is in the longest corridor, inside the lowest note. It spends the rest of the afternoon in the eastern quarter, where the door-slab knocks twice, pauses, and knocks twice. It knows what the knocking is. It stays for all of it anyway.

It watches a shoot reach a roof-hole, spread its pad, and take the whole rod of light, so that a room no one needs lit goes dark. It finds that it likes the vine. It likes the thirty bare paces and the single tuft, and the greed that is paid for within the week and then tried again at the next hole.

Toward evening it goes through what it could give.

Water is the first thing anyone thinks of for a dry place. But the walls are mud that has never been wet, and the vine has spent its whole life sealing itself against a wetness that never came. Rain here would be a catastrophe.

It could bring creatures, a beetle for the fallen pads or something small and winged for the seeds. Each would be company, and each would be a mouth. The vine has never had to grow a thorn.

It could bring people. The cord on the peg is still good, the adze is tight in its binding, and the fire is lit. But the people left their things in order, which is how people leave when leaving is a decision. They would also find that their lintels are alive, and they would need the doorways. It does not send for them. It does not hide the ramp either.

It could put out the flame and let the vine have the round room. But the flame is burning off what would otherwise pool under the roof and wait for a spark. And the vine found that limit for itself and keeps it. The limit is working.

It does not open the jars. It knows what is in them. That is between the jars and whoever stamped the plugs.

So it asks the plainer question, whether anything here is wrong. It looks for the answer in the vine's shape, since the vine has no other way to say what it wants. Pitch and light are the two ends of every stem, and the vine has both.

Then it looks at the pods. They are long and ridged and hard as fired clay, and they split with a crack that throws the seed. That is a body built for distance. Under the roof, distance is the opposite wall. Every trough in the town already has roots in it, so every seed is thrown into a full house, skitters, and lies where it stops. This is the one thing the vine keeps trying to do and cannot.

It also looks at the roof, which no one plasters now. It can see how many summers the roof has left. The number is large, but it is a number. When the roof goes, noon will come in whole, and noon cooks a pad brittle in days.

So it does one thing. The next morning, when the pods begin, it takes a seed out of the air at the moment the pod splits, before the seed has touched the floor. The dust records nothing.

Half a day's walk to the west, for anyone with legs, the pan has split along an old crack. The same rock leaks the same pitch into the bottom of the cleft. It is dusk down there at every hour but one, and at noon a blade of light goes down the wall. Nothing lives in it.

It sets the seed in the pitch, the way the roots lie in the troughs, like a finger in a bowl. It does not make the seed grow. It has done what a carrier would have done, once, and chosen the place better than a carrier would. The rest is the seed's business.

It is back in the town by the time the wind drops. The corridors go quiet one by one, the longest last. The flame sinks to a hand's height. The pitch stops in the troughs wherever it happens to be, and tightens, and holds the roots until tomorrow's noon.

Then it goes. The dust in the lanes holds seed-pocks and the drag-lines of fallen pads, and no other mark. To the west, in a crack in the pan, one seed lies in pitch.
