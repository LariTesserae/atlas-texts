It would come in without light. Light is the one thing in here that everything either runs from or has stopped needing, and a wise thing would know that before the first skylight. It wouldn't bring quiet either. The noise is the birds finding the walls and the chicks being found. So it comes the way the damp comes, taking up none of the dark air, and goes down the gallery as the eye does, from one pale column to the next, and then past the last.

It looks for what is wrong, because that is what powers are asked for.

It stops longest at the seedlings. It could open the roof over them. But a skylight is a roof coming down on full ledges, and what a skylight grows, by the evidence, is a few ferns and one bent shrub. The crops that spat these seeds spat others downslope, onto ground with a sky over it. The white lawn is what the forest pays for being carried. It lets the lawn be.

It watches a chick go over the edge of its drum, and the beetles come. It watches the snake's weir. It doesn't call these nothing. But they happen at the forest's own rates, no worse, and the tube is not a wound. It is a digestion. There is nowhere to put a hand that isn't already doing work.

It reads the soot fan, coat by coat, and looks at the pole, and here it recognizes something: creatures who take what they can reach and leave what is above it. It could make that a law in them. It doesn't. A law can be argued with, and cane can't. The pole stays against the bench where they will look for it.

Then comes the pinch, and a different temptation, which is not to mend but to finish. The power could have the pale animal standing on the lobe by morning, long-legged, sparing, its eyes closed over. The tube would finally have someone.

It declines, for two reasons. A cricket made that way would be one more borrowed thing, on loan from farther off than the forest. Belonging is what a place does to an animal at the place's own speed, and it can't be delivered.

The second reason matters more. The pale animal isn't anyone. No one is waiting to be it. The one who is here is brown, sighted, a little thin, and every account of it so far has looked through it like a doorway toward its descendants. Benevolence can only land on who is there.

So the power attends to the cricket, as a cricket and not as an ancestor. It asks what this one lacks. The cricket doesn't need carrying back. It walked here, the whip spiders are upstream, and "lost" is a word used by those who think they know where a thing ought to be. It doesn't need anything done to its eyes or its legs. It is hungry.

Nothing originates in the tube. Everything good in it arrives from somewhere else, on wings, and falls. That is the grain of the place, and the power works in it.

Some evenings a moth comes in under the last skylight and, having no moon to steer by, keeps on down the gallery until it is spent. One has come in tonight. It was always going to drop somewhere on the bare, ropy stone, most likely where nothing would find it. The power's whole act is the last forty paces. The moth comes down on the wrinkled skin of the lobe, an antenna's length from the cricket, and stops moving.

The cricket touches it with one feeler, then the other. Then it eats for a long time.

That is all the power does. It can't be told from what happens anyway, which is the test the power set itself. One meal doesn't make an ancestor. This is still, most likely, a cousin of one, and the dark beyond the pinch will decide that without help. The meal was not for the lineage. It was for the animal that was hungry.

The power stays a while in the still air with the screaming at its back, keeping company the cricket can't perceive and doesn't need. Then it goes up the gallery and out through a skylight with the dusk pour of birds. It is the only visitor that carries nothing away.

What it leaves on the lobe is two wings, the brown of bark. The people will never come this far, and the wings will mean nothing to anyone. But the power left them the way the pole is left, and it will be back, soon, as rock counts time.

On the lobe the cricket, a little less thin, lifts one antenna and then the other, and goes on tasting the air beyond.
