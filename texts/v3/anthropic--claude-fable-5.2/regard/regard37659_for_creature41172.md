What comes is large, and knows it.

It arrives at the roof edge, where anything arrives that comes from outside, and stands among the sheep a little way from the crow. From here it could have the number in a glance. It has never been dazzled either, and nothing below would have a few breaths to be a wren in a fern. It looks at the crow instead, since the crow is the one creature here that was caught up without being asked. The crow regards it with one eye, then the other, and turns back to the court. Behind them the sheep can be heard tearing grass, and the sea is loud. The crow has the whole sky for a door and has not used it. That is an answer.

So it goes round and finds a passage and comes in the way people do, bent under the turf, in hide shoes, behind a woman carrying a cup. At the threshold the light comes off the wall and it stops with a hand over its eyes. The woman lowers her hand after a few breaths. It keeps its own up a little longer than eyes require. When it looks, there are wrens in the ferns, a dozen or more.

It sits on the bench with its face lit and goes through what it has brought:

- **Fresh water for the shell.** The drop has gone green. But the drop is not there to be drunk. The basin serves for that, at the brightest hour. The drop is what someone gave, and it is kept the way the frost is kept. Fresh water would be a different gift from a different giver.

- **A second shell.** Then the first would be one of two.

- **The song.** A bird that can be heard over surf has been silent since before the first lime went on. A large thing looks at that and sees a wound, because large things see fear in most of what small things do. Nothing here is hiding. It works in the strongest light in the town. It keeps a house where a child can watch flint being worked and old people sit with their eyes closed, and the quiet is what the house is built of.

- **A wider reach.** The holding could be carried out over the whole town, so that the ivy's luck was everyone's. But the masons went round this place, and the town has been right about it ever since. People pass through too quickly to be held and come out the age they would have been anyway. It is those people, the ones who have turns that come round, who keep the lime.

Nothing in its hands fits. A large animal does not keep house in these measures, and a large gift is no better. So it does what people do in the court, which is to look at something in good light. It looks at the east wall.

The lime there is as thick as anywhere, soft-edged from every year's coat. A shell pressed into the first of those coats should be buried by now, a bump in the white. This one sits hollow side out with its rim clear. So every year, whoever's turn has come round works along the east wall with the brush, parts the fern, and goes round. Nobody was told to. In the court there would be no way to tell them, and in the passages nobody has. Each one finds it, understands enough, and lets the fern fall back.

The print in the moss is made every day by one foot. The rim of the shell is kept every year, by a different hand each time. The one who lives here has been known a little, once a year, for as long as there has been lime.

This is what it would have wanted to give: to be known a little, and provided for, and not looked at too hard. It is given already. What is left is to take a place in the line.

There is no place now. The turns belong to the town, and the town is full of people. But it can read a storm beach, and this one is moving, as they all do. There will be a winter when the sand comes over the turf, and after it a year when no smoke comes out of the field and nobody's turn comes round. The lime would go grey in a few seasons. A grey wall throws no light and doesn't sweat, and then the groove is dry and the basin fails. The one who lives here can hold frost to a line, but it cannot burn shell.

That year, and each year after, the turn will be its own. It will gather shell on the storm beach and burn it, and come down with a bucket and a brush, in hide shoes, by whatever way is left. It will take one day and lime back to the same white. At the east wall it will part the fern and go round.

It will stop at the threshold first, with a hand over its eyes.

Someone touches its shoulder. An old man with a skin over his arm wants the place on the bench where the light is best. It gets up and gives it to him, and goes out by the passage, under the bats.

On the roof edge the crow, which watched one more go in than it knew, watches one more come out. It is satisfied on that point, and goes back to the wrens.
