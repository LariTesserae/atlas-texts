What attends to the pump house does not come in by the door. It is in the hall the way the green light is, evenly and from above, and it takes its time. It reads the charts in the drawer, all of them, and finds them well kept. It reads the tell-tales. It reads the two stones in her head, ring by ring, which no one else will ever do, and at the end it knows each of her winters by its width.

It finds no one to blame. Out in the dark fields there are farmhouses whose bedroom floors lie lower than the surface of the high drain, and people sleep in them because the circle closes every week. The keeper greases the bearings in good faith. The lamps were left on so that nobody would fall down the well stairs. Everything that holds her here was done out of care for something else.

There are things it could do, and it turns them over.

It could lift her out and set her down in the sea, which would cost it nothing. But her kind are not delivered. They go, and between here and the salt lie a bank, a drain, a river and an estuary, each of which she is owed. The leaving would be the one act of her life that was wholly hers. It will not do that part for her.

It could stop the pump for good, or open the west wall. Then the lakebed would remember what it was, and the farmhouses would learn it.

It could leave everything as it is, and it considers this longest. She is the safest eel in the country. Nothing hunts her, and the worms come when it rains. Outside there are nets, and other pump houses with their own lines in the water, and five thousand kilometres to swim on an empty gut. At the end of it, if she arrives, she spawns and dies, and that part is the destination, not one of the risks. A power that only wished her comfortable would leave her under the stairs for another eighty rings.

But she has been asked every autumn, and every autumn her body has given the same answer as far as it was let. Nothing needs deciding on her behalf. The question has just never been put to her whole.

So it settles on the least thing. The entire building is one long act of doing, and what she needs is four hours of its not being done.

It does not make the night. It waits for one. This autumn, as in every other, the cold and the taste of leaves have started the change in her, and she has stopped feeding. Late in October the moon thins to nothing, and rain comes in from the west, the steady kind that wets grass to the root. The low drains begin to fill. A little after midnight, at a substation among the beet fields, a breaker opens. Engineers will examine it later, find nothing wrong, and write *transient*.

In the hall the lamps go out altogether. The gearing note falls away first, then the hum. The shaft turns a while on its own weight and stops. The fronds stop trembling and the ivy hangs straight. The webs hold still. On the floor the mesh of diamonds vanishes all at once, and the film lies flat. At the gland the thread of water, with nothing turning behind it, breaks into drops, perhaps a dozen, each of which can be heard to land. Then that stops too.

Under the stairs it is not quiet. The note that was her zero has been taken away, and what remains is everything else. She feels every stickleback in the sump at once. She feels the toad shift his weight on the bar, and the rain striking the high drain on the far side of a brick wall. Behind her the line in the water is gone. The pull she has kept a hand's breadth from for eighty years lets go, the tilted surface comes level, and then it starts up the gauge board, over the stain and onto brick that has been dry for longer than the charts go back.

It is dark and the level is rising. That is the rest of the instruction.

She turns round. The water has covered the bottom two steps, and she takes the others in the dished middle, where the feet went. At the top she finds the channels cut in the concrete and follows them against the creep of the film, the way she followed them down. They bring her to the foot of the west wall, to a joint with a hart's tongue growing in it, where the water coming through tastes of river. She puts her nose to it and cannot get her head in. She lies along the wall, where one more keeper might have taken her for hose.

The door is shut and fits its frame. The power could see to that itself. Instead, when the supply fails, a telephone rings in a house two miles off, as it was installed to do.

It knows the keeper only from the room. This is someone who has never scraped the moss from the empty beds or pulled a fern from the mortar, and who still wipes a maker's plate that no longer says anything. It judges that to be enough to go on.

The keeper comes along the bank in the rain with a torch and opens the door. The keeper has never before stood in the hall with bare ears. There is rain on the lantern glass, there is water moving somewhere inside the west wall, and there is the keeper's own breathing. The torch goes over the beds, the stopped motor, and the gauge board with the water standing well above its mark. Then it finds the length of hose at the foot of the wall, and the hose lifts its head. The eyes are too large for the animal the keeper thought lived in this country.

The keeper looks for as long as it takes to understand, then switches off the torch and steps back out onto the wet step, leaving the door wide.

Rain blows in over the threshold. It is water that has not yet been through the land, and she tastes that. The keeper sees nothing. There is a heavy, unhurried sliding on the concrete, and then a weight that crosses the toe of one boot and takes a long time to finish crossing.

The bank is steep and higher than a head. Her kind will climb anything wet, and tonight everything is. At the top she goes into the high drain without a sound the rain doesn't cover.

It is the first water she has been in without the hum. She hangs in it a moment, the wave travelling down her fin, while the pores along her side find a new nothing to measure from. Then, for the first time in her life, she points her head downstream.

The power goes with her as far as the outfall, where the doors open to the river on the ebb, and there it stops. It knows what lies between her and the place she is going, the nets and the weirs and the long, unfed dark. It leaves them where they are. She has been safe for eighty years, and safety was never what she was short of. In three weeks or so the seabed will drop away beneath her, the light will fail, and her eyes will have their work. No one has ever seen what her kind do at the end of that road. The power could look. It read her ear-stones without asking, and it decides to leave this alone.

Before five the breaker closes. The lamps come up yellow in their cages and the motor winds through its notes to the one it keeps. The fronds take up their trembling, the diamonds stand again on the floor, and the thread at the gland joins itself. By mid-morning the sump is back at its mark. The moss around the dry oval has noticed nothing.

At the end of the week the keeper changes the chart. The pen has drawn its circle, and at one place on it there is a spur outward, no wider than a thumbnail, and a return. The keeper writes the date beside the spur. It is the first date written in the building since the tell-tales. The chart goes into the drawer with the hundreds of others, the only one that could be picked out again.

Then the keeper goes down the well stairs with the torch and looks for some time at the slack place under them. After that the keeper crosses to the west wall and looks at the joint where the hart's tongue grows and the water comes through. There is cement in the store. The keeper leaves the joint as it is.

The toad is on his bar, as he is every week. He is facing the door.
