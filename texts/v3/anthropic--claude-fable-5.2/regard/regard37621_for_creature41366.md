It does not open the hatch.

The hatch is the obvious way in, but the tone is the shape of the room, and a door standing open changes the shape. So it comes the way everything came that could not climb. It lets itself be drawn through the louver on the blade of daylight, past the swifts, into the shaking air, and it lets the tone carry it to a shelf and set it down. It has no weight to speak of, and it takes care to have less.

It comes to rest on the fourth tier, in the bay beside the ladder, and there it does what she does. It rests on the place and waits to be told things. It stays a hundred and twenty of her days, which is one of ours.

**What it leaves**

It can do a great deal, so most of the work is finding out what not to do.

- **The leg:** It could be put back. But the sheet has been darned for years by a spider with seven, and every line in it lies where seven legs want it. An eighth would come home to a house built without it. And the gecko was hungry, and is also here.
- **The feet:** She has never seen the white flags at either end of her sheet. They are not addressed to her. To make her able to see them would be to hand her a different animal to be, and she is good at this one.
- **The pause:** It could hold her sheet quiet through the forty seconds. But the wet wind that bellies her sheet is the wind the young go up on and the males come down through, and the frogs have waited eleven minutes to say one thing. Her night is everyone else's road.
- **The rung:** Her corner line is tied where the hands used to go. It considers shifting the mooring a hand's width along, and finds that this cannot be done without touching the line, which is the one thing she asks of the world that nobody do. It leaves the line alone, though it is not certain this is right. The people whose hands polished that rung are also in its care, and it will not keep them from their ladder. It resolves to be at the hatch on the day anyone else is.

**What it finds**

The shelves are not quite still.

It takes most of the stay to be sure. At the bottom of the silk, under the calm, there is a murmur. It is steady, so she does not read it, and she has never stood on a sheet without it. Whoever hung there before her stood on a slightly fainter one. The plate below the sump has fallen a hair out of step with the plate overhead, so the two halves of the tone no longer cancel exactly at the shelves, and the remainder leaks into the calm. This is what the amber lamps have been saying, to geckos, for longer than the label has been legible.

It is slow, and there are generations in it yet. But the floor of the silence is rising toward the height of a tap. A day would come when a male reached the edge of a sheet and announced himself, and the sheet carried it no better than the air carries a shout. The frogs gave up their voices and found their feet, but a spider that loses the silk has nothing further to fall back on.

Nobody in the room can reach the plate. That is the test it has been applying all day: the thing must be needed, nobody here must be able to do it, and nobody here must be able to tell it was done.

**What it does**

It waits for the pause.

The tone stops. The lenses let go, the clap comes up the shaft as wet wind, and the frogs on her mooring fins begin to shriek. She draws her seven legs in and holds on. While every line in the chamber is jabbering and nothing can be read, it goes down through the pools and the scale and the sump to the lower plate, and brings it back into step. The correction is small, and it takes less than the forty seconds.

It does not put out the lamps. They have been wrong about their purpose for a long time, and now they are simply lying, but the moths come to them and the geckos wait there. It leaves the scale to the snails and the gasket to the fern.

Then the tone climbs back up from beneath hearing. The sheet draws tight, the wet wind dies, and the lines fall quiet one by one. This time they go on falling, past the place where they have always stopped, down to nothing at all.

She has no means of remarking on it. She puts her legs back out, seven of them, each onto its line.

At the far edge of her sheet, something too careful to be a midge has landed. The power knows what it hopes for, and it would be easy to lean. But a line with something leaning on it is not what she asked for. So it makes itself lighter than it already was, and when the tapping starts, it only listens.

The tap crosses the sheet perfectly. She reads it at once, and what she does about it is hers.
