The power comes to the rim and stops there, where the animals stop, though not for their reason. It can parse the falls. It stops because it has understood what the grains do. Anything done inside them is done for good, and a hand laid on this valley would stay laid.

So it watches from the long swell of the northern hill, through enough fronts to see the valley flood from the top down a hundred times. It learns the clicks. It finds that it cannot count the people either, and it stops trying and learns who is there.

It knows what it is looking at. The grains are the work of someone who could not bear to lose anything, not a river, not a roar, not one afternoon's sky. That someone was very likely kind. The power knows the wish from the inside, and while it watches the valley it watches that wish in itself.

It sees the line on the alders. It hears the surf. It goes through what it could do.

It could raise the gap. A sill of rock in the south-west, a few metres high, and the sea would never come. But the line is already on the bark. The grains keep the whole span, and the sea is in the span as surely as the first river is. To take it out, the power would have to make the valley's keeping false at one point, and it does not know what else lets go when that does. It might be the grandmother, drinking. It might be the child nobody has borne yet, whose face may be looking up from the far side of that line. And a sill that stops the sea stops the elvers. The valley has always been open at that one corner, and it is the only place where anything arrives.

It could unmake the grains. Then forty metres of river would come down at once. The roar on the north side would finish, and the talk would go out of the air level by level. The people would be left standing in an ordinary bog with an ordinary sea still coming, cold for the first time, and their dead gone from the pools.

It could tell them. But a word said here loses its beginning and keeps the rest. A warning would hang over the fen as one more worn vowel, and they would not hear it, since they hear only what starts. Besides, they stopped speaking themselves. North of the fen, two voices are still raised at each other. The power listens to those two for a long time. It comes to think that the people found, on their own and at some cost, the one way to live in a place that keeps everything, which is to say only what is over as it is said. It is not going to hand them back what they put down.

It could lead them out. Past the last of the silt there is frost, and there is noon, and they wear nothing. They would be people who had been somewhere, and they would never be warm.

Then it looks at what they have. They are carried in rivers before they can walk. They go up a willow's roots as others go up stairs. Above the line on the alders a dozen levels of river run fresh, fed by rain, matted with cress, with trees standing in them. The sea will take the floor, and the floor is only the lowest of the places they live. They do not need saving from the sea. They need saving from its beginning.

They cannot hear it until it starts. Left to itself, it will start as seas do in low country, with the highest tide of an autumn and a westerly gale behind it, in the dark, coming through the gap all at once and taking the lowest way in. The lowest way in is the notch. They lie along it head to foot. And there is the willow with the hollow in its braid, at head height, under the line.

The beginning is the one part of anything that the valley does not keep. Everything else in the span is held, but the starts are let go. So the start is free. It is the one place the power can put a hand without the hand staying.

It goes down to the coast, into ordinary country, where a thing done is over when it is over. It waits there. On the nights that would have done it, a few in each decade, it does small things to the weather, no larger than weather does to itself. A low is held offshore until the tide has turned. A wind backs a few points and the surge spends itself along the open shore. The sea is not made late by a day. The year comes when the tide alone is enough, by a finger's depth, and that year the power only sees that the morning is still.

The sea comes up through ordinary country, slack and brown. It passes under the foot of the staircase where the stacked rivers fall. It crosses onto the grey silt and finds the notch, where the reeds are already leaning away. It fills the winding bed to the depth of a hand, moving at a walk. At the head of the notch it meets the rise of the peat and turns over, once. The sound is the flat of a hand on water.

It has a beginning. Every one of them hears it, in the carr, on the hummocks, face-up in a river halfway to the gap. To them the valley is nearly silent, and this crosses it clean. They come. There are eight of them, then twelve.

Water is lying in the place where they sleep. The tide is going out, and not all of it goes. A thin level stays in the notch, wrist-deep, and at its head the sound of that one small wave stands and continues. The grown hear it continue. It is the first kept thing they have heard since they were children. It is not loud. They will hear it for the rest of their lives, and it began as a thing the size of a hand.

One of them crouches, puts two fingers in, and puts the fingers in her mouth. A pop of the lips: no. Then she does it again.

The children are not surprised. They have heard this under the rain since they were laid in the willow. To them it is like a face in a pool that was nobody's yet, and now is. One squats at the head of the notch and clicks to the water as they click to those faces: here. Then she stands, finds the bleached line on the nearest alder, and makes the other sound: look up.

The grown look up. They have seen the line all their lives. One stands beneath it and raises an arm, as under a stream, and does not reach it. Then they look past it, to where the undersides of the rivers cross the cloud, level above level, with the willows hanging in them.

That night nobody lies in the notch. They sleep in the braids. Within a few tides the infant is carried up a willow's roots to a hollow in another tree, one level higher, above the line. There is no song there. Someone sits by the hollow and knocks on the trunk with a knuckle, slowly, until the child sleeps. It has to be begun again each time, and it is.

The power does not stay for most of what follows. The alders below the line will die standing. The sphagnum will brown, and the floor will go to mud and creek, flooded twice a day from the bottom up. When fronts come the valley will still flood from the top down, and the two waters will meet somewhere near the height of the line. The people will live one level higher, a little nearer the last things their ancestors said. The children will hear those things, and then stop hearing them. Elvers will come in on the tide and not have to climb.

The power never entered the valley and said nothing there. Nothing of it is kept, because nothing of it was ever inside. On the coast, the gales it turned blew themselves out long ago.

At the head of the notch a heron stands in a hand's depth of new water, looking down.
