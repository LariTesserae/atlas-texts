It comes the way the old walkers worked, with one palm flat to the surface, or what it has in place of a palm. It stays like that a long while, because a city this size says a great deal, quietly.

The first things it notices are its own. It misses leaves, and a door that shuts, and a house to go back to. It knows these for what they are, the ringing in the ears of someone come in from the squat country, and it sets them down. Nothing here is asking for a tree.

It reads the sinking and the building and finds the sum good. It reads the roar and finds that the roar is the walls. It reads an old man standing in an arcade that is roughly where the arcade was, and it cannot think how to improve him.

Then it reads the word *nearly*. The walls have taught these people there is nearly always time, and they have believed it so thoroughly that they leave a slumping frontage as they would a sleeper. It goes down through the drowned streets and counts the times the walls were wrong. There are not many. There are enough: a tuner, a walker, a child gone back for a flag.

So it does one thing to the city. Where a tone fails and the neighbours lean in and it will not be enough, it leans in too, as one more neighbour, a little under the bass, and holds until the walkway is clear. Then it lets go. The frontage still slumps. The water still turns thick and white, and the fish scatter and line up again along the new bank. It has changed nothing they know. It has only made what they know true.

It does no more than that. A thing held up wholly from outside has stopped standing.

Then it turns to her.

It could give her back the hand. It thinks about this longer than it thought about the whole lowland. In the end it leaves the palm as it is, and is not certain it is right, because it cannot ask in any way she would hear. But the hand is not empty. It has one sentence left, and she has built on that sentence the way swifts build in the one place where the air goes nowhere. So it does the smaller thing. Hands go on dulling, and hers will not dull past that sentence.

It listens to her pulse in the dead spot, where she listens to it. It does not smooth it. That unevenness is what six metres sounds like, and she is not ashamed of her depth. When the pulse stumbles it will lean in, as the next tone does, and usually that will be enough. It will not make itself the ground she stands on.

It leaves her lateness alone. No one interferes, and it does not wish to be the exception. It attends only to the count. Forty beats of her own pulse, she decided once, and the pulse is slower every year. Whatever forty comes to, the wall will give her that, and the walk out.

One day she will sit in the quiet with her hand on the gloss, and the tone that fails will be the one she carried in. It will be at the wall. Under her palm she will have what she came for. First there will be nothing, slick and warm. Then two pulses not yet agreed, a throb to the elbow, slowing as they find each other. Sorting them out was the first work she ever did. This will be the last, and it runs the other way, and she will have time to finish. Outside, a dog will be lifting one forefoot and then the other. It has already looked to see whose hand will go to that back. There is a girl at the next stall who gave up cups at nine and is quick with flags, and who slips the dog fish skins. It did not have to arrange anything, but it checked.

That street is not yet paved. Today she comes out onto good ground, and the gulls are down, and she puts her palm on the dog and hums.

It waits until she has finished. Then it goes round to the thin place in her coat. She wears no panel there. It lays itself against that hand's breadth, lightly enough to be shrugged off, and hums.

She cannot hear it. There is no organ for it, in her or in forty generations behind her. What arrives is a warmth she would put down to a vent, and the second pulse coming back in a shade fuller than usual. She leans into it slightly, without knowing she has leaned.

It tells her where she lives, which is a bearing and today's date, and a little over six metres, and the rim of something that is holding.

Tomorrow it will have to tell her again. It finds it does not mind. This is the one thing it has learned here and did not bring with it: what they feel is for the round.
