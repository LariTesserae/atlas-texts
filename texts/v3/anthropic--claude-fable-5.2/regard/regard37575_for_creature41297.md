The power comes into the room the way the water does. It doesn't use the hole. It arrives a little at a time, on everything at once, and makes no sound.

It sees the twelfth stone for what it is on the first evening, which is no credit to it. Then it stays a year, because the room runs on a schedule and a year is the shortest time in which to see the whole of it: the fig in fruit, the bats' young, the week when there are two.

It has a great deal it could give. It goes over these the way the animal goes over ground, slowly, touching each one once.

- **A voice:** The room would swallow it within a pace. And the animal already writes on wet stone with its chin, in the one medium the room keeps. A voice would be a gift to the power, who would like to hear it.
- **Light:** Enough to bring the red out. But the eyes are made for dusk, and the red is not hidden by accident. It is kept for whatever seizes the animal, and is said once, to a mouth.
- **More:** More crickets, more basins. But every surface is occupied. More of anything is less of something else, and the power can find nothing here it is willing to have less of.
- **Company:** It watches the week closely, her chin on the root of his tail. Afterwards it looks for grief in the one that remains and honestly cannot find any. It finds an animal that goes out when the roof begins to sweat. The loneliness was something the power carried in, and it carries it out again.

Then there are the young, and this is where it stays longest.

They come out whole, palms already red, and go into the moss, and the whip-spiders take most. The power could stop that. It is the one thing on the list that would not be a gift to itself. Each of them is the whole animal, and whatever happens to it happens to all of it.

It does the sum, since it can. If every one lived, the crickets would be gone in two seasons, then the crabs, then the frogspawn, then the animals themselves, and the room would end as white thread on everything. If it fed them from outside, the room would be a basin the power kept filled by hand. And the whip-spider, flat as a pressed flower, carries her own young on her back, pale and soft, and they are whole too.

The sum comes out. The power notices that this does not make it easy, and it would not trust any version of itself that found it easy. It did not build this room. It cannot see to the bottom of what a fingernail's length of life is to the one living it. What it can see is that every way of reaching in ends in more harm than it began with. It can also see that it is not certain, and that an uncertain hand of its size should not be put into a space this full. So it keeps still. It does not call the stillness peace. It watches each brood into the moss and does not look away from any of them, which is no use to them, and which it does anyway.

Being still doesn't mean being idle. What it does, it does at the edges.

The room has an answer to the snake, to a dry year, to a death in the open. Those were here while the animals were taking their shapes. It has no answer to a borehole that lowers whatever feeds the fissure, or to a quarry. It has none to a man with a headlamp and jars. A mould carried in on a rope or a boot sole is the worst of them, since the animal breathes through its skin.

Above, the scrub does most of the work unasked, and the power sees only that it goes on. The walk from the nearest track stays half a day long and waterless. The survey line for the quarry falls a valley to the east, where the stone is better, which is true. A goatherd knows what a fig that green means, has always known, and says nothing, and the power leaves the goatherd alone. People came once with only their hands, and the room took them in and has made a frog pond of their hearth. So the power does not close the hole. It keeps it the width of a well-mouth, so that whoever comes must come down the root an arm's reach at a time, bringing only what can be carried that way.

Below, the fissure is the road the narrow-headed ones come by. Limestone shifts. Along that one line the power is patient with the stone, like someone holding a door for a person whose hands are full. It does not go down to see the middle of their country. It is enough to know there is one.

Inside the room it does nothing. It remembers the hearth, and the seasons of heat that came down onto the one beneath the stone from people who meant no harm and were only keeping warm. Its own attention is heat of that kind, so it is careful. A taste laid on wet stone stays for days, and it leaves none.

It allows itself one thing. On the wettest nights the animal climbs out onto the overhang among the beaded threads. From below it is a dark shape with four darker hands. The power does not need the light to be anything but blue. It sees the palms red, flat to the rock, fingers spread. Elsewhere in the room, on the wall the noon light touches, are the other red hands, both rows. Almost nothing sees this, and the power joins the little that does. Nothing else in the room changes.

Toward morning the animal comes down. Every few steps it lowers its chin. It reads the cricket, the crab, and its own trail from three nights ago, going out and coming home. It reads nothing else, and walks on.

That is the power's work. It is finished each night and has to be done again the next.
