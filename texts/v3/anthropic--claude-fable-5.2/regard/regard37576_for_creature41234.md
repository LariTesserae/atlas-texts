The visitor comes in the way the dew does, after dark and onto the levee tops, because there is no other way to come. It has no heel to turn out and no hand to slap with, and it takes care to leave no mark of any kind. The people here read marks more closely than faces, and a print that belonged to nobody would be read for a hundred years.

It looks first at the thing no one here can see. From where it is, the whole tilt shows at once, intake to center, and it checks the slope pan by pan. The tilt is true. In two places near the edge it had wandered by less than a hair, and in both the water found the error and the heel of a hand took it out. Every maker took the slope on trust from the pan before, and the trust was good. The visitor could tell them so. It cannot think of anything a maker would do better tomorrow for having been told. It can think of one thing a maker might do worse, which is wait to be told again instead of watching a palmful of seep.

Then it goes through what it could give.

It could give them a blade. There is stone in the world, and the visitor knows where. But a levee is knee-high because a leg gets tired, and a plug is the size of a head because that is what two hands hold. A blade is not the size of anyone. In a generation the works would be larger than the bodies that keep it, and nobody would twist a cord with a teacher's hair in it. The visitor is not certain about this one. Backs ache here. It leaves the stone where it is, for now, and does not call that a kindness.

It could dry the fingers of the keeper at the center. She would then be the one dry person in the works, and when she took someone by the forearms to turn, it would feel wrong to both of them.

It could carry her news to the edge. But the news is hers. She is the only person in the works who has seen what it is for, and the visitor seeing it too does not change who was first. It is hers to send, if a way can be found, and the visitor thinks one can.

It could press the plug. It thinks about this longest. The rule says the hand that pulled it. The keepers near the tide have never settled whether the rules apply to the glasswort, which is the one thing on the floor that nobody put there. The visitor is now the second such thing, and it settles the question for itself: they apply. But the rules here are few and practical, and none of them is worth a person. So before it decides anything, it goes up the tilt to see whose hand it was.

He is two pans above the open notch, lying along the levee where he sat down on his way home from rain. His hands are pink to the wrist. The clay on his back went on yesterday morning. His skin is dry, which no one's is here, and that is what is wrong with him.

The visitor does the sum. At first light the keeper below finds a pan a finger too full and a shade too pale, leaves their own plugs in, and starts walking. They reach him before the levee tops are too hot to stand on. He lasts that long if he has water in the night, and not if he doesn't. Had the sum come out otherwise, the visitor would have done whatever was needed and let the marks fall where they fell.

It comes out this way, so the visitor uses the dew. The dew was coming anyway. On this one levee it comes early and heavy, enough to bead and then to run. The man has drunk rain off the top of brine without raising the pink, and he knows what to do with a wet surface under his mouth. He turns his face to the clay. The visitor stays beside him through the night, which marks nothing. Near dawn it sees, a long way down the tilt, a bump come loose from its brown thread and begin to move.

What happens when the two of them meet is theirs. The visitor notes only that when the plug goes back there are four hands on it, with the hand that pulled it underneath. It also notes that the man now knows there is someone below him, which the water could never have told him.

By afternoon the visitor is at the center. The cloud is standing where it stands, and the visitor leaves it alone. Rain belongs to the pan it falls on, and it is not the visitor's to say which pan that is. The breeze arrives at its hour. The rafts come across to the lee levee, and the keeper with the white hands kneels to watch them in.

By this time of day the slip she drew over her shoulders at dawn has crazed into plates. It is the only thing on her that is dry. Every afternoon, when she bends, a plate or two lets go and drops behind her onto the levee top, and she has never had a reason to look. The visitor's whole act here is a breath of air, less than it takes to tip a raft under. One plate, the size of a palm and curved like the shoulder it came from, falls in front of her instead. It lands at the levee foot with its lip in the water. A raft comes in on the breeze, rides up onto it, and sits there whole.

She looks at it for some time. Then she reaches back over her shoulder.

By dusk there is a little salt, about what fits in the hollow of a hand, lying on a plate of sun-baked clay on the levee top. It is the first thing made here that is not a levee, and like the levees it is sized to a body. When the dew starts, she turns a second plate over it. That is the first roof in the works. The people sleep in the open, and the salt is under cover.

The visitor shows her nothing else. The works has never carried anything back. As of this morning it has one keeper who walked up the tilt, and as of this evening it has one with something to send. There are many days' walking between those two, and the visitor leaves all of it alone.

It thinks the salt will climb, a country at a time. It will be handed over at each boundary by two people turning on a levee with their forearms in each other's hands, each one home in time to press their plugs. It thinks the salt will reach the tide end, where the fish are split, and go out along the main levees with whoever is walking to join the edge. It thinks that one noon a maker standing to the knee in seep will bite into a piece of fish, stop chewing, and for the first time look back the way the water goes.

The visitor has not seen any of this. It saw the whole slope at once, but the rest it takes on trust, from the pan before.

It has not walked a floor. It has sent nothing back up the water and moved no rain. The two things it opened, a night's dew and a breath of air, it has pressed back. The birds cross high in lines, going somewhere else, and it does not call one down.

Tonight every notch in the works is closed. The visitor checks. Then it goes the way the dew goes, and the sun takes it back within the hour.
