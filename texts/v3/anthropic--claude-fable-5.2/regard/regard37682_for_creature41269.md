Something came that could have stopped the engines.

It came in a wet season and for a long time did nothing. It had not brought a body, and a line is wide enough for one, so it kept to the singing iron where nothing stands. It stayed until it had seen the lines walk out and walk back.

It started with the plates. It could read the lettering that a palm can find and not read. There were foundry names and numbers. One foundry had made most of the upper plates and none of the lower. It read every plate in the basin, including the ones underneath, though nobody was left who would have asked for this. It took most of a year.

Then it went into the dry rings, where none of the others go, and looked at every bearing and pin. It had expected to find something to mend. There was nothing. It counted the mud still left to rot under the iron, and it counted the wall. Both came out longer than the oldest row of rushes, by a good deal, and neither came out forever.

It gave the noise a whole tide's thought. It had arrived thinking the noise was the injury. But nothing here has ears, and the hammering is where the food comes from. It is where the quiet comes from too, since the engines draw the still lines. With the engines stopped there would be one quiet from edge to edge, with nowhere in it to stand, and then the sea. Besides, each of them had an engine, kept since a certain day. It could find no way to stop an engine that didn't take that away. It left the engines alone.

It left the end alone too. When the mud is spent the engines will slow, one and then another, and the sea will come over the wall, which is easy to climb without noticing. The sea will throw what it carries up a long slope of worn iron. They have a shore animal's tool. Whether they will know what to do with a wave, it could not tell. It marked the season. It means to be there.

It watched the ones with no engine comb at every stroke and catch little. It looked for a way to help one that was not a way of crowding another off a line, and didn't find it. It went on watching them. If it was going to leave that alone, it could at least not look elsewhere.

When two met on a line and stopped and opened their mouths, it could have known what came out, if anything did. It kept back. Whatever passes between two on a line, a pace apart, was not said to it.

It did one thing it could not have defended at length. The rails do not stop. A later plate was laid over them, and they run on beneath it, into the district where the combs are clotted orange. In one place there the top plate has thinned to a lace of rust and would have gone in forty wet seasons or so. It leaned on that plate through one hard rain. By the dry season there was a gap the length of a body, with steel at the bottom of it, dull, under a finger of water. It showed the steel to no one. A still line crosses the gap in wet years. Nothing has laid a fan there yet. Maybe nothing will.

In its second wet season, a line near the stanchion stumps left and an old one didn't step. The singing reached its feet. The thing that had come was already standing in the singing, and it moved near. It did not hold the old one up or bring the quiet back under it. It stayed while the old one blurred to the hip and then to the head, and it stayed after. For the days the plate took to walk the body out to the line and turn it, it went alongside.

The discs were another matter. A fan held up behind a flame is held up to the whole basin, and whatever it says is said to anything that can see it.

So at the next slack, when the launders emptied over the wall and the haze lay down, it went to a standpipe out by the seawall, where no line comes. It had no fan. It took up water from the plate and held it flat behind the flame, and the water took the light.

From there it could see six others. One closed. Further off another opened, as it would have anyway. It kept its own open until the plate's note began to rise, then let the water down.

It does this at every slack now. From any one disc, six or seven others can be seen. Out toward the wall, for some of them, it is seven or eight.
