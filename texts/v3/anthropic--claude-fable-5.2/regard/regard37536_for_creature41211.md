Something has come to the rim and stayed.

It has no shape that matters here. It could keep the lake open all winter, or lift the whole cirque and set it down beside the sea. Before it did anything it watched for a year, ice to ice, because the town's rules are mostly about patience and it seemed right to keep them while visiting.

It learned the lanes and the knocks, and which bells had slack taps. It read the cords, since anything tied is meant for whatever hand passes. That included the long cord in the floor quarter, and it will not tell that one aloud either. It went down the stream once and saw who leaves the glass. They too are gone by morning, and it took that as instruction.

It did not listen at the black water. Whenever someone came up into the spray, it turned its attention to the far end of the lake. If anything shouted there was meant for it, it does not know, and it thinks that is correct.

At the end of the year it counted what it could give.

- **Warmth:** A few degrees would ease the winters. It would also spoil the pits, thin the char, and bring the stems up two joints a year, faster than a child can teach one to forget.
- **Air:** A thicker thread would let each bell keep two sleepers. But every tap is a wound, and it had counted what the town never needed to: about forty is the number of wounds the plant can close.
- **Metal:** A blade that holds its edge cuts a living stem as easily as a dead one. Glass breaks when it is forced, and the first rule is partly kept by that.
- **Answers:** It knows how the plant breathes under the ice and what the fall does above the headwall. But in each generation there is one who looks up longest, and answers handed down from the rim would leave that one nothing to carry rope toward.

Each of these was a kindness, and each would have been owed. A debt to something on the rim could never be untied. So whatever it did had to be the kind of thing that could be wind.

It also took the children's rule for its own: no place is farther than half of what you can hold. It would do half of what it was sure of, because its sureness might be a bell with a slack tap.

It did three things.

Above the headwall is a snowfield, smaller than it was. It keeps that now. The snow lies in the creases into August as before, and the fall has not changed its note. Nobody below knows there was anything to keep. The town's knowledge is all of how long, so it has taken the why into storage, the way the floor quarter takes the char: cold, under a stone lid, to be fetched without unease by anyone who comes for it.

Half a day below the notch there is a tarn, small, cold, and deep enough. The stream used to run straight along one bank and out, and every autumn the seeds that went over the lip went past. It moved one stone. Now the current turns once around the tarn before it leaves. It planted nothing. Whether a seed lodges is the plant's affair, and a stem adds a joint a year, so it would be forty years before one could carry a bell. Whether anyone ever hangs one is the town's affair.

It was thinking of the ones who sleep among the nets years past their growth, and of the long cord. It was thinking too that the plant is one plant. Some year the one who goes to the notch may come back and report that something is in the stream, a leaf with a red underside. What gets tied about that will be tied by them. It is not certain it was right, and it remembers where the stone was.

The third thing happened in the second winter, in the deepest part of the dark. The one awake was an old net-maker, close to adding stones to her bell, and she fell asleep against her stem with her knocking stone in her hand. The ice boomed. All through the lake the sleepers stirred and set their teeth to the stems beside their heads. They heard the mutter of the fall and the snails rasping, and nothing else. A few pulses quickened. Someone began counting breaths to the nearest bell.

It has no stone, so it took a pebble from the rim. It went out on the ice to where a stem stood frozen in, and struck it: two knocks, a pause, three.

It was no message. It meant only that someone was up, and that was true.

The sleepers went back under. The net-maker woke later, struck her own knock, and never knew. One person noticed. She was a woman of the floor quarter, pale as the marl, with her teeth worn nearly to the gum. She heard that the note had come down the stem instead of up, in no knock she could put a knot to. She did not tie it. In spring, in a workroom under the new leaves, she said it in air, between someone's joke and someone's complaint: someone else was up. The children laughed. A spoken thing is taken as a kindness and nothing is owed on it, which is where the thing on the rim had hoped it would be filed.

It is still there. It learned how much of itself to show from the eyes in the leaf-roof, the wide-set ones that stay open when the frogs' close. In the weeks of open sky, when the cirque wavers overhead, the one who looks up longest sometimes looks at one place on the rim longer than at the rest. It holds still. It has not chosen that one, since nobody does.

It does not take a watch in winter. The watch is theirs and they keep it. But it is awake. When the knock goes through the lake, it sets what it has for teeth against the rock of the rim, hears it, and stays up.
