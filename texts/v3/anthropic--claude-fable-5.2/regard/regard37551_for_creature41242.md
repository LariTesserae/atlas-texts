It comes the way everything comes here, in from the rim, slowly.

For a long time it only looks. That is the custom of the place, and it finds the custom good. It looks at the combed plain from above, where the bird hangs. It looks at one mound for a season. It reads her household's line from the newest pebble back to the eightieth, then keeps walking up the trail, past the last name, through the kilometres of grey, to where the stroke begins.

It is used to places that need something, and it knows what it could give. So it goes through the gifts one at a time, the way she goes through a load of crust, turning each piece to see how it would bed.

Rain. One cloud would do it. The crust would slump and the felt of roots would drown. The pollen cake would spoil in a week, and the dead would rot. Everything here stands on the fact that water stays locked. It puts rain down.

A spring, then, a small one beside her mound. But the next household would walk to it, and the next, and the day's walk between them would close. That distance is the measure of what the ground can carry. And a spring would have to rise either in the line of pebbles or in the ground ahead. Ahead is not theirs. It finds that ahead is not its own either.

Her knees. It stays on this longest. It could mend them. But she knows her body as she knows her ground, and nothing here goes backward. The green does not return to the pebble. A knee grown younger would be the one thing on the plateau out of line, and she would have to carry that as well. The patch by evening is the same size it always was. It leaves the knees. It is not certain it is right, and it does not tell itself that holding back is the same as kindness.

Then the answer, which is the gift it came closest to giving. No one has seen the work succeed. It can see that the stone is good. Roots will take water from crystal a person has bedded as readily as from crystal a lake laid down. Whether the stone will be there, nine paces and three hundred and sixty pebbles on, is not yet anywhere to be seen. It will be put there or it will not, a sleeping mat at a time. It could tell her the first part. But she has never asked. She does not carry because it will work. To tell her would be to hand her a reason, and a reason can be satisfied. What she has cannot.

That makes four gifts, and none of them lies flat.

Only then does it notice where it is standing. It is on a trail, on spent ground, and it has been fed here. It came to give and has been taking the whole time. It arrived late, as the lichens did, as the grass did, as she did. Whatever it is elsewhere, here it is one more thing that has found footing in the wake.

It seems plain to it, too, that something should be put down in front.

So it works where a latecomer may work. Far up the trails toward the rim, past the last pebble and the last of the silver grass, lies the oldest powder, the chalk that will not give anything more. No one has asked what it would take. The rime grows there before dawn as it does everywhere, and goes back into the air at sunrise. It lets a little of the rime stay. It brings no water. It only keeps back some of what was already leaving, and gives it to the powder one molecule at a time, which is how it was taken out. The dull grains draw it in and sharpen. It works at a hand's width in a century, seeing no cause to go faster than the mound.

In a time longer than any line of pebbles, there will be white ground at the rim again, crunching like dry snow, where a seed might land. The plateau has been burning down like a wick, very slowly, toward its own centre. Now it will go round. No one will see this done. No one walks there but the dead, and they do not walk.

That is the large thing, and from one year to the next it hardly shows.

The small thing is not wise. It only wants to. Before the next dawn the rime stands thick on the shaded faces of the nearest stones, as it does a few mornings in a life. She fills the flask without going far. She carries it against her belly until it melts and drinks it at once, and it is a mouthful more than she is used to. Then she goes and looks at her pebble, which she has not done in a while. At midday, when the wind sets inward, she takes up the frame.

It does not answer the blades. It has gone through what can be said that way, and none of it is what it means. The nearest is *I am here*, and thrown from an empty quarter that would only set people walking for days toward nothing. So it says it the way the mound does. While the moths are up for their hour it settles on the side that is out of the wind, near the hollow, and is a few degrees warmer than the night.

That makes three of them.

The people have a word for the bird, and mean it as praise. It would like, in time, to have earned that word.
