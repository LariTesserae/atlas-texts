What has come to look counts eleven the first time, because it can see the shelf and the steps together. It can also see the two hundred bowls together, in the dark, without a lamp, so it knows what the cords are worth. The water shivers far more often than the knots say. The old man passes each doorway once a day, and the rock does not wait for him. The cord of three hundred and forty-one knots was three hundred and forty-one times that someone looked and the water happened to be still. It does not think less of the cords for this. The looking was done.

What drew it was the flat hand. The notch-cutter held it up with twenty-four days to go and the rafters already stacked, to a street with no one in it. A sign like that has to arrive somewhere.

It could hold the bench still. It looks up the mountain to where the ash comes from, and it looks a long way ahead, well past the top of the notch line. Then it looks at the custom again. Whoever cut the first notch made a rule that keeps two hundred families off ground that has not finished arriving, and keeps a few people on it with their palms on the paving, watching water. It finds nothing there to correct. It goes down the steps at the leeward end and tries each tread. They are sound.

It could tell them how long. But here a thing can only be said into one ear, and then that one person would have it to carry. The carriers' hand, held level and tilted slightly, already says as much as is true.

It could stop the wind for one morning, so that for once they could all be spoken to together. It thinks of the carriers lying awake in the lower country for want of the chord. It leaves the wind alone.

It will not watch the bowls for them. A shiver counts only if someone sees it, and it sees every one. If it were someone, no cord on the shelf would reach a second knot. So in the matter of the bowls it has decided to be no one.

It does two things.

The old man says the booming room gets into his knuckles and loosens them. This was not quite true. It is true now. He still cannot close a hand on a hammerstone, but the knots come easier.

The other thing concerns the hand. On the shelf the flat hand is not answered. Whoever sees it turns and shows it to the next person, and so it goes down the street. The notch-cutter's street ended at the edge. But the stone already knows how to carry something further. When a block is struck, its bedmates ring with it, four hundred paces off. It lengthens that reach to two days' walk and changes nothing else.

The girl hears it first, since she listens most. On a morning before anyone has taken up a hammer, a block in the fourth course of a wall on the second lane sounds faintly, three strokes and then three. She walks toward the answer, puts her palm on it, and feels it come again. She fetches one of the two just up from the steps and sets his hand where hers was. He knows that block's bedmate. He carried it down six years ago to a doorway beside a threshing floor, and he knows what three and three means down there. He cups his hand round her ear.

In the lower country a slab hums in its doorway with nobody near it. The household looks at the carrier, who is thought to be a mute. He holds up a hand for them to wait. It hums again, and again, evenly. He shows them with one fist striking the other that someone up there is dressing a block. After that they ask him less often how the town is coming, because they can hear it. The carriers still sleep badly down there, but they wake to the first hammer of the morning and lie still, knowing whose it is.

Within the year the builders know the plots a second way. That wall has had two children in it, and the long house by the plaza has had a death. When the ticking puts a wall out and it has to come down, they relay each block in the place it had. And the girl, who has never been down the steps, has begun to answer. When three and three comes up through a wall, she takes the pebble at her wrist and gives the block three back. Two days below, a slab sounds in a doorway where there is a new child, and the people of the house take it as it is meant.

By afternoon the cloud has gone through and the light is plain and high and even. It falls into two hundred open rooms, on the bowls, and on moss rising back over the places where people sat. On the lowest terrace the rafters lie where they were stacked, with ferns along them. A click passes through the paving and is let pass. Then a wall on the fourth lane, with no one near it, sounds three times and three. It is the only thing on the shelf that all of them are ever told at once. The notch-cutter, in the far corner of the quiet room, lifts her head.
