**The boots**

It comes down by the hoist. It could arrive any way it liked, but every other way is a blow.

The hoistman looks at its boots before its face. There are nails in the soles, as there would be, since everything a power knows how to do is struck: the sign, the wonder, the wall opened, the rock made warm again at a word. He has it out of them. He gives it felt overshoes off the rack and a length of cord for anything heavy in its pockets. It has a great deal that is heavy in its pockets. It ties each thing on and goes in.

**The looking**

For the first rains it does nothing but learn to be passed in a lane. It turns, presses into the padding, and lets the other by. It gives way to everyone, having more room to give than most. By the tenth rain it is green from collar to belt, and nobody has asked what it is.

It learns to speak on the out-breath and wait through the intake. This is harder than it expected, because it is used to having the gap.

When a window opens on the south wall it brings a stool like anyone else. This is how it has always looked at the world: a little dimmer and deeper than the real one, for a long time, without it counting as looking. It had not known anyone else did that.

It came to mend, and it goes through the town looking for the place. It finds fitters who would sooner re-route a main than cut a limb. It finds a rank that runs the opposite way to rank. It finds a book where the fault is written by the hand that made it, and the hand is better liked afterwards. Most of what it would have taught is here already, worn smooth, without the hard consonants.

**What it leaves**

It leaves the wall. It could round every edge under in a single shift. Then there would be nothing to pad, and no moss, and no green across anyone's shoulders.

It leaves the geckos. It could see how the matter is decided. It chooses not to, and understands the look a little better.

It leaves the rock. It sits at the west heading with one palm flat on the casing and feels the degree going. It could put it back. It keeps its hand there until it is sure of what it feels. The town is not standing on the heat. The town is the shape the cooling has made: the leaning, the ferns to the rafters, the old asleep in easy rooms. Warm the rock and nobody goes east again.

It leaves the number. That is the hardest. A child of a hundred and ten rains does the sum and sits three shifts at the window. The power sits on the next stool, and everything on its cord pulls. Then the fig turns up on the bedding, unbruised. It sees that the thing has already been set down, finger under, and that it has nothing to add.

**The drop**

It adds something anyway, and that is how the entry happens.

A shift later, on the limb that is in fruit, one fig has coloured. It is a deep red, the red of somewhere with seasons. The power is not sure what it meant by it. A kindness, perhaps, or a second fig.

Nothing under is that colour. The children go shy of it. People who detour to walk beside the Brook now detour to avoid the well. The red is the loudest thing in town. It is a clap. No one picks it. For most of a rain it hangs there, and then, ripe, it falls two levels and lands on a grating outside a room where a household is sleeping.

It makes almost no sound. The power knows that is not the point.

It walks to the book. It writes the date, and *one fig, coloured*, and then its name. At that volume its name is mostly breath, the hard parts worn off in a hundred rains. Whoever reads the entry next gives a short hum.

The year is named for it. After that, people in the lanes now and then press into the moss for it, which they had not done before. The old woman with no entries looks at it across the sixth level for some time.

**Up**

By then it has found the one place where the town itself might be dropped, and it is not under. It is the top of the shaft. For three generations the town has learned how to let a thing arrive. Nobody has done that for the town. The number will reach its end, and the hoist will come up for the last time into streets and weather, among people who clap and slam and call this the Kettle, carrying three hundred who cannot shout.

There is the other matter too: a cable thick as a thigh going up, and one rationed pipe coming down. Nobody under has taken to walking the sixth over it. It is a quarrel all the same, an old one, and only one side has ever known.

So it goes up, as the dead go up, and current, and once a bat. It leaves the overshoes on the rack and keeps the cord.

At the top it does not strike anything either. It has lost the habit, and it has learned what the fitters know. It follows the cold pipe back to where a hand rests on a valve, and it sits with the owner of the hand for months, as a tender sits. One day she lays her palm on the pipe for no reason and feels, very far down, eleven strokes a minute. She eases the feed a quarter turn. She would be surprised to have it pointed out.

Under, the Brook's sleeve thickens by a finger. The pipe sweats harder and is beaded its whole length. Nobody remarks. The town drinks it before it warms.

The rest is slower. At the shaft-head someone takes the nails out of their boots without knowing why. A lintel gets wrapped. A man sets his cup down, it makes no sound, and he looks at it. One at a time, without a meeting, a street's worth of people who have never been below begin to do one thing at a time.

It will take about as long as the number, which is the time there is. It hems the work to last longer.

Below, the number goes down by one, and nobody sees it chalked. The beam takes its breath and lets it go. At the top of the shaft, where the town will one day be put down in the weather, something has gone underneath and means to land first.
