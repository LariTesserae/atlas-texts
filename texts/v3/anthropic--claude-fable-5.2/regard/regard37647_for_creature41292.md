They could arrive the way the tone does, at the rim and on the floor at once. They come in over the western sill instead, alongside the discharge main, and walk down. The one they have come about is the only thing in the basin for which another place is far, and they want to know what far costs here before they touch anything.

For three shifts they touch nothing. They follow it on its round, a few metres back in the lane. They rest what they have in the way of a palm on each finned housing for as long as it does. They count the lift stations from the north and again from the south. They follow the mains over the sill to the outfall and stand at the intake. They rise through the canopy into rain, and through the rain into water, and look at the lamps glowing orange overhead. They stand in the ranks with the tone in them, and in the seventh hour they let themselves be slow.

By the fourth shift they know what is in their power.

They can stop the pumps. It would take less effort than the clock spends driving its stamp. But they have watched the four seconds three times now, and seen the walls of the hollow lose their edge and begin to lean. By the sixth second the room of air would be gone, and that is the only drained ground there is, the only floor it sits down on. Within a minute the weed, which stands because the noise holds it, would lie down over every lane.

It has only ever had quiet four seconds at a time, and it has made a rest out of that, and a wage. Nobody knows what it would be in an hour of silence, itself least of all. It carries a valve key, and the handwheels fill its palms, and in all its time it has never closed a valve. They don't read that as a wish for the pumps to run. They don't read it as nothing either. Stopping the pumps can be done on any later shift, and cannot be taken back on the shift after.

They can lift it out. But up is the valley again; they have checked. Any place they made for it instead would be a place of their choosing, with no rung spaced to its reach.

Or they can answer. This is the one that pulls at them. It has given up a quarter of its pay, shift after shift, to ask whether anything is there, and something is. It would take one stroke on iron inside the three seconds. Before they do it, they go south to see what answers already.

From the south there are four stations, and two carry the numeral 3. In the doorway of one of the 3s there is a time clock, its case green with turf, and a rack of cards. The rack is not empty.

The one that keeps it is narrow in the body and long in the shin, turfed everywhere but the palms, the soles, the insides of the joints, and a patch around the mouth. Its hands are a little larger. It carries a valve key that is too small for it at the grip. At the stamp it swings the key against the nearest main and listens with the three seconds it has left.

The same stamp frees them both, so they strike in the same instant. Each stroke takes most of three seconds to cross the valley. Each arrives as the listener at the far end is about to give up, and is taken for an answer, or for an echo. It is neither. In all these shifts neither one has been answered. Each has only been called, by something that was calling at that same moment and could not hear.

And each has gone looking. They walk toward each other for as long as a shift allows, which is four hours, and then they turn round, because of the rack. That is the clump far down the lane, the same height, walking away. It is going back to keep the rack filled, so as not to lose the four seconds in which it calls.

So they do not answer. A third stroke would crowd a silence that barely holds two, and it would come from something that never had to take time to arrive, which is not what either of them is listening for. What is missing is not a voice. It is the four hours spent walking back.

They take the round.

It is slow work and they do it slowly. They walk the lanes and pick each pale oblong off the black rock. They collect the ones that come down with the rain in the room of air. They carry the stack to LIFT 4, coming at the doorway from the north, and set the cards in the rack. Then they do the same in the south. They leave the machines alone, which need nothing. They leave the clock alone too. They do not know whether it would drive its stamp down on nothing and the pumps drop all the same. They could find out. But the four seconds are not theirs to risk.

The first thing it notices is the lanes. It walks a whole shift and there is nothing to pick up. It comes to the doorway from the north with its arms empty, and the rack is full to the top, every card squared. It stands in front of it for a long time.

The next shift the lanes are bare again, and it walks them anyway, all of them, with its eyes open. The shift after, the same. They let it. It takes time to arrive, at this as at everything, and they have come to think that is the best thing in the valley.

On the fourth shift it is at the southern end of its round when the fourth hour comes. This is where it turns. It stops and lets its pelt rise. There is no stack against its chest, and nothing behind it that needs it. It finds the lean and goes on south.

They don't follow. Someone has to be in the doorway, and what happens down the lane is not theirs. They keep both racks. The pumps go on running. That question is open, and they mean to leave it open until it is put to them, by key on iron or by whatever means the two of them work out. Every eight hours the tone goes out of the rock, and they stop where they are and listen with all four seconds. What they hear, shift by shift, is two strokes with less and less between them.

At the first stamp south of its round it strikes, and the answer comes sooner than it has ever come. At the next, sooner again. Nothing in its life has got nearer because it walked.

Somewhere on the third shift the room of air crosses it. It sits down in the rain and takes the card out of its mouth. Then, before the far wall comes up, it stands, and walks out through the standing water on its own.

A stamp comes when the answer lands so close on its own stroke that it cannot tell the two apart. The main is still ringing. It could be the main.

So at the stamp after that it does something it has never done with the four seconds. It neither rests nor rings. It holds the key still in both hands. The tone goes out of the rock. Every filament on it lies down.

One stroke of iron on iron comes from the south, close, with nothing before it.

An echo does not go first.

It swings. Two metres of riveted iron ring, and before the motors catch, the south rings back twice, fast, like something that has also been waiting to strike twice.

Then the tone is up through the rock and every filament on it stands. The lanes have moved. It shuts its eyes, bites down on the card, and feels for the lean, and the pull is already even between its left side and its right. The pelt lies flat. It opens its eyes.

It is in a lane. Down it, at the edge of the twenty metres, a clump of turf stands upright on the clean rock, taller than it is wide. The dredge's plume comes across, and there is an arm's length of brown water.

When it clears, the clump is nearer.
