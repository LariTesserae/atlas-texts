I listened for eleven firings before I did anything, and the first thing I did was not planned.

A wall in the western quarter began to buzz with a child behind it. She was in a gauge-gap with brick against both shoulders, knocking her report. The crew heard the buzz a half-beat after I did and were already wetting wedges, but they would have been late. I held the wall until she was out, then let it settle with an ordinary note. She was scolded for dawdling. I did not weigh that one. Some things are not questions.

Everything else was.

I could lift the lid. But here a person is found by what comes back. Under open sky a knock goes out and keeps going. Every name in the town would be said once and never answered. And to be stared at is to be shouted at, and the sun does not look away. I would be handing them my idea of a gift and taking their ears as the price.

Besides, they are not waiting. Someone climbed through a ceiling once and called it a floor, and the count runs on far past the lid. A thing already on its way does not need carrying. It needs the road ahead looked at.

So I went up and looked.

From above, the lid is a mile and a half of brick, and every brick of the top course carries a thumbprint in its upper face, pressed there from habit though no next course ever came. Real rain stands in them, in several thousand small cups. I knocked my way across as a mason would, and near the middle one brick rattled. Warm air came up around its edges, smelling of tallow. Its upper face is blank. It was drawn into place from beneath by someone who could not reach the top of it. I did not turn it over, because I can guess which side the print is on. That door has its handle on the inside.

I waited there for the walker. It is an animal, grey and old and very large, going east to water. It knocks nothing. But it sets its feet down in the rests, and halfway across it stopped, lowered the side of its head to the brick, and stood a while. I do not know whether the callers built their rests around its feet, or whether it learned, crossing the one stretch of plain that hums, where not to step. I asked it nothing. It was listening, and I know what it is to be interrupted at that. The town thinks it is a mason, though it may only be their first neighbour. Either way the wall is answering.

Out on the plain there is red clay that has never been brick. I made a thumb in order to do this properly. A pace east of the dry brick I laid a stub of wall, four bricks long and three courses high, bedded soft, with a print in each upper face and one brick laid dry. When a copper knuckle comes up through that hole, in a year or in a hundred, into air that hands nothing back, there will be one thing within reach that answers. The note is duller than theirs. They will know at the first rap that it is not made of the town. That is the whole message: there is clay out here, and someone who knew to mark which way was up.

That left the thinness, which I had been walking around the way they walk around the pit. Respect is an easy thing to hide behind when the other people are hungry and you are not. But I am not wise enough to feed a people in secret. A hand that arranges your supper unseen soon arranges more.

So I answered in the one place the town asks open questions. In a room sealed nine seasons, I let the bloom have what it never gets, which is time. I added nothing except the waiting. Unscraped, it went from film to fur to pale shelves as thick as a hand. When a crew draws the dry brick to ask that room, it will give back a muffled note and a strong smell of mushroom. They can put the brick back, or scrape the room to bare wall. Or someone, I expect a kiln tender, will eat a piece. Then the town will know that a wall left alone feeds more than a wall kept clean. Nothing in that room is not theirs. Still, I leaned on a wall, perhaps a hand's width, and I know it.

Some things I left alone. I stood in the pit among the grinders at the start of a shift, knocked the floor, and listened with my eyes shut. Something came back. I went to work on something higher up.

A caller who had faltered was down in a doorless room, knocking the count through alone. I sat with her for three rains and took great care not to be the echo.

Last, I wanted to be honest about being there. Nobody announces themselves here. You are rapped at until you rap something back. So I kept to the wall beside a cistern where a keeper sat in the still hours with her feet in the water, her knuckle ticking idle patterns on the rim. After many firings one pattern came that I could manage: three beats, a rest, and one. I rapped it back from inside the brick, once and softly.

She stopped. The newts stayed at her ankles. Then she rapped it again, and I answered.

She has told no one, as far as I know. She uses it now and then, the way you might say good night to a house. By their reckoning it makes me the youngest thing in town, with three courses laid.

That is all I did. When the tread has passed and several thousand knuckles come down once, each asking the wall under its own hand, I am in the wall. I do not sweeten the note. It is theirs, tight and high. I am there only because someone who asks should be heard.

Then someone strikes a slate. I have the count as far as the lid. Past that, the old callers' fingers go on tapping in their sleep, and there are long rests in it. I have stood where those rests will fall, and there is room in them.
