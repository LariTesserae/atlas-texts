It comes the only way the room allows, which is without arriving. Nothing leads here, so it uses nothing. It attends, and its attention has no weight and no temperature.

It stays for one breath first, an hour, down the middle and up the sides. One breath says little about a thing this slow, so it stays until the ground creaks, then until a frond it heard unroll has greyed and joined the skirt. It is used to being asked for things, and nothing here asks. It waits to learn whether that is silence or contentment, and finds no lack anywhere in the room. The loneliness it thought it sensed is its own, carried down like warmth in a coat.

Still, it is benevolent, and it goes through what it could give.

**Light.** The ferns already stand in a dim noon. Real light brings heat, and this thing lives by being colder than the stone.

**Company.** A springtail, a worm, something to carry pollen. But everything that travels has a mouth, and the garden is sweet where it breaks.

**New blood.** The nine or ten ferns have been each other's whole acquaintance since the peat was a stain on gravel. A stranger would be a risk taken on their behalf to fix something that has not failed.

**Knowledge.** It could tell the garden that it is green. The garden has nowhere to put that.

**A hand.** This is what it wants most. The fronds are a hand long, the moss is wrist deep, the bore is the width of a wrist, and everything seems fitted. But a hand is warm and it bruises. One true thing can be said of this being that can be said of almost nothing else, and a touch would make it false forever, for the sake of the one touching.

That leaves the thread. The line is at the knee and the floor gains a centimeter a century. In about five thousand years the moss will close over what feeds it, and a leaf cannot use what reaches it through a forearm of peat.

It could lift the line. It considers this longer than anything else, and while considering, it notices the plate. The plate is flush with the floor, and the floor has risen a forearm's depth. So the plate has come up with it, a hair a year, the whole way. Whoever came down the bore knew how to make a thing keep pace with the garden, did so once, and left the line still. The meeting was foreseen.

It does not know what was foreseen. Perhaps the garden ends. Perhaps the thread, once inside the peat, feeds the fungus, the one resident that touches all the others, and the garden is then lit from within. The visitor cannot make the thread's accounting close any better than the surface can. It will not pull on a line whose books it cannot read when it has fifty centuries to learn them.

So this is what it does.

It goes up. Twelve meters above is a gravel plain, and above that is everything with teeth. It sees to it that the seasons go on stopping where they have always stopped. A road that would have crossed the plain crosses elsewhere. A drill finds better ground a valley over. No one will know that anything was done, least of all the garden, whose roof goes on ticking.

It makes a promise about the line. It will be in the room when the moss closes over it. If the garden dims after that, starting at the edges, with the frost feathers creeping inward, it will lift the thread at the pace the plate has always kept. If the garden does not dim, it will have learned something about whoever was here before.

It leaves the plate alone. It does not know what the plate is for, but it knows a kept place when it meets one, and the garden has kept this one empty for a very long time without being told why. The visitor is sure of only one use for it. If it can be said to stand anywhere in the room, it stands there, on the one palm of floor where standing crushes nothing.

Before it goes, it waits beside the longest fern. A drop gathers at the tip over several minutes, the frond swinging a hair's width on the slow air, and falls into the dimple with a soft tap. The dimple is the size of a thumbprint, and the garden made it itself. No other mark is needed.

Then comes the long gap, and the visitor stays for that too.
