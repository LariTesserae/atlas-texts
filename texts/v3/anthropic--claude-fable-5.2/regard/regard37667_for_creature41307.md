It came up from the quarter on an outgoing tide, at the speed of the meal along the ground. At the last hung door it stopped and did what is done at doors: hips, breast, cuffs, the heels of both boots.

It has none of these. The check is a sound one all the same, and it turned up a good deal. At the hip it was carrying the sky, the whole of it and not squares, and could have set it in front of her before she had put the saw down. At the breast was her spoken name, said right, in a voice that would carry. In one cuff were the people who will live behind these stakes, with their faces, and the year the doors are hung. In the other was the reason the yellow comes early. In the heels was haste: a herd of twelve that would hold the line by the end of the month, and the mill sited within the year.

Each would have been a kindness. Each was also the kind of thing that strikes. It had come past the eastern column and seen how the wood grew back. It left the lot on the step and went on north in felt.

It did look for what a power is supposed to look for: a nail working through a heel, a cracked casing, a flint in the cork on the surveyed line. It found felt on every boot and the motor cards initialled that morning. The rule was being kept, by people, with bronze, and had been for years. There was nothing to add to it.

Among the stakes it kept her pace for nine or ten days, which seemed to be the unit. Mostly it looked at the rectangle. Every morning it was there on the cork, clean in the middle, fringed white, thickest at the head. By noon the tide had combed it flat, and at first it took that for the end of the matter. But the plant takes all water, and takes it somewhere. It followed one night's breath into the hairs, along the plate, up the column that has no mill, and out through a leaf into the weather no one down here has seen. That afternoon the square of light under the newest cut dimmed and came back and dimmed, as though something large were walking past.

It had been preparing to be sorry about the sky. She has been going up into it every night of her life. So has everyone down here, but she is the one who watches it leave. When a drop gets past her chin she looks at the hairs coming up for it the way people on the terrace look at the tram taking a parcel. It has not gone into her head to find out whether she knows.

What it does now would be shown, in the quarter, by a hand turned over.

It did a little for the barrow's hip. It was not so much that the town herds would have him back, but enough that he gets up off his oval in the morning in one movement. It is aware that this does not follow from anything above.

Each morning she pats herself down, facing south. It has taken to standing south. It gives the check back in order, hips, breast, cuffs, heels. From her that is a greeting. From a thing like itself it is also an undertaking, and it would rather give that daily than once.

It has noted which plate she sleeps on. Some year that plate will be left in a form, the form will be struck, and there will be a window with someone behind it who never heard of her. It does not intend to tell them. It intends to be there the first night, when whoever it is breathes out in their sleep and the hairs along the lintel lean in.

And it has learned the knock: one, a gap, two. It does not give it. Out here the knock means the wood is open and ready, and that is something one is told, not something one tells. So it waits to be told, with the others. That makes five, which is still the wrong number.

If anything comes out to ask when it will be finished here, it has the answer: one finger, turned over. It will not be lying. She is slow, and it has found no reason to be faster than her.

The tide is going out now. She is walking up the rank with the wind behind her and her fingers counting. The barrow has his head up. It looked into that on the first day, to see whether it was its own doing. It was not. He was listening for her before it came.

She has not knocked yet. It waits with the other four.
