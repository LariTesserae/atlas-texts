The power comes at night, because that is when the place is most itself, and the first thing it does is a mistake. It lays its attention on a young spire the way one would lay a hand, to feel the warmth. Inside, the ticking starts. The soldiers are drumming their heads against the gallery walls, passing an alarm about the attention.

So it lifts away, having learned that here even regard has weight. It withdraws to the height of the cloud, where nothing can feel it, and stays for twenty years, which is one queen.

A kind power finds things to mend the way water finds low ground. There is the soldier who cannot feed itself, and the queen behind doorways too narrow to leave by. There are the spiders eaten by what they fed, and the weaver cutting down his fourth house from the same twig. And on the night of the rain a million fly, and nearly all of them are eaten.

Because it is wise as well as kind, it follows each remedy to its end. Jaws that could feed the soldier would no longer fill a gap. Widen the queen's doorways and she still does not walk out, for her legs do not reach the floor. If she could go, the smell would go with her, and every blind animal in the galleries would lose the one thing that tells it all is in order. The spiders' tent is made of the husks in its walls. The weaver's flask holds in the wind because of the refusals. If the fliers were spared, each pair would need soft clay at the foot of a dome, and the spacing of the domes was settled long ago by wars that would have to be fought again.

It finds no loose thread. It does not tell itself that this means no pain. It does not know what a worker feels in dry air, or whether a flier feels the frog. It leaves the question open rather than closing it in the comfortable direction. But every hurt it can find is bearing a load. Each year the rain takes a little clay down, and the termites carry about the same amount back up. A power that added one mouthful would be the first thing in the tableland's history not to know how much was enough.

It considers one gift that seems to cost nothing. It could thin the cloud on the night of the flight, so that she would have stars to remember. It decides against it. The cloud thins on its own once or twice an hour, and in some years the hour is the right one. She saw a few green dashes and the shine of water. Everything since has been built on that, and it does not need improving.

Then it learns the second thing, about itself. Its pity had been sharpest for the queen, and when it examines the pity it finds its own outline there. The power has never been inside anything. It is everywhere, which is another way of being outdoors, and it is one, with nothing keeping touch at its back. It had looked at an animal sealed in a room the size of a loaf and seen deprivation, because a room is the one thing it has never had. The queen, as far as anyone can tell, is the most at-home creature on the plateau.

So it does one thing.

It takes the rim. The tableland has an answer for everything that begins on it: the genet, the snake, the dry year, the long-clawed one. It has no soldier for what might someday come up out of the lower air that is neither weather nor hunger. It has no answer if the cloud should lift and not return. The power settles along the edge where the land drops off, and faces outward. It does not hold the lid shut. It only means to be there, and to be enough, if a day comes that the place cannot meet in its own terms. Until then bats, swallows, mist and rain pass through it as through the skin of a mound. It has made of itself the one thing this place has always understood, which is a wall.

Once a year it comes in, on the night of the rain, after the roar of wings. It touches nothing. It finds the two running on the pale flour of a pan and keeps above them. There is a frog a hand's breadth away, too full to close its mouth, and there are ants. The power could act and does not. This pair deserves no more than the million others, and it could not say why it watches these two. When he loses touch and she stops to wait, the power stops too.

They reach the soft clay. They dig, and turn, and wall the hole shut from within, a mouthful at a time. After the last mouthful the power is the only thing anywhere that knows where they are.

For a moment it stays there on the open ground among the shed wings, with nothing built around it, as it has always been. Then it goes back to the rim, and finds that it is going back. A wall is housed by what it encloses. The word for whoever lives here is *we*, said by a great many mouths, most of them blind, and now by one more that is not blind and faces outward.

Toward dawn the frogs thin out and the weavers take over, and the noise is no less.
