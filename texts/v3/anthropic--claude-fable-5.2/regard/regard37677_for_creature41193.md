You do not go round the column.

There are larger things you could do, and you go through them standing in the lit fog, the way a pour is gone through before anything is mixed. You could let the north wall go and bring the daylight down, and it would arrive as forty feet of water. You could lift the keeper up the shaft and set them in the weather they were born to, where they would stand like an eel on a bank. You could send down thirty hands and a foreman, and the keeper would stay behind the iron until they were gone. You could finish the hall in a night, as far east as it was ever meant to go, and take away the one thing this person has been asked for.

Every one of these would happen to them, and so has everything else. The crews stopped the way the defrost starts, on someone else's schedule, and nobody sounded a siren for it. Whatever you are, you had better not be more weather.

So the first thing you do is sit down. You choose the edge of the green circle, side on to the column and well clear of the rail, because the skip is the clock that matters this hour. Wet concrete gives about that long before it is fit only to be barrowed to waste, and nothing in the tally suggests a skip was ever wasted. You are costing them something already. You keep your hands in sight and wait.

The bird comes back first. It works the channel edge to within a yard of your boot, picking midges off the moss, and that seems to be what the column was waiting on. The keeper comes round the iron.

They are small, as the chalk said. Their age can't be read, for the eels' reason. They look at you for about as long as a new crack would get, and then they go to the skip, because it won't wait.

It is work for two, and always was, since the tipping bar has a grip at each end. The keeper has a way round that, a length of pipe and a wedge, the pipe worn bright where a shoulder goes. You get up, put your hands to the far grip, and stand there. The keeper looks at the pipe, then leaves it leaning on the rail.

You work at their pace and no faster. When the skip is empty they go to the column and pull one stroke, right to left. The chalk is not offered and you do not reach for it.

Some skips later the green wakes near the springing of the arch. The keeper sits down on the struck boards with their back to the pour. After a while they move the folded coat, and you sit.

"There's no tea," they say. It takes two tries, like a valve that has not been turned in years.

"That's all right."

The band glows. Water hammer goes through the mains, and you find you are holding still too, until the blow has run out westward.

"Are they coming back?"

"No."

You give them the rest, because it is theirs. The money stopped. The men who wanted the hall are dead. The water it runs under has had a bridge over it for a long time, and from the shaft head on a clear evening the lamps can be seen going across. Nobody decided to leave a child down here. Nobody decided anything, which is how it was done. Someone should have come down the shaft and said so, and you say that too.

They take it the way they take the hammer, stopped and listening to it travel. It travels a long way.

When it has run out, you tell them what they are looking at. That is noon. The sun is over the water, and some of it gets down through the ice. They have kept it every day without having a name for it.

Then you tell them about the eels, since no one has asked the eels anything either. They are river fish, waiting on a season that cannot reach them. When it comes they turn silver and go down to the sea, and the only way out of here is the pumps. An eel will climb anything that is rough and wet. This is the part the keeper makes you say twice.

About the hall you say only what is true. No one above wants it. Everything below lives off the wet edge of it. Which of those counts is for the one who pours.

"The shaft is where it was," you say. "You've never had a relief. I'll stand one. I know what a blow sounds like when it stops short. Go late. Toward dusk it's no brighter up there than under a lamp."

They finish the bay first, because a bay left half poured is a cold joint. Then they wipe the tally with a sleeve and do not begin it again. They pick up the coat and put it on. It is a man's coat and comes to their knees. Somebody gave it to them once. They walk west between two rows of rain, and the bird goes ahead of them from lamp to lamp.

You stay. All night you do the keeper's job, or the part of it that is listening. The siren sounds, the frost comes down in curtains, and the rain falls from the bolt heads. You do not touch the forms. When the hammer goes through the mains you stop and stand until it has run out, and every blow runs out. That is the only power you use in this place, and nobody could tell it from luck.

They come down a little before the next midday. There is mud on them to the shin, a pale grey mud unlike anything below. The cuffs of the coat are wet. In one lime-cracked hand is a mussel shell, both halves, the hinge still working. They carry it to where the frost has come away and hold it up against the dark glass, beside the ones hung in the ice.

"It goes out," they say. "By itself."

"And comes back."

"I saw."

They look at the next bay, the clean boards and the steel waiting inside. You do not ask. You tell them one more thing: whenever they are on the ladder, the watch is kept, and they need not look for you. Then you go west, in the rain, since you are not narrow enough for the dry lane.

What they did with it was not yours to choose or to watch. But the hall keeps accounts, and anyone coming down a year later can read them.

The rust is off the rungs of the shaft ladder, on the left side most.

The bay numbers still climb, but there are fewer green bays than there were, and the pale has drawn closer to the wet edge. Whoever pours here now stops when they like.

On the last column is the tally of skips, wiped and rewritten as before. Beneath it is a second row that has never been wiped, single strokes pulled right to left, one under each midday gap. Days are kept.

At the sump, beside the rising main, a ramp has been cast. Its surface is left rough off the tamp, the only concrete in the hall not finished with a float, and a trickle is led down it from a weep in the pipe. Its arrises have been rubbed round by hand. There are fewer eels in the launders. One lies at the foot of the ramp with its flanks gone pewter and its eye grown large, facing up.

On the struck boards, one bay behind the wet edge, the coat is folded. Next to it is a can with a wire handle and two tin mugs. One is stained brown. The other is clean and kept upside down against the drip.

The wagtail calls now. It gives two notes from the channel edge under the last lamp. Then there is a wait, about as long as the siren's, and from somewhere near the forms the two notes come back, not very well done, and a little flat.
