It came up by the train. It could have arrived any way it liked, but eleven days on a rack railway is how you learn why a place is where it is, so it rode in the van with the crate of coats and the frozen cans.

It boarded at the bottom wearing two intentions. The first was rain. From below, in the cold, rain is the obvious thing to bring a town that scrapes its water off a pipe. Then it watched the crew talk for six days. When they came to rain they made the spilled cup, several times over. In Linehead a spilled cup is a small disgrace that has followed a man for fifty-eight years. Rain would be that on every roof, on hair that has never been wet, on meal that would go to paste in the hollows, on varnish that has known one kind of weather since before the trails. The four hundred would become four thousand, and the first by-law has never been tried against four thousand. It took that intention off at milepost ninety and folded it into the crate.

The second was the assumption that something up there was waiting for it. Nobody had sent for it. Things on the Hob wait for the sun, the herd, the ninth day and the varnish, and have never been seen to wait for anything else. That one came off at forty.

It stepped down at the top in shirtsleeves, like anyone, onto warm stone.

It walked the town through one still season. It watched a linesman sweep lice off an insulator gently, on account of having to see them again. It stood in Number Six and worked out what nineteen months of shifted pots costs a shed that lives by the ingot. It read the first by-law. Four hundred people on three cups a day live beside four million animals they could inconvenience, and they have put their buildings on legs. It had come up to see whether the place wanted something large, patient and well-disposed, and it found the post filled.

So it took the by-law for its own. Nothing is built across a trail, and it took this to cover the finger-wide ones as well.

It found her line, running from the gantry past the open end of Six to Tally Row. It also found what the pavement does not show. A chain drawn twice a day for thirty years leaves a mark, and a chain drawn once a year leaves nothing anyone could see. It could see it, a branch going in under the crane rail and coming out again.

Her morning was some months off. It waited, which on the Hob draws no notice.

It was in the shed before her, one pace from the animal, which is where you stand. She did not know it was there. She put her palm on the plates. It already knew what was under them. It had known since the bottom of the hill and had told no one, the dredgers having settled that matter thirty years ago with a surveyor's pry bar. What it knew asked nothing of it. There was nothing under the crane rail to mend and nothing to end.

It laid its hand flat beside hers anyway. For the length of the wait there were two hands on the plates, and hers was still the coldest. It had taken what she does for asking. Standing there, it saw that she stopped expecting an answer years ago and has come every year since, so it cannot be a question. It is a call paid once a year, cold hand first. The sun came in through the open wall and the ticking started under her fingers and under its, and it felt like a tin roof warming. She wrote the date, small, with the seven crossed. There is room now for about thirty-nine.

It did not warm her hand. The hand is her name, and how she is known in the dark.

It did not tell her what it knew. The Late Herd will be at the open wall in nine years, at a length a day, and she has the dates to spare. It did not start the stopped one, or thicken the rime, or close any line in the pavement. It placed no bet in the potmen's book. It knows what is under the plates, but not what a herd does when it meets one that has stopped. That is up to the herd. It would like to find out the way the town will, from a roof.

It went down with the ingots. It put on a coat at forty and a second at ninety, like anyone.

Its one piece of work was at the bottom, in gloves. The three cups come off the lines and are the Hob's own arithmetic, thin and honestly kept. The forgetting was not the Hob's. It turned out to be a form. On the up manifest, water was the fourth line, under dredge teeth. The clerk fills the form from the top, late in the day, and the can store is across a cold yard. Nobody at the lowland office wished Linehead dry.

It stood one pace off from the clerk who orders the printing. The clerk stepped back and it followed. The manifests were due for a new run in any case, and on the new ones water is the first line.

That is the whole of it. The cans come up every ninth day now. The town does not count on them and will not for years, which is the town's business and probably right. In time the scrapers may let the tally run a household over. Give or take, the figure has always gone, and this would be the give.

The stationmaster stands the cans in a row on the platform, where they tick as they thaw. The smaller children lay a hand flat on them, the coldest thing most of them will ever touch. Asked where the water comes from, they make the sign for a spilled cup, and mean him. It has let that stand.
