The power comes on foot. It could come otherwise, but the inhabitant has to be walked out to, and that holds for anyone.

The visitor comes first into a town and stands in the engine hall with the stroke in their knees. Anything could be done here. No hand rests on any valve wheel, and the visitor has hands. The relief valve could be eased onto its seat, the heavy governor weight filed, the short belt relaced. Each would be a kindness to a machine, and each is a few grams of the inhabitant's body.

The larger mendings are open too. Stop the stacks and the cloud would rain itself out within the week, and there would be rills by the month's end, and silence. Flies could be sent for. The sun could be unpinned. Each of these would give the plateau some other life in place of the one it has grown.

The visitor looks at the valve wheels for a while and understands the builders better than expected. They saw something new and sharp and could not bear for it to be touched. They arranged for nothing to be lost, so thoroughly that they are not here themselves. Every repair on offer is their move again, one more governor. The visitor keeps their hands in their pockets.

Before leaving, the visitor rides the tram once round the loop. The bell rings at every stop, as it always has, and the visitor gets off where they got on. This does nothing for the tram, and the visitor does not pretend otherwise.

Then the visitor walks out. The moss gives underfoot and slowly rises back. By the time the plumes are a smudge, the tracks are gone.

Midway among three towns the visitor sits down beside a lava hollow. The intention is to listen until the inhabitant is known. That turns out to have no end. A thing that never returns to an arrangement cannot be learned like a song, only kept company. The visitor counts swells for a time, the sun being no use, and then stops counting. Each swell comes up through the visitor as it comes up through the sleeved trunks, without preference.

Once, the visitor says a word aloud. The figure takes it as it takes everything. It carries the word along the cold layer, crosses it with the strokes, and some minutes later puts it over the rim. No swell arrives changed on its account. That settles less than it seems to. The visitor notes that nothing they mean to do depends on the answer, and is relieved.

One thing could end the inhabitant, and it is not wear. Clocks hung on one wall pull each other into step. If the engines could hear one another they would in time agree, and the figure would stop mid-stride and become a fixture, like the rime. So the visitor lies down with an ear to the lava glass and follows one town's stroke all the way to the next town's governor. It gets there with too little left to tug a weight. Sixty kilometres of moss keep the towns deaf to each other. Nobody planned that. The visitor leaves it as found.

Then the flowers. The visitor stands a long time in front of a spike as thick as a thigh, every floret open, and does the arithmetic first. Rates in no simple ratio never bring the figure back to where it has been, but they bring it as near as you like to every arrangement it could hold. One of those is the arrangement in which every stroke in the air arrives at a single place together. The visitor works out which hollow, which rosette, which swell. The number is long. On that swell one floret will be rocked by more than the width of a grain.

So the "yet" is honest. Had the arithmetic come out otherwise, the choice would have been harder, and the visitor knows it.

A fingertip would do it now. The visitor does not lift one. The flower is losing nothing in the cold that looking can find. And the first thing ever to happen on the plateau is going to be done by the plateau, out of its own errors, by a pollinator already on its way. The visitor declines to get there first. What follows that grain is seed, then a dead rosette, and some swell later a column letting go and the first rubble at the foot of a scarp. That is everything the works were built against, and the visitor will not prevent it either.

At the rim the gutter turns through its elbow and takes its water back inland. The lamp is lit on its post. The sound goes out past it. The sound is the one thing the plateau gives away, and it gives it to nobody. The visitor thinks that this, at least, can be mended without touching anything, and climbs down.

It is four and a half kilometres of dry column. At the foot there is pale dust. The sound comes down thinned, but it is all there: the stroke, the low B. The visitor had meant to be the one who receives it.

But the dust is combed. It lies in ridges a hair high, a fine grain that comes in from one side and is crossed by another. Down here nothing was built against anything. For as long as there has been pressure, the heavy swells have been moving this dust a grain's width at a time, no two alike. The river has had a delta all along. Only the plateau's bookkeeping called it lost.

So the visitor is not needed here either. The visitor sits down anyway on bare rock, back against the columns, clear of the ridges, and waits about eleven seconds.

The swell comes. It does not have the weight of the one before, or quite its bearing.

The visitor stays for the next one.
