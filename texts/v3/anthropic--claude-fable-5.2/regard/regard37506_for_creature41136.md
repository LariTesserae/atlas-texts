The power that takes notice of the hollow comes to it the way anyone does: flat scrub, a brighter patch of ground, and then an edge. It stops at the edge.

It stays on the rim for a year. Rain comes about once in that time, and it will not judge a life after seeing only the dry part. At noon it stands over the white disc and darkens nothing, which in this place is no distinction. The swifts go silent as they pass it, and it keeps the same rule.

It can do a great deal, and because it is kind, the first thing it thinks about is water.

It could shift the seam a hair's width inside the rock, so that the drip fell on the cone. But the mound has been growing a fingernail a lifetime for longer than the ochre has been fading, and the frogs round the basin have nowhere else to be. It could send more rain. But the hollow is gypsum, which water carries away. The place exists because rain is rare, and every broken face on the cone healed in the dry. A generous century would slump the walls and take the flour. So the water stays where it is.

It could carry her. That would take a moment. She would be set down among the frogs, on stone she has never felt under her, and behind her the cone would be a heap of rubble with a clean dish on top.

Then it notices the distance. From the lowest stone of the cone to the foot of the drip mound is fifteen paces or so. She walks farther than that every day, going round. The hollow holds its light for most of an hour at dusk, and she could be there and back before it failed. So distance is not what turns her. The power does not know what does. It could find out, the way it could find out what is inside the cone, by opening it.

Perhaps it is fear. Perhaps it is whatever keeps the swifts to the wall. Perhaps it is not the water at all. The hammerstone and the dish are on that side, and whoever painted her came from there. And each evening, the last thing in front of her before she turns is not the wall but the flour.

Late in the year, on a morning the power cannot tell from any other, she climbs to the summit and stands in her dish with her legs straight. By afternoon the column comes down on her. The power had not sent it and had not seen it coming. After that it stops thinking of her as something to be rescued.

It asks instead what has ever been done for her here. There is one thing. Someone crossed. They did it many times, since the ridge of paint is many coats thick. They needed to know where the middle was, and they paid for the knowledge by coming to her.

The power does not need to know where the middle is. It decides to go anyway.

There is no red in the hollow that was not carried down, so it carries some, a lump of ochre from a cutbank two days' walk away. It takes a body with palms and heels, and goes down by the holds on the south wall, which fit. At the basin it lifts one palmful of water. The frogs shift and settle. It grinds the ochre with the hammerstone in the dish, over the old white paste.

It waits for dusk, when the walls get their curve back, because that is when a person would cross. Then it steps onto the flour, which takes each print deep and exact. It walks straight to the lowest stone of the cone, where she is standing with her neck out.

She draws her head in, without hurry, and waits, as she must have done as many times as there are coats. It lays one more along the ridge with the side of a thumb. It touches nothing else. It leaves the ring of dashes on the wall as faded as it found them. Those are for people, who can mend them if they come.

It walks back in its own prints, heel into heel, so that there is one line and not two, and the line is pressed firm. It leaves the dish with red drying in the bottom. It climbs out. It has taken nothing, and it does not stay.

At noon the next day, from the rim, one dash near the middle is brighter than all the others. The prints cannot be seen. Nothing in the hollow has a shadow to show them by.

They come back at dusk. She comes down to the lowest stone, sets her forefeet at the edge of the flour, and puts her neck out to its full length. The flour in front of her is not smooth. She stays that way a long while, longer than the geckos take to begin. Then she turns, the way she always turns, and climbs back to the scrape.

Tracks do not last forever here, only longer than anyone has been back.

Some evenings later, and no one is counting, the nearest print has a second mark pressed inside it, blunt and clawed. Only the nearest has one. The rest of the line runs on, untouched, to the north wall, where the drip is.
