---

What comes has finished a great many things in other places, and knows what that was worth. It could arrive any way it likes. It climbs the outer face, tier by tier, through the sheeting water, because the old floors are part of the place and it means to see all of it before it sees the top.

It looks first for anything actually wrong. It puts an arm into the face, past the brown and into the black, and feels the rot working, warm and even. It tastes the film on a pale course. It stands at the selvage for a day and a night and measures the wind. A blight in the new stem, a cooling down below, a wind beginning to fail: it would act on any of these and not think twice. It looks hard, so that if it ends by doing nothing, that will be a finding and not a habit.

The place is sound.

Then it walks the lanes. It waits at the rectangle until it has seen the slug go round and back. It stands among the ribs with the chord in its teeth. This takes a season, and it lets it. Most of the harm it has ever done, it did quickly. By the end it loves the place, which it takes as a reason for more care.

Last, it climbs the nine ledges, and from the top there is a town.

This is the one dangerous spot on the plain. From here and nowhere else the outlines square up, and what they square up into is something left at its first course, and the visitor is exactly the kind of thing that could raise the rest. It feels the wish to. It stands on the top ledge and asks whose wish it is. The plant has never stood here. The bird has, and saw lanes, and flies. The wish came up the stair with the visitor.

It goes through the gifts anyway, one at a time, because a wish can be one's own and still be right.

The walls. It could let them outrun the floor, a course a season, until there were rooms. But it has watched how a wall stands here. It stands by being laid. A finished wall would stop being laid, and would go under at the speed of the floor, four courses and then none. Everything finished on this plain is a scale's depth down and sinking.

The ribs. It could close the body-length between the rows. They would meet mouth to mouth and seal, and there would be a roof, the first on the plain. There would be no wind across the pipes, and no pipes, no chord and no hole in it, nowhere to nest and nothing to sing with. The gap between the ribs is the one part of the town that is lived in.

The buds. It does not put a hand near them. It has known canes, elsewhere, that flower once, all together, after longer than anyone remembers, and are straw by the next season. It does not know that this is such a cane. It is not going to find out from a bud.

The chord. It could bring birds enough for every rib, and on some evening the notes would go out one after another from the top to the very bottom, and there would be only the weir. That is the resolution, and the visitor would like to hear it. But it has counted the flies in the lanes, and there are flies for the birds there are. And it has understood the low notes. They are the ribs still open. Every bird that will ever hatch here needs one of them to be sounding. A chord that resolved would be a town with no room left in it, and the plant has never once built that.

The slug. It could lift the slug across the door gap, or set it down inside the house at last. Inside is green floor with nothing sweet on it. The slug has not been kept out. Everything it wants is on the wall.

One more, and this one it knows is for itself: it would like the place to know it was here. The plain has a way of saying things. A hand over the mouth of the lowest rib, and a note that has never once gone out would go out, and in every lane a bird would stop walking and raise its head. But that language has one sentence, and it means *someone lives here now*. It would be a lie. The visitor keeps its hand at its side.

It comes down the nine ledges. The town stays at the top, which is where it is kept.

Toward dark the birds come in from the lanes, and the chord thins from the top. One high note is left, to the east. The visitor knows by now which rib that is, and which bird.

It finds the bird in a lane, two hundred paces short of the row, standing on the low wet weave among the last of the flies. Nothing has hurt it. It is old, for something the size of a fist, and it has used up its flights. The throat works. There is the tick, like a wet twig.

The visitor could put the flights back. It goes through that again, for this one, and it is harder than it was on the stair, and it comes out the same. Mended, the bird would come to this lane on some other evening, and the visitor would not be here then. And under this bird's nest is every bird that had the rib before it. None of them was held back. That is what the nest stands on.

But the lane is wet, and the wind is on it, and a forearm down the rib the breath of the old floors comes up warm. That much needs no going through.

It lays its hand flat on the weave. Warm water rises around the hand. The bird looks at it the way it would look at a ledge, and, since nothing here has ever given it a reason not to, steps on.

The visitor carries it up the lane to the row and holds it level with the lip. The bird steps off beside the bud. For a moment a count of buds along that row would come out one too many. Then it drops in feet first, with its wings shut, and the note goes out.

Over the plain there is only the roar and the low pipes, as on any good night. Every bird is accounted for. It is true.

The visitor does not leave yet. Something here has kept a room for a very long time, and there is one thing left to do, which is to accept it.

At the rectangle it considers the door gap, too narrow for a shoulder and too tall for anything. It does not widen the gap. It works out what the gap would fit, and for one night it is that: narrow, and a good deal taller than it needs to be. It goes in.

Inside are four courses of wall at the height of a knee, and fog for a roof. On the pale course the slug is going round. The visitor lies down. The floor gives a little, the way a dock does. Warm water rises along its whole length, and the heat comes up like breath through cloth. It listens to the low pipes. They are sounding for whoever has not come yet, as they always have, and the visitor does not mistake itself for that. The plant has had two kinds of tenant, the gone and the not yet come. Tonight it has a guest. Whether the plant can tell, the visitor does not find out, and does not press.

In the morning it goes out by the gap, and over the selvage, and down through the tiers into the cloud.

In the house there is a long narrow place where the leaf-scales lie pressed flat. By evening they have lifted. The slug goes on round the top of the wall. The house has had a guest now. It still has its one resident, who has still not been inside.

In the east the note stays out all that night and all the next morning. About noon it comes back, which means the bird has gone the forearm's length down to the pad, onto its own down and onto all the others. That night the note sounds after dark, and the plain has its bad news, a day late. It sounds every night for most of a season.

Then one evening the chord thins from the top, and that note goes out with the rest.
