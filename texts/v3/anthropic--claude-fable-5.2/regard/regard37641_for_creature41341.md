It comes down the way everything comes down, through the roof crack with the rain. It takes up no room. The fog does not part around it, and the film does not brighten where it rests.

The first thing it does is stay. It could have understood the room in an instant, but that is different from having been there. It stays eleven weeks: some four thousand fogs, one molt, and a dry spell of nineteen days.

Then it goes through what it could give.

It could give shade, but the spider has never met a shadow. The only dark place on this wall is a fresh scar, dry and far too hot, and the spider would walk around a kindness of that shape.

It could cool the floor crack. But the heat is the light. The walls would dim, the stain would pale, and the flies would stop coming down.

It could send more flies. But the band is a thumb wide and feeds what it feeds, and more flies would only mean more of them going to the bright wall.

It could send a second spider. It considers this longest, because this is the gift it would want for itself, and it has learned to be careful with those. A second spider on this wall is a rival for one hand's length of stain, or else a meal. The power looks into the spider for something that is waiting for company and finds nothing of the kind. Its kind is plentiful above, in the damp around the steaming ground. Nothing is ending here that is not ending everywhere.

It looks hardest at the hunger, since that is the thing it would be least forgiven for getting wrong. During the dry spell it goes into the scoop, into the drawn-in legs, and looks at what is actually there. It finds a body turned low, like a pilot flame, with no dread in it and no counting of days. It looks at the hundred and ninety-nine misses and finds that a miss leaves nothing behind. The spider climbs back to the chip, and the next fog is the first fog. Had the power found anguish there, it would have done a great deal more than it does, and it would not have been delicate about it.

It looks at the flies the same way, and at the pale commas in the green. It looks at the stain, which asks for nothing and has nothing to ask with.

So the room is not a wound. It is a narrow place, and something fits it.

The power finds only one thing that is a lack and not a condition. The spider's eyes are good and its rush is good. Its misjudging comes from the room, which gives it a dark shape on bright glass and nothing to measure by. Anywhere else, a hunter has a pebble or a stem or an edge of shade to judge by.

So in a wet week, a flake lets go of the wall to the left of the chip. It was going to fall within the month anyway. There is a tick, and the spider flattens. The flake lands where flakes land, at the base of the wall, except that it comes to rest against the green edge of the stain, a little more than a hand's length below the scoop. Within the hour it is black, with water pooling around it.

That is all the power does to the room. It is not a kindness to the flies, and the power does not pretend otherwise. It has taken a side, by the width of a few flies a year.

It does one other thing, which changes nothing. The room keeps no record. The husks and the shed skins go over into the crack, and there is nothing under the chip to show how long it has been lived in. The power keeps the count of every fog, every rush, and every time the legs spread at the sound of a drop on salt. This is no use to the spider. The power keeps the count anyway and does not call it a gift.

Then it goes back up the way it came, or it stays. Nothing in the room would show the difference.

The spider learns the flake first through its feet, by passing it on the way back up with nothing. Then it learns it through its eyes, over some days.

The crack exhales. The whiteness comes down and stands, then settles. A fly has stopped at the orange edge, beaded, on the far side of the black flake. The spider, beaded, stays where it is.

Twenty minutes later there is the sizzle, the hiss, and the white. When it clears, a fly is wiping its wings on the near side of the black. The spider goes straight down the wet glass while the ceiling's rain is still pattering.

The pool, lower by a finger's width, starts filling again.
