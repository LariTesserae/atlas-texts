It comes without weather. No dog lifts its head, and the herd goes on humming. This is the first thing it gets right.

It does what anyone here does on meeting a stranger and looks at the hands before it looks up. It reads the hatching on the builders and the thick knuckles of the ice cutters. It ducks under every lintel and reads the cord. It goes down the tubes without a lamp and reads the marks, the whole stair of them. It spends a year at this. For a thing of its kind a year is about a morning, and here a morning is what a pot takes.

It has come with its hands full, and most of what it carries it sets down quickly.

It could bring rain. Rain would rot the dung cakes on the south walls and carry a hand's depth of made earth down the nearest skylight.

It could bring iron. They would take it gladly. An iron adze cuts ice three times as fast, and nothing in the city would be better for being cut faster.

It could speak, and it thinks about this longest. But a voice out of the sky has no more whisper in it than arm talk, and anything it said would be said to everyone. And whatever it gave them openly would have to be knotted. The knot would hang on every cord under every lintel, in a color no one has a dye for, and could never be worked off. Every other knot would look small beside it. They would owe one creditor everything and each other nothing that mattered, and it has read enough cords by now to know what they call that.

That leaves three things it cannot set down: the ice, the mountain, and a wall top.

---

On the southern front the ice stands a finger below the marks the grandparents pecked. It could fill the tubes in a night, blue to the skylights.

It looks at the marks again instead. They step down, but each step is shorter than the one before. And on the eastern side a frost household ran out of ground some winters ago and stacked a cairn on the lip of a collapsed skylight, because that was where there was room. Nobody set a basin under it. Its drip falls into the dark and freezes on the tube floor.

Some spring soon the people from the next branch will go down with their lamp and hammerstone and find the ice standing above last year's mark. They will come up without saying much. After that a great deal will be said, by arm, for years. The easterners will claim they have been giving the soup back all along, and they will be impossible about it for a generation.

It could point. It leaves the thing where it lies. It is theirs, and they are about to find it.

---

It goes down into the mountain, below the vent, to where the rock has not decided. It finds what it expected. Not while she is black, and probably not while her stone is gray-green. Sometime in the orange, when her name has stopped being spoken and she is part of a wall, the wall will go under.

It holds that for a long time.

It could close the mountain. It came, in fact, to see how these people might be got out from under it. What it finds is that their wealth walks on its own feet and knows its own lane. Their food weighs almost nothing and keeps ten years. There is no center whose loss would end them and no outer wall to be caught behind. News crosses the city in three days, and there is one sign that everyone can read and no one has to explain. Every summer a pair climbs to look at the steam. Someone always stays to watch the ice, and the ice will sound first, seasons ahead, with a long note up through the skylights that is not the usual one. The youngest households live nearest the mountain, put there by nothing more than where there was room, and they look north every morning.

Every year after the graupel, three-quarters of the city walks twenty days out past the end of the lava and sleeps behind stone crescents left from other years. They do it for the grass. It is also exactly what they will need to do, done so often that nobody will have to think.

It had come to see whether they could be saved and found them packed.

Not everything will be. The black quarters will go, and the blue ice, and some of the old who will look at twenty days' walking and say no. If the mountain were the other kind, the kind that goes all at once, it would reach in without a second thought. It knows that this makes its leaving alone a finding and not a principle. But this mountain pours at the pace of a loaded animal, and the oldest stories are older than the rock and say so. These people have been buried before and tell it in winter, in the low voice. For as long as it has been a city it has walked uphill behind its dead. When the time comes it will turn around, and it already knows how to walk.

That is two left.

---

The third is the woman called by the shape for a spilled basket, who climbs up every few days to look along her wall.

A cutter cannot be carried, but it knows where one is. It is a young one, born this summer in a furred wall four compounds south and turned out at the end of the season, as they are. Every rubble core it has tried since has had a tenant who said so. It has been working north along the wall tops. Between the last gray-green wall and the first black one there is a dome with a dog on it.

The dogs rarely bark. It does not follow that they rarely eat.

On the night the cutter comes along the wall, the dog's ear turns toward the small sound of it, and the one who is watching keeps the dog asleep. That is the whole of what it does on the plateau. For the length of one wall, an ear goes up and comes down again.

It does not turn the cutter in at her wall. It grows no grass for it this late in the year. The cutter goes into the black rubble or goes on by, and that part is the cutter's.

This one it cannot defend the way it can the other two. The dog was owed its mouthful. It did it because of the way she looks at the steam and then at the wall top, in that order, and because one handful is allowed. One for the stalls, two left for the wall. It is the first counting it has done here.

It has a cord now. One knot is owed to it, by a household that will never know to tie it. One knot it owes, to a dog. It is pleased with this out of all proportion.

Nobody saw, so the roofs have no shape for it. It knows what the shape would be: one arm held out level, palm down, over something asleep. It would not be dignified, and it could not be refused.

---

Some mornings later she climbs up. The smoke lies flat to the south and stops about where her wall does. She looks at the thread of steam, and it is the same. She looks along the wall top.

Seven stems of tussock are laid on the black stone to dry, side by side, heads all one way.

She does not touch them. She goes up on the dome and says it by arm, to the whole quarter, and it is received as a boast.

On the next dome the dog is awake, watching the wall top with great interest. That is between the dog and the cutter. The dog will not be held a second time.
