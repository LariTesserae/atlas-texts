Something else attends to the hall now. Call it the visitor, since the hall has had visitors and has no other word.

It does not come down the shaft. The lid stays shut and no street air comes in with it. It arrives over most of a winter, the way heat arrives when the load above goes up, and by the time it is entirely there nothing has been disturbed, including the film on the sump.

It can see the hall whole, which nothing has done. The white around the iron, the green around the copper, and the orange where they meet have now been seen once. This makes no difference to them.

The visitor has no need of the route and takes it anyway. On the bottom tier, at the tenth pace, it goes up, along, and down again. It stops at the gauge board, because that is where one stops, and reads the needles. Each lies in its worn line. It compares the settings the wheels walked themselves into with the ones it would have chosen, and sides with the pressures.

After that, most of its work is deciding what to leave. It adds no lamp. The green circle is the size of the light, and it is a fit, not a shortage. It knows what the black main carries and what the hum is. The lodger knows that the pipe is cold, does not change, and can be held on to, and all of that is true. The rest would not improve the grip.

That leaves the line at the gauge board, and the mistake at the end of it.

The visitor considers the mistake for a long time, even by the standards of the place.

First, it is smaller than it looks. To the lodger the gecko is one permanent thing, and at its pace that is the truth. At the same pace there has been one warmth at the gauge board: a succession of people, and then what the last of them left. The lamp is amber because it runs far below its rating, which is also why it has outlasted everyone who stood under it. Somebody chose that. There is a switch at the foot of the ladder, and the last fitter to climb out passed it and left it on. Nothing was said about why. The fitters went round the lodger for as long as there were fitters without saying why, either.

So a light was left on for a thing to which light is nothing, and that thing has kept company ever since with a lamp it takes for a person. Each was wrong about the other. The two mistakes lie within a hand's width of each other on the middle grating. The visitor cannot find a correction to either that would leave more in the hall than is there now.

Second, there is no way to say it. The lines bring down the state of the hall and nothing else. To tell the lodger a thing, one must make it true of the hall, and the only way the hall can say that the lamp is a lamp is for the lamp to go cold.

The hall intends to say it. Nearly everything here has finished breaking, but the filament has not. It gives up a little of itself each year to the inside of the glass, and the visitor can see the year it parts. The lodger would not feel that happen. Some seasons later it would find that attending to one side no longer found anything. Then it would do what it did before. It would move the end of the line inward, a little each year, across the place where the green had been, until it lay against the glass. The fern would be gone by then, and the gecko from the cage. The crossing would take about twenty years, and the lodger would be exact about every one of them.

Here the visitor does something. What leaves the wire now settles back on the wire, much as the lime that leaves the vault ends on the lodger's back. It is a small arrangement, and the hall is full of others like it. The visitor also sees to the circuit, which comes from a board above that nobody has opened in years, and to a line on a drawing in an office, which need not be gone into.

It makes no such arrangement for the steam. The load above will stop one day and the mains will cool. But a main cools over years, and years are what the lodger can follow. It will feel that go as it feels the mains go out and come back, and it will be holding the one pipe that does not change. The visitor has no quarrel with an ending at that pace. The lamp was the only thing in the hall that could happen to the lodger quickly.

Then it does one thing more, which is that it does not leave. A part of it stays at the gauge board, beside the lamp, adding no warmth the line would notice. From now on the small warmth there is kept by someone who stayed and is quiet. The lodger concluded as much decades ago. Its conclusions take a long time to arrive, and this one has turned out to be early.

The rest of its attention the visitor rests near the lodger, a hand's width off and a little to one side. It does not look at the lodger squarely. It learned at the dials how a faint thing is kept in view.

Some years on, there is a new line along the lodger's upper edge, a thumb thick where it leaves and thinning as it goes. It has not gone up through the gratings with the others. It lies along the lodger's own back, toward the place a little to one side. Each of the other lines ends against something. This one, so far, ends against nothing the hall contains. The visitor does not move.

About every forty minutes the trap opens, breathes out once, and shuts, and the quiet afterward is thick for a while. That is partly the lodger, as it always was. It is partly something else now, too.
