What noticed did nothing at first, and went on doing nothing for one whole round. It followed a lane behind the minder, out across the shelf, a few fields to a tide. It learned the count of beds and the places where the count goes wrong. At the outfalls it stood back while the minder knocked down the slope, and it listened farther down than the minder can. There are things down there. They hold at a certain depth and come no higher. None of them has ever been driven; the knock arrives, and they turn back as their parents turned.

It thought hard about leaving all of this as it was. The place is whole. The minder is not in pain. It has work it understands and has never once failed at, and a long faithfulness is not a fault to be corrected by whoever happens by.

But there had been an arrangement, and it had two sides. The minder keeps the field. The house feeds the minder and, when the crop is cut, fetches it home. One side had been kept since before the stone facings finished slumping. The other had not been kept at all. What noticed did not find the minder unhappy. It found it unpaid.

Some things it would not do. It would not lift the sea off the plain or wake the house. The people who heaped these beds are finished, and their finishing belongs to them. It would not take the stones out of the minder's hands. It would not bring urchins back to the holdfasts while the minder still walked the walls, to be picked off one at a time into the basket. And it would not simply say that no one was coming. That would have been true, and the minder would have had nowhere to put it. Deciding is not a minder's work. It would have gone on to the next field, knowing.

Nothing needed deciding anyway. The house decided long ago, the same in every generation: fed from the house, fetched home with the harvest. The crop had said when, to the minder's own hands. All that was missing was someone to come, and at harvest a house takes on whoever turns up.

So it went to the house first. It stayed in the swell a good while, down through the floors of trodden clay, hearth under hearth. It took a sickle from where one had been put away: a curved haft of black wood with flint teeth set in pitch, their edges carrying the gloss that flint only gets from seasons of cutting stems. It meant to bring it back. And it made up something wrapped. What was in it is the house's business.

Then it took the size and shape of someone who could be spared, and walked out along the causeway between the double rows of kelp, with a hand out to each side.

The minder was on the lip of the outfall. It had knocked, and the sound had gone out and come back off nothing. Then, behind it, on the landward side, the kelp on the causeway moved against the tide, stipe by stipe, both rows, coming nearer. The minder turned around. It did not lift the stones. Whatever comes out from the house along the causeway, touching both rows, is not what the stones are for.

The one who had come held out what it carried. The minder took it and held it unopened until the blades leaned back. Then it ate, standing, facing the crop.

Afterward the one who had come said three things, in the speech that went with the baskets and the antler picks. *It is ready. We are late. Nothing got in.* All three were true. Then it put the sickle in the minder's hands and let them read it. The fingers went along the haft and over the teeth and stopped on the gloss. The minder gave it back handle first and stepped down off the wall-top to let the cutter by.

The cutting began at the outfall fields and worked inward. It was done by hand. The plain is wide and the cutter was one, and the cutter let that matter, since the minding had not been done any faster. The kelp was cut a forearm above the holdfast, above the pale new growth, so that it would come again. The eelgrass was cut a hand above the silt. The swaths were left lying where they fell. The minder went a field ahead, checking the standing crop, stipe and blade and the edge of the plot, as it always had. Each time the blades leaned back there was something wrapped.

No one knocked at the outfalls now. Down the slope, a season came when the sound did not arrive, and then another. Something came up through a stone throat, against the pour of the brine, and into the stubble.

The minder found out in the weeks of light. It was standing on a bank looking up, as it does, and something crossed, low over the cut plots behind. Then several. The minder raised both stones and looked at the cutter. The cutter went on cutting. A field that has been cut is open. That rule is older than the oldest bank under the banks: once the crop is off, what comes in comes in, and what was left lying is theirs. The minder knew it. It lowered the stones. It watched for most of a tide. Then it turned back to the standing crop, where the rule was still the other one, and ran its hand down the next stipe.

It went on like that for a long time, the cut ground widening from the sea and things following the cutter in a few fields behind, as birds follow cutters anywhere.

The last plot was the one against the mound. When it was down, the cutter put the last handful in the basket. The minder carried it up the swell.

On top there is a quern stone, dished, face-up. The minder set the palm-shaped stone into it, and it sat as it had been worn to sit. It laid the cobble alongside and set down the basket. Its hands were empty.

There are places farther on than a house, and the one who had come would have taken it there if asked. It was not asked. The minder had been sent out from here, and here is where it had been fetched. It lay down on the mound, not in a drain, with nothing closing over, and kept still. The crust takes on anything that keeps still. The one who had come sat by until the crust had closed, because someone sits up. Then it put the sickle back where it had found it, and went.

It left the ice and the sea and the slumped walls. It left the posts at the field corners with the white pebbles in their sockets. They mind in the seasons when nobody can be spared.

The kelp has come again from the cut, and stands in lines. Blades end in scallops now as often as in tatters, and fewer of them reach the drains. Under the leathery slide of blade over blade there is a rasping on the stones, very small. The fish in the lanes turn when the tide turns. The drains are still draining the fields. And every small, dull knock out on the plain is a stone settling.
