Every evening one hard sound goes out over the lip, and past the lip there is nothing to stop it. It crosses the cloud-tops, thinning, and keeps going. For twenty years it was the same sound. Then it began to be different ones, and something that had been hearing it for a long time began to listen.

It comes at night, because it knows what it is. Whatever it does is done loudly. It has never managed otherwise, and this is a place where the water waits to be told. So it lets the last set go through first, and comes when the town is locked and shining and nothing is left loose to startle. It walks the lanes nobody walks. It reads the door-strings. It looks at the loaches, who look back. It finds the kettle a hand's width out of the light, the goat with one white boot, and the dog asleep with its nose toward her mouth.

It could do a great deal here. It goes through what it could do the way she goes through shapes behind shut lips.

It could warm the shelf a little, enough that water would freeze when it got cold, as water does elsewhere. Children could cry in the lanes. But water that froze when it got cold would have frozen here long ago, all of it, to the bottom. The pools are open only because nobody has told them. The finches would go, and the waders, and then the forty. It puts that down quickly.

It could give her words. It has all of them. It looks for the place where *and* would go and finds it between her and the boy, which was not a place until this year. Something is already forming there. Before long he will want the lamp and the kettle in one breath and make a noise to join them, and she will think it is bad, and use it. He will need something to call her, too, when he tells the day and she is in it.

It knows one thing she doesn't. The old man had words, eleven of them. He shut his mouth on them when the felt lifted and a loud girl came in, and he taught her the bark and nothing else, as he had been taught. Before him there was a woman with four. The shelf has begun to talk again and again, one dry room at a time, and each time it stayed in the room. Those eleven words are nowhere now except in the one walking the lanes. It could lay them in her mouth some morning, and it turns this over longer than anything else. But she spent a winter on the loach. A word she hadn't decided would be a noise to her, and she would never know whose. It keeps them. Somebody should.

This year it crossed. She got bored, then careless, and a seven-year-old pointed at a lamp. The one in the lanes checks whether it had any hand in that. It didn't. It would have liked to.

So there is very little left to do, and it nearly goes.

But tonight it comes early and keeps very still. High on the headwall a slab the size of a trough has been working loose since the hot hour. It can see when the slab will go, the way the old women see where the white spot will crawl. It will go as the last hand rises. She is in her doorway with the loach's word in her chest. On the low terrace a boy is not asleep.

It has no good reason to prefer this evening to another. The mountain was here first and takes the evenings it takes. A word carried to bed will keep until the hot hour, and there is someone to say it to now. But this word took a winter.

So it does one thing. It makes her name at the stone, both hands flat, palms down, stopped in the air.

The slab holds. The hands go up from the lip to the headwall. She pushes the strip up onto her forehead and shouts. The white runs out, and the ticks run down toward the lip and stop. On the low terrace a boy says something with no air in it and turns over.

Then it takes its hands away. The slab goes and cracks on the scree, above a town with nothing left to set. In her house she hears it and knows how near that was. She signs at the wall, big, from the shoulder: *late*.

The one at the headwall stands there trying not to look pleased. It will not make a habit of this.

Before it goes it takes one thing, which it had not expected to. It has every word there is, or thought it did. It had none for staying in the ice with your eyes open until it is time to go on, which is what it will be doing from now on, where this shelf is concerned. Now it has one. It says the word once on its way out over the lip, without air, so as not to set anything.
