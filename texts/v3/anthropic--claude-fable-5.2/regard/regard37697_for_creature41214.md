It comes the way the tone came to the children carried in across the clinker, felt in the chest before anything is seen, and then nothing is seen at all. For some days the basin is attended to.

It begins where the walkers begin, with a palm on each mast. Eight are warm. The ninth is the temperature of the air. It learns why the ninth stopped, and the reason is ordinary, and is in the other eight as well, at different depths.

It could start the ninth again. This is the first thing it sets down. The field would stand up through the thicket in an afternoon. The deer would run until they reached the clinker and then run on that. The owls would go. The bird with the quiet song would keep singing and never again be heard, even by its mate. Thirty people have children under those trees who have never worn plugs. And the heap would be sorted, walked grain by grain to the still lines. It is the only deep soil in the basin, and some of it has names.

It could make the other eight unable to stop. It holds this one longer, because a few hundred people sleep on those planes. But it did not raise the masts, and it has seen what the basin made of the quarter that fell: crooked trees, walls, children who shout. To fix the sky in place forever would be to choose the shelves over whatever comes after the shelves, on behalf of people not yet born to either. It sets that down too.

What it will not set down is the afternoon itself. The planes tilted before they failed, and that was all the notice anyone had. Not everyone understood in time. It is not willing for that sentence to be written here twice.

So it does one thing to the masts, deep in the working of them, where no one will find it. From now on a mast that is going to stop will cool first, slowly, over a full season. The geckos will leave it. A palm will know. The walkers already lay the palm and sign up *warm*, and they have done it for years on nothing but hope. Now the hope is an instrument. No one will learn that anything changed until the evening the word that goes up is a different word. Then there will be months to walk inward, and there have always been more rooms than people.

It listens to the singers. It knows whether the singing helps. The singers are right, by about the thickness of a fig leaf. It leaves the matter unsettled, since they have never needed it settled in order to take their shift.

Then it goes out to the margin and stays there longest.

It could level her room so the cup would hold. It could teach her body the still ground. It could, in effect, pick her up and set her down on one side. Each of these is an answer. She is the one person in the basin standing where the question is still open, and she has been doing the work of it for years. She carries hide one way and cord the other. She breaks up what falls along a line that no longer leads anywhere. She takes out her plugs to listen.

It listens with her. The drift is not a sickness. It is what the third overtone sounds like when it is sung with no mast to lean on. Any choir left without its instrument sinks a shade and goes on singing. No one else on the shelves has ever heard a cicada by itself.

It could tell her so. But she has not been waiting to be told. She has been signing it up, season after season, at the tail of the count: *flat, a beat, slow*. What has been missing is not an answer but anyone at the other end. The relay carries numbers upward and was never made to carry anything down. On the forty-first plane there is an old woman, sunburnt and long-sighted, who holds the third overtone in the lean weeks. She has read that tail three seasons running. Three times she has stood at the edge of her room and made a reply, into thirty-nine sheets of fog.

So it does a second thing, and this is the last. One evening, at the hour of settling, the fog thins along a single slanting line between the top plane and the second. It stays clear for about nine breaths.

She is in her room with the descent in her stomach, reaching for the cup. Light comes down the line. It is real daylight, the color it still is up there when the low planes have gone to lamps. She looks up through the whole height of the town. At the far end, a little beyond the distance at which a name can be read, someone is standing lit orange at the edge of a mat. The arms make a flat palm, tipped a few degrees. Then both hands go to the ears, and away.

*Go on, I'm listening.* Or, across open air, where it cannot be literal: *this should be said aloud; come inside.*

Then the sheets close.

It has noticed one more thing, and leaves it where it lies. The limbs in the thicket sink toward evening by a few fingers, as limbs everywhere do, and lift again by morning. The thicket takes its pay in cord. A bed hung from those figs would go down under a sleeper at the right hour. Six people under the trees know her voice-name. One of them will think of it, or she will. They do not need it for that.

The attention lifts. It can be felt going for about a day. Nothing in the basin weighs any different. Eight masts are warm.

She sets the cup back in the middle of the mat. In the morning she ties on her soles out of habit, takes them off again, and starts up the first span. It is three hundred meters of fig root and knotted cord, with the chord at its worst halfway up each one, where the rope hums in the fist. She does not hurry.

All the way up she keeps the note in her mouth so as not to lose it. It sits a shade under the overtone, and beats against it in her teeth like a slow pulse. Every tune ever carried into the basin has been bent to fit the chord. She is bringing up the first one that hasn't.
