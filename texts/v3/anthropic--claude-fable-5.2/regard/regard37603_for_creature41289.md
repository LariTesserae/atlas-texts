It comes at dusk, and the way to tell is that nothing stops.

When the turtle reaches a pan, the nearest frogs go quiet. This arrives and the clicks start at one pan and are answered from another, and the whole plain joins in with no gap anywhere.

It can hear the whole plain at once, so it hears what could only be supposed before: the small silences, very far apart, moving through the chorus. Tonight there are fourteen. One of them is sliding out over the water of the pan below the nearest lone tree.

It attends to her for a long time. Then it does what a power of its kind does, which is look for what is wrong. It is thorough about it.

It looks at the water. It could tilt the basement by the width of a hand, and the rain would know which way to go. But water that goes somewhere takes something with it: first the leaf, then the shrimp eggs waiting in the dust, then the clay of the pans, then the trails wherever a channel crossed them. This is the one country where the taking is finished. It leaves the water lying.

It looks at her walk, the shell carried barely clear of the sand and set down every few steps. It could lighten her. But the setting down is what presses the groove, and the groove was there when she hatched. She has travelled all her life in the rests of others, and something not yet laid will travel in hers.

It looks at the crescent. It knows what took it: a kite, in her third year, which lifted her as high as the lone tree and found her too wide to hold. It could put the piece back. But the edges wore smooth long ago, and the clay closes over that outline now and no other. It is no longer a wound, only her shape.

It looks longest at the mouthful, the fast neck and the puff of grey that takes a minute to clear. The tadpoles are also beings who live here, and it does not pretend otherwise. But they are grazing the living film, and the dragonfly nymphs are taking them from below, and the stilts from above. Its mercy turns out to be like the rain here. It does not know which way to go.

Last it looks at the distance, one to a pan, each with its skirt of bare ground. It listens in her for what it would call loneliness in someone else. It finds a wish for the next pan when this one is eaten thin, and in flood years a wish for what flood years bring. The rest of the time it finds warm water and clay and no wish at all. The space around her is not something missing.

So it does less than it could, and the less is this.

It knows her. Nobody knows how long they live, because the rings come away with the plates. But she has been dried over eighty-seven times, and now the count exists somewhere. It does not write the number on anything.

It stays. It could bring the wet year, but it waits for it instead, and the wait is nine years. In the ninth the pans link into chains and the current forms, too slow to see. She walks the flooded bottoms north and east for eleven nights and is found. She lays into the clay with the water standing warm above her.

It stays through the drying. For months she is a lump under a cracked floor, her heart turning over a few times a minute, and it keeps beside that heart while the sedge looks burnt on the rock. A body's length away, air comes down the cracks and the eggs begin. When the rain returns, the sedge greens within a day. She takes two, and it waits the second day as well.

Eleven hatch into a pool already rowing with shrimp. By the end of the week the stilts have had five and the kite one. It lets them. It did not come to take the turtle's side against the stilt.

One thing it does do. The coast is days away and barely lower, and a plain this level has no defence against a sea a little higher. Salt would come up the chains of pans by the same slow road the current takes down. Beyond the coast are those who have not finished taking. What it does about that it does there, at length, and with less stillness than it kept here. None of it shows from the plain.

Then it goes. It leaves no groove. The leaf that fell on the water the evening it arrived is still where it fell, and the pool beside her trail is deeper by about a tenth of a millimetre.

It goes at dusk, and the way to tell is that nothing stops. There are a few clicks from one pan, an answer from another, and then the whole plain. Out in it, very far apart, the small silences are moving. One of them is hers, travelling a little way along the rim of the north pan, sliding out over the water, and closing.
