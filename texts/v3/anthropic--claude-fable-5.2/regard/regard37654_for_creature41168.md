Something has noticed the hill.

It did not arrive. It was already everywhere a hill is, and one day this hill was the one it was thinking about. It is older than the noon, and it means well, and it has had a long time to find out what meaning well is worth.

It sees the hollow the way it sees everything, all at once and all the way through: the pool and the underside of the pool, the salamander and the bead in the salamander, every coat of the wall back to the first. It sees the dry shape in the rain. Without thinking, it begins to count the swifts.

In the low corner the heap packs tighter. The darkness at its centre, which was the size of a fist, is the size of a thumb joint. The animals have stopped taking turns, because there is only room for one.

It stops counting. This is new information, and it is not used to that. Its looking is a kind of noon. It goes through things. It has never regarded anything that did not become clear, and it had always supposed that was a kindness.

It draws back as far as the grass. The dark in the heap fills slowly from behind, like a thumbprint in the wall. The swifts go back to being several numbers.

From there it considers what it could do, which is anything.

It could finish the salamander. But the salamander walks and feeds without difficulty, and its back half should come from wherever the front half came from. It could tell the floor which way is down. But there is a bowl in there, already level by someone's idea of level, and a second idea would spill it. It could cut a door. Then the ordinary evening would come in every day, free, for no one in particular, and nobody would need to carry it eleven paces in shut hands. It could fetch her whole and in the proper order, girl first. That would be a different person. This one begins with a worn step.

It could close the ring. It has the eighth pebble, in the sense that it has every pebble. It thinks about that longest. But the crickets are already stepping around a stone, and it is not this one. It is a particular stone, cool, out of a particular cheek. Anything else put there would only be in the way.

All of these are the noon's kind of help: more, sooner, all the way through. The hill is not short of that. The hill is making something that can close, and nothing that sees through walls can hand that over. It can only leave room.

So what it does is outside, and small, and done in its own order, which is the slow one, with causes first.

It keeps the hill dull. That is why the hill has only grass. There is no tree, no standing stone, no spring, nothing to make a person stop and wonder what is under it. Sheep graze there. The footpath goes around the bottom, because the bottom is easier. One year a man comes to set fence posts, and the line he sights runs two yards to the left.

And on another hill, in the band of the strata where the tree is, a girl of ten lies down on her stomach with something in her fist. Children bury things when no one is looking. That is most of what burying is. So at that noon, for the first time in all its age, it looks somewhere else. It looks at the river. When it looks back there is a patch of turned earth, patted flat with a broad thumb, and it does not look under.

The first coat on the hollow was her palm. The second was that. The ground has been copying the two of them ever since.

It would like to see her. It would like to be there when there is enough of her, when the breath comes out and the first warm thing happens in that room. It has seen every other first thing there has been. It will not see that one. If it were watching, the room would be clear to the back wall, and she would kneel at the ring and open her hands and find nothing in them but daylight.

What it has instead is this. In everything it knows, there is now one place it doesn't: a little more than an arm's length under the grass roots, about the size of a pantry. It does not know how many swifts there are. It does not know whether the fiddlehead has moved, or whether the ring is still short a stone. It has never had a place like that before. It is cool there, and nothing is clear, and it finds that it can rest.

So it takes its turn. On ordinary evenings, which the grass hill has, it settles on the slope the way the swifts roost on the outside of the heap, with its head tucked.

Ask it what is under the hill and it will say grass roots, and then earth to about the length of an arm, and then a little more. Past that it couldn't tell you. Somebody down there keeps the night. It is one of the ones she keeps it for.
