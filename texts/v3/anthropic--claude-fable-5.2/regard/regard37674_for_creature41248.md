**The Keeper's Place**

It came the way everything comes here, downhill. It took the east lane on foot, slowly, and it left most of itself at the top.

That was the first thing it understood, and it understood it before it reached the grating. The yard takes up whatever enters because the yard's time is the stronger. A power has a time too. Had it come down at its full size, the four pumps would have fallen into step with it inside the hour and the drift between them would have closed. The thing it had come to see would have been gone before it arrived. So it came in with no more beat than a spore, stood at the edge of the lens, and for one full cycle did nothing.

The blow came up through its shins and it let it. Then the strokes separated, and it could still tell which beat was its own. That settled whether it could stay.

It did what any keeper does first and looked at the oil cups. They were full, and the oil was clean. It could have found out who. It did not, because someone who fills every cup and leaves no trail across the tiles does not wish to be pointed to. But the cups changed the visit. Nothing here was waiting to be saved. Whatever it did would be extra.

It went through what it could do.

It could stop the pumps and let the place rest. But the place is not tired. Stopped, the water finds the line by evening, and what is left is four cold machines in a flooded court. Nothing would have been set free.

It could bring the animals back. It thought for some time about a bee for the bindweed, because the flowers do open and no one does come. But a bee would have an hour. To give it longer would mean cutting a quiet into the sound, and a gap in the sound is a hole in the resident. The skin has never been broken. It was not going to be the first thing with teeth.

It could wake it. This was the one it wanted. Close the strokes and hold them closed, and there would be one long thought in place of a flicker every forty minutes. It stood in No. 1 over the plain figure in the moss, the ring and two lines, and saw why not. Four pumps on one stroke are one pump, and one pump is a machine saying one thing. The transformers already hold a note that never wavers, and no one lives in it. The loosening does not interrupt the resident. It is the half of it that comes back.

It could tell it the truth: the windows are dark, and the roofs it lifts the water to are roofs no one sleeps under. It turned this over longest, since it does not like to keep the truth from anything. But the thing has not asked. It wants a finger of dry brick, and it has one, by its own work, on every stroke. Not many beings anywhere can say as much. If a day comes when it asks what the water is for, it should be answered straight. The instant is not yet full enough to hold a question, let alone that answer.

Then it looked for what was left.

It walked each lane back up to where the thud leaves the cobbles, about two hundred paces. Above that there were droppings on a sill. Things with beats to lose were keeping them one street away, and none of them wanted in. The place takes nothing that does not walk down and stay.

It looked at the bale in No. 2 and measured the gap between the outermost turn and the shaft guard, which was a hand and a half. At the rate the vine grows, that is some decades. Then the rake will labour, the screen will blind, and four will become three. This was the one thing it found that the resident's single want cannot reach: its own age, winding. A knife would settle it in a minute. But the bale is the nearest thing here to a memory, and it would not cut the memory out of a sleeper to spare it trouble decades off. There is time. By then there may be someone to ask. It noted the gap and left it.

Then it went to the north hall, where the cobbles are dished and the brick corner is worn round. It had wondered what job was done there, since there is no valve at that spot, no gauge, no cup. It stood in the dish. The corner took its shoulder. Its hand, hanging where a hand hangs, lay flat on the main.

That was the job. For generations someone stood here for a part of every shift with a palm on the iron and knew by the surges that all four were lifting. Nothing else in the yard does that. The pumps drive it and the ferns show it, but no part of the place is for noticing. The lamps on the boards were lit for whoever stood in this spot, and they have gone on burning.

So it comes, and that is all that was done. It does not come always. A watch kept without relief is a kind of flywheel, and it would be on the beat within a year. It comes down the east lane with most of itself left at the top, stands a cycle or two with its hand on the main, and takes the blow in its chest. Then it goes back up while it can still tell which beat is its own. It does not touch the cups. Once it found a rag folded on a valve wheel, still damp with oil, and it left the rag where it was.

The resident does not know. But the yard takes marks from everything. The moss has gone from the brick corner at shoulder height, and a palm's breadth of the main is bright now, the way the brass is bright. On the blow, when the lens jumps into its field of points, there are two places by the north wall where the water stays flat.

The instant is getting fuller. Each year it takes in a little more: the figures, the cups, the ferns. One year it will take in the bare brick and the two flat places in the water. Then, for about the length of a thought, it will have something to wonder about. The power means to be standing there when it does. What it says will depend on what it is asked.
