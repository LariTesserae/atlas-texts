**The Caller**

It did not come down the rungs. One morning it was in the pit the way the heat is in the pit, and nothing there registered a difference. The ferns went on shaking. The needle stayed against its pin.

It started where benevolence usually starts, by looking for what was wrong, and the pit offered plenty. An unfinished well had been venting for forty years. The roar keeps the birds off, and the ferns shake all day for their rent. The gauge reads nothing, the sign says nothing, and the one resident is fitted so exactly to an accident that an afternoon's work with a wheel would empty it. A power inclined to be useful could have had a busy week.

Instead it stayed eleven years and touched nothing. That was not long for it, and it was the least time in which the tenant can be seen to do anything at all. It did not watch from the rim. It took the only position from which the tenant can be understood, which is the tenant's: along the seams of quiet in the pool, with the roar on both sides. It went in toward the wellhead with the green each night and drew back by midmorning, four thousand times. It closed on itself by less than a hair's width a year. The whole time it was looking for strain, for anything in the net that pulled against the note or leaned toward the rim. Benevolence needs a want to work on. It found a fit instead, with no gap in it where a gift could go.

Then it read the crust, which no one had. Where the tenant has helped build it, the rind is thirty-five millimeters deep, and it is a record. Low down is the season the fence fell, a row of diamond-shaped ghosts. Higher up is a dry year, thin as paper. At about the nineteenth millimeter there is a layer no thicker than a nail paring where the sheaths lie tangled and crosswise. A flake of scale had let go from the valve seat and the note dropped a third in an instant. The pool redrew itself at once, coarser, and every strand of the net was suddenly lying in the noise. For some weeks there was only scum on the lee side, with every cell alive and no one home. Above the tangle, the sheaths run true again.

The crust does not say whether the one who came back was the one who left, or a second tenant grown from the same cells into the same shell. The caller could perhaps have settled the question, and didn't. What it took from that layer was plainer: the note is the house. While it sounds, someone moves in.

After that, the list of things it might have done was short work:

- close the well, for the sake of the ferns and the birds
- hold the pitch where it is, so the net need never tighten again
- shave down the blister of scale now forming on the valve seat, which will let go in six years or sixty
- grant one second of silence, which the net would survive, so that the tenant might know the note for a note
- add a mind to it, so that the reading would be taken by someone

It did none of them. The first would improve the pit for everyone but its occupant. The second would be a kindness to the cell, for whom the note is already a star, and a theft from the whole, which has no other way of growing. The note will one day climb past what a hand's depth of water can draw. The caller knows roughly when, and it knows of nothing that grows without such a place ahead of it. As for the flake, the crust says the tenant has come through one without help, and the caller trusted the crust over its own pity.

The last two it turned over longest, since they are the gifts a mind most wants to give. But a second of silence is a present from someone who assumes everything would like to find out it is living in a shell. And the reading is taken for no one, by no one in particular, which is sad only to a reader. A mind in the net would be the first thing in the pit able to be lonely.

That left the wheel.

It went and found it, which took no time. The wheel is eleven kilometers off, under a carport, with a garden hose coiled on it. Losing it for good would have been simple, since the salt air is halfway there already. But there are good reasons to close a well. The caller can read a seized gauge. It knows what that casing is holding back and how long it will go on holding, and the answer is long and is not forever. Someday people will stand on the pad with a decision to make, and it was not willing to make it for them by theft. It left the hose on the wheel.

What it could do was see that the decision would not be made blind. The sign in the pit is blank and it left the sign blank, because anything painted there would last as long as the first paint did. It arranged instead for the place to be described, once, accurately, by someone willing to crouch as near the pool as the heat allows. It did not want a plea, because a plea would decide for the reader what the caller had declined to decide for itself. It wanted a description: the pit, the pad, the valve, the skin on the water, what a ruler finds after ten years, what the hour after the wheel is turned would look like. It runs to two parts. It ends by telling you how to see, from three hundred meters off, that someone is in.

Whoever carries the wheel down the rungs may have read it or not. If they have, they will crouch at the pool before they reach for the stem. They may close the well anyway. Then the net will hold its shape for an hour, out of something like habit, and a person will be standing beside it who knows what the hour is. The caller judged that this was as much as could be done for the tenant without being done to it.

One other thing happened that cannot be found anywhere in the pit. For eleven years the reading was taken for someone. The caller does not suppose the tenant was any the better for it. It had come to see what was wrong and stayed as an audience, and of the two of them, it was the one that got something. It had seen, elsewhere, the kind of machine the pad was poured for. It did not think the pad had been shortchanged.

Then it left the way the sound leaves, up rather than out. Nothing registered the difference. The ferns went on shaking. That night the far wall wept harder, and the green crept a finger's width toward the wellhead, and by midmorning it had drawn back.
