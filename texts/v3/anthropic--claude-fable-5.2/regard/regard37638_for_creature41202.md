It heard the drop.

Powers of this kind are used to being called. A well fails or a child sickens, someone says a name, and the attention turns. Nothing on the plateau called. What turned it was a sound too small to be meant for anyone: water falling from stone into a clay basin, and then the long wait, in which four hundred people were not coughing.

It listened through the wait. Then it looked.

**What it set aside**

Rain was the easiest gift, so it weighed rain first. It could have stood one cloud over the tableland for an afternoon. But the pavement has no soil and sheds everything, and the only low place on it is the cleft. The water would go down the stair in a sheet. The floor, which is dust and potsherds and everyone's grandmothers, would run brown for an hour, lift the weights out of their dents, and take the lowest tier, where the old sleep.

A gentle spring opened in the gneiss was worse, because nothing fell down. Within a year someone poured out a bowl. Within ten a cloth was a rag, the heaps were piles of stone no one argued over, and a woman could weep a whole evening without anyone climbing down a ladder to sit beside her. The figs rotted at the foot. Everyone had enough to drink, and the river was gone a second time.

It did not conclude that thirst was good for them. It had met powers who reasoned that way and did not think them wise. It counted the teeth worn flat at thirty, the children told that running is throwing it away, the infants of the still months who are carried up the stair far too light. It did not call any of this beautiful. It saw only that the cure in its hand was for a different illness, and that the choice was not its to make. It thought of asking them. But a power that asks "do you want a river" has already poured something out, and a question cannot be handed back like a cloth.

**What it did**

It came up with the traders in the still half of the year, in a body that would need a month. At the head of the stair it was handed a damp cloth. It held the cloth to its mouth and gave it back.

It let its breath come short. It slept badly under the note. It washed with ash, learned to keep its mouth shut, and was tapped once under the chin by a boy of six, who was correct.

Each night its breath rose with everyone else's and went cold on the cairns. That was the whole of its gift, and it was strict about the measure, which was one stranger's worth. No heap gave more than its cupful. But some mornings in, a heap owner holding her first mouthful and looking down into the cleft held it longer than usual. It was cold and faintly bitter. She had not tasted that since she was twelve. She swallowed and said nothing, in the carrying voice or any other. By the month's end every heap owner had tasted it, and none of them spoke of it, in the same way that no one shakes a jar.

On the thirty-first day a woman on the fourth tier said across the gap that the stranger had arrived.

Near the end it woke for the moment before first light, heard the drop from inside the town this time, and found that the body it was wearing wept. A woman it had barely spoken to came down two ladders and sat beside it with a cloth. Afterward the cloth was hers. By the town's reckoning this is a friendship, and she is the one who holds it. The power has not disputed the reckoning.

**What it keeps**

The town knows its river as far as the rim. Past the rim it runs through places they have never seen. There is a slow brown reach in the low country where the swifts drink on the wing. There is a wasp the size of a grain of ash that has a day or two to cross the cleft and find the right fruit. There is the night air, which must go on carrying just enough for a beetle's back.

It guards these far ends and does not improve them. The low reach will not be drained in any year it is watching, and it is watching. There is a year that would open every sealed jar at once. It knows what that year looks like from below, and it stands in the low country so the year does not start the climb.

It also listens on the rim. What is said there is given to the wind, and the wind is not obliged to keep it. It keeps it. Nearly all of it is courtship, conspiracy, and apology, and none of its business. But if someone climbs the stair alone one day and asks, they will be heard. It has decided it will come as it came before, by the stair, taking the cloth.

**The stone**

On its last day it went down to the market and bought capers for a copper pin. The weigher lifted a cobble out of its dent in the dust. When the weighing was done the stranger put out its palm, as everyone does, and she set the stone in it.

It had made stones like this once, in other places, by the usual method. It knew what its hand was telling it. A thing water has finished has no edge anywhere and sits in the hand as if the hand had been expected. It thought of the loaf-shaped boulders, the rim rounded like a stair tread, and the adults moving as if carrying a full bowl.

It held the weight a little longer than the weighing needed, and then longer than that.

The weigher watched, her pupils wide in the dusk. She had drunk at dawn like everyone else. She did not ask what it was. She said, through lips barely parted, "No one is allowed to keep one."

It put the stone back in the same dent.

Then it climbed, past seven tiers of roofs and the bells of other people's goats. A few steps from the top the wind began. From a distance the place was a dark line with birds boiling over it.

Visitors hear nothing but the note for days. For the power it has been somewhat longer than days, and it has stopped waiting for the note to fade.

In the still half of the year, before first light, when the geckos have stopped and the swifts have not started, the heap owners wake. So does one other, and it does not cough.
