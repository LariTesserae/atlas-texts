The third thing to cross the pan could have arrived any way it liked. It could have stood at the central crossing without ever passing the throat. It walks instead, finds the chord by ear, and goes in stooped against the draft, in a body narrow enough for the lanes and heavy enough to be noticed.

The jars go quiet ahead of it and fill in behind. It lets them. Being heard is the only introduction the town offers.

Then for a long time it does nothing. It sits in the dust at a turning where two pitches meet and learns the intervals. Through each slack it keeps its feet drawn in, because in the slack the floor belongs to the residents. Feelers climb to its knee, and the animals fold. After enough slacks they stop folding. It stays until it has heard one jar's note creep up a full step, which is the length of a tenant's growing.

It thinks about water first, and longest, because water is what the town most plainly lacks. It could bring rain to the pan. Then every pod on every floor would wake in the same season, indoors, with roots in the dust and shoots feeling up the pipes. The chord would choke room by room. The tenants have never had to keep water out of anything, and they live in jars that would hold it. The town would die of receiving what it lacked, so it leaves the weather alone.

It could stop the closing, but the closing is the plant being alive. The finger's width is all the growing it does. The rise in pitch is no fault in the instrument. It is the one tune the instrument has, a single phrase that takes the whole life of the town to play, and it is not finished.

It goes sideways into the tight lanes, as far as a body goes, and waits through a slack beside a hole that takes one finger. A tenant comes along the wall with a pellet and sets it at the rim. The feeler comes out, passes over the pellet, and lies for a while across the back of the one who brought it. Then the bringer goes back down the lane before the air turns.

It could cut that hole wider. The builders cut this plant wherever they liked, and the plant healed over every cut. But the builders lived here and came back to the same cut each season. A hole opened by someone who is leaving is only a closing put off. Behind it is an animal that has not walked in lifetimes and has, at its rim, what most things want at the end. The power cannot think of anything to add to that.

The flush rims are harder. It lays its ear to them, one after another. Behind most there is clay and still air. Behind one, at the edge of the dead quarter where the wood is newest, there is a tick, and it is not the wood. The ticks come far apart. The heap under that rim is the freshest in the town.

So the bringers know. A sealed jar has no note to take away, and still the lane has found a way to say that someone is there. A thing is set down under the rim, slack after slack, by animals whose only other signal is a silence.

The power stays at that rim through many turnings of the air. As far as it can tell, the one inside is folded, lid to the mouth, as it has been at every alarm of its life. It is running down slowly on very little, the way everything here does. The power is not sure that this is peace. It is sure that a finger of wood is nothing to it. It is also sure that if it opened this jar it would owe the same to the next, and to every jar after for as long as the roof grows, and then the town would be its town. That is a reason, and it knows a reason is not the same as being right.

What it does instead is small and possibly foolish. It scrapes cork crumb from the floor, works it with resin from a wall plate until it holds, and sets the pellet on the heap. The first one falls apart. The second is too large, and it leaves it there.

That leaves the pods. For as long as the rooms have been roofed, the plant has hung out seed and dropped it on a floor where nothing carries it off and no wet wakes it. It is the one thing in the town that has been asked for again and again and never given. What is missing is not much, only a carrier and a little damp. Something that will not bring rain can still carry.

It picks a fallen pod from the main street and shakes it at its ear first, since a pod rattles and a resident does not. It fetches the quern. It moves only while the air is moving, when the floor is empty, and it steps wide over the lower lip of the throat, where the pits are.

Out on the pan, some way to one side of the throat and clear of the breath, it sets the quern down dished side up. Stone holds the night's cold longer than callus does. On the coldest indraws the saucer will take a film, not enough to feel as damp, but a little more than the lip gets. It cracks the pod on the rim, tips the seeds in, and covers them with a handful of the dust, which has been through the residents so many times that it is the nearest thing on the pan to soil. The quern spent its working life breaking seed. It can spend the rest holding some.

It lays the halves of the shell beside the stone with their mouths to the wind. They hum, lower than any jar. It does not carry a tenant out. None of them moves house, and it will not be the one to start that. But hatchlings come off the lip with the cold air behind them and go by ear toward a low note. If one of them, some season, turns the wrong way and walks out, that will be its own doing.

It may come to nothing. The power knows the odds, and they are poor. It could better them by staying to tend the stone. But a seedling that lives because something stood over it will need that something for good, and it does not intend to be needed here.

It walks back across the pan. Behind it the chord thins to the drone and the faint grain above it. If a second, smaller note ever stands in the dark out there, it will be a long lifetime before anyone could find it by ear.

In the far quarter, under a flush rim, the top pellet on the heap is too large and badly rolled. By the next slack there is another on top of it, the right size.
