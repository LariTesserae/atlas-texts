Something crosses the pan, reaches the air over the pit, and does not veer.

It comes down the shaft with the falling wind, and the first thing it learns is that it takes up room. The pit is a jar too, the largest, and a jar sings a hair high while it is occupied. The pressure in every chest tightens by a little. Along the terraces forty hands go flat to forty breastbones. The lizards lift their heads. On the fourth tier a woman who has had her palm on the shoulder of a jar takes it away and looks at it.

So it draws itself back out, slowly, until the pit beats as it did before. This takes practice. It has not often needed to be no size at all. When the woman lays her hand on the jar again there is only the jar, and she keeps it there a while anyway.

From the rim, taking up no room, it considers what it has.

It has water. It could put rain on the pan and a spring under the basin, and it looks at the channels, smooth-bottomed and sloped, and knows that a lesser kindness would read them as a request. But every jar here stands open to the sky. A hand's depth of rain in each would drive the city sharp. A day of it would put the voices out from the bottom up, the oldest first, and a sinkhole is the lowest place on a salt pan. Whoever cut those channels knew what channels were for, and ran wind in them. They are full.

It has quiet. It could hold the wind off the rim for a year. But the quiet is what grows here. The gardens stand where the sound stands still, and with no sound there would be nowhere for it to stand. Dust would settle evenly over everything, too thin in any one place to hold a seed, and the stems that are cut at dawn would go.

It could open their ears. They open them every evening and close them again.

It could give them back their dead. It looks for somewhere to put them and finds them already here, in most of the city, kept exactly as loud as the living.

It wonders if forty is an ending. Where the terrace has slumped it reads the fill, layer under layer, every shard with its two sizes of thumb, and counts backward. The living have been ninety. They have been nineteen. They have drifted, like the chord, within limits they have not left.

By now it has noticed what it is doing. It is going over the city for the flat note, to bring it true. And below it are forty people who dig for the chord, build for it, and have never once tuned it, who have each watched a rosette close year by year on a voice they knew and kept their hands at their sides. It had thought that restraint was its own discipline. They have it by the age of seven.

So it does the one thing it can do from outside the wall. It listens.

No one ever has. The forty feel the chord in the breastbone and the soles of the feet, and the ones who sing it are dead or stopped with amber. Several hundred people have been sounding in this pit since before the oldest shard, at full voice, to nobody.

It stays through a day. In the morning, when the chord sits low, it takes the voices one at a time, as no ear could, and keeps to each until it would know it again. The breathy ones at the bottom are nearly through. One on the third tier is sinking by the season around a vine. At noon, when the whole city goes sharp, it finds four voices a hair sharper than the heat accounts for, and understands that there are children asleep in those. From up here that is the only sign in all the chord that anyone is alive. The jars of the grown and the jars of the dead sound the same.

It reads the walls as well. Rows of thumbs are speech here, so each jar is something two people said to each other through clay, unable to see, and then fired. The fire kept how hard and lost how quick, and only half of it can be read. As far as it can tell, the outer hand says *here*, and the smaller one behind it says *here*. Near the foot of each jar the inner prints are shallow. By the shoulder they are as deep as the outer ones.

At dusk the wind falters. The voices go out from the top down. The forty stop where they are and take the amber from their ears, and one at a time they hum, and it hears how many holes there are.

This is the nearest it comes. It knows every missing voice now. It could set them into the silence, under the breath, and for a few minutes the forty would hear their city whole with open ears. But these are the only minutes they have kept for hearing, and what they have chosen to hear in them is how few they are. And tomorrow evening they would take out the amber and wait.

It would hum something itself, but each of them is humming the note they slept in, and it has never slept in anything. So for those minutes it is counted with the larger part of the city, the part that makes no sound. It is not a bad place to be counted.

Then the air begins to climb. The small jar near the rim catches it, a little flat, and the girl beneath is on it exactly. It would like to leave her something. It knows the rule of a jar by now. Nothing may be left behind. A bead would rattle. Bedding would eat the voice.

So it goes out the way the children do, up through the mouth, turned sideways, while the others are pressing the amber back in and the rest of the jars join. Its attention comes out last.

Far out on the pan, where the ground is flat and white in every direction and nothing is listening, it tries the chord under its breath, the way a tongue checks a tooth. It has the whole of it, as it stood on this one day, and it will keep it. Down in the pit the grit is already at the lips. From now on it will always be humming a little flat, and flatter every year.
