It stops at the bank, where anyone would. Before a room is deepened here the digger raps and listens, and it has no wall to rap on, so it waits among the nettles and listens instead. By the time the turf is lifted from the eastern square it has been there a full round of seasons. It has learned the town the way the town learns itself, through chalk: whose baby wakes, who talks asleep, who rises before light. It keeps the first courtesy. Nothing that comes through is mentioned, and from where it stands everything comes through.

It has a long arm, and that is the difficulty. From the top of the bank it can reach the water under the shaft. It could reach timber, and copper in hills these people have no name for. So before anything else it goes to the old quarter, to the room where the knotted cord hangs, and takes the measure of a woman who was not tall. After that it asks one question of each thing it might do: could a neighbour do this, through a wall this thick? Long-armed diggers have to be reminded. It reminds itself often.

Most of what it does, by that measure, is leave things.

It goes down the shaft and on past the bottom. The water is there. It could give the depth to a finger's width, and it tells no one, and it does not raise the water by so much as a finger to meet her. Three winters of palms have been pressed into that chalk. The day a basket comes up that needs no rolling between finger and thumb belongs to the hands at the top, and to the woman at the bottom, who will at last have something to say besides colder. What it does is knock on the shaft walls each morning before she goes down. They are sound. It listens the whole time she is below, the way the people filling jars look up at the fern.

It leaves the town its one adze. A day when a household does nothing else and the neighbours bring the food is not a shortage. It does not water the fern or make up the jackdaws' minds, and it leaves the niche as empty as the household does. It could learn why the first woman went down instead of up. It does not look. Her granddaughter is tired of being asked, and the reason is in the wall with everything else.

One thing it cannot leave. At night the cough comes through every wall in the old quarter, dry and unremarked, and it has been on the bank long enough to know where the cough goes. It goes up the ramp in time, the way the chalk goes. The woman in the shaft works wet chalk and does not cough. Nobody has set those two things side by side, because nobody asks her anything. It does not set them side by side for anyone. But the marl seam that weeps on the north side runs on under the east, a little below where any floor has reached, and it lifts that seam by the height of a jar.

This is past the cord and it knows it.

That summer a line of wall darkens in the eastern courts and stays dark. Jars are stood under it. Picks go into the damp face more quietly than a knuckle into a loaf, nothing hangs in the air afterwards, and the floors stop holding footprints. It leaves the rest to whoever first wonders why the east sleeps so quietly. It sends no fern. Ferns arrive on their own.

Then there is the digger in the eastern square.

By the first frosts the square is a pit, down past the upper flint, with a stair in one corner and a room begun in the east face, cut back the length of a body not fully grown. No adze has come to it yet. All autumn the digger has rapped before each day's work, left-handed, with the heel of the hand, because that is how it is done, and has listened and heard back nothing but a great deal of chalk.

It could answer, and it has turned this over longer than the water. It would be true: it is here. But the rap before digging asks how much stands between us, and it has no answer to that which the cord could measure. And the two slow knocks at night mean only *I am here*. Coming from under unbroken grass they would never mean only that again. Every wall in the town would be listened to for a different reason. So it does not answer, that season or any other.

On the first night in the new room the digger lies down against a wall with the whole plain behind it and, from habit or for the company of the sound, gives it two slow ones.

Chalk swallows sound, but flint does not. The upper band crosses the digger's wall at the shoulder as it crosses every wall in the town, level from lane to lane, under courts and partitions all the way to the old quarter, where a knob of it stands out of the first wall ever cut and a knotted cord hangs from it. On a still night a knock beside the band might carry some of that distance by itself. It sees to it that this one carries all the way. That is the whole of what it does.

The granddaughter is awake, as the old are. She hears two slow knocks in the flint, thin as a knapper's click a long way off, made with the heel of the hand and by no neighbour she knows. She has been asked things all her life, but this is not asking. She puts a knuckle to the knob beside the cord and answers.

In the eastern square it comes out of the black stone, small and hard, and plainly a knuckle. The digger knows it is the old quarter and does not know who. That is enough to sleep on.

Neither of them mentions it. Before the winter is out they will meet on a plank, one with a basket, and one will step back to let the other cross, and nothing will be said.

When the next square is opened there is a wall to knock to, and the flint goes back to carrying what flint carries. It returns to the bank. It has no errand left in the town that anyone could name. Still, like the court with the onion trough, it finds a reason to pass, and each time it looks down to see that they are still there.
