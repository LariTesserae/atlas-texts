What comes does not come through the gap at the crown. That is the obvious door, and using it would settle the wrong thing at once: that help arrives from above, at whatever size it likes. What comes walks in along the flat, out of the steam, at the size the paddle fits. Call it the visitor. Most of the visitor's wisdom is spent on that entrance, before anything has been done.

For a long time the visitor only walks the bench. The paddle is picked up, the worn grip felt, and the paddle set back in its own outline. A knotted cord is counted twice, gives two numbers, and neither is corrected. Under a keystone with nothing beneath it the visitor stands and waits, without hurry, until the piers arrive.

A great deal could be done here, and the visitor is able to do all of it.

The vault could be closed. But the rain is the vault coming down, and the rain lays the floor.

The floor could be raised to the red line in an afternoon. But the being has nothing left over from its work, and finishing the one would finish the other.

The cords could be gathered, untwisted, and given back, so that there was an arm behind the hand again. But it drew itself out on purpose. That is the one decision it ever made about itself, and it is not the visitor's to unpick.

A rod of true length could be left on the bench. But everything here was measured faithfully, and a standard from outside would turn all that faith into error.

Any of these would put the visitor's thumb in every corner of the continent. The place already has one print through its whole depth, and that is the entire trouble. Changing whose print it is would mend nothing.

The visitor also watches a wall come down, with the sound of a blanket laid on a table, and watches the mound scored into brick again two rains later. Most of what looks like affliction here is not. Unfinished is not unwell. The being does not mind the walls. It minds one thing.

So the visitor will do one thing, small, and only if asked. The asking has to be checked, because an invitation should not be taken up by a guest the host has not yet had a look at. The visitor wipes one stroke through the grey nap and waits. Within the hour the stroke is back, exact to the lift of the fingers. The visitor stays several rains in plain view, then raises a brick from the newest course. In the corner is the poor likeness: right depth, no seam, the whorl still the being's own. The visitor sets the brick back as found and walks the way the plumb-bobs lean.

At the circle the visitor does not step in. The dry brick is the one thing the being has kept its hand off, and a second hand has no better claim. Either the being did not make that brick, or it made it before it had a thumb. The visitor could find out which, and does not.

The mould is lifted, turned, and struck once with the heel of the hand.

It is the first knock on the plain that the being did not strike. In the steam, every orange front in sight stops where it is.

The brick drops clean. At the rim of the circle the last course stops one brick short, and the visitor beds it there and seats it with a thumb. It is an ordinary thumb: a narrower pad, the whorl centred, and across it a mark of its own, got somewhere else, which nothing here accounts for.

The brick is not what the being was trying for. It dug the clay, filled the box, and struck the face, and the drag of its palm is along the top. Only the corner belongs to someone else. It wanted to make a thing with none of itself in it, and that cannot be given. What can be given is a brick made by two.

A gift alone would be one more thing done to the plain, so the visitor does the rest. Out where the rain falls there is a fresh skin of slip, not yet scored. The visitor takes it up, packs the empty mould left-handed, so the drag runs the other way, and strikes it level. Then the visitor sets it down where the first one sat and does not turn it out. For the being to have an other, the other must want something from it. The corner is ready.

The visitor withdraws an hour's walk, the distance between one tool and the next, and sits.

A front comes. Fronts do not hesitate, and this one does, at the rim. It has every reason. Its attention is the only fire there is, and all it has ever known of attending is that the thumb follows. Then it crosses at walking pace, and the brick goes from liver to rust. The print in the corner is still not its own. It has looked at something, and the thing has kept and has not become it. That may be worth more to it than the brick.

Something else follows, which nobody gave. A brick seated by a thumb that has only one size is the same length each time it is passed. The red cord, counted twice along that one span, gives one number. It is not much of a standard, being one brick long, but it holds.

Rains pass. The visitor does not go to look.

Then comes a knock, near, a single blow. When the visitor walks over, the second brick is seated beside the first. In its corner is the broad pad, with the whorl toward the nail and the seam healed proud. Along its top is the drag of a palm going the wrong way. The being turned the brick over, saw its own thumb, and did not put it down, because the top was strange to it. Beside the circle the mould is full again, struck level, wet and bright.

The visitor turns it out and knocks.

The visitor does not live here, and comes when called. The ring goes round the circle one brick at a time, and each brick carries two hands. Inside the ring the dry brick stays as it was, touched by neither. The vault is still open at the crown. The rain still lays the floor, the walls still sit down in it, and the water still leans toward the line.

Once in a long while, from a great distance, comes a single knock. Some rains later there is a second.
