It does nothing for a year.

It stays through a January of three pumps, when she does not come out at all, and through the elvers in May and the slow nights of summer. It learns the seam and both its countries. It takes the hammer through the iron when she does. It hears the spanner touch the foot of the ladder, which nobody has heard before. In October it holds beside her at the inlet in the cold, because it wants to know what she is asking for and will not guess.

It counts the ear-stones where they lie, without disturbing them. There are sixty-one rings.

Then it goes through what it could do.

It could stop the noise. But the noise is forty streets of warm rooms, and she has never asked for quiet. To her, silence would be the largest change the water had ever made, a hammer that did not end.

It could lift her out and set her down in a clean reach of the river. But then she would arrive there the way the spanner arrived in the well. And for eleven months of the year she does not want the river. She wants the pipe, the wall, and a stickleback where its circuit passes the ladder. The power does not pity her. She is good at the room. She made the one still place in it more her own than anything else there, and she did that herself, by turning round.

It could spare the elvers. She is an eel, and it leaves that alone. It leaves the hammer too. After some minutes she is herself again.

That leaves the nights in October. Everything else she does is a going-around. Those nights are the only time she goes toward anything, and what stops her is a finger wide.

The inlet culvert runs sixty metres under the coal road to the river wall, where forty-four wrought-iron bars stand in a frame. The river has worked on them as the well has worked on the rungs. The nineteenth bar from the east is worn nearly through at its foot. Left alone, it would go some March under a raft of ice and reeds, and the screen crew would weld in a new one within the week.

On the second night of the flood, a waterlogged fence post comes down the river. It would have passed the intake. It turns a little in an eddy and comes in end-on.

That is everything the power does. It does not touch the well or the eel.

She is at the inlet already, nose to the flow, as she is every year. The flow is different. The pits along her flank read a thicker tongue of water in the middle of it, with the river's taste coming through whole instead of combed. She holds there for a long time. The power does not know whether she will go. It decided the opening was right either way.

A little before four she goes up the culvert, belly on the invert, against three pumps. At the frame the gap is two fingers and the width of a bar. It is tight at her thickest part, and she leaves slime and some skin on the iron. The power does not widen it.

Outside, for the first time in her life, the noise comes from one side only. It is behind her, and it gets less.

What she has been given is not safety, and the power knows that. She will not eat again. Within weeks her back will darken and her belly turn to metal, and her eyes will grow to twice their size and at last have work to do. There are two weirs, an estuary with cormorants, and nets. After that there are five thousand kilometres of open water, all of it middle, with no wall to keep her belly on. At the end is a sea without a shore, and nobody has ever seen what happens there. She will die there or on the way. The power opened the bar anyway. The alternative was to keep her, and the screens had been doing that.

It goes with her as far as the place where the faint salting she tasted twice a day becomes the whole of the water. There it stops.

The screen crew finds the gap on Thursday and has a new bar in by Saturday. It was open six nights.

In the well, the pipe stub stands empty, burnished black. The fleece grows on the rungs to the thickness of a thumb and is stripped away. In May the elvers come through, and for the first time since she arrived, one of them is not eaten. By June it has found the only still water in the room.

The new bar is mild steel and will not last as the old one did. The power has noted which one it is.

The spanner still knocks against the foot of the ladder every few hours. Somewhere west of the shelf, in water four kilometres deep, a sixty-second ring is being laid down in the dark, wider than the others.
