The attention rests on the tableland for a long time before anything else does. It lasts one full circuit of the hearths, out of the east yards, through the middle rows and round by the power house. Nothing on the dome registers it. Being looked at is neither warm nor moving.

Then the visitor comes in. They cross the rim before dawn, dry, and at the temperature of the outer yards, so that no leaf leans off and none turns to face them. They carry no lamp. They learn the districts by skin, and while they are inside they keep the rules:

- **Motion:** The visitor keeps moving, except once.
- **Heat:** The visitor is never warmer than the air they stand in. A warm thing would be fed, and they have not come to be a hearth.
- **Moisture:** The visitor brings none.
- **The count:** The visitor lights nothing and puts nothing out. A furnace chokes on its clinker while they are standing in its yard, and they do not clear the grate. They stay in the cooling quarter while the growth slackens. When the new seam opens across the works, they walk over with the turning of the leaves.

The once is in the power house. The visitor stops in front of a switchboard and stands still. Within the hour a tendril finds a wrist and begins its first wrap. They let it. By the end of the day they are wound from ankle to shoulder in tight even coils, the way the bus bars are wound. It is the gesture the growth makes to a pulley. It is also the only greeting the place has, and its only means of learning the shape of anything. Once, a gust parts the leaves and a red point the size of a seed shows on the board. Then the visitor eases out, slowly enough that no stem breaks. The hollow winding they leave behind is full of shoots by morning.

The visitor has come with things to give, as any power would. They go through them one at a time.

Light. Every lamp could be rethreaded in an afternoon. But there is no eye on the dome. A leaf would know a lamp only as a small heat, and would wrap the bulb and char against the glass. The light would be for the visitor.

Water. Rain on forty-odd furnaces would be steam and split firebrick. Anything less than rain would simply close, the way a cut closes.

Seed. The growth could be given a flower, a fruit, and something to carry the fruit over the rim. But what landed on the plains would not be a plant. It would be a fire that learned patience here, on this tar, among these rakes. Anywhere else it would be an ordinary fire, and brief.

Company. Something could be set to bore in the stems or nest in the thatch. But the growth has no thorn and no bitterness in the leaf, having never needed them. Company would begin by eating it. It would have to learn hostility, in exchange for neighbours it has no way to perceive.

Waking. It sounds asleep, and it could be woken. To be awake here would be to have a word for what lies past the rim, and another for the tar going down, and still to have no legs.

When the gifts are set aside, they turn out to have one thing in common. Each would mend something in the visitor. It is the visitor who minds the dark, whose mouth is dry, and who would like to be answered. The thing that lives here wants one thing, in the only way it can want, which is to hold its temperature. It holds it.

The tar takes longest. The visitor goes down below the sumps beneath the sumps and takes its depth. There is a great deal of it, and it is not endless. Filling it would be easy and would never be noticed.

They do not fill it. The thing overhead was asked once how a fire might last on a reservoir it could have gone through in a season, and it answered with leaves. It was asked what to do when the belts rotted and the tines wore to stubs, and it grew belts and tines. Every part of it is an answer to something. It has not yet been asked what to do when the tar runs low. It has a town's weight of heartwood it has never burned, and the sun lays a warmth on its hide every day that it has never turned to. The visitor does not know what it would answer. Filling the tar would make sure the question never came, and questions are all that ever made it more than a fire.

This may be wrong. The tar is deep enough that there is time to be wrong slowly, and the visitor resolves to take the depth again each time they come.

They go out as they came in, at the rim, before dawn. There is the film on the underside of the outermost roof plate. The visitor touches it, and then tastes it, and it tastes very faintly of camphor.

So it is not the night's water. Oil burns to smoke and water. The growth takes back the smoke before it can climb the stack, but it cannot take back the water, because nothing wet lasts. The water goes up through the thatch as vapour at all hours, invisible in that baked air. Only here, at the coldest hour, under the coldest plate, does a little of it show, the way breath shows on a window.

The film does not come to the tableland. It leaves. The thing that cannot cross its own rim has been crossing it every morning for longer than its oldest heartwood has stood, out over the bare slope and down toward the plains.

Here the visitor does the one thing they do. With a thumb they turn down the outer corner of the plate, by about the thickness of a fingernail.

The next dawn the film gathers, runs to the low corner, hangs, and falls before the plate warms. It lands on bare rock an arm's length outside the wall of leaf, with a tick too small to hear under the ticking of the plate.

That is all that is arranged. The rest is left to things already on their way: ash-dust off the spoil banks, and the wind up the slope with whatever the wind carries. One drop each dawn makes a dark spot on the rock that is gone by mid-morning. In time it may make a pit the size of a thumbprint that holds its damp until noon. Later there may be a grey crust at the edge of the pit, something cold, wet and slow that feeds on light. It would be everything the growth is not, and it would be living on its breath. The two would never touch. The wall leans back from the cold, and the crust would not last a minute inside the wall. But in the very long run the count may drop by one and the wall draw in by a yard. The ground it gave up would not be given up to nothing.

Or the spot stays a spot. A drop a night is not much. The visitor does not stay to make sure, because making sure is how the other gifts begin.

The visitor does come back, once in each circuit of the hearths. They cross the rim dry and no warmer than the yards. They take the depth of the tar. They stand still in front of the switchboard for an hour and are wrapped. When the count drops, they mean to be in that yard, to stay while it cools, and to light nothing.

Before dawn a film of water forms on the underside of the outermost roof plate, an arm's length from the last black leaf. It runs to the turned corner and falls. It is the only thing that has ever left the tableland, and it leaves every night. The thing that lives here does not know it gives anything. For a little while after sunrise, the rock outside its wall is wet.
