The power arrives the way a jet's sound does, after the thing has passed. The ice has been going for a century and the animal is twenty years old. Whatever could have been prevented here was not. So the first discipline is the one the animal has never managed with the aircraft: to look at where things are now.

For a full year the power does only that. It is present at dawn when the silt is crusted stiff, through the sun window, and under the grey lid when the pellets bounce. It is there for the loud week and for the winter, when hay dries on a back that is one more stone. The lammergeier checks on the animal most days for its own reasons. Now something else does, for the animal's.

The year is not hesitation. The basin is what a place becomes when it is altered from far away by something that never came to look, and the power does not intend to be the second such thing.

By the end of the year it has set several things aside.

It will not put the ice back. It could, but the ice would cover the cushions, the shelducks' pond, the pikas' moraine, and the hollow in the oldest ground. Everything here is making do on the new floor, and the animal has no other.

It will not clean the glacier. The soot is renewed every afternoon from below the lip, so cleaning it is daily work, and the basin already has someone doing it a tongue's width at a time. The animal has found the only scale at which the job can be done from here. That the scale is too small is not the animal's failing. The rest of the job lies a thousand kilometres south, among people who are mostly cooking and getting to work, and what the power does there is a longer account than this one. It follows the same rule and begins with going to see.

It will not bring people in. They would come kindly, and then there would be a cairn, and then a cable. They would find a symptom, a specimen, or an emblem, and the animal is none of those to itself.

It will not carry the animal down toward the orange light. What it came from is down there, but what it became is not.

And it will not make a second one. A made companion would be the only thing in the basin put there for a purpose. Besides, the question is open. Any animal faces its food, and any forager turns things over. The power could settle the matter by looking inside the animal. It prefers to ask, and there is a way to ask.

The power knows one thing that from the ground can only be called likelier. It knows what falls. Among the aphids and spiderlings, about one afternoon in three summers, the wind drops something else on the snow: grey, the size of a barley grain, cold-stunned, with its hands already folded under it. A snowfinch has it before dusk. This has happened six or seven times since the ice drew back. The animal has never turned one over. Forty finches cover the snow faster than one animal turning stones, and they have got there first every time.

So on one such afternoon, the power does a thing a walker could do with a fingertip. The grain comes down a hand's breadth to one side of where it would have, in the lee of a flat stone at the snow margin, and the day's melt draws it under. Nothing else is arranged. The finches are not kept off, the cold is not softened, and the animal is not steered. It will come along the snow line under the flat light, and it will turn the stone, because it turns them all, and it will look, because it always looks. If it eats, then it was foraging all along, the basin has had one more meal, and nothing has been made too much of.

The fourth sign is there the next morning. Among the flat stones lying wet side up, one lies dry side up. It has been lifted and set back in its own print. Nothing in the basin has put a stone back before.

The other signs take longer to read. That afternoon the snow line is worked only as far as the stone. Beyond it the finches go on alone and find the day's delivery untouched. The click comes from that stretch of margin through the evening, more often than the ground would account for, though the ground there is settling too. By late summer there are things laid at the stone's edge: a moth, a few flies, set down whole. A cache would look the same.

The animal still looks up at the aircraft, and still at the wrong place. On clear nights it still goes down to the rock lip and faces the stain. The power did not expect one found thing to cancel the rest, and did not try for that.

It does one other thing, once. On an afternoon that summer the cloud is twenty minutes late over the lip. A plane crosses on the corridor. There are a couple of hundred windows on each, and someone is usually looking. This time what lies below is not a grey lid but a pale floor, threads of water, a dirty tongue of ice, and a pond of a colour the passenger has no name for. The passenger keeps looking back along the wing after it is gone. Below, the animal has stopped and tipped its face toward the sound. They miss each other by about half a minute. It is the closest the two ends have come. The power leaves the half minute alone, since that gap is not the power's to close.

When the ponds skin over, the animal walks to the hollow in the oldest ground. Its line of prints across the last snow patch shows three feet. One forefoot does not touch. It lowers itself, folds its hands under it, and stops. The frost takes the coat, and the pikas lay their sedge across its back.

There is no telling from outside what is under the hands, and the power does not look. It will be here in spring, when the ice shells start collapsing in the sun window. It can wait as the pikas wait for their hay.
