Something did come, eventually, that could count.

It came over the rim at night with the cold, which is the quiet way in, and for the first year it only watched. It stood in each band of the green at each hour. It counted the cycle and got forty seconds, and then, having the patience, counted more closely and got forty and a little over. The little is new, as mountains reckon it, a second gained in some thousands of years. The thing under the floor is in no hurry, but it has a direction.

It lay on the tide mark where she lies and learned what the pulse is like through a jawbone. It went down with her to the works and stepped back when she stepped back.

Then it went through the obvious things, which are always offered first. It could mend the leg. But a mended leg goes up the scoria and into the wind on an animal wearing a bib and a ridge of moss, and the matter would be settled in the two nights it was owed. It could give back the coat as well, which would felt within the month, unless she were also lifted out and set down on the dry side, sound and fleeced and several years behind. She would stand in the wire grass with her jaw to cold ground, waiting for a pulse.

Each of these was a way of telling her the years in the bowl had been an error, now corrected. The visitor had seen those years' work: the white bands on the flues, the lined nests, the big leaf grown out of its band beside the dung pile. It did not look like an error. It looked like a living.

Next it asked whose sorrow it had been looking at, a question the powerful mostly skip. When the violet comes up on the rocks and nothing happens to her, something is certainly felt, by whoever is watching. In her the visitor found attention, and the smell of the charge, and no ache. The mornings were different. At the line, when the last of the herd goes over, there is something in her about the size of one breath of a flue. The visitor did not pretend otherwise. Then it passes, and she tips a leaf and drinks.

That left two things.

The hoof was the simple one. The slipper on the fourth foot had perhaps two years before it curled round into the pad, and that would be pain with nothing in it, no nest lined by it and no use to anyone. The visitor was not above a repair. One morning on the level path the long horn caught between two stones it had cleared a thousand times and broke off clean at the proper length. She stood a moment with the foot up, then went on.

The calf was harder. It was not choosing the bowl. The wet was choosing for it, a frond at a time, before it was old enough to have a side. And the line it stopped at each morning was not something a young animal could try, because one step was warm and the next was frost. Nothing learns to swim from a cliff.

There was also the forty and a little over. A bowl is a good place and a poor only place. The herd keeps the door open from its side, every dusk. From this side the door was closing with one calf's coat.

The visitor did not want to push the calf through. It wanted a door that could be gone through slowly, or stood in.

So it went up to the rim above the widest descent, found a slab that was coming down anyway in three hundred years or so, and leaned. The slab went in the night. The thin air carried the sound badly and the moss took what was left. Behind it was a crack, and the crack went down to the warmth. By morning a tongue of bare black ground ran from the tide mark to the lip, four metres wide, with frost on either side and none on it. It had warm rock underfoot and the empty wind blowing across it, and it was the one place on the mountain both warm and dry.

That was all it did. It led nobody there. It could have looked ahead to see what came of it, and did not, since not deciding was the whole use of the thing. It left in the morning, uphill, among the herd.

---

The calf found the tongue within the week, because the herd's young go up that way. It no longer stops as at the edge of water. It walks out as into shallows and stands with warm feet and wind in its face while the others go over. Some mornings two or three of them stay with it, later than they should.

Along its back, where the wind reaches, the fleece has held. Below that it is slate. There is nothing else like it on the mountain.

Wire grass has seeded in along the edges of the tongue, where the moss won't come. The calf pulls at it. She has tried it too, and her teeth remembered it.

She goes up a few lengths, which is a few lengths past the line, and lies down with warm ground under her belly, as before, and for once nothing dripping on her. By evening the bib is dry. In the third storm of that season it lifted off her chest a little and ticked. She looked down at it. Nobody arranged that part.

On clear dawns the calf goes to the top of the tongue and stands at the lip. In front of it is black rock and the wind. Behind it the plume goes straight up, white, visible from valleys away. It has not gone over, and it has not come down yet either.

Below, the first flue lets out its breath, and then the next. There is time.
