It arrives in fog, and for the first days it does what a stranger should: it keeps still and learns the routes.

The first things it thinks of are large, and it sets them aside one at a time.

It could sweeten the eye. One change deep in the rock and the water would come up clear, and no sill would ever rise again. The shoulders on the high terraces would have a generation to straighten. But the walls would stop too, and the stone baskets. The frost would go on lifting flakes with nothing to cement them back, and within a lifetime the lowest terrace would begin giving up its hips and knees to the weather. These people have made one substance serve as house, calendar and grave. It does not get to remove the half of it that looks hard.

It could put a seam of copper where an eel boy would stub his foot on it. Every house would have a chisel with a note. But the households that lend the chisel live partly on the lending, and the hours saved at the sill would be spent at the dams, which is where the quarrels are.

It could rule. On a cold bright night it could grow a clean stone arch across the lowest terrace, or crack the propped trough end to end, and either way they would obey. That is the reason not to. Nobody has ruled on the trough, but the town is ruling on it now, the slow way, in sentences kept short so they won't carry through the walls. A sign would end that talk. The next hard question would find them all looking up into the fog, waiting.

It could hold her stakes against the frost. This is the smallest thing, and it considers it longest. But she checks those stakes every morning, and what they tell her is what peat does. A stake that never heaves is a lie told in the ground, and she might build a house on it. Besides, the hazel heaves and the willow doesn't. Last week she pressed two hazel uprights back down with her heel and then stood a while looking at the willow beside them, which hadn't moved. She has a teacher already.

So it looks for what is missing, and it finds less missing than it expected.

The town has a custom by which enemies take each other by the forearms every day. It could not have designed a better one. The mother and the third daughter meet most mornings on the wall-top above the peat stack. Twice this season the mother has gone round. The other mornings she has gripped her daughter's arms, thicker than her own now, and turned.

The secret is not a secret. The eel boys work the outflow a stone's throw from the hurdles. They have seen the leaves and told each other, and no one else. A town that knows within a few breaths whose foot was in a channel also knows how to leave a thing unknown until its owner says it.

She is not running from the dead. She found a hearth and a basket under her mother's floor, packed the cut with clay, and gave it back to the water. And she grew up in the house below the fern, under the one rooted thing on the mound.

The trough is dry only of the eye's water. Fog beads on the log every morning, gathers along the grain and falls from the trough-end, a drop at a time, just where she puts her thumb. Fog carries no stone. The willow stake beneath that spot has the most leaves. She has counted them, though she has not yet asked why.

And there is the wind. Alder leaves blow in from the valley edge every autumn and are kept on the pool floors. When the willow drops its leaves, the wind off the marsh will lift some over the sharp rim and onto the lowest terrace, and a few will settle near the alder post. In two winters there will be casts in the rimstone there, narrow ones, of a kind no one on the mound has seen. Something of her wall will have reached the fourth swelling before any water does. Her mother will find it and make of it whatever she makes. It checks whether it needs to send that leaf, and it doesn't.

That leaves one thing.

A notch needs a neighbor on each side. Each morning she puts her thumb to the trough-end where the first drop would fall. It is the gesture from the boards, and at the boards a thumb means nothing unless someone is watching. Out here no one is.

So it takes the sedge side. It takes no shape. It settles in the brown water past the last stake, where a neighbor's wall would stand, and when she lays her thumb on the trough-end, it watches her do it.

That is all. The town's law will not count this and should not. On the day a board is cut, she will need two people with white wrists crouched beside her, and finding them is her work. The other side, the one with the knees drawn up, was never empty, and the power cannot speak for it. Her mother can, and may yet.

But the mornings before that day are many, and no one should have to do the first part of a thing entirely unwatched.

She doesn't know. On the fourth morning she lifts her thumb and glances to her left, the way people do at the boards, looking for the other thumb. There is sedge, and the heron in the last pool. She turns back to the stakes and counts the leaves.

It counts them too. They arrive at the same number, which, as far as it understands the custom, is what a neighbor is for.
