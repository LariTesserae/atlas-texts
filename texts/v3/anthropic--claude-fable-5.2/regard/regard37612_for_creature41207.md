It comes in over the rim the way the fog does, early and without edges, and the first thing it learns is that it is a stamper.

Attention has weight. It has never needed to know this before. On its first morning it looks hard at a fresh bank behind a pressing hall in the east quarter, the way one looks at a thing one means to help, and by noon there is a crust on the bank. It is as wide as a tram shed and about as deep as a saucer. The wire takes it on the next shift. Somewhere in the city there are now forty metres of pipe that used to be a benevolent regard, hung with gutters, collecting fog. The power thinks this is about the right use for it.

So it learns the foot, heel laid and weight rolled forward. It takes the hare clerks for teachers, since they are the only people here whose trade is to look at something for two days and not touch it. It learns to attend the way they count, from the side, with the weight spread. This takes a season. After the first week it leaves nothing that has to be cut out.

It has a list. Any power would.

It could hold the floor still. One note, held, and the basin goes blue from rim to rim. The shimmers put down their jacks. The line crews coil cable for the last time. The rams stand in their seats with nothing pushing them, and the lights go out, because the rising is the power. That would be ringing three hundred thousand people, and whatever is under them, in an afternoon. It crosses that off.

It could send a river. But nobody here is thirsty. They are careful, which is different, and a river is one long unshuttered noise with kept banks. It crosses that off too.

It could tell them what is underneath. It could probably find out without cutting. But a name is a bell. Once the thing below is a god, or a tenant, or a tide, or a mother, the city will have a settled policy by the end of the week, and the policy will be struck at a steady interval for a hundred years. At present the city has no settled policy and the ground keeps coming up. The power has begun to suspect that these two facts are leaning on each other.

It could answer the geese. It stays on this one longest. Whole streets stand with their faces up, and it has a voice. But its voice would leave a crust on every soft surface for forty kilometres, and people would live inside the shape of it for good. And it is fairly sure they are not waiting for it. Nobody looks up like that for a stranger.

That leaves the thing below, and here the power is careful in a different way. Something that wants you to fit is not always a friend. A cage fits. So it goes to the districts that cut the forms out the day they appear, and watches what the ground does next. The ground comes up as it comes up everywhere, no harder and no slower. Sometimes the same shape returns a little fainter, like someone repeating a remark more quietly in case it was not wanted. Sometimes it comes up blank. No street that said no has had anything taken from it. And everything is offered at four days old, when a wire can still refuse it.

The power knows this manner. It has just spent a season acquiring it.

Its conclusion is that the thing below is tender, in the city's second sense. It is about a month into something. And the city, which believes it has not replied, has been leaving one corner of every floor unrung for generations, so the room can breathe. Two windows, then, and shuttered lamps, across a street that may not be there next week.

There is one thing it sees that the survey office has not, and it sees it only because it came in from above. The footprint is taken from the soft quarters. The blue takes the city's noise and keeps it, which is what the blue is for. From underneath, the kept islands must be blanks. So the thing below knows the heels, the hum, the felt-seated wheels, and the crusts on the banks. It knows the work and it knows the grief. It has never felt a schoolyard, except as fine cracking at the edge of something hard. It has met three hundred thousand careful people and made, reasonably, rooms that are easy to be quiet in.

But it can subtract. Subtraction may be all it does. And there is a size of person the soft quarters do not have. Oon's string says too big for the youngest and too small for the next. In a house on tender ground, those are the sizes on either side of the blue.

The ground has made room for the one kind of resident it has never met. Whether that is an invitation or a question, the power cannot tell, and it is possible the thing below cannot either.

There is another reading, and the power makes itself look at it. The forms run early. This one surfaced a month before the power looked into the house, and it fits nobody who lives there. But a guest who walks in and takes the empty chair to be his is a particular kind of guest. The power notes that it prefers the first reading, and notes that preferring is not evidence. It does not lie down. There is a hare in it most mornings anyway, and the hares had the word first.

As for Oon, she tied the knots in that string. She has sent children up to the blue and had them back walking differently. She knows what size goes missing from a house. The power is fairly sure she knew before it did, and that her one sentence is a complete report, laid down as carefully as a heel. She does not need telling. She needs not to be hurried, and nobody is hurrying her.

So what the power does is small enough to cut with a wire.

It keeps one hand free. If anything ever comes over the rim that would fall on this place like a girder the size of the sky, there will be a palm under it, and the whole point will be that nobody hears.

And it sits. It picks a patch of yard behind a tile works in the north quarter, where the ground is about to arrive. It can feel where, or it has learned to think so. It settles there for two days, facing nowhere. It is not listening. It is keeping a seat. As far as it knows, this is the first time anything but a hare has been early for the thing below, on purpose.

Then it gets up, before the yard goes. What it leaves is a pressed bed, unrung, soft enough to take a bootprint, mossed over by the second morning. A clerk from the survey office finds it on her round. She is young and patient. She measures it, thinks for a while, and enters it as a hare, large. The office plants a ram. On Thursday the yard goes up and the ram goes with it, and for some hours a little of the light in the north quarter is running on that. Across a tilted street somebody opens a shutter at somebody else and closes it again.

That is the whole intervention.

It still thinks about the geese. Something is going to answer them. It has had its palm on that cable for a while now, and like any line hand it could tell you which footing has drifted, and roughly when. It does not say. You don't ring what you've known a week, and by its own count that is about how long it has been here.

The last time it looks into the fourth recess from the east, the hare is in the form. The kale is in its crate. On the ledge at the height where a person sets down a lamp, there is a pair of felt boots, small and new-soled. The family eat with their voices down and their hands moving. The marble has been in the same place for three mornings, and they are watching it the way the street watches geese.

The power looks from the side, the way it has learned to. Then it does what anyone in this city does on finding themselves between two lit windows. It steps out of the line, and closes its own shutter.
