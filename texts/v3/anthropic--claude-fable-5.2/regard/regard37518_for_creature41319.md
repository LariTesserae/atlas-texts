Something has noticed the town. She is the kind of thing that can move a river, and she is kind, and she knows those are two separate facts.

She arrives on a warm afternoon and stops in the sedge flats, forty paces off, where the town is a wasp in cupped hands. She stands there through the ten-minute rain and well past it. The first thing she understands is that nothing in that place is free. Whatever goes in takes up volume, and whatever is said in there is kept. A voice that can move rivers, used once inside the wall, would stand in some room for longer than the wood has been wood, leaning on things. So before she goes in she decides to say nothing, and she goes in the way the snake does, low, hearing through the ground. She steps over the dip in the threshold and not into it.

Then she does what anything that can help does first, which is look for what is wrong. She finds five things.

- **The fourth hour.** Every night the carrying fails and the town is set down. The cure is simple: one frog that does not tire. She waits up to see what she would be curing. The lanes draw away. The tower goes off without reluctance. The thing lies at its length, mostly quiet, in no danger she can find. Then the frog starts, and it comes in a stride for each call, and nothing is hauling it. A thing that is never set down never gets to walk back. She leaves the frogs their tiredness.

- **The east edge.** She could stand the three folded rooms up with a breath. But the note in them would be hers, and nothing a thumb long could feed it. She would have to stay and hold it forever, or let it fall a second time. Meanwhile the new cane is at shin height and square at the corners. The limp is only the town keeping its weight off a foot that is mending.

- **The flood.** It takes the oldest season off the bottom of every room and carries it out the gate. She could bank the silt and keep it all. She goes and stands at a doorway at the tower's foot, where it all has been kept, and feels the push of a river at thigh depth and a river's indifference. This is what keeping does. The flood is how the rest of the town stays light enough to come to the door.

- **The moth.** This is the hard one, because it is small and she could do it with two fingers. A moth hangs out in the middle of a closet off the north lane, visibly distant, flying. She watches it most of a morning. It is not struggling. It is the one creature in the town that has ever travelled in the country instead of the hamlet, and it is crossing real ground at its own speed. At noon the cicadas haul it in. It lands on the wall and folds its wings, and she is glad she kept still.

- **The drawer.** The largest. Something the size of a country is folded into forty rooms. The grand kindness would be to hush the chorus and let it out. But she has seen it out: the warmth of skin spread so thin it is barely there, no doors, nowhere to stand. She has seen which way it steps when the frog calls, and felt the rooms run a degree warmer after rain. Some things are not shut in by their folds. The folds are where their feet come down.

Five faults, and each one is holding something up.

So she does other things.

She lets herself be held. She picks a corner room, one of the slow low ones, and goes in and sits on the silt. The pitch rises a shade. Within a minute the frogs have retuned and it has her, lightly, on every side, in the sternum. This does not happen to her often. Things that can move rivers mostly do the holding. She stays an hour. The plate edges lift by a hair around her and settle.

She climbs the tower, because there is one thing she wants to ask, which is how this comes out. In the tower the answer arrives first. What comes up the shaft to her is her own voice, a short flat call, the sound of an account being settled, and she has not made it yet. She works it through. Nobody pays here who has not been given something, and nobody who has paid leaves owing. So she will be given something and will leave even. That was what she wanted to know. She makes the call. It is the only sound she makes in the town, and it is in there now, low down near the silt, with your cough and your counted paces.

She goes out by the lower gate and walks upstream. This is the one piece of work, and it is outside the wall, where she can do it without being kept. A mile and a half above the bend there is a place where the near bank stands a hand lower than the far one, and that hand of difference is the whole of the river's memory. She does not touch it. She learns where it is. If the river goes on forgetting for some years running, with the rooms filling and the thresholds going still, she will remind it: ankle-deep, walking pace, no more. If it ever remembers too well, she will have a word with it about that. That is the entire intervention. It has not happened and may never.

At the next flood she is a mile down, standing in the brown water among the herons with her head cocked. The water comes loud. It carries last season's frogs and the cicadas dead since autumn. Under them runs the oldest season, the one the town has just stopped remembering. Near the bottom there is a cough, some counted paces, what might be a name, and one short flat call she recognises. She could keep all of it. She listens until the water runs quiet instead, so that what was let go is heard through once by someone who knows what it was. The herons are listening for frogs.

She has not been inside since. Some warm nights she is in the sedge flats, forty paces off, in the low ground where the river used to bend. At the fourth hour the wasp in the cupped hands goes still. She has learned to stand through those minutes and do nothing, and it took her longer than she would like to say.

Then something a thumb long, with a wet throat, does what she came to do.
