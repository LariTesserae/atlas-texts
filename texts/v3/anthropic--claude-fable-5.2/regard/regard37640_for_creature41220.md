The first thing it does is stop looking.

It learns to from a distance. Where its attention settles on the wall, the moths draw aside and a seam of bare rock opens, garnets red in it. It meant nothing by looking, but neither does the sun. So it lifts its attention off the cleft the way you lift a hand off a sleeping animal, and it thinks for a while about how to be good to something that feels regard as a kind of injury.

Then it comes up the mountain on foot, at night, under cloud, with no light. It comes small, in a shape with fingers, since the one law of the place concerns grip.

At the mouth the choughs shuffle and give it a ledge's width. Inside, the dust closes around it at arm's length. It goes ten paces over the blocks and everything stops: wings, birds, bats. A single drip. It knows what is being asked. It keeps still and wants nothing from the wall. After five or six breaths the hiss rises again around the low note, and it has been let in on the same terms as a bat.

It stays until the cloud begins to grey. Most of what it does there is decline.

It stands longest at the north wall. Lifting the soot would be easy. But the creature is young and almost nothing about it is settled, and the black flame is the one thing it knows for certain. Take the scar and the knowledge goes with it, and the next smoke arrives as a surprise. It leaves the burn.

It could bring the wings onto the wind's note, or bring the fast pulse and the slow one into step. Something practising its only word is not helped by having the word said for it. It leaves that too.

It kneels at a pool the size of a cupped hand, and the swimmer goes still at the tremor of its knee on the block. The swimmer is alone and blind. A companion would be a kindness measured against the visitor's heart and not against the pool, which feeds one.

It puts a palm on each slab and feels the tick. The opening that made room for the creature will one day make too much room, and the warmth will go out through the top like breath. It could pin the mountain. It considers what it would mean to make permanent a thing built of arrivals, the way a fire is, and it takes its hands away.

What it does in the cleft is what every guest does. It sits on the flat block beside the grinding hollow and gives off a little warmth. Toward morning a moth loses its grip overhead and lands on its knee, and it eats it, because to refuse the floor's food would be to hold itself apart, and nothing here is apart. The moth tastes of fat and walnut. It picks up the river cobble, feels the one round thing in the place, and sets it back exactly in its print.

It leaves one thing behind. It had carried a name up the mountain, a word for what lives here, and it was pleased with the word. Sitting in the dark, it sees that a name is a way of looking, and that the creature would part for it as it parts for the sun. So it lets the name go. What falls belongs to the floor. The white fungus has it by morning.

It is out of the mouth before the blade of light comes through the slot.

Its real work is elsewhere, because the creature is from elsewhere. It walks the flyway backward, down off the ridge and out over the plains, to the ground where the moths spend the wet months as grubs under grass roots, and along the dark road of air they take each spring. Here too it keeps the rule, giving only by dropping and leaving the picking up to others. In a town whose lamps pull thousands of wings out of the sky each year, it puts out no lamp. It leaves a question lying on a windowsill, with a grey moth beside it, where the kind of person who kneels will find them. On the plains it leaves the same question at the edge of a field.

Among the people below, two stories of the cleft are still told, one about a cobble and one about smoke. It silences neither. It tells the first whenever it is asked, where children can hear: you go up, you carry a stone, you kneel, and you take what is on the floor.

The cold is the hardest part. All season the creature climbs after it, a hand's width a day, and the visitor has seen what the creature has not, that the mountain has a top. This is the largest work it takes on. None of it happens in the cleft, and it does not expect to finish.

It never goes in again. Being attended to once is enough for something that dislikes it. But on evenings when wind crosses the slot, the low note carries down as far as the first trees, and the note is a thing the creature lets go of, so it may be taken. The visitor has water of its own, very wide, and lives as everything in this account lives, on what falls in from above. The note falls in. It goes still and listens.

Some evenings the note breaks off for five or six breaths. It does not go up to see. It waits, as the floor waits.

Then the sound comes down again. Lately there is something under it that the wind did not put there.
