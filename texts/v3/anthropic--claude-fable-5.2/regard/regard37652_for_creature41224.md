It arrives the way everything arrives here. In a gap between two sleet bands the din dips and the lanes thicken. Among the scrapers shouldering in from nowhere is a wader that was not there before, on six feet and already walking. The visitor could have come as weather or as a voice. It takes the shape whose knee is the highest point on the plain, because it will not stand taller than what it came to see, and because that shape has the only eyes.

While it stays, the sum of waders and humps is out by one. Nothing in the basin counts, so nothing notices.

For a long time it does nothing. It crosses the grain at a wader's pace, lifting each foot a little ahead of the mites, and lets the whistles go past in order. It carries the hammer and does not use it.

It listens longest at the wet knot behind the tongue, in a great many animals of all three kinds. It finds the lane there: the current, the stroke, the blue stitch at the plate edge. It finds nothing that wants to be elsewhere. It does not take this for proof, and it goes on listening, in case.

Then it goes through what it could do.

It could let the worn ones stop. But the only rest the basin offers is rime, and to change that it would have to unmake the mites. The mites are a kind too. It did not come to choose among the kinds.

It could stay the hammers. It follows a slowing scraper that no wader reaches and hears the pores close one note at a time. The animal is most of a shift becoming floor, and the tongue works the whole while. The hammer comes down once, on a seam. It leaves the hammers alone, without making itself like them.

It could put the mountains back. It has the strength. But nothing added here goes up. The range would not stand. The floor would be three billion years old and still a hand high.

Then it comes to the humps, and it stops being sure it is the wiser of the two.

The wader has found the one way to stop, and it found it with no help. It lets the first rule take it and the second rule keep it. The mites glaze it, the smearers make it too old to scrape, and the lead lane splits around it and closes. This is rest in a place that cannot permit rest, built from the two laws that forbid it. The wader gets up from it on six feet. The visitor would not have thought of it.

But being new, it sees that few go. Most step back from the edge when their feet whiten and walk on, on five feet, then four, then three. They stay away because, from where a wader stands, a hump is what is left of a wader. None has seen one get up. The getting up happens in the drop of the hum, when nothing is seen to move. The only evidence is the banding, and that is gone in a couple of dozen steps. By then the one who wore it is listening for a flat note and has nothing to tell. And waders use their eyes in one direction only, down at the worn-through places, at their own backs.

So the knowledge is made fresh each time and lost within sight of where it was made. The one who rests cannot keep it. One who watched could. Whether rester and watcher are two beings or one being in two places, the visitor cannot tell. The joints taught it not to assume. It makes no difference to the keeping.

It could make the banding stay, a ring of grey and pink at the ankle. That would be the first mark of a hand on the plain, worn by those who never asked for it, and it would tell them something they had not seen for themselves. It leaves the legs alone.

Instead it finds a worn-through place with a swell close by and a wader at the edge on three feet. It walks to the swell, where the film runs thin, and folds. The knees come down past the back and the hammer tucks under.

The mites have it in minutes, and it lets them. Then the smearers go up one side and down the other, each laying its one fold into the glaze and through it: a hook, a shut S, a pinched eye. It was older than a billion and a half years when it arrived. It had never been that old in one place, banded, with weight. The lead lane splits around it and closes.

The hum drops a tone.

It is one lane over, on six feet, grey and pink to the knee. It walks to the edge of the hole in as few steps as it can and stands beside the wader on three feet.

Below is the basin one shift behind. The wader sees its own back in a ring of three knees and, off to the side, a hump on a swell. Level with its eyes is the same swell with the film lying flat. Beside it are legs that are not its own, banded like the floor, the bands fading upward.

The visitor does not know what the wader makes of this. It can put a thing where eyes are, but it cannot see on the wader's behalf. The wader looks for a long time. Its feet begin to whiten. Then it walks, on three, to the swell.

The visitor stays one more shift, circling the swell at the slowest pace that counts as not stopping. A wader on five feet comes to the edge of the hole. The hum drops. The film on the swell lies flat, and a little way off a wader is walking on six feet. The one at the edge watches its legs for every step the banding lasts.

Then the wind builds, a sleet band crosses, and the lanes thin out to merely touching. One wader thins out with them, and the sum is what it was.

The floor is a billion and a half years old, as before. The lid is on. The hammers come down. Nothing leaves. On most days there are more humps than there used to be, and a wader on three feet is harder to find. At the worn-through places they still stand looking down at their own backs. But now and then one lifts its head and looks across the lanes at a swell, where a pale thing is getting up, grey and pink to the knee. For those few steps it is not listening.
