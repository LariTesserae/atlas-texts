The plateau was noticed once, by something that could do more than the town can, which is saying a good deal.

It looked first, the way visitors splash. In a moment it had the depth of every course, the count of the shrimp, which households had a fox, and how the groove on the southern lip was made. Then, from habit, it listed what it could do. It could have a river running by evening, graded and branched, over gravel that had never been anywhere else, and the ashes on the east side could go into it. It could put a second groove on a step the surveyor's boots had never touched. It could tell her what it knew. It could give the children their answer about the geese.

Then it saw that every item on the list had already been thought of by someone in town with the hands to do it, and declined, and the declining taught to children. It had come expecting to be needed. It found forty thousand people doing its own work at a smaller scale, including the half of the work it finds hard.

So it came up the escarpment on foot. It had nothing in its pockets and stopped at the bin anyway. It splashed. Someone raised an arm at five hundred metres, and it raised one back, and then there were several minutes of walking with nothing to do, which it was not used to. By afternoon its stride had flattened. A flock lifted while it was in the middle of a thought, and it stopped, because it had not been interrupted in a very long time, and found that it liked it.

At the southern lip it stood at the markers with its hands behind its back. Then it got down on the wet stone and put an ear to the groove. It already knew what there was to know about a finger of water moving over a few grains. It stayed down longer than the adults nearby thought dignified.

The surveyor was the part it liked least. It knew what she wanted to know, and saw that the knowledge was worth less than she thought. A boot set down in some earlier year, with nothing in mind but the next step, is not a drain. It is a thing that got away, and by the town's own ledger that counts either way, like the voles. The town has not noticed that its ledger says this, because it has left itself out. But an answer handed down goes in the column with the straw. And most of the children ask why the geese are allowed. Sooner or later one of them, born here and without any thought of having come, will refuse to let it drop, and will ask why the surveyor isn't. The visitor left it to her. It did not look ahead to see which child or what year. It could wait, having spent a day learning how.

After the next thaw, the surveyor saw someone on the lip a kilometre off, walking toward her, bent, with a palm to the stone. She did not know the gait. An arm went up and she raised hers. When they met, the stranger said, low, that there was nothing on that stretch. She nodded, since that is the usual report, and each went on the way the other had come. She had walked that lip alone for fifteen thaws.

It fed a fox before it left. This is discouraged.

On the way down it noticed that it had never looked to see where the rivers will go, and that it did not intend to. It left a request with the surveyors to be told. Everyone would agree that is the right office.

The town did not know it had been visited and did nothing to its visitor on purpose. The visitor crosses water differently now, and stops when it is interrupted. The town did not intend this, so by the ledger it counts.
