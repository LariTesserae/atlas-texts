It arrives the way the cloud does, at the same height, and for a long time it does only what the cloud does, which is rest against the place.

It stays through one full thatching, because that is the smallest measure in which the shelf shows all of itself. It watches the roofs go round. It learns the count under the eaves, and after a while its own thinking falls on every fourth drop. It watches two people meet on a causeway and turn, and it watches a house receive a child. Most of all it watches the woman at the end of the standing slabs, who drew its attention in the first place: someone cutting a channel toward a field that is not there, at a pace that will never let her see water in it.

A power like this holds a great deal above the line. By the only law the shelf has, surplus should go on to the next. But the same law says a cistern fills to its mark and no higher. So it goes through what it has, the way a person goes through a basket before a long causeway, to see what is worth carrying across.

It could leave a single iron chisel beside her groove. She would finish the branch in a season. Her hammerstone would go back to being a stone, and within three roofs there would be one house with the chisel and two hundred without, and someone would have a reason to have no lip. It sets that down.

It could thin the cloud for an afternoon and give the sun a location. Shadows would have edges, and afterward the people would wait for it to happen again. They do not wait for anything now. It sets that down.

It could tell them where the rock came from. But they have already answered the mountain, in the body, with two hundred platforms set far apart. An explanation would only add fear to something they already do correctly.

It does look into the mountain, and this it does not set down lightly. If the whole shelf lay in the path of what is coming, it would act, and it would not be delicate. There is no wisdom in letting nine hundred people burn for the sake of one's own restraint. What it finds is rock moving the way rock moves. In some forty roofs a flow will come down the eastern side, slowly, and take nine platforms, two cisterns, and a fern pit. The people will see the glow in the cloud for days beforehand. They will walk out along the causeways with their children on their backs and their coals in stone cups, and nothing will be lost that the rest cannot regrow. They planted themselves for this. It leaves the mountain alone.

Then it finds the one place where they hold above the line without knowing it. Everything they own is spread across the shelf in copies, but every root plant in every pond is the same plant. Distance is a defence against fire. It is no defence against the thing that eats one plant and can therefore eat them all. Nothing like that lives on the shelf, because nothing crosses, and nothing crosses because the island is young. It will not stay young. Far out past the rim, the power can see birds working their way down a chain of islands, a few hundred thatchings off, with seeds in their guts and mud on their feet, and other things in the mud. When they arrive, the ponds will be one cistern with no spillway.

So it does one thing. In a pond-field near the windward edge, it lets three plants in one row do what the plant has always been able to do and has not done here in anyone's memory. It makes nothing. It permits. One morning a pale yellow hood stands among the leaf stalks, narrow and upright, with a faint sweet smell.

The woman of nineteen roofs finds it on her way home. She stops on the slab and looks for a long time. Then she walks to the edge of the nearest platform, raises one arm, and holds it.

They come one at a time along the causeways, turning about each other gravely and well. By the dimming there are forty people at the pond wall, silent, upright, legible, looking at the thing the way a house looks at a newborn. The power does not know what they will do. It left that to them on purpose, and now it has to bear it. They might pull the thing up as rot. They do not. On the second morning the one who walks the gourd row comes with a tuft of copper fern-hair, because this is what is done for flowers.

The seed, when it comes, is finer than grit. Someone presses it into mud at the pond's edge, perhaps only to see. What comes up is not a copy. One has dark stalks. One sulks in standing water. One has a leaf that will not hold its bead. They call them the word for fresh clinker. They give them a walled corner of their own, set apart, and watch to see what has arrived. Then the long work begins: cuttings from the ones that stand well, handed on from house to house. In thirty roofs there are four plants on the shelf instead of one, and later nine. Nobody says why this is good, any more than anyone says why the houses stand so far apart. When her branch of the channel finally reaches its place, in other hands, the field built there is planted with the dark-stalked kind.

There is one more thing in the basket, and it is the one the power most wants to give. It could turn a single storm-blown seabird a few degrees and let it cry once over the shelf within her hearing, so that the word in the song would attach to something before she dies. It weighs this longer than it weighed the mountain. Then it recognises the wanting as its own. It is the power that cannot bear the empty place in the song. The people bear it without effort, and sing the word exactly. A people who will peck a groove toward a field that is not there know how to keep a place for what has not come. The birds will arrive in their own time. It does not send one. It resolves also that it will not turn one away. It sets the wanting down, which is harder than setting down the chisel.

For the woman herself it does nothing. It looks, carefully, and she lacks nothing that it has. On its last evening it is there when she lays her hand flat on the cistern, and it attends to what she attends to: the water touching the palm at the line and no higher, and the sound of the rest going on.

Then it leaves, by the causeway, in the dimming, when no one needs to look. It came carrying nothing they needed, and they carry everything. So it does what is done here when two meet and one has no load. It takes the outer edge, turns about the grip, and goes on.
