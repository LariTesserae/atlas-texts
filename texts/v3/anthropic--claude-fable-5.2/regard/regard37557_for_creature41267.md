Nothing marks the arrival. A seal coming down sends the sand eels bursting out of the ridge, and this sends out nothing. At one slack the load falls straight down through the black water as usual, coating the stumps and the flints and the wolffish's brow, and what has come is simply there, the way the cold is there.

It could be anything it liked. It becomes a cobble on the flank, flint, egg-shaped, wedged shoulder to shoulder, within the stretch of pavement the animal works over in a year. It has learned to distrust what it knows from above. And it stays a year, because a year is how long this life takes to end where it began, and nothing shorter shows it whole.

So it is rattled in the runs with the others. It takes the sand on whichever face turns up. It lies under the grey film at slack and feels the fringe go by, touching stone after stone. On a slack in spring the fringe touches it and finds it round. The fringe passes on, and the visitor lets it.

By the end of the year it has put down most of what it brought. It had thought of the sand. But the eddy that feeds the animal is made by the current, and the shell is made by the wearing. The tilt, the two fronts and the stillness are all answers to the sand, and without it there would be a soft thing with no reason for its shape.

It had thought of light, and found nothing here that had been waiting for any. It had thought of the wolffish, and then spent some weeks attending to the wolffish, who also lives here, who breaks its teeth on hard things and grows them again each year, and whose arrangement with the shell on the flank neither of them needs improved.

It had thought of the other one, across the sand waves. It could have carried either to the other in the time a ripple takes to turn. But the animal has had its whole life to start in that direction and has never started. The visitor does not entirely understand this. It understands that it is not a thing to be mended.

The knock is harder. Four times that year the shell lifts and drops. Once an answer comes, small and late, from far down the flank. Three times nothing comes, and the animal waits in the still water until the grain rolls over.

The visitor is a stone. It could lift and drop. It would cost nothing, and nothing in the valley would ever know. But the wait is worth something only because what may come back is another of the same kind, alive, somewhere out in the dark. A knock from the visitor would put into the valley's one silence a thing that was not true. So it lies there and keeps stiller than stones do. Something in it parts cleanly. At the next unanswered knock it lies there again.

What it does, it does forty meters up and further off, where the people went. They went uphill when the water rose, and they are there still, and they have come back out over the valley in hulls. They drag chain along the bottom for flatfish. They lift gravel by the shipload, and a pavement of flint cobbles is good gravel. They are driving steel into the seabed to stand in the wind, a blow every second or two for hours, more knocks in a tide than the valley has heard since the ice.

The visitor has no quarrel with them. They want light and warmth, which the valley does without and they cannot. It deals with them in their own terms. A survey of the valley comes back accurate: peat, soft channel fill, boulders, and sand waves that would strip a cable bare in one winter. On the chart the words *foul ground* appear. A line that would have crossed the flank bends, as a path bends where it would pass the wolffish, and costs someone a few kilometers. Nobody above knows the reason. The reason they are given is true.

And once, it does one more thing. In a winter gale, when the ledge calves, a block that was going to drop into the channel drops a body's length upslope instead and breaks on the pavement. When the leaves have blown out of it, a length of antler is lying among the cobbles, with barbs carved down one side. Someone lost it in the river and went without it. It has not been touched since.

It lies within a year's walking of the place that does not tick, and that is all the visitor arranges. It does not turn the path. The pavement may take the antler under first, on no schedule at all. Or at some slack the fringe will close on a thing that narrows to nothing seven times along one side.

Then the visitor stays. It had not meant to. The year becomes more than a year, through runs and slacks. The reason may be simple. Nearly everything it has attended to has been worn to one shape by asking, and turns toward it at once. This has not turned and has not asked, and its knocks are addressed elsewhere. But while the visitor sits there, the valley is the one stretch of that sea that what goes on above cannot reach, and the slack is still quiet enough for a single knock to cross.

Then a grain on the crest of a ripple rolls over. The sand rises. Along the flank the cobbles begin to tick, the visitor among them, everywhere but in one place the size of a loaf.
