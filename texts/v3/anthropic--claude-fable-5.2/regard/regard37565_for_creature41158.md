It comes the way water does, as cloud.

The deck lifts on an ordinary morning and washes over the summit, and this time there is attention in it. No one can tell. Iron sweats, walls darken, and the streets run. It condenses on everything, and that is how it reads the city, all three kilometres at once, from the pale rings to the black.

It reaches the crown while the wire is still stopped. The sawyers are standing back from the cut, so it stands back as well. The pale one comes over the lip of the hollow, setting each foot flat. It is hot enough that the cloud lifts off it, and it walks in a small clear space of its own making. The power could close that space and know everything about it. It doesn't. It doesn't count the legs.

The pale one stands in a simmering puddle for two strokes of the engines. Then it finds a channel and follows it down. The wire starts again.

After that the power goes round, as a leveller does, to see what wants a quarter turn.

It can do a great deal more than that. It could thicken the air until a newcomer took one breath to a step. It considers the sentences cut to fit a breath, and the arguments carried on by lamp over sleeping hammocks, and it leaves the air thin. It could round every edge on the summit in a night. It considers a hammock hook polished by more hands than anyone has names for, and what that hook would be worth in a city where everything was smooth, and it leaves the edges.

It could finish the mountain, which is what a power is usually expected to do with something unfinished. The spine would stop. The saws would stop, and the creep, and nothing more would go over the rim. The walls would stay warm for two hundred years and longer. After that there would be ice at the summit, where ice should be. The power has the word for this and the city does not. It looks at what the city has in place of the word: the screw-jacks, the studs travelling out along the radial streets, a door carried inward to pale stone. It keeps the word to itself.

In a niche in the black walls there is an egg with no bird on it. The stone is doing the brooding. The power adds nothing.

Twice it leans in.

Late in the day the crack comes from deep in the spine. Hands stop, and the power goes down. This is the one place where it would act, and it goes ready to. It is not so taken with the city's balance that it would leave the city under a danger. It follows the sound down the throat of the mountain, past the grooved walls, to where the column had stuck fast and come free, a day's rise taken in one go. Below that it finds heat, enough for longer than the tally office has numbers for, and nothing gathering in it. It is back at the surface by seven. It counts eight and nine with everyone else. Nothing happens, and the hands start again.

The other is a ringed block, built in low beside a channel in a brown street. Someone is asleep a hand's breadth from its face. The block is closed and warm. This troubles the power more than the crack did. Something is shut in there, and may be for three hundred years, and may go over the rim without once having come out. To open the block would be the work of a thought. But the holes that do appear in ringed blocks are smooth at the lip and made from the inside. Whatever is in there can leave, and it has not left. So the power does what the city does. It stays a while, a hand's breadth off, and hangs no hook.

Then there is the keepers' question. The power could settle it. It would only have to follow one court over the rim and down through the talus to the root of the mountain, and see what becomes of old city there. That would be marking one, in the only way a thing like the power can mark. It goes to the oldest cistern instead and counts the dark shapes along the outward wall by their seams. It gets the keeper's number. It looks no further.

Before it goes it does one thing on its own account. At a door in the innermost ring, a door a good deal older than its wall, a halved hollow is set as a basin. The cloud has filled it. The power rests there for a moment, bare, as anyone does coming home. It is the only stone here that can be touched, so it is the only stone the power touches.

Toward evening the deck drops away. At the rim a tenement that has leaned for some years goes with it, as they do on cloud days, and for once it is seen. The power doesn't slow it. It goes down alongside, as far as the talus and no farther, and watches it come to rest among the window openings and the iron stairs. The rooms are still warm.

The cisterns are a little fuller than they were, as after any cloud day. That is all it leaves. The city dries from the centre outward over the course of the next day.

If it were asked afterward what it had done there, it would answer in a sentence cut to fit a breath, the way a leveller answers who has been round her street.

The mountain is kept.
