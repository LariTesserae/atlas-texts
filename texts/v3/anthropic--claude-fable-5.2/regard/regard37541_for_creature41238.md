It comes up on an afternoon when the true cloud rises, in through the open steel of the ninth bay, which is the one door in the tower nobody has to open. For an hour it can't be told from weather. Then the cloud drops back below the deck and it is still there.

It keeps the first rule, as the creature does. It stands in the eighth bay among the glass crates and does not move a tray. Drips land on it, and by the third rain the water has learned it and goes round.

The hall's own advice is to stand an hour. It stands five rains and a night. Then it reads, because the creature it has come about was drawn before it was alive. It reads the sheet with *condensing surface, vegetable* on it, and the figure, and the tolerance. It reads the attendant's book, two years of four words. It reads the chalk, which takes longest and tells it most.

It is a power, so giving is the first thing that occurs to it.

It could bring a stone. The creature has never tasted one, and there is a whole mountain of them four hundred feet down. But a stone would come up the hoist in a crate, and the creature knows about crates. Most of what it has came in them. What it has of the hillside is one grey-green crust on the outermost rib, and it has that because it went under the rope to where the cloud could reach it. The mountain is on its way up at the rate the creature can take it. The power puts no stone on the hoist.

It could have the fig entered on the drawings, in the proper ink, with a tolerance either side. Then nobody could cut it. The fig would also become one more thing that had been handed over, and it is the only thing the creature ever took. The power leaves the sheet as it is. It does look to see whether any instruction is on its way up from the drawing office, and none is. Nobody has had the heart, and the power judges the heart will hold. It has read the chalk. Men who sign a column, and name a moss for one of their gang who went down, will need telling twice before they put a saw to that fig.

It could tell the creature which lines are itself. But the only writing the creature takes in is chalk, and it reads chalk by growing over it. A ring drawn round the header to say *you stop here* would be green in a month and soft to the thumb.

So it gives nothing. It goes looking instead for what is actually wrong, and that is under the cloud.

The steadiness in the soles of the feet is engines, and engines stop. These have not stopped once in two years, which is why the creature takes them for ground. The power goes over them as it went over the chalk. The gland on the feed pump is going. The standby pump is in the yard, still in its crate. Left alone, the gland will last until the first week of February and fail at night, in a wind, and the hall will get nine hours of February with nothing alive in it that has ever been cold.

The power could mend the pump. Then it would have to mend the next thing, and stay on mending, and the creature would have a root that could not fail and a power for an attendant. The creature can't be spared a winter, but it can be given a small one first.

So the power chooses the night. It lets the gland go in the last week of August, on a still evening, an hour after the hoist has taken the men down.

That is all it does. The rest it only stays for.

The header stops ticking and begins to cool. The relief valve sighs, and eleven minutes later does not. The dynamo runs off the same engines, so the caged lamps go amber, then out, and the hall has its first dark. At the eighth bay the fog seam has no warm air to stand against. It thins and lies down, and the mountain air walks in as far as the begonias. The trembling goes off the standing water.

For a while there is only a list of what has stopped. What hasn't stopped takes longer to notice. The leaves go on dripping for an hour, more slowly. Each bromeliad has its cupful. The felt is wet through to the wire. The six glazed bays, which the leaf outran, keep their warmth in until nearly dawn, and the concrete, which has done nothing in its life but cure, gives back a day's heat through most of the night. The moths are out under the ridge. The snails are on the brown frond.

Then the frog calls. There is nothing sounding in the pipework for the note to be mistaken for. It calls again, and from a gusset in the fourth bay a second frog answers.

Nobody has told the creature which lines are itself. The lines that weren't have gone quiet, and it is everything still going on.

It is also the first night it hasn't been counted. The glass says *clear* and keeps saying it. For nine hours the creature is not a clock. Over the glazed bays there are stars through glass. Over the open ones there are stars.

The power stays on the deck the whole night. It does one more thing, and that one it couldn't defend if asked. Around what would have been the fourth rain it walks the length of the hall, ninth bay to first and back, slowly. The fronds lift behind it and settle.

The night isn't free. By morning the cress at the open end has gone glassy, and the filmy ferns on the two outermost columns are black at the edges. The begonias in the seventh bay have dropped leaves. The snails know what those are for now. The crust on the outermost rib has noticed nothing. It is the only part of the creature that has done this before.

Below, the engine men get the standby pump out of its straw and coupled up by six. Steam is in the header by seven, knocking. The creature quickens bay after bay, like something running upstairs. The glass is cold, so the first rain comes early and heavy and lasts most of an hour.

The attendant comes up on the first hoist with his face set. He walks all nine bays before he opens the book. He has four words and a line for each hour, and he fills in the night from the look of things: *clear*, nine times. Then in the margin, where nothing is ruled, he writes *D.K. all right*. It is the first time the name has been put in ink.

The riveters say D.K.'s had a night off.

The creature doesn't know it was visited. What it knows, if that is the word, is that there was a night. Some other month something else will fail, and nobody will have chosen the hour. But there will be things in the hall by then that have been cold once and dry once, and two frogs that have heard each other.

The power goes out the way the moss goes, under the rope, the next afternoon the cloud comes in. It leaves the chalk 10 white on the bare plate, and the eleven for somebody else.

By the fitters' reckoning the creature is still two years old. By its own it is three and a half thousand rains and one night.
