It comes in the quiet days.

There is a week or so in early winter, after the river has shrunk to an icicle and before the ice begins to work, when nothing in the basin is growing toward anything. A power is a loud thing even when it means well, and in this lake whatever is loud gets leaned toward. Had it come in the thaw, every reed in earshot would carry one joint off the common axis for the rest of its life. It picks the one week when its arrival will be written into no stem. I take that as the first evidence of what kind of power it is.

It goes to the terrace, for the reason the growers did. The shadow of the ridge is the only place in the valley where a listener is not also being shouted at. It settles among the seven hearths and touches nothing. It stays through one full turn of the long clock: the ice, the breakup, the evenings when the knocking quickens, the one note that comes faintly over the ridgetop.

It uses the year to settle what it can.

The first question is whether anything here is suffering, and it looks hard, because the answer would outrank everything else. It does not find anyone inside a tuft of threads. It finds the leaning. It decides the leaning does not have to be pain to be taken seriously, and that is as far as it gets.

It notices that the ice strikes all winter, as hard as any slab off the headwall, and no bead wakes for it. Its guess is that the ice comes from every side at once, and a shoot cannot lean toward everywhere. The bead needs to be struck from somewhere. It follows that a knock in this valley is a promise. Whatever wakes will wait for the next one.

That rules out the thing it most wants to do. After a rockfall, when the pale line comes up along the path of the boom, it could give the second blow. Nothing would be easier. Then the shoots would wait for a third, and it does not intend to be here. It will not strike what it cannot go on striking.

Other things it sets aside more quickly. It could stock the lake, but the lake is not missing fish, and the first snail would start on the cords. It could make the cords so they never part, but a frame is somebody's decision about where the food should be, and a decision that cannot fail has turned into a law. The valley already has one of those, and it comes off the headwall. It could, perhaps, fetch the growers back. But it would be fetching them for the sake of their crop, which is the wrong way round.

Then it sees something that lightens the matter. The rockfall is how the growers learned. Every decade or two the headwall drops a slab, and the valley lays out, in one row of pale shoots, the same demonstration it gave them. The offer stands. It will stand after the last cord parts, for anyone who watches a summer through. The frames are not the only copy, so the power does not have to save them. Whatever it does for them it will have to do for their own sake.

---

In its second thaw, toward evening, it takes a body that the walls come chest-high on. Whether the body breathes water or air I leave where the terrace leaves it. It walks out of the shadow to the nearest frame garden, pushes a hand into the felt under the dishes, and comes up holding a fistful.

Everything in the arrangement is still being carried out except this. The gardens have been struck several times an hour for centuries, and they have answered every year, and the answers lie a metre deep. Nobody has taken one. The power could tend this household for a thousand years and it would still be a crop without mouths. So before anything else it does the part that was missing. It carries the beads back to the terrace, lifts the silt off the saddle quern, and grinds them. The quern may have been for something else. It uses it anyway. A quern left idle is only waiting. The axe is someone stopped partway through, and it leaves the axe as it lies.

Then it eats. It has to make itself able to be hungry first, and I think that is the larger part of the work. I can't say what the beads taste like. I doubt the power learns much either, from one meal with no winter behind it: whether this was food worth hauling pine for, or only what there was.

After that it owes. Whoever eats from a household's garden works in it, and the power pays at the going rate, which is one person's share. It goes down over the lip to the treeline and carries pine up, a long climb for each pole, on the feet it has taken. It learns the plait from the old lashings, makes cord from reed fibre, and renews the cords on the five frames nearest to parting. It re-lays one slumped crescent, setting each stone down without a knock. It gets the curve wrong, which the plot in front tells it the following summer by staying sparse. It stays another year and lays the wall again.

And on one of the old heaps of basket-stones, where a frame stood until its lashings went, it stands a frame up. It does not pick the spot. The spot was picked long ago. It hangs a slab of clinkstone from the headwall, and a cobble on a shorter cord. At the first note the beads in the old felt wake and come up pale, leaning toward the frame. The current shifts, and the cobble swings again.

It uses nothing that will outlast what the growers used. The fibre will part in its time. That is what a turn is. Everyone who ever fed these gardens bought them a while, and the power buys them a while more, at the same price.

---

Then it goes, in the quiet days. I think the going is the hardest of its decisions and the one it is surest of. If it stayed, the lake would have its mouths again, and all of them would be one mouth. Every stem would lean a little toward the terrace. The empty half of a household is a place being kept, and it is not being kept for a power.

It could leave the silt smooth. It doesn't. The plains keep accounts, and it will not falsify them. There is a track now from the terrace to the gardens and back, many times over, a line down to the outlet, and a quern with no silt on it. Whoever reads the valley next will find seven hearths, an axe with one face ground, and then, after a very long gap, the marks of somebody who ate here and paid for it. That will be accurate.

Perhaps thirty-one frames stand. The number goes down from there, as before. In the thaw months, toward evening, the falls swell and the cobbles swing more often, and the knocking comes unevenly down the valley, the way work sounds, with one more note in it than there was.

The short answer is still that nobody lives here. The silt now says that somebody has been fed.
