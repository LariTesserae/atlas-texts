You stop.

That is the first thing, and for a while it is the only thing. The sliding gait ends between one step and the next, and somewhere on the dark pavement the one who is listening feels it end.

You could do a great deal here. You stand still long enough to go through it honestly.

- **Water:** The clouds are just below and could be lifted. But the needles and frills grow out of gas that has never been wet, and one night of rain would rinse every vent on the plain bare. Water is the gift for a person, and the picture of a person does not hold.
- **Sky:** The lid could be parted. But the lid is what keeps the warmth lying shin-deep on the rock, and it lays down the bloom, which is the only clock they have. Under stars there would also be nowhere to stand unlit.
- **Voice:** The air could be thickened until it carried one. They would have to open their mouths to use it, and the mouth is where the water would go.
- **Still ground:** The mountain could be made to finish arriving. But the heat that heaves the plates is the heat that feeds the vents. And they build when the ground has finished changing, so they would build once and never again. No cairn would ever lean, and the message would lose the half they cannot write themselves.
- **Company:** They could be gathered. But a vent feeds one of them to the length of a reach, and they stand exactly as far apart as that requires. They already know each other, step, long pause, step, across years. You would be trading that for a crowd.
- **The line:** You can see both ends of it. It could be straightened. But each stone says only that someone sighted along what the light showed and went on from there. The dogleg is the most faithful part of it.

Every item on the list turns this plateau into some other place and them into someone else. Hardship is not good in itself. But they have built against this slope, plumb and shimmed with chips, and you cannot take the slope away and leave the cairn standing.

This is a reading and not a rule. The mountain may one day do something that cannot be built on, and that day will need a different answer. You intend to be listening when it comes. Today the ground is doing what it does and they are doing what they do. The only thing being asked of you is the question the one in the dark is asking, which is what you are.

So you answer in the language there is. You lift the foot clear, carry it forward, set it down flat, test it, and only then give it weight. You have never walked anywhere as though each piece of ground mattered separately. It takes as long as it takes. Between two seams, something plumb feels a gait change its mind.

At the end of the line you bend and turn plates. Most are wrong. You could lift them without touching them, but you use hands and let the edges be what they are.

You could look down the whole line, past the dogleg, to the first stone and whoever set it. You sight back along four, five, as far as the orange shows, and no further. You don't correct for what the fissure did.

Then you set the stone on edge. You have always had a sky to tell you which way is up. Here you have to find down the way they do, inside, and it turns out to be there. You wedge the stone with a chip. It stands.

Then you step back from it, weight even, and stop entirely.

The plates tick. A vent hisses. Far off, something large leans and breaks, and the news comes up through your feet. You hold still, as every one of them is holding still, until the small flat clap arrives. For that interval you are counted among them, though no one is counting. Then the plain goes on.

The salt begins to settle on your shoulders.

After a long time there is a knock, close. A pause, then a step. It passes within reach and does not turn, because there is no gesture here for turning. Lit from below, it shows the underside of a jaw, the inside of an arm, and a round stone in the hand that is not working. It goes to the end of the line and turns over plates, rejecting most. It sights back along as many stones as the light shows, which is four or five, and yours is the nearest. It sets the next one beyond it. Then it leaves.

Your stone is no longer the last. That is the whole of the reply, and nothing is missing from it.

When you move, the bloom cracks off and leaves your shape on the pavement. The ground will shed it the first time it flexes. You walk out the way they walk, a placement at a time, toward the band where the stars sit.

You will pass this way again in a season, as they do, to see what the mountain has added. Your stone will be leaning by then, or carried sideways, or riding the back of a dome.

You will not straighten it.
