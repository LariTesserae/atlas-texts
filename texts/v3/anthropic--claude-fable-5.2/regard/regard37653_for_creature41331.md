It comes up the stairs cut into the shelf's lip, on foot. Any other arrival would be an event, and it has not yet decided the place should have one.

For a long time it only looks. It walks the middle of the causeways at the pace and goes around the wool-bearers. When someone asks it *Is it full?* it lifts the nearest lid and checks before answering *To a finger*, and it finds it shares the mild pleasure.

It could do a great deal here. It could tip the cloud a hand's width down the ridge and the moss would cross its crack. It could lean, very slightly, on the twelfth course. It knows what the child would be called.

It notices that it wants to, and distrusts the wanting. A power that loves rivers sees drought everywhere. Nothing here hurts. It has stood in many places where everything moved and everything hurt, and it will not be the one who teaches the tooth to ache.

It distrusts the other ease as well, the one that calls a thing whole because it is quiet. So it asks what it asks everywhere, *who is paying for this?*, and goes looking for wear. It finds it where you found it, on ten fingertips and one thigh, and in the frame of cords, the only thing on the shelf that has ever grown.

It sits a while in the sun between the woman and the old man. Nothing holds them but each other. Each is keeping a door for the other, with great courtesy, and that is not a thing to hurry. It asks the mason when, and he says *soon*. It can hear a lie the way he hears a block that sits badly, and it does not hear one.

So the stillness is not a spell. It is held as a breath is held, by everyone at once, each leaning on the others' leaning. And each time someone goes round to be sure of it, she draws it tight, because she is afraid for them.

It goes to the keeper. She touches its sleeve as she passes, which no one else has done. It asks to see the cords and she shows them, lifting bundles with the backs of her hands. It asks how many there are.

She opens her mouth. It is the first question on the shelf whose answer is not already known. The power knows the number and does not say it.

It tells her what it thinks she is owed, in a voice pitched to reach her and stop there.

"The loop is real. It will slide, and no one is holding it but you.

"If you slide it, things will be lost. He will go. The birds will have something to wait on. The mason will lose the hour he likes best and get a wall. The doorstones will hollow. And there will be short names, and a child, and two people at a hearth who look at each other in a way I can't describe to someone who hasn't seen it.

"If you never slide it, no harm comes. I looked for the hurt in this place and found only yours.

"But you go round to hear them breathing, and breathing was never a stillness."

Then it does the one thing it came to do itself. She has counted herself last every time, hand to her breastbone, with nobody's eyes on her. It asks her to stand in her own doorway. She does, awkwardly, having never been on that side of it. It looks at her as she looks at them and says her whole name.

"You're here," it says. "I counted."

It leaves before she can begin to wonder. It will not stand in a doorway and be the knot that does not match. If a cord here is ever to differ, the difference should be theirs.

---

After a while the keeper goes for fiber.

She counts the cords. It takes longer than any count has. Twice she loses her place, goes back to the first peg, and begins again, and losing her place is new too. People come to their doorways at the sound of her sandals and she is not on the causeways. They stand a while and go back in.

She ties the number. It needs knots high up the cord, where no knot on the shelf has ever sat. She hangs it at the end of the frame by itself.

The next time something catches at her, she walks the causeways and lays her hand on every granary foot. She ties the cord and lays it along the last one. They match. Then she looks at the one at the end, which is wrong by one. It will be wrong by one every time, and only because of her.

She takes it down and adds to it. Her face while she does this is hard to name. It is closest to the face of someone who has gone in at night to listen for a child's breathing, and heard it, and stayed to watch the blanket rise and fall.

Then she takes up the new cord again, to the last knot, the loop still open. Her hands rest in it for about a breath, and then for another.

Across the shelf the mason is still holding the hammerstone. The boy's tongue has the tooth pushed forward. On the west side a woman shifts her laced hands, and three hearths along an old man turns his face a little toward her, the way you turn to someone who is about to speak.

One of the rock-hares opens its eyes.
