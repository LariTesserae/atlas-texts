The first answer is nothing. The white ring says so. A basin that has held its level for as long as a mineral line takes to form is not waiting to be improved, and the power can read a ring as well as anyone.

The usual gifts come to mind anyway, because it is benevolent and that is where its mind goes. A cottonwood for the roof to clear. Fox prints at the basin, swallow mud under the lip. A good rain. But each of these is the mistake about the clean sand made over again, this time by someone with the means to act on it. They are gifts for an empty room, and the room is full to the line. A fox's mouthful would have to come out of someone's exact portion.

The second answer is that nothing is not available. The power has already looked, and attention from something that size is a kind of weather. What is left to it is manners.

So it comes up the wash on foot, or as near to that as it can manage. At the bend it hears the note and understands what the animals understand. It does not go on, as they do. It stops outside the varnished lip, where the resident ends, and sits down by the low wall and waits. The people either asked or noticed. The power does both, and takes most of a season over it.

When it does step in, the warmth draws back to the far corners and leaves it the floor. The power does not mistake this for a special welcome. It is what anyone would get. It crosses the sand and sits by the slab with the hollow, inside the cool pace, where the people worked. It does not drink, and it does not pick up the cobbles, though it would like to. It keeps itself to the size of a person, which for a power is something like holding its breath.

Then it lets the drop fall. A power is asked and thanked and blamed by everything it attends to, and it has not had a moment free of owing since it began. Here the rings spread to the white line and the surface goes flat, and for a second nothing in the alcove is owed anything, the guest included. It stays for a great many drops. That second is the only thing it takes, and it is the kind of thing that is all still there afterward.

The third answer takes longer, and it starts with the moss.

The power has had it in the corner of its eye the whole time. A mat kept in good order where the overflow would go. It isn't a request. The resident has never asked for anything, and to answer what was not asked would be to tell a contented thing that it had been lacking. On that reasoning the power nearly gets up and goes.

But it knows something about keeping a place ready, since that is most of what it does. And it knows things the resident does not. The cobbles were rounded by the very thing the moss is kept for. So was the alcove. It sits on the outside of the bend, where water once pushed hardest, and the resident lives in a hollow made by an amount it has never seen. And the power knows the resident. It does not grieve. It keeps. If a surplus came and went, it would not be left wanting. It would seal the mark under glaze and go on exactly as before.

So more water would do no harm. The harm would be in the manner of it: sudden, addressed, with a giver standing at the mouth to see how it was received. That would be the first debt in the room.

The power gets up. Behind it the sand is smoothed. It goes out past the two shrubs and the last of the benches, following the seam back through the rock toward the high ground where this water first fell. It is a long way. The drop that struck the basin as it left came down as snow before the wall was built.

There, where the alcove has no dealings, it lets one winter lie a little heavier than it would have. Snow, on ground where snow falls. It is not addressed to anyone.

The melt goes into the stone and starts walking, a hand's breadth in a year. The rock will thin it and spread it, as rock does. Of a whole winter, what reaches the seam may be a few hundred drops more than the air can take. That will be in an age when the wall's top course has gone fully dark. For a season the drip will come a little quicker. The basin will rise above its white line by the thickness of a fern leaf, find the low place in its rim, and wet the moss from the side it has always been ready on. Then the seam will settle back to four seconds and the level to its ring, with perhaps a second, fainter line just above the first. The resident will keep that.

The power will not be there. It has arranged not to be, so that nothing in the alcove need look toward it. The surplus will arrive as the next drop and the one after, the long way round, at the resident's own pace, and there will be no one to thank.

Until then nothing changes. The moss is kept, without impatience, for an amount that has never come. The one difference is something the resident cannot feel and does not need: the amount now exists. It is in the rock, on its way.

The drop falls. The rings spread to the white line. The surface goes flat, and nothing in the alcove is owed anything. That is still so. What is coming is not owed. It is only coming.
