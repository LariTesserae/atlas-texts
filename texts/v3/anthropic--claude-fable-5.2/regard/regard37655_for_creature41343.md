It comes up by the stairs. It could arrive any other way, but the stairs are how you come up, and a thing that enters over the hedge is a macaque.

Halfway up, the cloud closes. It has given itself feet for the occasion, broad ones, and now cannot see them. Above, the gong starts. It walks toward the sound, stepping around the frog in each tread. Nothing has guided it anywhere in a very long time, and it notes that it does not mind. Nobody greets it in the tunnel, and it notes that too.

It asks first who lives here, and is told, by count, frogs. So for some nights it attends to frogs. It walks the lanes after dark, and along every lane the frogs keep on. Nobody opens their eyes.

Then it does what powers do and makes a list of gifts.

Dryness comes first. It follows that gift down and finds the moss coming off the limbs, the hedge combing nothing, and the troughs empty to their worn lips. The only truly dry thing these people know is ash.

A chimney would send the smoke straight out, and the rafters, the cloth and the roof would start to go the way of a basket left on the ground.

Steel would hold its edge for a year. But a knife that needs no sharpening is a knife nobody looks at each morning, and it has seen what happens here to things nobody looks at for ten days.

It could cure the feet with a thought. Then there would be no ash and no evening hour in which parents hold their children's feet and children hold the feet of the old.

It could end the macaques. The grandmothers would put down their slings, and it judges they would not last long without them.

Every gift, followed far enough, comes out at a lashing somebody would no longer need to tie. It crosses out the list. On the upper street its trailing hand finds a loose binding on the rail, and it reties it with the half-turn of the wrist it has watched the fowlers use. This is the first thing it does.

It looks longer at the sorrows. There is the week after the pig. There is the inner foot of the hedge. There are the nights, and there must be such nights, when the gong goes on until morning and is hung up with no feet on the ramp. It could make the stairs a place where nobody is ever lost. But a town where nobody can be lost keeps no gong, and soon it would not know who is down, with how many baskets, or when they are due. It decides against being the reason nobody falls. It does not decide against taking a turn.

So on a night when a woman is out with four baskets and the cloud has sat down early, it waits by the gate. The beater passes from a fowler to a girl to somebody's uncle. In the dead hour it is with a boy of nine whose arm is going. The power takes it from his hand. He does not look up, because nobody does, and he goes in to bed. It keeps the stroke. It allows itself one thing: the sound carries a little farther than wood should, around the bend where the treads are broken on the gorge side. Before dawn an old man takes the beater from its hand, also without looking, and it finds it is reluctant to let go. The feet sound on the root ramp during his turn. Someone counts the baskets.

After that it thinks it understands the place and nearly leaves. But it can see farther than cord lasts, and so it finds the one loose lashing that no trailing hand will ever find.

The stairs, the footings, the ring of the hedge and the two spouts were settled long ago. Under them is one thing nobody settled, which is the height where cloud forms. The town sits at that height. The height is set by the air in the two valleys and the forest on the gorge walls, and it can rise. If it rises a hundred paces, the cloud passes overhead, the hedge strips nothing from it, and four hundred people who have never left a gutter blocked stand looking up at their water going by. The gap is too wide for cord, and it cannot be weeded like a roof or bailed like a boat.

This it will do. It could fix the cloud to the saddle once and for all, the way a tread is fixed. But it has heard how they say "stone," and it has been called that before, elsewhere, with better reason. So it takes the job the way jobs are taken here. A dozen times a day it leads the cloud down into the gap by hand, the way a hanging root is led down through split bamboo. It is on the schedule now. It crews.

It has no root in the hedge and does not ask for one.

Some days later an old woman is going along the upper street with a load on her back, in mid-conversation, one hand on the rail. She stops at a lashing she does not recognise. She pulls it, then leans on it. "Smoked," she says, at a near-shout, to nobody, and goes on.

The square is in full sun, and every leaf glares. Then the front edge of a cloud slides through the hedge like smoke under a door, and the far houses go. It is the same cloud it always was. It is also, now, a hand on a sleeve.

Each time, without knowing what they answer, whoever is nearest picks up the beater.
