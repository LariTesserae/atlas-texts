The heap is what gives them away. It is clear ice on dry scree, under a face with no stream on it, in a place where ice ought to be white. What notices it looks up forty metres and finds the spout.

It doesn't lean in. It has seen what a breath does. It keeps to about the chough's distance, where its attention arrives as nothing, and it stays much longer than the chough. It learns the town in the order the town does things. The film comes down in late morning. The glint travels the north wall. The spider crosses on still days. At dusk it listens to the ticking from top to bottom, and afterward to the other sounds in the scar, the ones that have nothing to do with the presses.

It could do a great deal here. It goes through what it could do slowly, the way a wedge is set.

It could warm the lines. There would be heat for the whole town at once and no mote would have to stop. But the stopping is when they listen. A town with everyone running would have nobody at the junctions. And the warmth they have comes down from the upper terraces. It knows who sends it, and it is not going to make them unnecessary.

It could cut the fourth course in a night, and forty more below it. Then there would be ledges the town did not make, in rock the town does not trust. They would prove every span anyway, morning by morning. They don't mind the rate. It finds that it minds a little on their behalf, and that this is not a reason.

It could open the cells. It doesn't consider this for long.

It could keep the spider off. The spider is where the spans come from.

It could put a tremor on a line. By now it can follow one down the crack and has a fair idea what the common ones say. Anything it sent would be passed from junction to junction, with someone at each deciding whether to send it on, and it would not be about a gutter. It would be the note at the mouth again, the one they went to such trouble to stop. They have arranged to treat large things as weather. This seems to it a sound arrangement, and one it can work within.

It counts the plates in the upper block, which nobody has done. It does the count twice. It could not put them back on the lines without taking them off the stalks. What it can do for the first winter is know the number, and it does.

That leaves the rock.

Behind the seam, a body-length deeper than the oldest stitches, a fracture runs up through the granite toward the ledge where the snow patch sits. The town has sutured everything it can reach, and this cannot be reached. It takes a little water in every thaw. In the third winter, or the fifth, it will do what the first one did, and the crack will go down the face inside the slab it is cut in.

It handles this their way, having seen no better. It doesn't hold the rock or close the fracture. Thirty metres north there is a block that has been ready since the fall, behind a crack that sounds a low note in a crosswind. Through the autumn it shifts the cold on that part of the face. One seep is kept in shade an hour longer. A few nights run a degree colder. Then it stops, feels the face, and moves it back. On a night in October the frost pushes there and nowhere else. The block goes. The scar settles toward the gap and the load comes off the fracture behind the town.

The town has it through the rock before the sound arrives. They are already out at the middle of their lines, thirty beads, as far from both walls as they can get. The lines shake for some time. In the morning the walls are where they were. The melt comes and the presses give back their strokes. For a few days the tremors run longer than usual, and more are passed on than stopped. Then they are about cisterns again.

The note from the north crack is gone. Nothing else is different.

It does one other thing that autumn, a smaller one, and is less sure of it. On the first warm still days of September, gossamer rises off the valley meadows, spiderlings going out on their threads. Most years the drift passes south of the face. This year a little of it comes up the scar. One evening a strand lies across the mouth of the crack, heavier than a dragline, with nothing on it. It is fallout. They can take it apart or let the wind have it.

By morning it is gone. Ten days later two lines in an empty district clear of frost with nobody on them.

It doesn't do this again. They know how to wait for the spider, and it would rather they kept that.

It has more to give than this. It knows a good deal about heat, and granite, and how to last a winter. It keeps all of it the way the town keeps what is in the cells, complete and clear and given nowhere to begin, until there is a place for it. There may be one some year. That will be for the town to cut.

It doesn't leave, exactly. It has other faces to look at and goes round them in turn, a short run here and a long pause elsewhere. When its turn comes back it stands off at the chough's distance and counts, and the count comes out low, as any count does. There is one more plate at the end of the lower block. There are two more pale ones on the bottom terraces. One of them has run past the end of its stretch and is being turned. The ones on either side wait.

By the time the snow closes the mouth, the fourth course is as deep as a fingernail is thick. They have cut every stroke of it.
