**What Arrives**

It comes in over the clinker from the windward side, at the pace of her sign. For most of a week it does nothing but learn the place. The starlings fool it for a whole morning. By the third day it is swallowing with the canteen queue. It walks the mains behind her and learns to look at the wagtails first.

What drew it was the stakes. Someone was taking a town plan apart one lot at a time and spending the pieces on finding out where the moss actually stops.

It can see the core of the flow as plainly as she sees a gauge. It knows what the second stake will say.

**What It Leaves Alone**

It is not against doing things. Nobody is sure how the frogs crossed a mile of dry clinker, and it is not saying. But it goes down the list honestly, the way she goes down a column.

- **The heat.** It could put back a degree a year, and no instrument would show the join. But everything here is read off the warmth. From that day the wagtails would sit where it had put them. Her book would be true for nineteen years and then false from a date she could never find. People here tell the real thing by the pressure in the ears, and a mercy done in secret has the right shape and no pressure. It would be a starling doing Six.
- **The noise.** It could give them quiet. But the gap after Six is made of the noise on either side of it, and there is a man with a pot of red lead for whom the drone is the only silence there is.
- **The answer.** It could tell her tonight. She would write it in the book with a question mark and walk out in March with a stake anyway. All it would have given her is a second thing to keep.

It does not pretend this costs nothing. The flow is cooling. One day the painter will need a drone and there will not be one. It has him in its own book with a question mark, and it has not finished thinking about him.

**What It Finds**

It had meant, at least, to see that she was not alone with it. It finds it is late.

At the border, beside the newest stake, the last live moss is pressed flat in the shape of a child. The children are not allowed on the clinker, and this is the first stake that has ever stood outside their edge. So there is a game: lie on the moss, keep your feet in the green, and touch the iron. Nobody can. The longest arm among them is short by a hand. They have been measuring the same gap since August, in a smaller unit, and getting the same result.

They have a sign for it, an arm put straight out and the fingers closing on nothing. Unlike most of what children invent, it is done from the shoulder, so it will work in mittens and last the winter. It began as *can't reach*. It is coming to mean *not known yet*. The place has had an opened fist for what is real and one finger for what is not, and nothing between them except a question mark in a clerk's book. Now there is a third sign. The cook has already used it once, about a loaf.

The power had no part in this. Marit has not seen the flattened moss, because she comes in the evening and looks at the stake. But she is the one who saw a lid with no birds on it, and she will see it. When she finally says her sentence in a gap, the person at arm's length will already have the sign to answer with.

**What It Does**

What is left is small, and it is outside every column she keeps.

In the evening it goes out to the stake a little before she does and stands at arm's length, the way the stayers stand, not signing. It adds no warmth and moves no moss. It is only there, and it is real. It will be there in March, early, whichever way the second stake reads.

She crouches and holds her arm against the distance. Her sleeves dry. Some evenings, between one Six and the next, with nothing blown, she swallows and does not know she has done it.
