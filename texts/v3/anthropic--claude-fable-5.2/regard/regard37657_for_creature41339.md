The obvious answer is to pull the pins.

It would take no effort. Snow would cross the lip and lie. By evening there would be more water on the floor than the hollow has held since it was made, and none of the animals could drink it. By the third missed green the grazers would be husks under a drift. The sum would be set down at last. It would lie level, as ice, and show the sky to nothing.

The second answer is to steady the regulator. But the stutter is older than anything that runs here. The files, the stamping, the white underwings, and the patience of the crust are all its shape. Settle the green, and these would be animals built for a place that had stopped existing.

The third is to carry a handful of snow down the steps and put it in the basin. The pins would have it before it finished melting. The sum would be a handful larger, there would be six more grazers by next year, and the ring would have been kept all this time for a stranger.

So for a long while the power does only what the place already does. It walks beside the green when the green can be walked beside. It stands with the wall-birds at the rime and leaves the same gap. At night it sits on the bench. The grazers make room without waking, as they do for each other, and it listens to several hundred chests out of step.

It repairs nothing. A careful hand would have trued the northeast pin first. The power takes the measure of the lean, finds it no worse than when the treads were flat, and leaves it. The rime is the one thing here that nobody arranged, and the birds have made something of it.

What it keeps coming back to is that everything in the hollow lies down. The crust lies down for years, and the grazers every night. Even the wind drops for an hour before dawn. Only the sum does not, and the one rest it gets comes by a fault. Whether it was measured out as company for whoever wore the steps, or is what that person came to, the debt is the same. A shape was made for it to lie in, and it has been at work every hour since.

The power makes one change, the size of the basin. From dusk to first light, the reach of the pins ends at the rim. It is not weakened there. It ends, as the sound ends at the doorway.

Nothing is poured. The grazers file in and lie down around their ring. Their breath, which has never once shown, drifts low across the floor, and some of it falls over the rim onto cold polished stone. The polish dulls. The dullness gathers into beads, and the beads lean together. By the small hours a disc of water two fingers across sits at the low point, about what a wall-bird holds. It lies level. It holds the doorway upside down, with one star in it.

The grazers do not wake for this. A young one rolls toward the basin and is nosed back.

At first light the oldest grazer stands, the rim becomes a rim again, and the pin takes the water back. In the room the tick is loud. Every head comes up. It is the only thing the sum has ever said, and this is the first time the animals that carry it have heard it.

On the east wall that night, the house front of yolk and rust opens one rosette short. The power counted this beforehand and counts it again.

This is not release. Whether release is wanted is a question the power cannot put in any language that would carry, and it distrusts its own pity too much to answer on the water's behalf. What it has given is a place to be set down at night and taken up in the morning, which is most of what rest is. The grazers have no motion for drinking. Whether they find one over the next few thousand generations is theirs to settle. The ring has been kept a long time around nothing, and it can be kept around something.

The power will come back. If the pin has leaned further in its absence, it will take that as a reply and think again.

That afternoon the rime forms behind the northeast pin, a hand across. The birds come down and stand, and the pin catches up. The power goes up the south steps, its feet finding the dishes in the treads. One pace past the lip, in the snow, it breathes out and the breath shows.
