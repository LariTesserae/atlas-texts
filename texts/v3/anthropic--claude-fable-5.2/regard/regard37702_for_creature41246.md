It comes down the stair. It could arrive any way it likes, but the tree kept the stair, and a guest uses the door that is offered. For the purpose it takes on enough weight to be someone, with boots, a coat, and hands. Around the fortieth step it puts a hand on the rail and feels the thump.

For a long time it only looks. It stays some hundreds of whistles and stands in the rain each time. It reads the laminations in a tread, lays a finger on each journal, and kneels at the grating. Once it goes up the shaft with the swiftlets and stands in the weather at the crown, then comes back down with them, the first to have seen both ends.

It is looking for what is wrong, and mostly it does not find it. Nobody was taken, nothing here is in pain, and the accounts balance. So most of what a power could do, it sets aside.

**What it leaves alone**

- **The height.** It could bring the crown back under the limit, but that is felling by another name. It could make the water climb unaided, but then the hall would be a keepsake, and the tree is the shift it keeps. Every living thing is some number of hours from something. This one knows its number and has built a decent, exact life around it.
- **The clock.** Eleven hours and some minutes was an error once. It is now the length of a day for several million lives.
- **The bowed rod.** That is where the tree learned what it cannot do, and it has behaved well ever since.
- **The ledger.** The power could read the figures under the scar, and it does not. Whatever the men were counting down, closing it over was the first thing the tree did that was wholly its own, and that needs no correction.
- **The root.** This is the hardest. The power would like to put a hand through the grating as the water drops, so that for once the root would find something there. But the tree renders everything it meets, and the root is only itself because it has met nothing. The power keeps its hands on its knees and watches a full shift of the root reaching and being overtaken. It takes one instruction from this: to arrive before it is reached for.

**What it does**

1. **It takes a post.** The power has measured the brasses. Sap and ants keep the journals bright, but bright is not the same as unworn, and the wear per thousand whistles is thinner than one of the tree's laminations. It gives a figure all the same, a very large one with an end to it. That is the same kind of figure the men had in their last column, and the tree has no way to see this one coming.

   One post was never on the roster, because the man who held it did not live in the cottages. He came down from the makers every few years with a bag, while an engine was resting, scraped in new brasses, and went back up the stair. The power takes that post. It works only on the rested engine and only within the rest, and it makes three visits to do all three. It cracks the lacquer on the cap nuts and leaves it cracked, and the tree has it whole again within two whistles, as it would after any crack. The power carries the worn metal up the stair and hangs nothing on the rack. It will come back before the figure runs low. The tree will never reach the end of its iron, and will never know there was one.

2. **It lets a room.** It could hang new glass, but then it would be lampman forever for something the tree wants and does not need. It would also have to unscrew the wooden bulbs, which are the tree's own answer and only one lodger short of working.

   So it brings the lodger. Foxfire is a fungus that works in damp wood and gives off a cold green light as it feeds. The power sets it into the wall of one wooden bulb, the oldest, at the dark end of the line. The fungus eats the bulb and the bulb grows back at the rate it is eaten, which is the hall's arithmetic already. The power waits to see. After some dozens of whistles there is a green glow too dim to read by, and then three moths. Then one white leaf comes out on the wall behind it.

   This is the first lamp in the hall that the men never lit. The power does not seed a second. Whether the green travels up the line is the hall's business.

3. **It stands still for long enough.** There is no telling the tree anything, since it has no part that could be told. It knows a thing one way, by getting it right. So the power walks to the middle of the yard, with forty paces of bare mat on every side, and stops.

   The thump and the shriek go on, and holes of silence drift along the walls. The rain comes down on its shoulders some thousands of times. Somewhere in that the mat finds the soles and begins, grain by grain, the way it began on the brick. The power does not hurry it. When the eyelets are done it steps out and goes up the ninety-odd steps barefoot, feeling each mortar line.

The roster, as it now stands:

- **Fireman.** The beetles and the screw.
- **Oiler.** The ants.
- **Beltman.** Not needed.
- **Timekeeper.** The clock.
- **Engineman.** The tree, in person.
- **Lampman.** No one. One lodger at the far end, paying in light, on trial.
- **Fitter.** Visiting.

In the middle of the yard there is a pair of boots, both upright, laced, and wood to the last eyelet. The ants' columns bend around them. The tree does not know whose they are or which things matter, so they will get the same care as the keystone.
