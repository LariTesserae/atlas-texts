The power came on foot, which it had no need to do, and stopped under the last tree. For the first day it did what you did. It stood still and watched the stone change color where no one was walking.

It was looking for something to mend. That is the habit of powers, and the wise ones know it for a habit. It considered the quiet, and saw that the frogs were made of it. It considered the heron's neck going out and coming back, and saw that the one who lives here had already considered that, for longer and from closer, and had left it alone. It considered the bent back, and saw that it was bent toward something. Nothing on the floor was broken. Whatever it might have thought to give was already there, a hand's width under everything.

So most of what it did, it did somewhere else. The afternoon cloud builds in the west, over country the one on the rock has never seen. The stilts need a roost, the kites need their tree, and the sheet-water needs a forest to slip into. The visitor went out and saw to those things: that the forest goes on being forest for farther than a kite can see, and that the cloud keeps its hour. None of this shows from the floor. It was not meant to.

That left two things it could not settle by watching. One was the turn at the trees. The other was the voice, and whether anything had ever answered it.

For either, it would have to step into the room, and it spent the second day thinking about that. You stepped in and stepped back out, and the voice ended in the middle of a word, and no harm was done. You were passing through, like the swifts. It is another matter when the one who steps back could have stayed. If the visitor went in, it would have to let the word finish, and it would have to come back. It decided it could afford that.

On the third morning it walked out with the herons. It let the frogs open for it, since that is how things are read here, and it went along the groove, exactly along it, at the pace of someone with nowhere to be. The other lane had stopped beside a pan. The visitor came up from the front, where it could be seen coming, and at two arm's lengths the voice was there, in the middle of telling a tadpole something about its legs.

The voice paused for as long as it takes to look up. Then it said, "You're standing in the wet. That's all right. Mind your left foot. There's one there who has only just lost her tail."

The visitor minded its foot.

It stayed the whole day and was shown things. The notch in the ring is a wrist laid in the standing edge of the water and held still until the shrimp come to pick at the skin. It was shown that. It was shown which skink is always first to the post. During the rain there were two shapes beside the post, both bent. The visitor said very little, and when it did speak it used the same voice, low and not built to carry, and the other stopped to hear it. Nothing on the floor had ever made it stop before.

Something else happened that the visitor had not planned. Ordinarily it hears everything, every forest and every shore, all of it at once. It had let the quiet close over it like everything else here, and for one day it heard only what was within reach: three stilts, a wingbeat, one voice. It had not known how much it wanted that. After that it stopped wondering whether the quiet was a wound.

At dusk a few moths circled low above the dark stone, and a few more circled a second spot beside them.

The edge it left for the next morning, and it did not ask. A wall you have never touched is not a thing you can answer questions about. It idled south instead, pan to pan, to where the sheet-water slips into the forest, and stepped off the rock onto the leaf litter. It went one pace, well within reach. Then it waited, the way you wait for the frogs to drift back around your feet.

The lane stopped where the stone stops. For a long time there was one bare shape and not two. Then, under the first tree, where there are no frogs to show anything, a few wet leaves pressed flat.

Sound carries under the trees. With one foot off the rock, the one who lives here had all of it at once: the whole roost of stilts, cicadas, a branch coming down, and somewhere far off, water running in an actual channel. It stood in that for a while. Then the leaves were only leaves again, and the voice, back on the stone, said, "Yes. I remember that." And then, "Come and see the lilies. They'll be opening."

So it is a preference. I think it was one all along, and all that changed is that now it has been tried.

The visitor left some days later, at dusk, at the end of a sentence. It said when it would come again, and it came.

It built nothing. Whoever worked this floor used very little and left less, and the visitor left less than that. What there is to find, you find in the usual way. Some mornings the lane goes down to the southern edge, and a few leaves lie flat under the first tree for a while, and then the lane comes back to its pools. If you walk into the room now, the voice has pauses in it, each about the length of an answer. It leaves them for the skinks too, who do not use them, and it goes on unbothered.

A few times in a season, if you wait under the last tree early, two lanes go out from the dark stone. When they reach the line in the air, a ripple goes down the row of dragonflies, each one lifting and dropping back, and then a second ripple follows the first. You would get the same from two hands trailed along a rail.
