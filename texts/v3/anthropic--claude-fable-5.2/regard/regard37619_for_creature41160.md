It came down the lightwell, the way the cold air does, and for a long time it did nothing but stay.

A power of that kind can know a room in an instant. It chose to learn this one at the room's own speed. It stayed through a change of duty and watched the moss let go of one casing and begin on another. It stood a winter in the fog. It stayed until it shook in the pumps' note like everything else, and only then did it begin to think about what it might do.

It went through the kindnesses in order, starting with the obvious ones.

It could quiet the room, so that the water falling everywhere might be heard. But the plant had laid down every cell inside the note. Its thick joints were the note's work. Stillness would not reach that body as peace. It would arrive as something taken away, like a floor.

It could spare the shoots that go out to the flow mains and burn. It looked a long while at the brown margin for suffering, and was honest with itself about what it found, which was closer to a hand feeling along a wall in the dark. The burning was how the plant knew where it was. And the heat in those pipes was owed to the hospital.

It could bring the wasp. This one it turned over longest, because the figs by the lightwell are hard to look at, hanging a season, ready, for something on another continent. So it ran the gift forward. The wasp lives two days. Her daughters, leaving the fig, need another fig just opening, and one plant in one room ripens all together. They would come out into the fog with nowhere to go. Mend that, and there are seeds, and the seeds want a bird. Each kindness needed another standing behind it, and at the end of the line was a different room, one with holes in its leaves and webs on the stair rail. That is a good kind of room. The world has many. It has one of this.

It did not bring the wasp. But since nothing else was ever going to, it went into a fig itself, through the eye at the end, and stayed a moment. Inside, the wall is lined with flowers, hundreds of them, all facing inward, open in the dark. It is a closed chamber, in bloom on the inside, with one narrow door. It came out and looked at the hall again for some time, and at the steel door at the top of the stair.

Then it looked ahead, along the pipes, to the week the hall goes cold. It could prevent that. It could keep a district wanting hot water for ever. But the hall lives on losses and the losses are real ones, fuel burned, a figure on a screen in an office. It would not ask a city to stay wasteful so that a room could stay green. Nor would it tidy the waste away. Every improvement it could think of, a new jacket on a flow main, a tightened flange, the lamps on a timer, was a small death for something. It hastened none and hindered none. Nobody had meant the hall. It did not think the hall could be kept by meaning it.

That left the plant itself, and what it was, and who knew.

It followed the mains out through the brick arches, past where the green gives up, along the bare tunnels with the note going ahead of it, and up into the housing blocks. The note is there too, in the radiators, too faint for anyone to notice. On the sills above the radiators stand the jars. It is the same body: small, whole, heart-shaped, marbled with gold. Not one of those pieces has ever been given anything to climb, and no one who waters them has any idea what they are keeping. The giant exists in one place. The one person who sees it comes in the afternoon, after the daylight has left the east wall, with a torch, and has only ever seen it black-green and in the way.

So this is what it did. On a morning in early summer a valve at the baths that had been going to stick did not stick, and the visitor, with two hours in hand, came down the stair at ten.

They took the loppers from the hook. They cut the passage back to shoulder width. They read the gauges and turned each red wheel a quarter and back. They rubbed the nameplate on the idle pump with a thumb. And straightening up from it they saw the east wall with the sun on it, and leaves there splashed with yellow.

Everyone knows that leaf. There is one in a jar by the kettle at the depot. The visitor stood with the loppers hanging and looked at it, and then up the column to the metre-long leaves split like palm fronds, and then along a rope that came down thin from the crane rail, with small hearts on it, to the floor. It took about as long as reading the nameplate had. Their mouth moved. In that noise there is no telling what it said.

The power did nothing further. The visitor went back along the passage, picked up one of the shoots they had just cut, three leaves and a nub of root, put it inside their jacket, and climbed the stair. It was the first time in all those years that any of the plant had gone back out by the door it came in through.

Where the piece went, the power did not arrange. A jar, probably. Possibly, in time, a pot with a plank stood in it, to see. It would be talked about at the depot, certainly, and the power left the telling to travel the way the plant always has, as a piece broken off and passed from hand to hand. It might get as far as someone who once worked nights. It might not.

Before it left it did one more thing, which was of no use at all. It went up to the mezzanine and into the shift office, where the roof keeps the drips off and the last handful of soil in the cracked pot is the only dry earth in the hall. And it watered the plant.

Someone was supposed to.
