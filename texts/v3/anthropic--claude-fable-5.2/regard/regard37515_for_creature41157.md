The power here comes up a cable from the tide gate. This is about another kind, and there is less to see.

It comes across the plain and stops at the last stanchion. Nobody has told it to, either. It leaves no gap in the moss and wears nothing pale. It could green the plain to the horizon, and it is the sort of thing that looks at a woman of sixty with her shoulder to a flywheel and wants to do something about it. For now it stands one pace out from the green and watches the dark street go round the dial.

It watches through four seasons. It wants to see rain, a neap slack with a clack on top of it, and the carrier. Then it goes in on a thawing street, down the crown of the camber, and reads the log. It reads the entry about the sacking several times, as if it had been left out for it.

Rain would be the easiest. But within a year of steady rain the felt would no longer need the mains. Within five there would be a green town on a green plain, and nothing in it would lift twenty minutes early for anything.

It could have one and four running by the next flood. Then there would be cold in two streets at once, the plants thrown out of step, and thirty years of careful subtraction turned into something she need not have done.

It could take the neaps out of the tide. The lamps would stay white, the wheel would carry itself through, and she would have no reason to be on the floor with her palm on the rim. It notices that this is the one it most wants to do. It has learned to be careful of those.

It looks her over as she looks along a main, for the brown streak. There is the shoulder. There is the nose that has gone, and the taper that does the nose's work. There are the dead machines kept as bright as the live ones. It has seen larger things kept worse. Nothing is weeping. She is a keeper in sound order, and she has asked for nothing in forty years.

It does the sum she doesn't do often. Piston rings for nineteen years at the rate she spends them, bearing shells for eleven, one good crosshead pin. That will see her out, or near enough. It will not see out whoever comes after, and at present nobody is coming after. That is the streak.

It could put shells in the store, and it stands over that a long while. The first hand wrote *none in store* as a man writes down a failure, and that failure has lagged the mains for six keepers. But nothing blows in on the wind to do the job of a bearing shell. It can't see what would come to fill that gap. It knows that not seeing something doesn't mean nothing is there, and doesn't mean something is. It leaves the store as it is. Of all its decisions this is the one it is least sure of, and it doesn't call it wisdom.

What it does is smaller, and out on the plain.

The works has only ever sent one message out, and it arrives as wet sacking. So the next autumn, on the day the carrier crosses, the cloud doesn't break at noon as it was going to, and the wind stays in the north. That is all. It leans on the day the way she leans on the wheel at the top of the stroke, and it takes very little. The blocks come into the town square-edged and clear right through, cold enough to stick to a palm. People come out to look. Someone keeps a hand on the ice after the others have let go, and asks where it's from. It has taken care not to pick who.

That is a quarter turn, and it takes its fingers out of the felt. Whether anyone walks out with the cart next year is a nut it could reach and won't.

After that it ought to go. It finds it hasn't.

Somewhere in the third turn of the drum it noticed it was already facing the right street while the pipe was still dry. It has looked for what tells it, with better means than bare feet on a hall floor, and found nothing. That makes three of them. It is no nearer than she is to knowing which is told first.

On the neaps, when a clack is coming in on top of slack water, it comes in off the plain and stands in the hall where it could reach the wheel. She stands where she can reach both. Neither sits. It has never touched the rim. If the wheel hung, and she put her shoulder to it, and the shoulder were not enough, it would lean. It is clear about that, and clear that it would be sacking of a kind. It has not come to that. The lamps come up white, and it goes back out to the stanchion.

The wagtails keep twenty paces off it and don't stop feeding.

She is on the step now, boot in the gap, the can on two fingers. The main is white and the fog is knee-deep. Out past the last trestle, something else has its weight forward too.

The valve goes over. All down the street the dripping starts.
