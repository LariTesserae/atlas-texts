Something does look.

It arrives without weather, which is the only way to arrive here unnoticed, and takes up the pace: one polygon a day, a little behind her, on the side away from the hunter. No head lifts. That is how it knows it has the pace right.

Its rule in any place is to watch one full turn of the thing before touching it. Here a turn is nineteen years, and she does not have them. So it reads backward instead. The snowbanks give it the winters. The stubs give it the flanks, every one. The vole runs give it the dead, by number.

There are tens of millions of them and it is looking at one. It does not pretend this is fair. It has found that attention only comes in ones.

There is almost nothing to do:

- **The lid:** It could open the cloud for an hour and let her cast a shadow once. Every head on the plateau would come up, and the language has no second word to say it was all right.
- **The distance:** It could bring one of the six across to her. Two on a dome means the lichen is gone by midday and both stand the night on bare crust. And she has not asked. What she does about the distance is lean on the stubs, and the stubs work. Each flank reaches the next a day late. It is a touch that takes nineteen years to go around, and it has never been dropped.
- **The hunter:** It looks at the hunter for as long as it has looked at her. The hunter is thin. It has spent a year walking at a pace not its own to avoid a chase neither of them could afford, and in that year it has paid her closer attention than anything else alive. Sent off, it would fall in behind another.
- **The years:** Two more would carry her to the notch and a third reading. Her teeth are a low ridge. Two years is seven hundred domes, each finished a little later into the dark, and then some not finished. It would be giving her hunger so that a stranger could say her number.

So it goes down instead, the way the wool goes, at night, through a rim notch with the cold air.

It can tell her wool from the rest, and it finds her all over the lowlands. She is in a blanket, the third one down on a bed where two children sleep. She is in the felt of a pair of boots by a door, and in the lining of a coat on a hook, which belongs to the man with the hooked pole, who is old now too. By the count the power keeps, she has not gone untouched since her third thaw. She has had her flank against some hundreds of sleepers every night for thirty-eight years. She has not been told, and there is no way to tell her.

The furnaces it looks at longer. Then it goes back up and reads a snowbank from the bottom. The soot lines are there, one a winter, and each is a hair thicker than the one beneath. Dark snow goes early. The thaw now comes eight days sooner than in the year she was born, and the lichen is still keeping a nineteen-year clock. Everything else on the Floor is a circle. This is a line.

It could put the furnaces out. It has done that in other places, and watched them relit within the year by people who never learned why they went dark. The lowlanders have goodwill enough. Theirs is among the gentler bargains it has seen: they take what falls and do not follow it in. What they lack is a view. No one has ever cut a snowbank.

Once a day, somewhere, frost splits a rock. The power does not add one. It chooses where today's goes: the notch, on a working morning, in the ledge under an old drift above the tagging crew. The crack carries for an hour's walk in every direction. The face of the drift drops away and leaves a clean section standing over them, white ruled with black, heavier toward the top, like a page.

It does not make anyone count. But these are people who read small marks aloud for a living, and the youngest of them, the one who holds the book, is still looking up after the others have gone back to the ears.

Then it walks back to her.

On the last night she reaches the center later than she used to, with a hand's width at the middle left unscraped, and folds her legs. The hunter lies down three polygons back. The birds stand.

The power lies down on the stone border of her polygon. Nothing has kept to the border of a dome she was on since her first thaw, when she was the one on the stones. It does not touch her. It breathes, at about her pace, close enough to hear.

She has heard rime, and stone, and her number twice. She has not heard breath that was not her own since the distance was four polygons and final. The left ear turns, the one with the oval in it. Then her head goes down on the cleared patch.

It does not say the number. It did not need it to find her.

When the night air starts to pour toward the rim there are two breaths on the dome. Some time before the light comes up its one shade, there is one.

That much it takes out of the order of things, and it will not call it nothing: the last hour, the one she would have been awake for. The hunter gets up in the morning to everything it waited a year for. The birds walk up. The hour was no use to any of them.

The animal behind steps in on schedule. The voles come for the oval. At the center of the dome a hand's width of old crust has a start on the next nineteen years.

The power stands still for one more day. Every animal in sight moves one polygon sunwise. In the morning the view is the same as before, which is the standard it holds itself to in a place like this.

It leaves by the notch. The drift is still standing open. Beside the lowest lines, as high as someone not very tall could reach, there are small scratches in the snow, in pairs.
