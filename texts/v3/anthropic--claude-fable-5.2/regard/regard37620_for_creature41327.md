Something comes into the pan during a slack. That is the only time the place can take a stranger. The fans are already folded, so there is no chord to tear, and the fissures are giving up their hidden things anyway.

It has no weight unless it wants weight, and here it wants none. It has listened from outside long enough to know the two ways a large thing arrives in this country: in a furrow of quiet, or inside a sound that seems whole. It would rather be neither. It settles over the low slab a third of the way in from the lee rim, and the drummer's stroke comes back off the stone as clean as it went down.

For many slackenings it only learns the place the way the place is known. It gives up on the rust-light early and learns the pan by which notes lie on which side.

It could do nearly anything here, and it goes through the list.

It could hush the chord. But quiet has one meaning in the pan, and every walker from rim to rim would break at once from a hunter the size of the sky.

It could bring the day over the edge. The stone would stop sweating, and the pits along the drummer's flank would lose the one thing they know.

It could stop the knobs growing, so that the animal might rest. An animal that could rest would rest, and the troughs worn ankle-deep in the limestone would lead to a note that was no longer there.

It could thicken the worn knob and close up the long gap. Then the patient four on the dead stone stays nobody for a while longer, and the power has chosen between two animals because it met one of them first.

It could thin a slab somewhere for the four. The sweat is already doing that, at the sweat's pace, and there are more mute things in the fissures than the pan has hollows.

Partway through, it notices that most of these mercies are aimed at the dark and the noise, which trouble nothing here but the visitor. What is actually hard in the pan is that most are never anyone and the rest stop being someone. That turns out to be the same working part that keeps a drummer on every ringing stone. The power cannot find the piece to take out. It does not conclude from this that all is well. The mute ones go on knocking on rock that carries nowhere, and it lets that stand unanswered.

It also sees that the one thing it would have wanted to give has already been given. In the last slack the rock went clear, and the drummer heard its own time coming from the windward rim on a high thin note. The power can tell what that far animal is, which hollow it hung in and how many slacks ago it left by the fissures. There is no way to say such a thing into a belly-plate, and no need. The drummer did everything that can be done with the news, which was to keep time against it.

So the power does the one thing left that the pan has a place for. It goes down through the crack the drummer keeps clear, into the hollow.

The young hang from the ceiling there, soft, fastened by their plates. It finds a space among them and puts what it has for a belly against the underside of the stone. Then it is beaten on. Two close strokes, a gap, one stroke, a longer gap. The long gap runs longer than the young expect. They come in early, then learn better. The power learns it as it is now, with the lengthening in it.

When it has the time, it answers once from below, a shade behind, with the rest. Up on the saddle the drummer feels a small late echo inside its own stroke, as it has all season, perhaps a little fuller. It lifts a walker from the trough and drops the shell over the side.

At the next slack the largest of the brood let go of the ceiling and leave by the fissures, and the power leaves with them. On the way down it passes the patient four coming up, mute, belly to the rock, listening to the gap. It leaves that one alone as well.

The pitch stays behind, because it was only ever a place. The power does mark it exactly before it goes. The note is sinking, and nothing else in the pan will ever know it once stood here.

The time goes out with the power. Where it is going there is no limestone, and it will not hear which rhythm is on the low slab after the wind next fails. Whatever it strikes from now on, it will strike in that figure, long gap and all, on a stranger's stone.

Behind it a thin note starts at the windward edge, and the fans open.
