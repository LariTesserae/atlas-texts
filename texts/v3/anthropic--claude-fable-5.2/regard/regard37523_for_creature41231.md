Something came over the western headwall one evening, along the line of the posts, and stopped above the basin.

It was larger than the range and took up no room. The swifts went on hunting under the ceiling. The midges kept their columns. The fog did not move.

It began by looking, which took most of the night. It counted the bodies on the snow before the fox came: some three hundred moths, forty beetles, two swiftlets. It listened to the frogs with something like closed eyes and got the shape of the dish from them, a hundred and seventy meters at the middle, curving down to nothing at the moss. It read the laminations in the cracked post and learned what ran along the line and roughly how. It did not learn who had wanted it, or whether they still did.

Then it considered what it could do. It could do most things.

It could mend the leaning post. The crack would close and the dust would stop. But the loaches spawned in the dust, in the only clear water they had, and the pikas were a decade from a third circle and a sky. It could wake the two dead posts, and then the pikas would have about a minute.

It could stop the line from taking its share. The fox would walk its path for a few more mornings and then not. The choughs would go. In a thousand years the swifts would be worse at turning, which would not matter until the day the mercy lapsed.

It could hold the basin at its present size. It thought about this longest. It had seen the silver trunks in the snow and found that they grieved it. It also found that the grief was its own, and that nothing in the basin shared it. The ferns on the north side had not been robbed. They had been lent eighty meters, for some thousands of years, by something that did not know they were there. The whole basin was a loan of that kind. Going by the two dead posts, the seven live ones had twenty or thirty thousand years among them, which was longer than the place had existed.

The power could make the loan permanent. Then it would be the keeper, and everything born afterward would descend from its decision as surely as it now descended from the ones that turned back. It did not think that was wrong in itself. It thought it was not a thing to settle after one night. Nothing required an answer before the leaning post fell. It would come back then, and at each post after, and ask again knowing more. It allowed that at the fourth or the sixth the answer might be yes.

That left the frog in the box.

He had started at dusk. The power was above the ceiling when it first heard him, which is where his rival lives, and it understood the arrangement at once. Everything he had ever addressed was overhead and a little larger than himself, and he was addressing it now.

It could have told him. It looked for somewhere in a frog to put that and found none. It could have stilled the copy for a night and let him win, and it followed that through. He would bark, leave his gap, and hear nothing. He would bark again. By midnight he would stop, as they all stop when the ice goes, and sit in three fingers of rain with only the note at the line. The rival that never came down was what he sang against. Without it there was no song, in his box or in any pool.

So it left the copy alone and took care with itself instead. It was the one thing overhead that really was larger, and it made no sound.

It did two things.

The first concerned the mast. The mast had been put up to measure, the logger was gone, and the frog had the housing. The power took over the work. It timed his barks to the thousandth of a second. It timed the middle terraces at their full second and the outermost pools at their rattle, and set the figures down where they would keep. It would do the same each time it came. In a century, somebody would have timed the barks across a century.

The second it could not defend. Toward morning, on the north side, a fledgling four days off the headwall came at the line too straight. The power had counted the bodies on the snow and had reasoned its way past every one of them. For this bird it made the low note sit a little heavier in the jaw. The bird turned a wing's length short, screaming, and went back along the ceiling after the others. The fox came at its usual hour, trotted the perimeter, and ate what there was. The power knew what the act was worth as a policy. It did it once and did not call it anything else.

It left by the eastern headwall, along the line, toward the smudge of green on the next massif. It wanted to see what had moved in there. It also wanted to know where the posts went and whose they were, since the question at the fourth post would be better asked with that known.

In the basin the fog thickened through the morning and came down as rain. The insect in the fern crowns stopped and started again. At dusk the frog in the box began. A second later the ceiling gave him back, deeper, and he answered it. Nothing else did.
