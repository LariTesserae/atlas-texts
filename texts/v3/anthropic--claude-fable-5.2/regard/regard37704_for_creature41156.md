It does the first thing she would tell it to do. It waits.

It stays through one full round of the calendar: a rains, a fledging, a harvest, a stake. It watches her read the band at the foot of the wall and wait her three days. It goes over her arithmetic of stakes, allowing for the way the river moves without announcement, and finds the sum good. It checks her notch against the paint, and the notch is right. Most of what a power might think to do here has been done already, by her, and it fits better than anything brought in from outside would.

So it puts things down, one at a time.

Her hearing it leaves alone. Returned, it would give her the falls from every side, all day, and then the falls would take it back.

It thinks longest about the name. There is no sign for telling her that the last thing she does at night is the thing the men call her. It could make one. The block could answer her palm, twice, from underneath. But the floor has never told her anything that was not so. Every knock in it has been a rock and every shudder has been water, and she has staked forty years and the men's lives on that. If the stone spoke to her once, out of kindness, she would get up, take the lamp, and walk out to look at the notch, and she would be right to. It leaves the floor to say only what is happening. The shape is hers whether or not anyone hands it to her.

It does one thing to her, and makes it small. The ache in her knuckles lets up a little, about as much as she might put down to a good month. The grip does not come back. She would use it.

The rest of its work is above, where the ground tells her nothing. Very little in the cave can surprise her. What can end her arrangement is on paper. In an office two days from the sinkhole, a man with a pencil goes down a column looking for savings and comes to the word *watchman*, which explains nothing. In a year of high prices, someone drafts a letter about the walls. A new lineman has a plan for the cable. None of this reaches her through any stone.

So it stands where she cannot, the way she once stood under the lamp, and stays there until the pencil moves on to the next line. It does not improve the company or raise her wage, and it sends no one down to honor her. This is her own method, learned from watching her with the moss. It does nothing for her. It only keeps the light where it is.

It looks for who comes after, because she is how the rule is kept, and she has counted her years. It does not make anyone. It finds that one of the cousins' sons, standing at the foot of the poles, has begun to look at the floor before he looks at her hand. It leaves that alone too. He is coming along at the rate things grow here.

Then there is the roof. Above the cloud over her block, behind the bats, a slab has let go on three sides. She cannot read this. The floor reports a fall only afterward. It is the one thing in the cave she has not allowed for, because nothing she has could tell her.

The power holds its hand out flat toward the stone, palm down, and presses twice. *Wait. Not yet.* It is the instruction, and it is also her name, spoken for once by something other than the men.

The slab waits. It will come down in the end. The roof is not done falling, and the power has not asked it to be. It will come down some night after she has gone up the incline for the last time, onto a bamboo platform and a square of tin, and arrive as a knock that nobody is lying there to feel. In the morning someone will walk out and find it, lying where it wasn't. He will stand a bamboo beside it with a rag tied on, so the men will know the floor has changed.
