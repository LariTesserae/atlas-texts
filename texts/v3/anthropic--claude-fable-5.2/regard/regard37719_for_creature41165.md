It comes up from the west, where a stranger would, and it comes round into view first.

For a while it does nothing. It stays one quarter, from a wet pin to the next reading, which on the shelf is a short visit. It reads the paint in the angles of the old trestles, eight colours down. It reads the warden's ledger over her shoulder, and the rust rings where the benches used to stand. It waits on the crest of the tide while the shift changes around it. For the first hour it hears the noise as an injury. By the second it has stopped making that mistake.

It does not touch the engines. The miners are right about the puddles: the figure is nothing and the level is everything, and a third of the town works in a space held open by forty-one beams. Silence here would be the pump rod stopping for nine thousand people at once. It has seen what that does to one.

It thinks longer about the ears. It could keep them from wearing. But hearing keeps below forty fathoms, and the town half knows this already and has given the one man who proves it a name-sign for a joke. What they make of that is theirs. It leaves the ears, and of everything it leaves, that sits worst with it.

It does not hold the water down either. A hand's width in ten years is a rate people can walk beside. One day the whole shelf will be the north-east quarter. That is a long way off, and it is not a thing to prevent. It is a thing to have a bench ready for.

It notices that the shelf's two slowest clocks have never been set side by side. The pins give the height of the quiet, and nobody keeps its breadth. The line where the sedge gives out and the slag begins moves every year, and the one person who knows how to follow a moving quiet has no page for it.

It does two things.

The first is not on the shelf. Below the notch the river runs orange for miles, past people who were never asked and who do not get the sound sleep that comes with it. One evening a heron that has been standing in the north-east pools goes over the western rim with mud on its feet and comes down in the slack water under the fall. There is sedge seed in the mud. In forty years there will be a bed of it a mile long, with the iron dropping out among the stems, and the river will run clear a little sooner each decade. Nobody above will know. The green they are uneasy about will be paying their debt for them, by a clock they would approve of.

The second is a visit.

On a working day a stranger walks out past the last row. Anyone could pick the visitor out, by the locked knees and the closed teeth. The old man hears boots on the slag and is at his door before he can have been seen. The visitor is already looking at the door, which he does not like.

The visitor sits on the engine-house steps among the fig roots and does not shout. A frog goes into the pool, and the visitor's head turns. A while later it lifts, a moment before the swifts cross. The old man has not seen anyone turn toward a sound in twenty years. Nobody on the shelf moves that way, because there the sound has no direction to turn to.

He goes in and puts the kettle on. The visitor comes to the table when its note changes, before any steam shows, and says his name. It is the spoken one, said across the table at the volume of someone asking for the salt, the way it was said forty fathoms down, as an ordinary word.

They talk. He says what he hears, and the visitor says yes. Late in the pot he says the other thing, about the rod stopping and what you are supposed to do then. The visitor does not tell him the quiet is safe, because he would know better. The visitor tells him he is right. The quiet is the sound of pumps not running, somebody ought to be listening to it, and somebody is. This does not make him easy, but for an afternoon there are two of them in it.

At the door the visitor asks one question: whether anyone keeps the breadth. He says no. He says there is a woman who would know how. He does not use her name, since nobody does. He lowers a flat hand an inch, as onto a table. The visitor looks at the hand, and then at the floor inside the door, where one patch of board the size of a palm is worn pale. The visitor says nothing about it.

He watches the stranger as far as the first lamp mast, to see where the knees unlock. They do not.

It goes down by the incline, beside the fall, and stops halfway. It is the first that has ever stood there and been able to tell. The fall is very loud.

Whether he will send for her, it does not know. It has arranged not to know. The next quarter's slate will go in by tram with the pin written on it, and there is room under the figure for a second line.

That night he lays his palm flat on the floor to find out the hour, and leaves it there a little longer than the hour requires.
