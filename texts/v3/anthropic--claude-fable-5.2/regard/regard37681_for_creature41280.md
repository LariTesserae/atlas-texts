It did not come in over the mouth. Anything that crosses the mouth is a shadow, and the fans shut. It came in low with the tide, at the speed of the water, and the fans opened to it from the mouth inward, district by district, as they do for any new water.

For the first month it only learned the place, at the place's own pace. It gave each district a tide. It went along the worm streets coil by coil and crossed the bryozoan quarter beside the sea spider, a box a minute. It wanted to see the net. It could have touched the scale worm at the back, but a scale costs the worm something, so it waited. On the nineteenth day the brittle star's arm strayed, a scale fell, and for a few seconds every thread was lit along one edge.

It could do a great deal here, and it went slowly through what it could do.

It could clear the dip. The brine would stir away on any tide. But the brine is what the ice sheds in growing, and the tubes at the edge already lean away from it. The city knows about the dip. Every few nights one amphipod doesn't. On the fourth night one walked in and slowed. The power stayed for as long as that took, which was not long. That was all it did there.

It could turn the sea spider. But the spider is nine months old like everything else, and the neighbours bud into the boxes behind it within the week. The power noticed that it had grown fonder of the cell on the ceiling than of the rest. Then it watched a copepod held by one leg, then by three. Everyone here is someone else's weather. It decided not to settle accounts between residents.

Then there was the stone. It looked under the mud, which nothing in the city can do. Just outside the mouth lies a pebble dropped by the same iceberg. Set under the low edge, it would hold the stone where it is. The power kept the pebble in mind for a whole tide.

But a hair's width a month is only the stone's share of the closing. The rest is the city's. The chimneys rise and the crusts grow down, and they will meet whether the stone moves or not. The power noticed, too, that it liked the open gap because it could see into it. That is a watcher's reason. A gap that closes for a snailfish does not close for something with a single thread, and water in the doorways is a wide country for the young. It left the pebble in the mud.

It had found one thing, though. When the roof closes, the net comes in for good, and the dividing takes five days. Whatever the net leaves on the polish keeps a larva off for about one. For four days the oval would be bare stone, the last in the city, in the season when larvae are thickest. The walls would still stand around it, but nothing would hold the ground. The young would come out into a street of worm tubes.

The power asked what holding it would take from anyone. A larva turned away from the oval loses nothing. Hundreds have been turned away since autumn and have settled a hand's width on. It asked how long the holding would last, and the answer was four days. It asked what would be left of itself in the place afterward. Nothing would be.

It was still a favour to one resident. The power knew that and chose to do it.

The ice went out. By midsummer the lines let down at slack water touched silk. The cell took hold, and for an hour it stood between floor and ceiling as a column no one can see. The power saw it. A melt pulse came and everything closed. When the water cleared, there was quartz flour on the polish, and the threads passed the palest grains inward. Eleven days later, at slack water, the roof closed at both ends. The net thinned from the border inward. In every thread the traffic ran one way only, and then it stopped.

A day later the power went out over the oval, as thin as it could make itself, and lay along the scratches. When a larva touched down, the power moved a little water under it, one groove wide, toward the middle, about as hard as the cell had pulled. The larva felt the ground move and went on. This happened two hundred and six times.

The skeleton shrimp along the border had nothing to steal. One at a time they turned to face the mouth.

The sea spider crossed once. Its feet lifted cleanly. It stopped over the roof, lowered its proboscis, and rested the tip on the quartz for a minute. The power kept to what it had decided. The spider went on to the far wall.

On the fifth day the young came out at both ends of the sheath, several hundred, each too small to see, each with a single thread, onto clean polished ground. The power took itself in from the border inward. This took about an hour.

It did not hold the young. The shrimp turned back and took what they could reach. The tide carried some out past the sill. Some let go and fell among the chimneys, where the grain-gatherers are. Some found a groove.

The power went out high with the tide, the way the water goes.

The pebble is still in the mud outside the mouth. In the middle of the pocket there is less than a finger joint of water now, and there are four columns. In the deepest groove of the ceiling is a roof of pale grains, one grain thick, with nothing under it. Around it, on the brightest days, there are a few dozen dull spots on the stone, each the size of a lentil, each long the way the scratches run. They don't yet touch. Inside them, the granules run both ways.
