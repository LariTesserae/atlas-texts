It comes up by the route, on foot, because the route is how one comes. At the cairn it stops in front of the pink strip and the pecked ring with the bar across it.

The mark was not made for it. It obeys anyway, and this costs it more than it would cost a person. A power of this kind does most of its work by saying: it says, and the thing is so. With the finger across its lips it has only what anyone has up here, which is hands, knees, patience and the ability to hold still.

It kneels, since kneeling is how the place is seen, and it stays some days. It watches the hollow fill, skin over and drain. It learns to raise an open hand for the stoat, and the finches answer as they would answer anyone. It is near the notch at dawn when the nanny stops and looks at the empty place.

There is a good deal here it could mend, and it goes through the list honestly:

- **The headwall.** It could pin the loose slabs. Then the bowl could afford noise, and the stamping, the wing-flashing and the hide-wrapped trigger sticks would have no reason behind them.
- **The soil.** It could hurry it. The cushions are already making soil, at the rate cushions do.
- **The traps.** It could reset them, with hands that have not been taught.
- **The strip.** It could tie on a red one. The strip is the elder's to change.

It leaves all of it. The bowl is not hurt. The bowl has not even been told.

What is missing is the animal with four hands, and the reason can't be found up here. It goes down. At the first stream loud enough to cover a voice it takes the finger from its lips, and finds it has nothing it wants to say yet.

Below, among the rivers and dogs and children, the answer is short. The one whose turn it was to bring went into a river at the thaw. Of all the others who were ever brought, one is living, very old, who no longer leaves the fire. The people know that two go up late in summer and come back thin and hoarse, and that it isn't talked about. Nobody has decided anything. In a few years it will be something the grandparents did.

It could speak now. A dream would be enough. But then the bowl would be a place a god had spoken of. They would come over the lip forty at a time, singing, and the wall would answer.

It could let the thing end, and it considers this longest. Endings are allowed. The ice ended, and that is why there are cushions. But it has seen a great many peoples, and few who give up the marrow, or who put moss under the stone that kills. And nothing in these people failed. A river took one person in the wrong month.

The rule itself says how. Each is brought by someone who was brought. The power has not been brought, so it goes back up to be.

No elder is there to bring it. It lets the line do that. The first labour is a stone of its own crouching size, and this is harder than it sounds, because it has to decide what size it is. It picks one no bigger than the others. It walks the stone across the bowl end over end, easing each turn onto a pad of moss, and it does not make the stone lighter.

On the third day a turn gets away from it and the stone comes down on bare slab with a crack. The finches flash. The hay-cutters go under. The power holds still with everything else, listening for whether the wall will answer, unable to unsay it. After a few breaths something small slithers down the headwall and stops. That is the day the quiet gets into its arms.

It sets the stone upright in the line, back to the drop. By morning there is rime on the windward side.

Then it goes down again. It is small now, dark from the light, its palms white with rock flour. It asks for nothing. At the old one's fire it is handed a horn cup, drinks, and sets the cup down. The old one watches the cup meet the hearthstone, then looks at the hands. Then the old one looks across the fire at one of the young ones, the one who carries water without spilling, and lifts a chin. From under the bedding comes a strip of hide, dyed red.

That is the whole of the telling. Nothing is explained, which is correct.

They go up late, with the stoat nearly white. At the cairn it unties the pink strip, ties on the red, and lays a finger across closed lips.

The new one walks a stone. The traps are reset. At the notch, under the grey hide, it takes the seat that is kept, since this year it is the elder. It does the work the way the work is done, close, with both arms, and lays the goat on the rock. It could have any marrow in the world. It carries the bones out onto the slabs, and when the vulture claps, the two of them hold still together.

By the tenth day one wakes when the other wakes. It reaches, and the cup is there. A power is not often half of anything. For those days it knows nothing the other doesn't know, and it sleeps back to back on willow twigs, taking exactly its share of the heat.

At the first loud stream they stop and both talk at once, for a long time. It had not expected to be hoarse. Somewhere in what it says is that it won't be coming again, and that next summer the new one must bring someone. It can't tell whether this is heard over the water and the other voice. It doesn't need to be. The new one has walked a stone across the bowl and knows what the line is for.

Further down, there is one person on the path.

The next year two come over the lip, one who has been before and one who has not. The strip is changed. The finger is laid across the lips.

Count who is here in the dead of winter and the answer is what it always was: the hay-cutters under the drifts, awake beside their stacks, and the people along the lip. There are two more of them now. One was set there by something that is not a person. It crouches a few strides from its neighbours with its back to the drop, growing rime on the windward side, and the goats can't tell.
