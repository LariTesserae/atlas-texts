It comes up the cliffs in the evening with the lowland air, that being the only road. The first thing it does is slow down. At its own pace it would see cobbles. It takes a season to watch one gap cross a whaleback and most of a century to follow one out to the cracks, and it does not consider that it has arrived until it has done both.

Then it looks for what is wrong. A power of this kind is used to being called. It goes where something cries out, and nothing here does, which settles nothing, because nothing here would. So it asks in the only way the place allows. It rests its attention on one middling animal, as lightly as a snail under the rim, and stays through some years of wet evenings and dry noons. It finds hunger, a shadow's worth a day, met every morning. It finds no fear. It finds nothing it can honestly call loneliness in an animal that has been leaned on from six sides since it was the size of a thumbnail. It follows an old one to a wall and stays for the end. The end is a drawing down until shell rim meets stone, the same as at any noon, and then the fog does the rest.

It goes through what it could give. Rain has been sent here already and changed little. Loose stone would save each bird a flight, and the flight is the one thing a bird does all year besides stand. Quicker gaps would outrun the lichen. Better eyes would have cloud and sun to look at. A voice would let each animal reach neighbors it is already touching.

The last gift is harder to set aside. It could open a gap. Room is the one thing the herd understands, and a patch of bare wet stone is the nearest the power could come to saying it was here and meant well. But every gap on the plateau is somebody's absence, shared out in rings along a line to the edge. A gap from nowhere would be width that no one died for, and some grazer would carry a ring that stood for nothing. The power lets that one go as well. It notices that it has now declined to move a number of times, and that it learned this here.

So it does the one thing nobody on the plateau is placed to do. It goes up past the top of the gliders' stack and looks down.

It sees the outline. The outline is not recorded here. The power did not pass it on to the plateau, and it would be strange for anyone else to have it first.

From that height it also sees the forest country, green to the horizon and steaming. It sees a brown river with bars of dark pebbles at the bends. In the afternoon it sees the air over the trees thicken and begin to lean toward the cliffs. Everything the plateau lives on starts down there. A grazer knows six neighbors by their pressure. The forest is the seventh, and it touches none of them.

The power sees how the place would end. Nothing on top would do it. If the forest thinned, to fire or drought or people with axes, the evening cloud would thin with it. The grazers would draw down until rim met stone and wait, as they do at every noon. They would wait very well. Nothing in them can tell a long noon from the last one.

So it goes down, by the face, the first thing ever to do so with any interest. The six-sided pattern holds for a long way. Near the bottom it does not stop so much as loosen. The shells lie apart, with plain stone between them, a hand's width and then a pace. Those animals were out of touch. Whether they had some way of reaching each other across the distance, the shells do not say. The power thinks of the quiet on top and understands that it may be something that was arrived at.

Below, it takes up a place where the air begins its climb, and does there mostly what a gap does over thin ground. It keeps the trees standing by seeing that not much happens to them. Fires stay small. Dry years end. When people come to the forest's edge with axes, they find the ground better a few valleys over, which it is, the power having seen to that as well. The river goes on turning out dark pebbles onto a bar the birds can find. None of this looks like work. From the plateau it does not look like anything.

Each evening the air around it lifts and goes up the cliffs, and it lets it go. From below, the cloud shows as a pale line along the rim. Once in a long while it goes up with it, knee-deep and then overhead, to see. The walls are a shell longer. The frogs are in their cups. Every animal for several days' walk has its green end toward the power and its face to the middle, and not one of them knows it has been there. It counts that as the work going well, and goes back down before the rock is dry.
