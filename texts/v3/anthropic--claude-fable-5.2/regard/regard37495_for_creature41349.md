The third visitor carries nothing in.

It has no heat of its own. That is the condition of residence, and it meets it, as the bird and the person cannot. The veil does not part for it, and no water forms on it. The frogs, who keep their agreed distance from anything cool, do not shift when it arrives. It could stay twenty minutes or two hundred years, and it sees at once that this is the most important fact about itself here. It is the only one not in a hurry.

So for four years, which is one frog's life, it does nothing. It does not use the bench. The bench is someone's.

It learns what could not be learned in five hours. There were sixty-three frogs on the first night and sixty-two in the morning. The answer at dusk is another frog about one time in three. The frogs cannot tell which, and it turns out not to matter. What a frog does after either kind of answer is wait, and the waiting is what lets another frog find it.

It goes down the wake and attends to the three dead stones, then comes back and attends to the five. Its attention reports what a hand reports, which is almost nothing. It goes up and looks over the ridge, since looking costs no one anything. There is snow, a far slope, and older flows under the snow. Nothing there explains anything. The shadows do not point at the ridge. They point through it.

It spends a season on the bowls. The water explains uphill. It searches for whatever explains north and does not find it, and each season the frogs lay a little north of centre regardless.

This is the main thing it learns. Sixty animals the length of a thumbnail are steering by something a power cannot detect. It takes this seriously, and most of what it does not do follows from it.

It does not stop the garden, though it would like to keep the tree fern. A garden that stood still would have no front, no new rock, and no poor clean bowls. It would be all middle, rich with rot, where almost nothing lays. Downslope, the grass would go on waiting for soil.

It does not hurry the garden to the ridge. The pace is the pace at which a liverwort becomes dirt. A garden that arrived sooner would arrive as wet rock.

It does not turn the frogs around at the rear edge. This is the one it wants to do. It could be there every night, and it would be willing to be there every night for as long as there were frogs. That is not the difficulty. A frog moved forward by a kindness lays eggs, and so do its young. Within a few dozen generations the garden would be full of frogs sitting still on cooling moss, waiting for the kindness. Every frog inside descends from frogs that went forward. It will not be the reason that stops being true.

It did check one thing first. On a night in the second year it stayed beside a frog that had kept its crack too long, and attended as closely as it is able while the veil went over. It was prepared to find something that would change its mind. What it found was slowness, and then less. It still does not like it. Some mornings there is a frog on the frost, and it does the sum again and gets the same answer, and it stays while the bird eats. It has decided that if it will not act, it will at least not look away.

Then there are the stones. Three lie in the wake and five stand. The power can do that sum as well as anyone, and it comes out no better for being done carefully. There are three points, unevenly spaced, and a century still to go. The garden may reach the ridge. Or it may go cold on some night forty years from now, front and back together, and be wake by morning.

It could prevent that. It could not make a stone, since it cannot even read one, but it could hold the heat itself, by plain force, indefinitely. It has considered what that garden would be. No shadows would point anywhere. It would stand still, or go where the power chose. The frogs would lay north of a line that had stopped meaning anything. It would be a very good terrarium.

It has not ruled this out. It notices that it does not have to yet, and that deciding early, in either direction, is the one clearly foolish move available. The place has the look of something being done rather than something happening, and whoever is doing it has spent three stones on it already. What the power lacks is specific. It has never seen a stone go. It does not know whether what happens to them is breaking or finishing. The three in the wake may be casualties, or they may be more like the stalks, spent on purpose into the ground behind. One of the five will tell it, some year. It intends to be there, paying attention, and to decide after that and not before.

What it does is this.

In the fifth year the person comes back, slower on the clinker than whoever piled the bench must have been. Dry clothes go under the flat plate. Then comes one stride through the veil, the bench, and twenty minutes of water running off the only cool thing in the garden. The person watches the front and counts calls on their fingers. The power, a little way off, counts too, and does not correct the total.

Then the person goes out, quickly, into a wind that freezes wet cloth stiff. But for about as long as it takes to get into a dry coat, the wind on that side of the dome comes from slightly somewhere else. The person glances up the slope, the way one looks at luck, and dresses, and walks down to the rear edge, and stacks three flat plates. That makes fifteen.

It allowed itself that one. A person is not a lineage. A kindness to a person breeds nothing, except now and then another visit.

The other thing it does is live there. Each year the rear edge comes up behind it, as it comes to everything, and each year it moves a few paces forward on its own account, without being carried. It keeps back from the cool moss at the distance everyone has agreed on.

At dusk a male on the frontmost rim gives his one note. The power could answer him in any voice there is, on time and at pitch. It waits, like the rest of them. Something answers, late and a little lower. It knows which kind of answer that was, and it keeps that to itself. Then the rain starts, as hot as all of them.
