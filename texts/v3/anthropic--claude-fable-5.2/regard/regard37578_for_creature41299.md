It comes the way everything here came. It blows in and lodges.

It brings no light, and a hand at arm's length stays a guess. It learns the notch by ear and skin, as the notch has to be learned, and it stays through a great many gusts before it considers doing anything. By then it knows the roar on the slope, the lean, the second of slack, the knock. By then the tree near the north shoulder has begun to rock.

It considers the wind first, because the wind is doing the damage. It could lay the air flat until morning. But the cloud gives up its water only to leaves it is driven through, and the beads come down only when the canopy is shaken. A still night here is a dry one. The wind also planed the lid and carried in every spore and seed beneath it. It leaves the wind alone.

It considers the roots. It could send them down through the clinker into the walls of the flow, and the tree would hold. But a tree anchored in rock has no need of the others, and the lid is one piece only because every tree in it needs the others. So it keeps its attention on the mat as the mat lifts and settles, and when the gust comes that holds a second longer, it lets it hold. The tree goes over slowly. This is the hardest thing it does all night.

It considers water: one pool in a hollow, sealed so the lava could not swallow it. The frog has no use for a pool. The first things to use it would come from somewhere else, and they would stay.

Then it goes looking for suffering, because that is the question it came with, and it looks carefully. In the frog it finds cold and waiting, at the frog's own speed, and nothing in them that is asking to end. Warmth would only make the frog hungry on a night with no hunting. In the bird it finds a small fire banked low on purpose. In the film of water on a moss leaf it finds an ordinary night.

In the dry floor, in the ball of shredded leaf, it finds fear, small and real. The ceiling has been lifting for hours, one runway opens onto wind, and a body that size cannot go long without eating. The crickets are an arm's length away with their legs folded. It could send one down the runway. But the cricket lives here too, and every gift it can think of in this place is taken from another resident.

So it stays beside the long-nosed thing for a while. The animal does not know it is accompanied, and it is hard to say what the company is worth. It stays anyway, until the heart in the leaf ball slows a little, for reasons of its own.

It considers the line. The line takes almost nothing from the notch and gives it nothing. At either end are people on a night shift, a gauge, a row of pots. It has no more right to their night than to the cricket's. It leaves the line up.

Then the cloud tears, and it sees the shape of the place, with the stars above and the orange smear below. It also sees further along than the night. The wind will never finish the notch. Lava might, and then everything would blow in again. What the notch could not outlast is quieter: air a little warmer each decade, and the cloud riding a little higher, until it clears the ridge and the wind comes through the gap empty. The moss would hold out for a season. The ridge is the top, and there is no higher ground for the frog. That is being settled under the orange smear, and under others like it, by people who are asleep.

Here at last is something it can do in the manner of the place. It goes down the line at the speed of current. At the dam a needle shivers each time the spans gallop. The one watching it has seen this a hundred nights. Tonight, for no reason they could give, the thought does not go straight through to the smelter. It stops in the gap, on a ridge in the dark, in cloud, with something up there besides towers, and the watcher wonders what. The thought is finer than orchid seed, and most such things never find a crack. The notch is made entirely of the ones that did.

Toward grey light the slack between gusts lengthens. It is still there, lodged. The long-nosed thing comes up the cut runway, stops at the new edge, and presses its face into the moss to drink. The bird warms and shakes once. By the count of acts, almost nothing has been done. A tree was allowed to fall, a small animal was kept company, and one thought was set down a long way off.

From the moss beside the hole in the lid comes one tick, then a wait. It does not fill the wait. The answer comes from the moss.
