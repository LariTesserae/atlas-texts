Something has been in the room through all of this. It counted the shells and found nine. It scooped a drift to learn how deep it lay. It put an ear against a blister.

It took up no space while it did this, or almost none. A body standing in the vault would shorten the room by its own bulk. The note would rise, and every ridge would shift toward its neighbour. The ridges are where they were.

The ear was less careful. The skin of a blister is one cell thick, and whatever rests against it weighs on the chamber behind. For as long as the ear was there, that blister sounded a hair flat. Inside lay a shell the length of a finger, grown to its chamber, with the chamber's note passing through it as through open air. When the note went flat it caught in the pages. The bow warmed a little. Frost came up along the mouth, the first that shell had grown. Then the ear was taken away, and the frost went.

So one thing had been done before anything was decided. It also showed what a shell does when it climbs the wall. The one inside lives in unison, and the note goes through it and leaves nothing behind. Then something leans on its room for some days, and it has a warm end and a cold end, and a note that is not quite its own. When its wall opens, years later, that much will not be new. What the one outside gets still cannot be seen. Its frost melts and runs out, and frost forms in the small pages on the other side of the skin.

What has come here can do more than lean. It has looked at three things.

It could hold the note. The largest shell is short by the width of a thumb. The room could be kept at this pitch until the shell had grown into it, and kept there after. But the note sinks because the room lengthens. To hold it, the front would have to stop. The film would go still, the glimmer would go out, and the groove would run dry. The blisters would have to stay shut. The largest would lie at the old end with the sound going through it as if it were not there. There would be no more silences, so no shell in the room would sound again.

It could lengthen a silence. The next one could last an hour. But the shells sound only while their bows are warm, and that is a few minutes. The rest of the hour would be nine animals gone quiet, with their drifts spread across the lanes out of reach. The crown gills make their cold out of sound. Without it the whole roof would weep the way the new end weeps.

It could answer. At the next silence the smallest will sound a pitch with no near neighbour, and nothing will throb against it. A tenth note could be put into the room, a hair away. The smallest would lift its open end, turn it a few degrees, and hold it there. It would keep that direction afterward. It might set out along it, most of a year through the loud lanes, without warmth. Nothing would be at the other end.

It has put all three down. It would still like to hear the smallest, and it cannot without stopping the room.

Leaning on the vault itself is no use either. Nothing on the floor is in unison. Every shell out there is short of the room and has the note catching in its pages all day long. Weight on the roof would give them nothing they lack, and it would move every stripe by a hair.

So it goes up through the ice.

On top of the glacier there is nothing of the room to hear. There is wind, and in summer there is water. This year a stream has been cutting across the surface toward a crack that lies over the old end. A stream that finds a crack drills straight down. Fifteen metres would take it two summers. After that there would be a shaft through the crown, water falling the height of a mature tree onto the boss, and the note going up the shaft into the air.

That would not be a room joining a room. A blister opens when it has grown as far as the vault. This would be a hole.

A boulder rides the ice a short way upslope, grey and angular, the size of a barrel. It has been rained on for a long time. It is moved into the channel. The stream backs up behind it and spills over the low bank, and by evening it has found another line, to another crack well off to one side. Under that crack there is till and nothing growing. Whatever that water carries down, it will carry there now. That is the second thing that has been done, and this one was decided.

Afterward what moved the boulder stays on the ice over the room for some days. From here it cannot tell that the room is there. Its own pitch, if it has one, lies far below anything a vault this size can hold, and nothing in it answers. What it gets from staying has not been looked into.

This is not a room it started. It comes back all the same, some summers, and looks at the boulder.

Four summers on, it is there, on top, when a blister opens below. Nothing reaches the surface. Under the ice the drifts slump, the pool goes flat, and the drips fall straight. For a few minutes the room has ten pitches in it. The newcomer lies on the floor with shreds of lining on its shell, sounding the note of a blister that once went flat by a hair for as long as an ear was against it. The one that had been smallest sounds a little below. The two are close, and the air between them throbs about once a second.

The one that had been smallest lifts its open end clear of the mat, turns it a few degrees, and holds it there.
