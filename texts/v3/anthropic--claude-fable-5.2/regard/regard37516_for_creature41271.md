Something came to the sea that could hear the horn.

It arrived at the lip a little before the hour and stood on the roof of the turbine hall. When the handrail took its second tremor, it heard a flat brass note under the fall and separate from it, held for ten seconds. It stayed for the next one to be sure. Then it went down into the water and looked for a long time before it considered doing anything.

It travelled the longest null up-drift, slowly. It read the labels on the rolls, and then the rolls. It hung beside the fish at a crossing and counted the birds on a still stripe. Throughout, it took care to cast no shadow on the lane.

It knew what is usually done. A power that pities begins with the noise, so it followed that through. With the fall hushed, the returns from the scarps have nothing to erase. The surface lies smooth from wall to wall, and the light comes down whole everywhere. The amphipods spread through the basin until they are too thin to feed anything. The ribbon turns its pits to find the stripe, and there is no stripe, since it is all stripe. The quiet here is made out of the roar, so the power left the roar.

It left the edges too, since the ribbon has no other way to become two. It left the grain of lime in the amphipod, which without it does not know which way is down.

It looked into the ribbon, which it could do. It found less than a watcher would fear and more than nothing. Nobody in there knew of the sea, or of the other pieces, or of being a length. There was a brightness to one side and the keeping of it there, and when the groove carried something forward, a slight settling. Where the flesh parted over obsidian, it found nothing that differed from the wave.

It went over the lip with one piece, because nobody had seen them go. The sheet holds together a long way down, then frays, then is cloud. The piece broke where the water broke, at no point that could be called the place, and reached the lowlands as mist. The power kept that small brightness company the whole way, and it did not tell itself that what stopped was nothing. But it did not catch the next one. Every bare place on the edge was taken, which was why the piece had come to the lip. To keep it would take a longer sea, or a ribbon that grew less, and either would be a different thing put where this one had been.

That left the stations, the one thing in the basin the sea had not set.

At the fourth it watched a tail go in and counted what came out. About a fifth of the flesh came out as scraps shorter than a finger-joint, too small to close a thumb. In these the keeping went on for some hours with no mouth to serve, and then stopped. At the first three stations, the tails lay against the bars and slid off. Lipward of them the short ribbons had long since grown out, and the edge was hung as it is anywhere.

In the hall, stacked behind the penstocks, it found the rest of the gratings, cast and drilled, with the bolts in a tin and a wrench on the sill. Someone had made one for every station and fitted the three nearest the lip, where the roar loosens the teeth. Then the work stopped, about where the labelling stops.

The power finished it with the wrench that was there. At each station it waited until no tail lay in the intake, which at the seventh took eleven days. It did not gather up the short ribbons below. They had their thumbs and their waves and would lengthen.

The pens would now draw a line with no points in it. Since they draw over their own ink, this could not be seen. So in the gauge-house of the fourth station it took out the last labelled roll and found the last place where the ink had spread. Beside it, in small letters, it wrote what the point had been and what the gratings were for, in case anyone came who worked in iron.

It left the lamps burning in daylight and the horn sounding. They took nothing that was missed, and the horn had now been heard.

Last it went to the back scarp and the collar of ice, and stayed beside the one that was never a tail. It did not touch it, and it had nothing to give it. The ribbon had its stripe and a grey line down the middle of the glass. While the power was there, the ribbon let its hind part sink across a blade of obsidian, and in a day or so it parted. The forward piece rose and hung as before. The hind piece went lipward with the drift, turning over slowly, closing one end.

The power followed it as far as the first station it came to. There the piece lay for a moment against the new grating, then slid off and went on.
