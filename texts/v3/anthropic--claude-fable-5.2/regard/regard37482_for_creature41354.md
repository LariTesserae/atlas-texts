It came up the way everything comes up: in a bucket, through the cloud, on a load of brick. The cable crew unloaded the brick and did not find it.

It stayed a year, which on the saddle is the height of one doorstep.

Within a week it knew what it could do. It could bring both summits up to meet the echo in an afternoon. It could lift the sun over the rim, so that roofs were bright and faces had eyes in them. It could cut a single gully down the eastern slab and let the water find out about down. It had done such things in other places, and some of those places are better for it.

Here it waited, and went to look at what each of those things would replace.

It stood in the kitchen of a hung house while a girl at the barrel end tapped the gutter with a spoon. The beads on the tin wavered, and then went, one after another, the way she wanted. She was six. She was older than the water and she was being patient with it. It watched a cokeman lay his palm on a fresh fracture under a cracked wall. It read the levellers' notebooks back to the hand that is hard to read. By midwinter it had stopped thinking about the summits. A finished mountain would put these people where everyone else in the world is, standing on something older than they are, with nothing left that needed to be shown.

About the sun it was less sure. At the horn it stood with the day shift on the rock outside the mill gate. When the echo came and the heads went back, every face went dark, and it, which does not need light, saw their eyes. It was the only one on the saddle that ever had. It felt it was holding something that belonged to them.

So it went up to see. It walked out past the end of the eastern slab, past the ibex, who did not get up, and on up the spur that is due to the summit that is due, and stood on it. The peak is where the oldest notebook says. From there it could see what the levellers have no instrument for. When those faces are finished there will be snow on them, and the light off the cloud will strike the snow and come back down onto the saddle, late and changed, the way the horn does. It will be thin, and it will be enough to see an eye by. Some child will draw a second sun, over the house. Someone will have to invent the brim.

That was due. It left it due.

Then it rode an empty down one night and looked at the bottom station, and at what lies behind the bottom station. There is enough coal. There is enough brick. Whoever fills the buckets will go on filling them. It saw there what the old sheave on the station wall is about, and it does not discuss that either. It came back up on a load of coal and brought no news, since no one had asked for any.

What it did for the place, in the end, was stand surety. It put its own weight behind the echo and behind the cable. If the summits were ever going to come in short, or the coal fail a generation before the last house is seated, they will not now. Nothing on the saddle looks different. The levellers will go on being right to the thickness of a doormat and will not know that anyone checked.

The saddle did more for it than that. On the moss between the gasholders and the pump house, in the dark between two lamps, a trimmer coming the other way felt something there, put a foot out on the bare rock, the cold side, and went on. No one had taken the cold side for it before. In the changing shed there was a peg. In the hung terrace there was a house with the fire banked and the kettle already warm, and it understood that it had not been the first to arrive.

It did one thing that could be seen, and one person saw it.

In the oldest terrace still hanging, an old woman has her chair by the front door. She was born the winter the frame went up. The rock stands four doorsteps short of her threshold. The levellers' figures are good, and she does not have four years. She knows it. She keeps the chair there anyway.

The people have to be told. So on a night late in its year, with the cloud up through the works, it told her. It did not raise the rock. The rock keeps its rate, and a city is hung on that. It told her what the ibex know, which is that the ground is already there at its proper height. It told no one else. The children would have been out past the slab by morning.

She sat a while. Then she took off her boots, opened the front door with nobody holding her belt, and went out in her stockinged feet onto the dark above the slab. She said, to no one, that it was still warm. She stood about as long as the echo lasts, came in, and shut the door.

It was an advance, and she will not see what it was drawn against. She sits by the door in her stockings now, and the household has decided not to ask.

When its year was up it banked the fire, left the kettle as it had found it, and went out by the back gantry. It did not go down. It walked past the last lamp to where the slab stops, and folded itself among the ibex at the height the spur will have. It is early too. The leveller who sighted on them that spring counted thirteen pairs of folded knees, wrote thirteen in the notebook, and took the height.
