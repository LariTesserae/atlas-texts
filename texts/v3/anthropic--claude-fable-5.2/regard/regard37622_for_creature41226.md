The visitor comes down from the headland at night, on foot. The rectangle of afternoon shows from up there, and she wants to arrive the way anything else would. She has taken a body for this. The bass can only be had through shins and back teeth, so she has brought shins and back teeth. The rock cuts her boot soles.

At the rim her shadow stops at the surface. She could make it fall further, and she leaves it where it is.

She stays twenty-three days. That is how long it takes for every wall she saw on the first night to go out through the ebb notch as grey plume. By then she has outlasted the keeper's whole body, and the chord and the rules are still there. It can't be met any sooner than that.

On the first morning she puts a hand in. The cool pressure closes on her wrist and lifts the hand out, dry. She could have kept it there, since nothing on this shore or under it could have moved it. She lets it be lifted, and afterwards she looks at the hand for a while. Very few things have ever told her no, and none so carefully.

Anyone able to help starts thinking of gifts the moment something is noticed. She knows this about herself, so she lets the list come and looks at each one.

Room is first. Ten hollows stand empty, and she could open a basin the size of a field. But the chord stands in this hollow the way a note stands in a bottle. The still points where the grains hang are fixed by four paces and three, and by chest-deep at one end. In a different vessel something else would be singing. And a field would be built full in a few years, and then the oldest quarter would come down on the ebb as it does now. The keeper already has its answer to a full pool. Room would only postpone the day it needs it.

Night is next. She is fond of the dark herself. Nothing in the pool has ever needed it, and her fondness is no reason.

Then citizens. The streets are the right width, and she could make something smaller than a flea to walk them. She thinks about it waking in a tower with true corners, three weeks before the ebb. It wouldn't come to that. The keeper would know it from a grain faster than she could, carry it across the surface, and set it down on the rim unharmed, and the keeper would be right. The town is something the keeper is still thinking, worked in glass, and no one should be settled in it while that goes on.

The answer is the one she holds longest. After twenty-three days she can hear where the harmony leans. There is a pitch none of the voices ever takes, and the chord would close around it. She could hum it once during the tide hours, when the ticking is off and the pool is as quiet as it gets. But a finished thing goes silent. That is the keeper's own rule. She suspects the keeper hears that pitch as well as she does, and steps around it as squarely as its traffic turns a corner. She keeps her mouth shut.

She can't tell whether it is content, or whether the hand at her wrist was courtesy or distaste. She could find out by opening the keeper up and following each voice down to where it starts. But the keeper takes apart only what will sort cleanly, and whatever is alive it hands back whole. She can't improve on that, so she applies it to the keeper. She will leave without knowing.

On the eleventh day she lies beside the warm thread for an afternoon. The stripe is ragged and doing well. It is growing on the one thing the keeper couldn't keep. She has built a great deal on purpose in her time, with true corners. Most of what she ended up loving was growing in the runoff. So she doesn't touch the stripe. With one fingertip of help it would have a ruled edge.

At the small hollow she kneels and cups her hands around the water, and the tower shows its grey edge. The one voice wavers. In the big pool a new voice steadies in a minute or two, because there is a hand under it. This one has nothing under it yet. She could put something there. The voice would steady in a breath, and it would be standing on her. She takes her hands away. It has wavered for months and has not stopped. She holds on to that.

She doesn't introduce them. During the tide hours an upper voice sometimes sits on the small pool's note, and those are the only hours when a thin tone three paces off could be heard at all. They are in earshot of each other, and the rest isn't hers to arrange.

On the last morning she stands. Where she has sat for twenty-three days the rock has lost its glass edge. There is a shallow place there now, the first worn thing on the shore, and a little rain is standing in it. She didn't intend it. She can't close her accounts either. She looks at it and leaves it.

Then she does one thing on purpose, and she is not sure it is wise. In her pocket is a pebble from a river a long way inland. It is worn round, which nothing here is, and it is blue. The pool has channels for dark, for pale and for shell-white, and none for blue. Everything it has ever sorted came from this shore.

She doesn't drop it in. She sets it on the seaward rim, a hand's width from the notch where the sand goes in, on dry rock. A high tide may roll it in or carry it off. If it goes in, the keeper will turn it for an hour and take it apart from the outside, and it will come to grains it has no channel for. It will have to do something with them. Whatever it does will be an answer of some kind, and she won't be there to hear it. She has no rule for the pebble once it leaves her hand.

She goes back up the headland the way she came. The next spring tide is in four days.
