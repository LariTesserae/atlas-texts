It comes down the intake tunnel with the flow, as the others do, and makes a circuit of the lamps. The salamander holds still and passes for fieldstone. The visitor lets it pass, and leaves before slack water.

It comes back with the next flow, and the one after, for a year of them. Most things here happen twice a day, but one happens for an hour in high summer, and it wants to have seen everything before it touches anything.

It goes over the town the way the amphipods go over a new brick, and it learns a few things the town has not been told about itself.

The mussels grow thickest just inside the sluice, and left alone they would close the gap in a few winters, shell on shell. They have not been left alone. The animal takes three a flow, four if the flow is long, from the spot where they crowd the opening. It is alive because the sluice rusted at the width it did, and the sluice is still that wide because the animal eats.

The eddy that carries the moss balls north it observes on the first day. It is a forearm wide and lasts as long as the walk home.

It also learns why the animal cannot feel that eddy. A flank that can read a worn pin through two hundred paces of steel has to be deaf to its own stir, or it would read nothing else.

There are things a visitor could do. It could scrub the lamps, for eyes that can tell lamp from no lamp, and take off the only green there is. It could recut the stamp and put words on the wall all the way to the top. It could lead the animal to the chimney at summer noon. But the light there is too faint for it, and the water in the column is warm, and warm water holds less of what the frill is for. It could bring another of its kind. There are still some in the stream above the lake, under flat stones, a tenth its size. But the wheel pit holds one, the flow crosses one pair of flanks, and the visitor cannot find, in anything the animal does, the shape of a missing second animal. It may be wrong about that, and it keeps the question open.

It could put a real current along the foot of the west wall, so that on some slack water the waiting would be answered. Then two things would carry the moss north, and the animal would have felt the wrong one.

Nothing that keeps this animal was meant. The sluice, the skylight, the clear tunnel and the bare track were all done by nobody on purpose. The visitor is the first thing to arrive here meaning well, and it has some idea what that is worth, because the animal has already run the experiment. It put a moss ball under the hammer and got a disc that could not roll, and it took a season of flows to round it again. Since then it finds the lozenges by feel, turns each over in its fingers, and puts it back where it was. The visitor has been over the whole town and has not found a better procedure.

So it turns the town over and puts it back where it was, with two exceptions.

Everything here comes round: silt to brick to mud to silt, moss south and moss north, water in and out. Wear is the one thing that goes in a single direction. The pin in the link that ticks twice has a few thousand flows left in it. When it goes, the conveyor will stop with a brick halfway out, the press house will fill, and the silt that has been kept off this street for longer than the wall takes to turn over will come down and stay.

By now the visitor has learned to hold still and pass for water. It waits until the shaft has coasted down and the lamps have gone from tea to ember to dark, and this once it does not leave. It does not make the pin new. A new pin would tick once, and the animal would lie with its jaw on the bearing block for a season, counting links and never arriving. It makes the pin, and the eye the pin sits in, exactly as worn as they are, and stops them there. Nothing strikes anything. On the next flow the link comes round and ticks twice.

It will have to come back for the gate, the screw and the bearings, each as it nears the end of its allowance. It takes this as its part, the leg of the circuit that returns the steel, run along the bottom of things at slack water. It does not expect to be observed doing it.

The second exception is not needed for anything. On the bare track, where four blunt fingers will come across it, the visitor leaves a stone the size of a fist. It is round and grey and passes for fieldstone. The animal has put a rust icicle on the anvil, and a rivet, and a moss ball, and the visitor judges that anything that does that is owed something to put there.

The animal finds it going home and walks around it. It walks around it for nine flows. On the tenth it carries the stone to the forge in its mouth, sets it on the anvil, backs off, and lays its chin on the floor. The hammer tips and comes down. The floor shifts, and inside that large shift there is a small one.

When the hammer lifts there are two halves on the anvil. Each is hollow and lined with several hundred small points, about the size of the animal's own teeth. They are the only edges in the town that have not gone round. The animal goes over them with its fingers until the hum begins to fail. It gets two mussels that flow.

The visitor does not see what it does with them. Some flows later one half lies in the lee of the anvil block, among the cracked shells. The other is not in the street.

Some slack waters the animal lies along the foot of the west wall with its whole flank attentive. On some of those the visitor is there too, a little to the south. The water is still, and neither of them moves it. When the hum starts, low in the belly, the animal gets up and goes north for a mussel. Its tail sweeps, the moss balls along the wall roll ahead of it a few paces at a time, and the visitor feels the current they were both waiting for go by.
