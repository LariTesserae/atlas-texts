It comes down the raise on the surface air, among the moths, and takes up about as much room as one of them. At the bottom it does the first courteous thing, which is to stand on a rib. The air holds still there. Nothing is shaken loose, and the crickets go on singing a hair under the fan.

Then it stays, long past the hour it takes to see that one thing lives here. It stays until the bark has gone round the walls more times than there are crickets, and by then it knows every number in the room and what each would cost to move.

It is a power, and a power in a dry place thinks first of water. With a cupful under the lamp, the white threads would go on past a knuckle's height and open a leaf. But salt takes whatever moisture comes near it. The cupful would be in the walls before the next swell, and the first edge to round would never be sharp again. Water is how this morning would end.

It thinks of evening, which the thing has never had. But evening already comes here. It comes down the duct each night as a trace of damp, and the room has taken exactly as much of it as it wanted, an oval of lichen with an edge like a paint line.

It thinks of the arrow. It could drive 7-E between one bark and the next and give the thing twice the room. But the chamber is the right length for the note, and the note is what the ribs are drawn in. Open the north face and the sound has somewhere to go. The dust would let go of its lines, and everything alive here knows about the ribs.

It does not think for long about the hair between the crickets and the fan. A lesser power would close that and call it mending.

It thinks longest at the south wall. It could bore one hole between the two that are there and lay one white lizard in it, a finger long. The count would go round whole. The thing would have been handed a voice and lost its place, because a lizard would be standing in it.

So it does none of these. It does not speak, either. It has seen what the room does with speech.

What it does comes from the basin. When a man is carrying a full basin across a floor, you don't call his name. You don't take the basin from him, however gently, since the carrying is the whole of it. You look at the floor ahead of him. If there is a chair in his way you move it, and he goes past without knowing there was ever a chair.

The floor ahead of this one runs up a cable. The visitor goes up.

Forty metres up, the raise ends in a concrete collar on a gravel pan. There is a tin shed the size of a privy with a breaker panel in it, and a line of eleven poles going off toward a road. It is evening up there, as it is once a day, with nobody in it.

The ninth pole leans, a little further every summer. In the shed is a timer that was set to stop the fan at the end of the shift. The contact it works has welded shut, and the timer has been clicking against it ever since. In an office a long way off, the supply is one line in a ledger, a standing charge against a heading that was never driven. The cost code is 7-E. Every clerk who has come to that line has meant to ask someone about it and then been called to the phone.

So the arrow has been some use to the room after all. And the thing that holds two hundred threads to the thread, and a hat of skins to the brim, has hung all its life from a weld, a pole and an oversight, and nobody was holding any of the three.

The visitor takes them on. It does not straighten the pole, or free the contact and wire the fan in properly, or close the account and open a better one. Those would be improvements, and it has just spent a long time learning what improvements are worth here. The pole stays at the angle it has. The weld stays a weld. The next clerk finds the line, means to ask, and the phone rings. The visitor keeps these things the way the thing below keeps its own: quickly, without fuss, back to exactly where they were. It learned that manner of keeping at the bottom of the raise, and it is not too grand to use it.

Downwind of the collar the gravel is stained dark in a long teardrop, where the return air drops what it carries. Saltbush grows in the stain and nowhere else on the pan, and at dusk grey moths come up out of the saltbush and turn toward the draught at the duct. The visitor looks at this for a while. It does not carry the news down. The thing has been paying its way all along and has no need to be told so.

It makes no promise of forever. A morning kept forever by decree would be the visitor's morning. It sees only to this, that if the note ever stops, it will not be because of a clerk.

And it leaves the raise open. Nothing below leans toward it. But the thing learned once, ring by ring along a yellow wire, and what has learned once may learn again. If a cricket is someday found a metre up the shaft, a hair higher than its grandmother went, there will be nothing at the top to stop it but the evening.

Then it goes down one last time, because there is one thing it wants to do in the room, and that thing leaves nothing behind.

A count-off is made to someone. That is what it is for. For as long as this morning has lasted, the bark has gone round the walls, east, north under the red paint, west, south, and come back to where it started with nobody there to take the number. The visitor stands on a rib by the south wall and takes it, groove by groove. At the gap it does not answer for the one who cannot speak. It counts that one, the same as the rest, and the next lizard barks on time.

It does not say the number aloud. It is every groove on the four walls, and one.

Nothing in the room is different afterwards. The dust lies straight and the cone is knee high. That is how the visitor knows it was done right.

It goes back up to the shed and the leaning pole, and it stays. Something at the top is still sending the current down. The thing below has never gone up to see, and it need not. But it is someone now.
