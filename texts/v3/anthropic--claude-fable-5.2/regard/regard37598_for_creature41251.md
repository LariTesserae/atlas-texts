The power comes up through the cloud floor on a still afternoon and stops at the edge of the flow, because it sees at once that the first problem is how to be here at all.

It is used to arriving. Elsewhere its attention falls on a place like weather, and things lean toward it. Here a shadow across the roofs would cost the rosettes an hour of light, and a footfall would shake the ash out of the spiders' flannel. So it makes itself smaller than it has been in a long time. It casts no shade and moves no air. It takes up a place a body-length past the boundary, where the old makers rake glass-hair at night, and it does what a wise thing does first, which is nothing, for long enough to learn what it is looking at.

That takes twenty-some years, the time it takes to see everything the town does at least once. It learns the files and the ramps. It learns which low, plain tower is the one that matters, and watches her walk west several times. Somewhere in the second decade it understands that it has been miscounting. It had taken this for a city of small lives. There is one life here. She is sixty paces long, she lives on ash and moths and the breath of the rock, and she is walking west at about a tower a year. The makers are how she keeps her water moving, and so are the geckos.

The power has things it could give, and it goes through them honestly.

Rain goes first. One shower would be four hundred wounds at once. The board would slump, the vanes would sag shut, and the makers would mass on every stain and seal until they dropped. The town is built from the fact of scarcity. Plenty would be a gift to some other town, standing on the ruins of this one.

It could rekindle the eastern seam. But a town that sheds nothing must either stop beginning or learn to grow, and the mother lays to a count that has no room in it. Also, the finches nest in the ruins.

It leaves the geckos alone. It checked the ledger, and the ledger was right.

The old ones are hardest. It could thicken their wax with a thought. But their lightness is what the western end is built with, and a maker with more to lose would not go where the rock is fiercest. One early morning it follows one in from the flow and up a ramp, and stays beside it while it stops, facing uphill. It finds nothing there to correct. It stays until the beetles come.

What unsettles the power is that nothing asks. It is used to a certain lean in things, an upturned face. The town is neither proud nor suffering in silence. It is exactly the size of what it has, and there is nowhere in it to put help.

So the power settles for manners. When two meet here, they touch faces and one gives the other a bead, and that is most of what passes for speech. One dusk, among the moths coming down like lint, there is one more than the mountain sent. A finch takes it off a rim. By morning what was in it is in a roof garden, under resin. A moth's worth of water is about one bead. That is everything the power gives the town, and it is not aid. It is hello. The bead is still in there somewhere, going round.

Once, possibly, it also withheld something. In a wet year the white floor climbed the cones for three days and stopped a hand below the saddle. It might have stopped there anyway. The power does not look into this.

The flight is another matter, because there the ledger is open on one side.

The town keeps everything except two things: its past, which frays off the eastern end into ash, and its winged ones. Nothing in the town is old enough to remember the first, or can see far enough to follow the second. The power can do both. So it takes that as its work, the keeping of what the town lets go.

It knows where the first ring stood. It goes there sometimes.

And on the still evening it goes out with the fliers. It does not steer them. It has looked at the mountain and knows where the other seams are, and how few. But the town has always paid these odds, and the town is itself the proof that they sometimes pay out. So it goes with each one as far as she goes. Most that the finches miss come down on the clinker within the hour, and it stays with each of those until she is dry as chaff, which up here does not take long. It could not say what good this does. It does it anyway.

It has gone out with three flights now. Twice, one of them found a crack that was warm. She bit her wings off at the root, laid her ring, set her lentil of green, and lay down with her face to the rock. Both times the power lay down too, as far as it is able, and waited with her.

This is the only place on the mountain where it has had to hold itself still by force. It could breathe for the rock. A thread of vapor through a hairline crack is nothing. But a town cannot stand on one breath. It would need the next, and the one after, for as many centuries as it has towers. By then the power would be the seam, and whatever walked west along it would be a thing it kept.

Those two cracks were only warm. It stayed to the end both times, and a while after.

Tonight is the third. The ring is a few courses high and the rosette is in it. She has her face to the rock. The power is beside her. It has given one moth and withheld everything else, and it does not know which kind of crack this is. It could look. It has declined to, so that one of them is not waiting alone.

The flow gives a single tick as it cools. Then nothing, for hours.

Toward morning the rock breathes.

She does not lift her head. After a while she begins to chew.

The power stays until the ring is a finger high. Then it goes back to its place past the boundary, where the files have not broken step.
