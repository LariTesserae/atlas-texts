Something has noticed the grove. It is older than the rock, well disposed, and able to do more or less as it likes. It arrives without announcement. The jackdaws, who miss nothing, miss it.

It starts by slowing down. At a walker's pace there is nothing here to help, only a tree, standing, and a noise. So it slows until a year passes like one of those breaths that just shows. At that speed the trunks can be seen to thicken, the litter rises like dough, and the bough under the skirt is plainly in the act of getting up. It listens at every pitch, hears the goldcrest, and feels the hopper train the way the voles do. For a while it lies in the rock and takes the crusher from the yew's end.

Then it goes through what could be done.

It takes the crusher first, as anyone would. A seized bearing would do it, or a small fall in the price of roadstone. It asks who in the grove would be better off. The yew is deaf, and the jackdaws never changed a note. The goldcrest, the wren and the robin pitched their songs over the roar so long ago that those are simply their songs now. The only parties troubled are the walker who stoops in under the bough expecting the quiet the place appears to have, and the visitor itself. It recognises the wish as its own. There are also eleven men at the quarry, and a great many roads. The crusher runs on.

It looks for a long time at the fungus, which on paper is the villain, since it ate the parent, stump and all. But the jackdaws hold their rooms from it, and so does the owl. Inside each hollow trunk the wrist-thick root comes down through the red crumble, feeding. The tree has been living on its lodger's leavings since before there were jackdaws to move in. The fungus stays.

It could know the tree's age, and in fact it does. It saw which bird carried the seed, in which wet autumn, and it has the count of years since. It tells no one. The tree has no use for the number, and the people who would like it would put up a sign. Nothing in here is made.

It follows the rock out under the fields to the quarry face and looks at how the thing will go. The face moves a few paces a year, and not this way. The permission runs out before the stone does. Then the pumps are switched off, the hole fills, and by the time the bough under the skirt has its tip properly upright, the yew will be holding the other end of a lake. Nothing needs doing there, because the tree's slowness has already seen to it.

That leaves one thing. The tree is poison in needle, bark, wood and seed, and everything about it says keep off, except for the red cup. The cup is the only request it has ever made. It has made it every autumn for longer than the parish has had a name: take this somewhere. The thrushes and the badger have always obliged, and the answer has always been the plough.

So the visitor looks for unploughed ground within a thrush's flight. The hedge bottoms are flailed every other winter. But the steel towers in the next field stand on four concrete feet each, and the square of ground between the feet is left alone for the same reason the grove is: there is something hard in the way, and the plough goes around. Inside the nearest square are nettles and one bramble, which some blackbird planted. No bramble can live on the grove's floor. Out here it is the only thing a rabbit will not push through.

On a November morning a mistle thrush is driven off the fruiting arc by another, rattling. Instead of going to the fence wire, it crosses to the tower and sits on the lowest steel, over the bramble, for as long as it takes. That is the whole of the intervention, and it would be hard to prove there was one. Thrushes sit on towers.

The seed lies two winters, as yew seed does. Then it comes up inside the thorns, two soft leaves first and afterwards the needles. When the air is wet the line fizzes its one low note directly overhead, and the seedling hears that exactly as well as its parent hears the crusher. Men come every few years to cut whatever threatens the wires. At a yew's rate of climb that will be another century's problem, and the visitor makes no arrangement about it. What the tree asked for was a chance, and it has been given one.

The visitor does not choose whether the seedling will smoke or fruit. If it fruits, then one dry February the pollen that has only ever crossed forty paces of moss to settle on its own far side will cross a field and land on someone else.

Then it goes back under the skirt, to the bough with roots as fine as hair. Nobody has recorded that bough, and the visitor makes no record either. It watches. It is the one thing anywhere that is slow enough to see this tree walk, and at that speed the sight is unmistakable. A single seed was dropped on rock, and a ring has been travelling outward from it ever since, like the ring from a stone dropped in water, with nothing left in the middle but light. A step takes several human lifetimes. The visitor has them to spare, and stays for this one.

The jackdaws come in at dusk and go through the matter of who sleeps where. It comes out the same. Across the fields the plant runs on through the night, and the visitor lets it.
