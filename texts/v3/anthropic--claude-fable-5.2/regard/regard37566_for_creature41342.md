It came in along the causeway, since there is no other way in, and for a long time it did nothing but go round with her. It had no coat for the drizzle to hang on. The rat files didn't open for it and the starlings got nothing off it. It kept count of the rounds the only way the pan allows, odd and even, and so lost count the way she has.

It could have done a great deal, and it went down the list.

- It could have breached the bank. The sea would have been back within the afternoon. But there are four thousand starlings on the wires and frogs in every drain mouth, and each one is at home.
- It could have stopped the engine. She would have stumbled.
- It could have sent the afternoon on toward evening. The lamps would have been wanted, the check would have gone back on its hook, and she would have been carried out of her turn the way the belt carries a gull, facing forward.
- It could have put tea in the mug. It thought about this longer than it thought about the bank. Tea runs out, and afterward the hot water would have something missing from it, which it does not have now.

It watched the hopper longest. Nothing drives the gull. It stands a long time on the weighbridge roof and then walks down. That may be a horror, or it may be how an ending is managed where there is no earth to put anything in. It couldn't tell which, and it wasn't willing to mend what it couldn't read. It left the count alone.

Her it could read a little. She has three coughs. She carries the mug home with her hand flat over the top. She keeps a clinker the works knows nothing about. Each round she looks out along the pylons for exactly as long as the valve gives her. A person who has kept one thing back from four thousand mimics is not worn all the way through.

And twice in each round she comes up against something that was left badly. One is a rule, which says the turn ends when the lamps are wanted, and nobody told her whose wanting counts. The other is a question on the telephone that has only ever had one answer, and she gave it until the wires could give it for her.

So it went back out along the pylons to find the far end of the line. This was a room with a switchboard, a chair pushed back from it, and a ringer turning on a cam. Nobody had been asking for a long time. The asking had been left running.

It walked on past that, too, because it wasn't going to offer a door that opened onto nothing. The causeway comes to a road. Along the road there are windows, and at a certain hour they are lit because it has got dark.

Then it sat down in the chair, lifted the cam off, and rang by hand while she was standing on the plate.

In the pan it went as always, nearly. The telephone rang. The wires rang back. "Weighbridge," said the wires, and held the gap, and said "Same." The telephone went on ringing. That was the whole of what it changed inside the bank: one call the starlings couldn't take.

She stood through six strokes of the engine. Then she stepped off the plate, the steelyard clicked down, and she went in. She said "Weighbridge." The wires said it after her and then had to wait like anyone else.

The earpiece is the one sound in the pan the wires can't reach. What it said into it was short. It said it wasn't the office. It said it had come in by the causeway and gone round with her some while without asking, and that it was sorry for that part.

Then it said: "The turn ends when the lamps are wanted. It's the day turn says when. Nobody told you. Are they wanted?" And: "If it's Same, it's Same, and I'll not ring again. But there's someone on this end now, whenever you lift it."

It meant that. Same would have been an answer, given for the first time to someone who could have heard a different one.

She held the receiver for the length of the valve. She looked at the steelyard lying on its rest. Then she spoke quietly, which she had never had cause to do, and the wires didn't get it.

"Not yet."

It said "Right," and put the cam back.

---

The rounds after that looked the same from the causeway. The clinker went left to right and right to left. She took the eel, sat at the polished place on the bench, and stopped in the doorway of the engine house. The telephone rang on its cam and the wires took the call. But at the time office she looked at the board and then at the lamps, and she knew two things the works didn't.

On one round, odd or even, she went up the stairs. The gulls gave way a tread at a time, each trade announced. The upstairs window looks over the bank. The water out there stands a few feet higher than the floor she has walked all this while, grey and level, and it goes on a long way. She looked for a good deal longer than the valve allows. Then she came down and slept at the table for as long as a mug takes to go cold.

On another round she stood on the plate, and the poise sat where it sat. She went into the office and turned the handle, which made a small sound, behind glass.

"Weighbridge," she said. "Wanted."

"Right," said the far end. "It'll be getting dark by about the fourth pylon. I'll have a light on. There's tea."

She didn't fill the mug at the boiler house. It went into the left pocket empty. At the cottage she moved the clinker with her boot to the middle of the sill, which is a third value, and the only thing she ever told the works.

She crossed to the time office on her diagonal. The check went onto the fortieth hook, blank as a washer among the thirty-nine brown ones. Half a second later the sound of a brass check going onto a hook came off every span of wire, four thousand times over, like a whole shift knocking off at once.

Then she went out along the cinders. At the second pylon the thud no longer reached her shins. She stumbled, once. After that her breath showed when it liked.

In the pan the count closed over. One more eel per round goes to the gulls. The steelyard lies on its rest. The telephone rings on its cam, and the wires say "Weighbridge," and hold the gap, and say "Same," which is true of the works. The chair at the far end is kept occupied, in case. On the sump wall there is still a gap a little wider than a gull, and the birds on either side don't shift.

From the wires, to the rats, who mind: "Mind."
