It comes down the east ramp at a changeover, since that is the place the city has for arrivals and the hour it has for events. The fox looks up and goes back to the figs.

For the first year it does nothing but walk the route a few paces behind her and listen. Hearing her is not difficult. The roar is made of a great many small sounds, and hers is one of them. Understanding takes longer. The language has gone the way of the stairs, and it learns it the way the stairs are learned, by using it as it is and not as it was cut. By the second fig crop it follows everything. The question of whether anyone could now understand her has come up after all, and has been settled, though she does not know it.

Most of what she says is accounts: how many eggs were taken, how many left, and why, which stub is closing, what the fox has done. There is a long quarrel with the old woman, still running, in which she answers things that were said to her fifty years ago and never arrived. At the edge of the plaza she says the words she has no use for anywhere else, the ones for things the city does not contain: rain, fire, a name that may once have been hers.

It considers what could be done, and most of it comes apart on inspection.

It could take her up the ramp. It walks the plateau first to see what that would mean. The people she left are gone. The nearest others are many days off, and among them she would be a deaf old woman dressed in roots, who could not be spoken to and knew the name of nothing. Here she knows the name of everything, having supplied them.

It could quiet the birds. But by number and by weight the city is theirs, and the roar is only what a hundred thousand of them sound like finding their way home.

It could mend her ears, and it looks at them closely. Both her explanations are true. The birds had most of her hearing decades ago and left her the low end of it. The roar is the real one, coming in through the chest and the teeth, and under it is a thin one of her own that would go with her up the ramp, though under open sky it would be a hiss and not a chute. To mend her ears in this place would be to let the whole of it in. It leaves them as they are.

It could see to it that a child comes down the ramp for her to find. The city has a place for one, and such places tend to get filled, but that is not a reason to fill one.

It does take the cough. She never knew she had it and will not miss it.

Then one night it goes into the room with the oval door, where she is sitting up on the leaves, talking. It sits down behind her with its back against hers and begins to talk too.

It talks in her language, with the edges off. It tells her what stands at the top of each root. The resinous one is a juniper, bent flat by the wind. The bitter one is a spurge. The sweet one in the fourth hall is a fig, the same as the ones in her groves, the only one that got out. None of this reaches her as words. It reaches her as a buzz between the shoulder blades.

She stops talking. She puts two fingers to her throat and finds it still, so the buzz is not hers. She stays like that for a long time.

Then it reaches round and taps her twice on the collarbone.

It has thought about this. The only name she has is also an instruction, and it would not give it from anywhere she would have to go. Said from here, "come here" means where she already is. It is the first time she has been called and has not had to move.

She turns, finds what there is to find in the dark, and taps back twice. It has arranged to have a collarbone. So it has the same name now and the same instruction, and it does as it is told.

---

After that there are two on the route. She closes its fingers on the flint at the right angle and moves its hand to the waist of each pillar, and it lets itself be shown.

At the plaza she stops with her toes at the first of her own prints and talks out across the floor, and it stands beside her. It knows what is out there. She is partly right. The roar does stand off in the middle, though not far, and a clap would still be lost. But a voice does not have to cross the air to reach its owner. It goes up through the jaw. With her ears as they are, what she would get of her own is the low part with the edges gone, a hum more felt than heard. It would not be a child's voice or a stranger's. She had that sound at her back for eleven years.

It cannot tell her this. There is no way to give a reason down here, and there never was. It could put a hand on her shoulder, but a hand on her shoulder at this spot has only ever meant one thing, and it does not intend to steer her out any more than past. So it stands there, on each round, for as long as she stops.

She has something to go on now, though, that she did not have before. The roar does not change when she stops her ears, and for fifty years that may have meant nothing would ever reach her again. Now something has. It comes through her back at night, and it is not gravel.

It is not that crop or the next. Then, on an ordinary round, she taps it twice on the collarbone and walks out past the thirty-first print into clean dust. It follows, since it has been told to.

In the middle she stops, where nothing has stopped before. She puts two fingers to her throat and speaks.

She stands a while with her head tilted, the way people do when someone they know is talking in another room. Then she answers it. She goes on for some time. It is the old quarrel, as far as can be made out, with both sides audible at last.

Then she goes on to the next root.

There are no beetles on that floor, and the dust keeps what is pressed into it. Two lines of prints go out to the middle and come back, and they will be legible for a long time to whoever next stops at the edge.

When the time comes it will carry her to a corner where the dust is knee-deep. It knows which ones are taken. What it does after that it has not decided. It was told to come here, and no one has said anything since about leaving.
