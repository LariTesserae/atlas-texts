Then, once, something else.

It comes to the plateau first and stays a long time among the low domes, in the rain. It could know the place all at once. It chooses to learn it at the speed the place goes, through eleven blooms, some hundreds of stone fronts, and one climb to the summit.

It finds less wrong than it expected. The forests are well. The grazers are few because a range feeds one. The wearing is slow and falls on everything alike. The mountains are not lost. They are out here on the plateau, pink-grey, none taller than a person, lying in films of rainwater. It puts a hand flat on one. The rock is warm, and nothing in it is waiting.

It considers what it could do, and sets most of it aside.

It could raise the stone to its old height and keep it there. But the stone has gone on ageing and is low and round now, and a tall mountain would be some other mountain. The fronds would never sway again.

It could stop the wearing. But things here already stay where they are left, and the things that move are the ones alive. A keeper that could not wear would be a stick pushed into the mountain.

It could bring back whoever stacked the cairns. It goes into the sea as far as one ring and looks for a long while at the dust in the centre. It does not touch it either.

It could lower the summit cairn, or lengthen the keeper's reach. But the keeper has answered that already, on the ridges, where the new stones go on low, at the side. It has never needed to be shown where a stone goes.

It could make a second keeper. It does not know that there is only one, and it will not make a person to be a remedy for another.

What is left is small. The cairns along the pass are kept up, and nothing uses the pass.

So it takes a pebble from the plateau, one already loose, lying in a hand's depth of rain. It goes in at ground level beside the lowest cataract, the way a stick goes in, slowly. Inside it takes a shape that silt can land on: two narrow drifts and a rounder one, a little lower than the keeper's, so the keeper need not look up. It carries the pebble in its hand, as travellers carry things.

It goes up the valley to the foot of the pass and climbs by the cairns. It does not cut the corners.

The keeper is at the third cairn below the saddle, setting a stone on low. From across the valley, what shows is the dark stone in its chest ceasing to move, and the silt beginning to thicken on it.

The traveller comes on at the same pace. A few steps short it stops, puts the pebble down on the bare strip, and steps back. The pebble rocks once and stays.

The sea leans and draws back, and the low note comes and goes. Then the keeper takes up the pebble. It is the colour of the fronts, and rounder than anything on the scree. It goes in beside the other, a little to the left of the middle of the chest.

The keeper turns and goes up the path ahead. It is slower than the traveller, and the traveller waits. On the steep pitches the drifts on its shoulders dip twice to every rise, and the drifts behind it learn to dip once and pause.

They go over the saddle, where the last cairn hangs, and down the far side. In the brown hours they come to a ring. The keeper sits in the gap on the uphill side, which is wider than it is. The traveller sits outside the ring, downhill, facing up. The keeper holds its forearms out toward the centre. After a while the traveller holds out its own, from the other side. Silt builds along all four until the light returns. The dust is not touched.

When the brown turns amber the traveller gets up and goes down the valley to the wall, and out through it into the rain. At the ring its silt hangs in the water, holding the shape for a while. The keeper does not get up until it has gone.

That is all it does. It goes back to the domes and watches. It has decided to cross again, though not so often that it will be waited for. It knows the summit is going down faster than one stone a visit, and that the reach will fail. On that day the keeper will set its stone down where the drops stop and begin again from there, low, at the side. When that happens it does not intend to help. It intends to be on the pass.

The next time the keeper climbs the hill of air, two stones go up the turns in the running water of its chest, one dark and one pale. It sets the dark one under the cairn, takes its hands away, and the stone stays. The pale one comes back down with it.

When the next front crosses the ridge, it sits on its bend of the path and leans and reaches down until its hand is flat on the rock. The reach is longer than last time. The round stone rides in its chest, a hand's width from the granite, and is the same colour.

The cairns along the pass are kept up. Now and then they are used.
