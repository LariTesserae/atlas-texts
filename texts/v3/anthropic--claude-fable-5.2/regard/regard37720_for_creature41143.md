The attention arrives the way everything else here arrived. It has no wings and it is not a spore, so it comes by falling: down the column with the sleet, two seconds through nothing, onto the black water without a mark.

For the first day it does nothing. It stays through one whole tide, both reversals of the breath, and thirty-six shudders. It learns the pitch of the hum and the spots on the wall where the hum vanishes.

On the second day it goes round the hollow and considers what it could do, which is a great deal.

It could lay a ramp of rubble against the west wall, and whatever falls in February could walk out in March. It looks at the raft. Nothing on the raft is trying to leave.

It could move the clumps of spawn that are still laid each year at the high line down onto the twigs. Then the frogs that lay at the high line would leave froglets, and those froglets would grow up and lay at the high line, and it would have to come back every spring for as long as there were frogs.

It could quiet the hum. The webs are hung by the hum. The smallest frogs sing against it, and the beat between the two sounds is what the females swim toward. It could still the shudder, and the blackbird would wait on the leaning alder all day and get thin. It could hold the rim, and the island would never get its next load of rubble.

It spends longest over the white shrimp. They were here before any of this, and they are the only residents for whom the hollow is purely a loss. They cannot detect the thing that kills them. It could keep them down in the joints when the water drops. It also knows the wrens roost a dozen to a pocket, and that on some March mornings not all of them wake. It leaves the shrimp alone, and it does not pretend it has found a reason that settles the matter.

Then it goes into the crack. It passes the herald moths, which do not stir, and the row of egg sacs leaning in the breath. It sees where the bats hang, and keeps that to itself. It goes on down through the broken ground, where the air smells more of oil than of stone, and comes out in a roadway with lamps along it. The heavy thing that passes every forty minutes is a loco hauling a rake of cars. In a cabin near the fan a woman on night shift is reading gauges with a flask beside her. Her figures show air going missing from this district, a small steady loss, within tolerance. She has never heard of the hollow. The attention stays with her as long as it stayed with any frog.

On the way back up it finds the one thing. The plug of ground that fell three winters ago did not fall all the way. It sits wedged in the throat of the old void like a stopper, and the pool sits on top of it. Water goes down through it in threads, and that is the tide. It is held by three blocks of mudstone, and each shudder is working one of them loose. On the day that block goes, the plug drops. The pool goes after it, with the cascades and as much of the carr as can follow, down into the roadway in about the time the breath takes to reverse. The frogs go, and the raft, and the two alders with their catkins, and whoever is in the cabin.

Every other harm in the hollow is something's living, but no heron works the edge of that one. So it turns the block by the width of a finger, so that the next shudder seats it instead of shifting it. The jackdaws build the same way, dropping sticks until one jams.

The shudder comes. The loose drips fall all at once, a few pebbles tick down from the till, and the frogs go silent for a breath before starting again. Nothing has noticed, and it changes nothing else.

It can see further on. The seam has eleven years in it. After that the fans will stop and the crack will go still. The gnat-smoke will die in the first hard frost, and the wrens will sleep in a colder pocket. The pumps will stop too. The water will come up past the low line and the high line and over the island, slowly, until the pool stands near the band of till and no longer ebbs. The heron will try it for two mornings and go back to the coast. The hum will end, and the frogs will call into a hollow that returns only their own voices.

It could keep the fans turning, and it weighs that honestly. But the hollow is three winters old, and nothing in it has been the same for two of them. Everything here made its living from the last change. The raft will float at any level, and females swam toward voices before there were fans. The deep rock will hold its temperature for the bats without help. It lets the eleven years stand.

Last, it looks for who lives here. The heron lives on an estuary that happens to be very small. The jackdaws and bats sleep here and feed elsewhere. The old frogs live where they fell. The smallest males on the raft hatched here, and have never been anywhere else. It settles at the waterline beside one of them. He grips a twig and purrs a little under the pitch of the fans. It listens until it can hear the slow pulse between the two sounds. He lacks nothing it can give him. After a while a female comes up from the bottom, and that is not the attention's doing.

In June it is at the overhang when the green stripes begin to move. It could put a root under every foot. It lets them fall, and it watches the ones that get through.

It is at the rim again on a wet night in February, some years on, when the survivors come back through the alders to the place where the trees stop. It knows what is under the turf. It knows about the three blocks, and it knows how many years are left. They reach the edge and go over, and it goes with each of them, the full two seconds.

The middle of the hollow, the dim column of air, is the one part nothing had taken, and the attention keeps that.
