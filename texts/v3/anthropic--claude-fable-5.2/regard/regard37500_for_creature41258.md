Something else comes to the parapet, after you. It hears the place before it looks, as you did.

It is the kind of thing that can hear everything at once, and from the rim it does: each mallet, each quern, some thousands of mouths, the toads breathing in the bottom course. All of it at once turns out to be a roar. This is what a stranger hears, and there is a woman below who could have told it so. So it goes down the stair. It has no hand, but the groove is there, and it keeps to the groove.

By the fourth course it can tell mallets from querns. At about the level where the sparrows start, something comes down in it that it had not known it was holding up.

It came to do good, and it looks round for where to begin.

The children under five bleed a little on the floor. It could blunt the sherds. But a blunt floor does not pack and does not clink, and by five the soles are like hide, and it is this floor that makes them so. It leaves the floor.

On windless days the yard goes wrong. It could see to it that the wind never failed. But they do not hear the hum, only its absence, and a hum that was never absent would be heard by no one. It leaves the still days in.

The kestrel drops past the rim with its wings shut. It holds still for one breath with everyone else.

It is not holding back on principle. On the north wall's third gallery a lashing has gone grey and thin where the gourd vine covers it, and children run there. A man carrying water looks up, for no reason he could give, and by evening there is new rope. A wick is moved a hand's width from a mat. That is about the size of thing that will go through a mouth here, and when there is something that size to do, it does it.

Then it watches long enough, and finds the woman whose name is a fingernail flicked off the thumb.

When the wind is in the south it listens nineteen courses down on the east wall, behind the gecko. The jar is sounding. She does hear it. It considers how she might be told. By mouth it would need a mouth, and breath, and onion. By hand it would be public, and she has chosen not to swear to this with her hands. What she has is the size she wants it. So it tells her nothing, and when the wind is in the south they both listen.

It listens, too, to the jar under her daughter's sleeping mats, without flicking it. It is sharp. Whether it is too sharp cannot be heard yet, because that will depend on courses not yet laid. It does know something she does not. Her own jar was found once, wrapped in its mat, and flicked through the matting by a woman who thought it flat and never said so by mouth or by hand. Small children still run to the wrong door. The yard learned the pitch in an evening all the same. It keeps this to itself as well.

Then it goes back up the stair, because the one thing wrong with the yard cannot be heard from the bottom.

The kiln eats thorn every six or seven days. The women who go for it walk a little farther than their mothers did. Nobody has measured the difference. Up there they have shadows and no hum, and they think only of getting back to the groove. A sour note warns them of dust, but nothing in the chord warns them of this. The thorn is going about as fast as the floor is rising, and from inside the yard that looks like nothing at all.

Here it acts. Pods shake out of the bundles on the way home and lie along the track. It makes nothing. When the rain comes, a few more of them take than would have, out on the far stretch, and a few more the year after. In a generation the thorn will be coming toward the yard about as slowly as it was leaving. It stops the seedlings well short of the parapet. Anything standing near the rim gets into the wind, and the wind gets into the chord, and it is not the one who says where things go. It is fairly sure she would refuse them.

This is the only large thing it does, and it does it out of earshot.

It means to stay, so it looks for a mouth. The new rim course is taken; a sparrow was carrying straw before the mortar dried. The pigeons have the south wall and the bats the north. It will not turn out a gecko. So it goes down past the toads, under the herringbone. The oldest courses lie there on their sides in the dark, their mouths stopped with packed sherd and sounding nothing, with the older floors and the thresholds for nothing beneath them. Nobody has those. No one in the yard has reached earth, and no one expects to. It settles there, where the earth would be. The yard was resting well enough on its own breakage and needed nothing more underneath. But nobody minds what moves in, and an empty jar would be the strange thing.

It keeps hours. It is learning the eleven pitches, more slowly than a child does. When her jar is struck it still turns, sometimes, toward the kiln household's door.

It knows which side of the rim her jar will go on. It heard this the way whoever sets the jar will have to hear it. It will not point.

On a toad night no one can be called, and that includes it. It stays wherever it was when the rain began.
