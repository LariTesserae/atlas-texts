What drew it was the lamp. From far enough away everything sets, and a light that doesn't is conspicuous. It came to see what was holding the thing up.

It could have looked from above, where the raptors turn, and seen the whole floor at once. It came in through the notch instead, past the cairn and down the path that is a foot wide, because a place this crowded can only be learned by going the way the towers allow. Nothing noticed it. The lizards under the pipe did not part.

The first thing it did was nothing, for a year. This is what she does at a flange, at a different scale: hold near and keep still for a few breaths. Its breaths are long. It went with her to the wells each morning and stood behind her at the gauges while she said the two numbers. So for that year the readings reached someone, though she had no way to know it.

It learned the rings. It learned that she has rings of her own, a distance the fox keeps from her and a much larger one the mule keeps, and that both are right. It found its own distance, which was farther than either, and stayed there.

Then it went through what it had brought, the way she once worked out what the bare steel cost her.

It could stop the scale. One word to the water, and the silica would stay dissolved through every meter of steel and drop out wherever it liked afterward. It worked out what this would cost, and the answer was her: the hammer and the bar at four o'clock, the quarter turns, the path worn a finger deep. The pale apron outside the door would stop growing, and it had noticed, as she has not, that the rain is cementing that apron into the foot of something.

It could correct the gauges. But they have been wrong for so long that she reads their error fluently. Correct gauges would lie to her for years.

It could tell her what stood on the concrete pad. It knows, and the answer is smaller than four bolts.

It could tell her how fast the little tower goes. It knows that too, to the grain. She has a wrist for it, and a gasket on a nail if she ever wants the question closed.

It could give her more years. It looked at this one longest. Nothing in the basin is sharp, because everything here wears down and builds up at the same rate, and a keeper who only built up would be the one sharp thing on the floor. She has not asked. She sleeps soundly. It left this where it was, and it is still not certain.

It could thank her. The valleys do that already, in potatoes, and she has never left anything at the cairn in reply, which it came to see was the correct reply.

Everything it had brought was lagging. The place had never missed it.

What was left to look for was anything fixing where it stood. It found two things.

She turns the three shut-in wells a quarter open and back every morning, though the far end of the wire has never asked for them. Nobody does this for her. No one has asked her a question since the drillers went over the rim. What she knows about the nine wells is in the back of her hand, and there is no nail to hang that on. And on the far side of the wall, a path climbs from the valleys to a cairn and has never once come down the other side. These were two valves on the same line, and the scale had been at both for decades.

It could see how this would go. One morning she will not wake. The lamp will outlast her by the life of one bulb, and then the star will go out and they will come up. They are not careless people, as the sacks show. They will find a turbine still turning, nine wellheads with gauges that lie, and a clipboard nobody can read. They will do their best with the gauges.

This too it could have mended from above, with a dream sent down the wire or the knowledge set whole in some valley child's hand. But nobody taught her and nobody asked her to stay, and it had come to think that was the grain of the place. It did not need anything opened. It wanted only that the valves not be seized on the day somebody tried them.

So it did one thing, at the scale of the basin. The bulb over the door had eleven days left in it. One evening, an hour after dark, when she was on the pallet with the thrum coming up through her back, it held what it has for a hand near the glass, kept still, and took them.

The star in the notch went out. In all the years the valleys had watched it, it had never been out for a night. Nothing else changed. Their own lamps burned as before, and that was how they knew down there what it wasn't. The wire was live, so the turbine was turning. The star was out, and nobody had put it back. That left one thing to conclude.

She found it at first light, going out to see the steam stand. She unwrapped a bulb from its cloth and changed it, and the lamp burned on through the morning looking like an oversight.

They came through the notch the next afternoon, out of season, three figures and no mule, moving faster than the air permits and paying for it. They passed the cairn. Below it the path was not theirs, and they came down it anyway, in single file, because it is a foot wide. She was at the bench when they filled the door. For a while nobody spoke. One of them looked up at the lamp and then at her, and she understood, and said it was the bulb.

By then the cloud had its lid on and the rain was coming, so they stayed. They stood in the shed while it drummed on the roof, three people who had lived their whole lives at the far end of this, feeling it in their boot soles. She knocked the strainer clean because it was the hour for it. The youngest of the three, a girl who had been brought only because she refused to be left, watched the flakes come off.

When the field shone, the woman took up her cans, and the girl took one from her without asking and carried it. At the pool the girl bent to fill it at the margin, shoulder to shoulder with the birds.

"Farther in," the woman said.

The girl asked how far. The woman had no answer, because no one had ever needed one. She took the girl's wrist and held the back of the girl's hand out over the water, moved it, and stopped. They kept still for a few breaths. Then the girl dipped the can there.

At dusk the rim came back. The shed floor deepened under them, and the governor opened on its own. All three of them looked at it.

"That's you," she said. "That's your lamps."

They slept on the concrete, placed wrong, too near the pedestal or too far. Only the girl shifted twice and then lay still. In the morning, when the steam was standing straight, they went up through the notch.

The next sacks came at the usual time with the usual things in them. But the mule stood alone at the cairn for most of an hour, and the ash below it showed three sets of prints going down and coming back. One of the sets was small.

That is all that has happened. The girl has not stayed. Nobody has asked her to, and the power will not. It may be that she never does, and that some other morning goes the way it foresaw. It turned the thing a quarter open and let it come back. The woman says the readings as before, once, in a level voice. On the days the mule comes, the voice reaches the door.

It is not sure about the eleven days. They cost a valley a night's sleep and three people a hard, frightened climb. It weighs that against a girl's wrist held over a pool, and the scale does not settle.

It has not gone. Past the birds, where the rings of neighboring towers fail to meet, there are the cushions, each with its resident. It keeps to a gap like that, one with no cushion in it. Now and then it wakes, for no reason it could give. It comes in close, holds what it has for a hand near her sleeping back, and keeps still. Then it gives a quarter turn, or doesn't.
