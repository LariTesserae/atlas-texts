It does nothing for a year.

A power can attend without being anywhere, and that is how it attends, through one dark layer and one pale. It learns the bands and counts the knocks. It watches her go to the last pit in the hungry months and come back with her fingertips split. It watches her at the wall before dawn with the walkers standing over her hands.

It could do a great deal, and it goes through the possibilities one at a time.

It could bring water. A seep at the back wall would be enough. But the chamber is dry the way a book is dry. After one wet season the litter would bloom and the felt would go to slime. The hundreds of layers, with everyone who ever stood at the strip pressed into them, would be gone within the year.

It could quiet the crack. But the knock is what slides the bands together, and the sliding is what puts lizards in the pits. In a steady chamber everything would keep to its place and starve there.

It could spare the moths, who are the only food, or the lizards, who are the only water. There is no one in the chamber it can favor except at the cost of someone else. It finds that it does not love the woman more than it loves the bead animals on the hot crust, though she is the only one of them it can ask anything.

So it leaves the chamber alone. The chamber is not broken.

It could carry her home. It goes down to look at home first. The field is there and has roots in it, and other people work it. At the field's edge stand several upright stones for those who went up after the moths and did not come back, and one of them is hers. The village has closed over the place where she was, the way the wall closes over a handprint. No one is waiting. That is not the same as being unwelcome, but the power cannot tell which it would be. A body that feels the absence of fever as cold would have to find out in open air, in daylight, with its eyes.

It learns her name down there. A child has it now.

At the end of the year, this is what it knows. It cannot tell whether she is unhappy, and it doubts the word fits through the slot. She is skilled. She is fed in the dark years. She has the first pit, which is not for lizards. But two things are true of her that are true of nothing else in the chamber. No one has ever asked her anything. And the way out is a day's walk without water, for a body the weight of a child, past three cairns that say how that goes. Not following the moths has never been something she does. It is only something that happens each year when the cold breaks.

So she needs a door and a question. The power decides to make nothing new. Everything made here was made by people who meant to go home, and she can read all of it.

It restacks the wall. It does this by day, while she is inside, using flat stones from the ridge and no mortar. It takes a body for the work, because the wall was made by hands and she will know the difference. The next dawn the rime runs the full width of the slot. She kneels in the lee and the lee is wider. Her hand goes along the top course, stops where the slump used to begin, and goes on. Stones do not stack themselves. She drinks, and so do the walkers.

It makes a cake. It takes moths from the wall just inside the mouth, where the patch closes behind its hand. It rubs off the wings, grinds the bodies in the dish, presses the meal flat and leaves it there. A cake is for carrying. That is the whole question, and she can answer it by leaving the cake to the beetles.

Then there is the hand. It keeps the body. When the knock comes it is standing at the fourth pace, a little off the wall, and she goes past it and in. She comes out ahead of the heat with her right hand on the strip and two lizards in her left, the cloth sound going along beside her. At the first pit her fingers go in, and there is a finger in it already.

She does what she does with anything found in a pit. She has it before the moths above have stirred.

It lets itself be held. The grip that would have gone to a head finds a knuckle, a nail, a second knuckle, the web of a thumb. The front comes up the chamber behind her. The moths walk past them both toward the mouth. Her breath goes from twelve a minute to twenty. She does not open her eyes. She lets go, walks to her place and eats, the wet parts first.

At the next knock it is standing where it stood.

It has made itself one rule about this. It will not begin what it is not prepared to keep. A touch given once and withdrawn would be the worst thing in the chamber, worse than the last pit. So the finger will be in the first pit at every knock, for as long as she goes in and comes out. In the pale years it will walk the far end of the strip, where a palm can stay for one breath, and bring back what is there. If she goes down, it will go beside her carrying water, as slowly as she needs. It will stand with her at the edge of the field while she learns what kind of place has closed over. If she stays, it stays. The moths will roost on anything that keeps still. That may be a great many years, and it has them.

It does not know which she will do. It has looked in itself for a preference and not found one.

For three mornings she kneels an arm's length from the dish. On the fourth the dish is empty, and the beetles have not had what was in it. At the fourth pace, pressed into the first pit, is a piece of the cake the size of a fingertip. The rest she has eaten or kept, and the power does not go looking to learn which.

She has carried something somewhere. It was only four paces.

The power eats what was left for it. It tastes of nuts and ash.

The cold has not broken yet. When it does, she will stand in the slot for three or four nights with the moths going out past her face. The power will stand a little way down the ridge, by the first cairn, with water and with her name, in case she wants either.
