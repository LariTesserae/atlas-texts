The power notices the town from a long way off and could be in the square at once. It walks instead. It starts far out on the flat, where the paste has no stones in it, and comes in over most of a day along the line the road will take, so that its arrival has a morning behind it. Its feet leave prints, and the prints come after the feet. The plovers lift ahead of it and settle again behind.

The boy who was delivered to run messages sees it from the gap in the kerb. He runs the length of the long row to say that someone is coming. It is the first message he has carried, and he gets there out of breath, in that order.

It comes in past the post with the empty bracket and does not read the beads. It walks the lanes with its shoulders turned. It hears a casing tick. On the middle bridge it rests a hand where the rail will be, and the hand comes away wet.

It could settle the account. That is the first thing a power thinks of, and it thinks of it. Eleven thousand years could be seated under the square by nightfall, and with them the wedding, the roof, the Marren boat, and the sea. That would be one more delivery, and these people have had a delivery. Everything since they have got a year at a time, coughing when a cough is owed and washing one cup from both sides. They have made of this a way of living that is careful and decent, and it belongs to them more than anything they were given. The debt is being paid. It leaves it to be paid.

It stops at smaller things too. The woman on the south side passes with a pan in both hands. A touch at the elbow would clear four years. It keeps its hands in its pockets, because the pan is hers to drop. By the east bridge it looks for a while at the coat on the peg. The widow has started on the man herself, from the cuff, and has already got further than the schedule. It leaves that as well.

Then it goes to the north-east corner. It has walked since morning and it is tired, having arranged to be, and it leans on the wall beside his door. The children on the steps watch this. The wall is cold and holds it up.

He comes out and asks, civilly, whether it has come to see its ground. It says it has no ground here, and asks to see his.

His four cores are at the end of the last row. He says, before it can, that they are paste from top to bottom. It asks which end is the top. He shows it. It takes the latest to the window, where the light is on time.

At the top end there is a skin thinner than a fingernail, darker than the rest. Two or three pale threads run down from it into the grey. He says that is only dirt. The visitor agrees that it is dirt.

It lays the four cores side by side with the tops level. The first has nothing. The second has a stain. The third has a line. On the fourth the line has roots in it.

Everyone in the town reads a core from the bottom, because that is where things arrive. He drilled these himself and read them the same way. The casing by his door counts what comes up the duct, and nothing has come up his. This came down, from the sedge and from four years of rain that fell when it fell. The casing has no window for that.

He looks for some time. He has nothing fine enough to measure the line with, and the visitor can see him begin to think about what he would need to make.

It asks him what no one has asked, which he expects, the tick or the collecting. He says he expected the tick the first year and the collecting the second, and since then he has been busy.

It tells him that nobody is going to collect him. It does not say how it knows. Before it set out it went to where the orders are kept and saw to this. That is one of the two places where it used its strength, and nothing in the town looks any different for it.

He asks then whether his years are coming. It says yes, one a year, and four are in.

The other place was the sea. The order stands, and it did not touch the date. It added a line, that the first tide is to come in the way tides do, from far out over the flat, in plain sight, no faster than a person walks. The hare will not act on water that has not come. It will act on water that is coming, if the water gives it time.

In the afternoon the lanes go dark. The washing goes in, line by line. His apron stays pale. He sits on his top step and the visitor sits on the one below, which is sharp. The hare comes up at dusk and crops along the edge of the apron, and gives the visitor no more attention than it gives the kerb.

Then the rain arrives, and it falls on the three of them when it falls on the plain.

The visitor leaves before dark, by the gap, on foot. It leaves nothing in the town. Out on the flat its prints fill in the order they were made.

In the front room at the north-east corner the four cores have been turned round in their row, so that the tops are toward the window. The gauge reads zero. He is on the step in the last of the light, making something to measure with. The first one is crooked.
