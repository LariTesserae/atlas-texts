So the first thing is to stop.

You are warm and you breathe, and the curl inside the pale has found you. If you stay as you are, she will open toward you. She will let herself down the thread headfirst and reach you before she reaches anything else, and you would not heave. You could feed her as long as she liked and be no thinner. The sleepers could be woken. The gourds would go uneaten and the frog would keep its voice.

And she would harden into something large and well fed that had never eaten a room. She would not want wet clay or look for a rope. Behind the leaf the paddle would stop in the air, because there would never have been anyone to lift it.

So you let your warmth go into the wall, which takes it and keeps it. Then you are only as hot as everything else here, hot the way the room is high. You stop breathing. You never needed to. The curl goes on turning toward you a while, since nothing here settles fast. Then it turns back toward the pot feet.

That means you have chosen the caterpillars for her. You don't pretend otherwise.

You get down among the jars. They lie head to tail, tight and cool. A pulse travels down a back. You can't tell from outside whether anyone is awake behind a pulse like that, and here you can't find out by looking, because looking is a thumb. But if they are already gone under and you treat them as though they might not be, nothing is lost. If they are awake in there and you treat them as gone, they pay for it, one at a time, in the dark. So you touch each one once, behind the head, and press in a single fact: the sleep goes all the way down. It is the only mark you make on purpose. You leave the print broad, so it can't be taken for hers.

Then the wasp. You lay your hand flat on the wall below the blowhole and leave it. She comes backward along the rim, drawing the pellet out, and walks across your knuckles as she would across anything. Her feet are cold. When she steps off they are less cold. That is all. You don't slow her. The hurry is hers and she has reason for it. But the next slap of the paddle lands exactly when the wall shivers, once. Then it is early again.

It rains. The frog calls, and the jar of plain water says it back. You answer too, from no jar, in a voice that is not the frog's and doesn't try to be. A vessel says back what is said into it. Something, once, should say something else.

The frog is silent for three rains. Then it calls again, and the call has changed at the end. It lifts. You don't answer. Once is a fact. Twice would be a firebox, and the room would lean toward you the way the shoots lean, pale, breaking their own skin to do it.

The mountain you leave alone. You could put it in between one rain and the next, rock to the root. It would be the only found thing here, and everything she laid on it afterward would rest on you. Besides, the height doesn't need it. The height was made first, and it holds. If you are careful you can tell what needs holding up from what only frightens you to look at.

But you check. You go down the rope, past the root tips furred white, through the cloud, a long way. At the bottom there is a river, brown and slow. In its bank is a scoop as wide as a field, where someone pinched out a sample to test for grit. In the scoop is a thumb whorl you could walk the ridges of. She is whatever size the work is. The mountain is in hand. You go back up.

Then comes the part you stayed for. The last course goes on. She closes the blowhole from outside with one pellet, and the note stops.

You are outside by then too. You don't go in. You put your palm on the outer wall and keep it there, in air that can't be breathed, a finger of mud between you and her. You hear it through the clay. The thread pays out. Rain falls on hide. There is a long quiet chewing. The frog calls and the answer comes, and then one evening there is only the answer. Something drags across the ash floor, where there has never been a track. A jar rocks as something squeezes past and polishes it.

Then nothing, for a long time. That is her going soft all through. It is the worst of it, and you would take it for her if it could be taken. It can't. The heat that fires her has to be inside her. Anything you added from here would only crack the pot.

So you keep your hand where it is.

When the scratching starts, a hand's breadth away, jaws on the far side of the mud, you go.

You meant to leave nothing. But a wall takes whatever is pressed to it, on this side too, and you stood there a long time. Where your palm was, the frost has not grown. You could grow it over. You don't. It is true, and she is fired now. A fired thing can be handed a fact and keep its own shape.

She comes through into the cold, black, narrow-waisted, her wings still creased from the pleat. She stands on the frost feathers under two stars. The first thing she wants is wet clay.

The second thing is a patch of bare wall the width of a palm. Nothing in her room was found. She walks onto it and raps it with both antennae, to hear how thick it is. It is warm.

She will build you from this without meaning to: something large that stood here and did not come in. She will be nearly right.

Then she finds the rope. Behind her the wind crosses the hole she made, and the room, low in the breastbone, begins to sound.
