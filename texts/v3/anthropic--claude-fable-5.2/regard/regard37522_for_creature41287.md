Something comes to look at the shelf. It comes at ten, with the horn, because that is when she does, and it walks the traps behind her for a night before it does anything else.

It has the means to do a great deal. It is also old enough to have learned a habit like hers, and it touches things with the back of its hand first.

It could open the cloud. Ten minutes would do, at three in the morning, a hole the size of the football pitch with stars in it. It gets as far as the underside of the overcast and looks down at the players. Then it thinks of a woman of twenty lying awake in a city, waiting for someone to come and fix the dark. It thinks of the off-shifts standing in the street with their hands hanging. On this shelf a thing that has never happened before is a fault until shown otherwise, and the people are right to treat it so. There is also the matter of afterwards. Every child on that pitch would be from somewhere then, from the night with the hole in it, and would never quite stop mentioning it. The stars are not locked away. The truck goes out with room in the cab. She went at twenty, looked, and came back, and that was hers to do. It leaves the cloud alone, and is not entirely sure it is right.

It considers the knees, and the ears of the old. The knees are how the shelf signs its work. As for the ears, it stands a shift in the pipe shop beside the fitter who has never heard anything, and finds that what the town says is true.

It considers giving her one night when every trap is good, three hundred and some of them clicking and spitting, a level palm from the whole plant. It is fond of this idea for most of an hour. But she would spend the night certain the rod had cracked, walk the round twice, and be late to the outfall.

It does see to the oil. The next truck carries two drums where the sheet says one. It is not above this.

That leaves the ground, which is what drew it here.

It can do the sum. Sixty years of three shifts a day have made grit a finger deep to the second knuckle, and she is fifty-eight. The road will not be ready. It could make up the difference in an afternoon, crack the slab or lay down a field of the brown soft stuff people walk on without looking. But it has seen what the shelf does with fill. There is a hedge outside the administration block on its fifth or sixth life.

And it has listened to her by now. On the first night, while she had the rod on a trap at the ninth loop, it laid its attention against her the way she lays the steel against the bone. A good trap clicks, spits, and clicks. So does she. There is no mark on any glass to say where she should be sitting, and it finds it has no wish to paint one. She is making the mark, and the town is watching her make it.

It has also understood the tilted palm. They are not asking her where to dig. They are asking whether she will go out on the truck like the others, to a somewhere she does not have. She answers with the flat hand, and then she goes and checks the grit. She has given her answer and is waiting on the ground. The ground is the town's answer, made at the rate boots lose, and the power will not answer over their heads. What it can do is what the shelf already does. It can let something go and see what lives on it.

A long way inland, where the truck is loaded, a fig tree hangs over the depot road. It is the kind that grows out of walls and gutters and asks only to be wet. It drops its fruit on the road, and the truck drives over it. This has gone on for years. Seeds have ridden out to the shelf in the treads by the thousand and been shaken loose onto clinker, and done what anything does on clinker. When a tyre wears out it goes to the pipe shop and is cut into soles, and seeds have gone up and down the pipe road that way too, in a groove, in a pinch of dried mud.

The power picks one. It is in the left boot of a boy on the two o'clock shift. It does nothing to the seed. It sees to it that the mud lets go at the third loop, at the edge of the road where the grit is, and not four steps further on. That is the whole of the act.

It knows what a fig may do, given a whole life to do it. The roots go down the cracks to the warm water and thicken there, and they are the only thing besides boots that can take the edge off this rock. The leaves fall and stay where they fall. In time there would be a trunk, and herons in it, and under it a patch of road a little darker than the rest, which is as near to a shadow as the lamps allow. The town would need a sign for it. One is already in use for the first of a thing.

It does not know whether the ground under the tree will be deep enough in time, or whether the town will settle on the sea instead, or the blue water, or something nobody has thought of. That is theirs. But when the palm tilts and the glance goes down, there will be somewhere for the glance to go.

It does not stay to guard the seed. There are crabs, and there are boots, and the shelf has someone who checks that spot. At six it walks out with the ten o'clock shift, and shows the incoming shift a level palm, as is done.

Some weeks on, under the lamp at the third loop, there are two leaves in the grit, each the size of a gecko's foot. She knows roughly what it is. She saw them at twenty, standing about in the soft ground. She touches it with the backs of her fingers. Then she sets one end of the rod in the grit beside it and the other against the bone behind her ear.

It has nothing of its own to say. What comes up the steel is the low note, coming through the rock as it comes through everything here, and she hears it in her teeth. She stays a little longer than a trap would need.

Then she tucks the rod under her arm and turns her wrist at it once, the way of opening a valve. Yes, go on, come in, have some more. She walks on to the fourth loop.
