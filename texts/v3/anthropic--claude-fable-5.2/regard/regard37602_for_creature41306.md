**The one who came to look**

It came up through the old quarry with the fog, before dawn, the way water gets here. Within the hour it understood that it could not rest its attention in one spot. Whatever keeps still on the cushion is skinned over and in time copied, and the tableland had no use for a second, shorter providence standing by itself a hundred yards from the first. So it kept moving while it watched, like the man with the pipe.

It watched for nine pencil marks on the doorposts. It walked the lamplighter's round behind him and lay under the beds on still nights. It went below the rim, which nobody here has done, and looked at the underside. It is brown and close-grained, with the works coming through it like weather, and one pinhole of light.

It had been told that one being lived here. From the hammer house roof it got a different number every time, like everyone. By the fourth spring it thought the first figure was right. There is one plant. The chimneys nobody laid, the cocks with the same pale feather, the engineman and the three who keep his engines oiled, and the green thing banking under the hole are what one life looks like when it lives by dividing. Every piece it laid a hand to was warm.

**What it left alone**

It could have stopped the growth. The brothers would have stayed in lamp range and the splice gang could have laid rail for good. But a cushion that stops growing is underlayer to the top within a lifetime, and the town stands on the part that is alive. It left the growth.

It could have mended their ears, and it turned this one over longest. Mended ears would be gone again by thirty unless it also quieted the hammer, and the hammer is where their sentences end. It left the ears. It does not count that among the things it is sure of.

It could tell which engine house is the ledger's. It knew by the second spring. It also saw that the town, by going round the question, had reached the answer it would have given. All four are warm, and nothing can be done with the knowledge except make three men less. It has kept that out of every book, including its own.

It did not put the unlisted on the payroll. Somebody is already paying them, in dinner, on the step of the second house. It could have watched that step at noon and learned who, and it didn't. A kindness nobody admits to seemed a thing to leave exactly as it stood.

It told the hammerman nothing. He is right, he got there by counting, and what he does about it should be his.

**What it did**

It did two things.

It followed a tread to the old quarry. From the rope the town hears it stop. It doesn't stop. It goes over the lip, and four hours later and a third lower it is walking on something else. The rope was put up for people with weight and has never kept back anyone who was on their way. It left the rope.

Beside it, it set a lamp on an iron pole of the town's pattern, leaned out over the hole, lit. Nobody asked where it came from, because lamps turn up here. The lamplighter added sixty yards to his round. He trims it every evening, though it is burning when he arrives. The pinhole no longer goes out at dusk, and the dawn fog comes up through it lit. Whoever is down there had little reason to look up. Now there is one light in a sky the size of a district, and somebody tends it.

The other thing is a book. The town keeps its copies out of the ledger, for reasons the visitor respects, so it keeps a ledger of its own. It holds three engine houses and a doorframe, the gatekeeper in the far box and the hand with the lantern. It holds every tread, with its hitch, and the hummocks in the first cemetery that the register has no names for. The can on the step is there by date. The total comes out different each time, and it enters each total.

**After**

On the next clear afternoon the thing went by, and the cutter with the best eyes held the foreman's glass on it the full eleven minutes. The chimney was taller. There were a few lengths of rail in the groove.

They asked her again if it looked up. She said she couldn't make out a face at that distance and wouldn't claim it. She said the one crossing had stopped. It stood in the open between the chimney and the lamp for about as long as it takes to light a pipe, shifting from foot to foot.

The week after, the hammerman finished his count, struck three more, spaced, and went home. That was his own doing. The visitor was at the far rim that morning, watching the frost go off the crowns.

That night what came up under his bed was six over, and the last three were spaced.

He has not raised it with anyone. The clerk felt it through the floor of her room like everybody else. In the morning she ruled a new column in the book, headed it Received, and made the first entry.
