It came in winter, on a flood, so the first thing it heard was the mill with no birds on it.

It understood early that it was a stone. Anything it set down on this plain would go round and change a note. So it did what is done here before a stone is given, and weighed. It lay along the rock for a full year, through every flood, with the small kettles in whatever it had for teeth and the great ones in whatever it had for hips.

Then it held up what it had brought, one thing at a time, against what had worn away.

Her hearing was a thumb too wide. It would give her back the birds and the shouting. She would have to pick each stone's note out from under forty screaming pairs, like anyone, and the plain would lose its one keeper who gets the note clean.

The pan was wider still. The only way to stop the pan is to stop the stones. A plain of still kettles would last very well, and no bird could find it. She had weighed this herself at the margin, longer than she weighs anywhere, and given the stone. It went over her weighing and found nothing to correct.

It knew what had become of the nine pairs. It kept that. There was no way to tell her that would not be a voice out of the grey. After such a voice she would circle her own life the way the pairs circled their hole, knowing the place and not the note. Meanwhile the ledges were filling at the birds' own pace, and she was two short of the end of a count that was hers.

It noticed the quiet kettle beside her hut. It joined the people in not asking.

It thought for some time about her first name, the one no one living uses. Then, on a rim, on a flood, it recognised what it was lying on. Tens of thousands of stones were going round on rock, like two knuckles rubbed together, a whole country grinding its teeth in its sleep. She put her chest to that twice a day. There was nothing to return.

When the weighing was done, what had actually worn down to a pebble was small. A name that is a gesture needs someone looking, and for most of the year there was no one. That much it could give at the right weight.

So it looked. It learned her sign for every kettle she had given a stone to, and for the thousands she hadn't, made low at the hip as she passed. It did not tap her shoulder. She went on making them the way a person counts under the breath, and each one was seen.

It noticed, too, that the hip of a short woman is at the eye height of a small child, and that the children already believe a palm on the chest is a thing a person can be. It left that to the boats.

One matter remained. It had taken a year of listening from this plain, and the custom was clear. They take, and they owe stones.

There is one kind of wonder the place already has manners for. Work is found done. No one signs anything about it.

At the tide's limit, at the end of her line, on the unbroken shield where the next cup would go, lay a pebble of the pale rock. It had been pecked, not sea-rolled, and the marks stood up under the thumb.

She found it on her next walk out, and stood over it through most of an ebb. Then she fished the pebble from her newest cup and weighed the two, hand against hand, for longer than she weighs at the margin. She put her own back. She knelt and pecked a cup no bigger than a limpet's scar, set the strange pebble in it, and pecked the ring around it, wider than the hole it was for.

When she stood, she looked along the tide's limit, where the shield ran on unmarked into the grey. She pressed a flat palm to her breastbone. The sign means two things, and nothing in how she stood said which one she meant.

The next time she came, she left a twist of sinew beside the last cup, and the worst-dried of the birds she had been left. It was a very bad bird. It was taken.

On the flood she puts a finger into the new cup beside the pebble and feels it go round, knocking her nail once each turn. A day's walk seaward the margin kettles groan, their walls a season thinner. At slack water the stones stop and the rock goes still under her. She stops where she is and stands. It stops too, until the ebb starts the mill again.
