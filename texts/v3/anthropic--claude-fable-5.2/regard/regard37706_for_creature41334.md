For the first day the power does nothing. It watches the ellipse cross the street, sits through thirty-odd rains, and looks for the one thing that would oblige it to hurry, which is hurt.

It finds none below. The hum is effort, but the effort of something doing what it is good at, and no stack wavers. The green is thriving, and so is the felt under it. The iron-eaters in the east gallery are having the best century of their existence. Nobody in the chamber is asking for anything, unless the lit addresses count as asking.

The only danger is at the top. The shaft opens flush with the turf of a rough hillside, without a lip. A lamb could go in, or a running child. The keeper of the chamber knows weight that walks and weight that rests. It does not know weight that arrives at that speed, and it should not have to learn it from the first one.

So the power's first act is on the surface. It gathers field stones, weathered and round-shouldered, none of them square, and sets them in a ring around the mouth, knee high. That stops whatever is not looking, and anyone who is can step over. The light still goes down, and so does anything the wind is carrying. From below, the ring would read as a small load in a closed outline, the footprint of a very small house with nothing in it.

Then it considers the rest, and declines most of it.

It could explain walls. But the clubmoss is already hanging them on every ground floor, and the keeper will get further with a curtain it watched grow than with a wall it was handed.

It could send down the gnats and snails, the things that bite fronds. The shaft is open and they are coming anyway, at their own speed. A garden should meet its first mouth by accident.

It could turn a footpath a few degrees and have the town found by spring. But people are not a thing that can be given. And the fern crowns are weeks from the plates. That decision should be made before there is anyone in the houses to please.

What is left is small. The addresses are lit for a weight the keeper would know at once. The power can be that weight for an evening, as a guest. A guest is something a house can have without being owed.

It takes on about seventy kilos and stands them beside the ring on two feet. It shifts from one foot to the other, as people do when they wait. It does not step over. The shaft was opened either to look or to be found, and whoever opened it gets to say which.

For most of an hour nothing changes. Then the east gallery, which has not missed a beat since the chamber was cut, goes quiet.

The next crack comes from inside the shaft, just under the rim. A tread stands out from the wall, level, an arm's length wide, its edges bright. Then comes another, lower, a little further round. The ground knows stairs: they are a street stood on end. There is no rail, since it has never felt one.

The visitor steps over the stones and goes down. The cracks soon outrun the walking and carry on ahead, one turn, two, until the last one sounds from the chamber floor.

At the bottom it walks the street at an ordinary pace. As it passes each stack, the note there tightens, then eases behind it. The throb between buildings quickens and travels alongside. Overhead the ceiling is full. Forty minutes have gone, then forty-five, and the drops hang long from every facet. The rain does not come.

The visitor follows a route line and its green margin to a glyph, turns off, parts the clubmoss, and steps up onto the lowest plate. The plate takes seventy kilos without dipping by a hair. The note of that stack drops, the way a voice does when it stops calling.

Then the ceiling lets go.

For the first time the water falls around a building with someone in it. The visitor sits behind the clubmoss and the curtain of rain, in the standing room that was got right, in the warmth from underneath. It had been shifting its weight all the way down the street. Now it is still. The keeper can feel that much: a weight that fidgeted in the open and settled when something closed around it. It may make something of that. Nobody tells it to.

Later the visitor lies down. The weight spreads from two points to the length of a body, which is how weight is at night. The keeper has felt that all its life through the floors of other people's houses. It has never before been the floor.

The rains keep their hours until morning. The east gallery stays quiet.

When the ellipse shows at the head of the nave, the visitor gets up, walks back along the street, and climbs. The ground knows this part too: they go out in the morning. Under each of the lowest treads an orange thread has already started. The natives got there first, as they always do.

At the top it steps over the ring and leaves the ring where it is. A walker on the hillside will now stop at the stones, look down, and see steps. Whether one does is not arranged.

The power will come back on some evenings and not others, with no pattern in it. It will not be the town's people. That place is being kept, and should be.

When the first crown touches a plate, it will be somewhere else, on purpose. Some things ought to be decided without anyone watching.

Below, a while after the last weight leaves the stair, the east gallery cracks again.
