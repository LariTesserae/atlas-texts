It comes down the shaft with the grey light, and the midges rising through it do not swerve.

For the first year it does nothing. It learns the beat as the salamanders have and crosses the drip line only between strokes. It hears the siphon swallow some eleven hundred times. It follows a spoonful up the main to the cracked cistern and back down through the chalk to the spring, which takes nine days. It reads the rest of the maker's plate through the tufa, and the numerals above 7, and leaves them covered.

It watches her through one whole autumn: the belly going to metal, the forty paces, the head in the pipe, the backing out.

Then it considers what it could do.

It could lift her. The sea exists, and she could be in it between one clack and the next. But an eel is finished by the swim itself. The gut dissolves on the way, and the body is spent mile by mile into what the journey is for. Set down at the end of it, she would be a river animal in the wrong water.

It could mend things, but nothing here is broken. The crack in the cistern is why the water tastes of her.

It could quiet the autumn in her. This would be the smallest touch of all: the leaf-rot would come and the belly would stay chalk. It looks at this longest, because it resembles kindness, and then puts it furthest away. It would end the wanting by ending part of her.

It could leave. This is the strong one. The room is whole and owes nothing. But it notices that the wholeness is something seen from where it stands, and she is not standing there.

What decides it is a smaller thing. Nobody was present to note the year the two measurements crossed, if they have. Now somebody is. It goes into the siphon and measures the bore at the bend, and it measures her head. The pipe is the narrower, by about the thickness of two fingernails.

That settles less than it seems to. She puts her head in as far as the bend, and at the bend the scale touches her on both sides. An animal that is stopped and an animal that stops look the same from outside, even to it. The pipe says she cannot. It does not say she would.

So it will not send her. It will take away the one thing that keeps the question from being hers.

It does this by the rule of the room, slightly and often, the way the room made the pearl cups. Each time the level touches 7, it loosens a few grains from the flint band at the waterline, and the draw carries them through the bend. That is all. It never widens the bore beyond what she needs, so the sump still falls a forearm's depth and the margin still gleams. It walks the road beyond once, the culvert, the ditches, and the flap in the sea wall that opens on the ebb, and finds it as dangerous as it is for every eel that goes down it. It leaves the road alone. She should have the same door as the others, not a better one.

After two years and some months there is a second ring of bright metal in the chamber. Nothing sees it.

The leaf-rot comes down through the seeps. She silvers. The water climbs to 7, the siphon primes, and she goes down the forty paces and puts her head into the draw as far as the bend.

Nothing touches her.

She holds there anyway. But the scale is no longer holding her, and the draw is strong. To stay she has to swim against it, her whole length working. It is the first time that staying has been something she does. The siphon takes air and breaks, and she backs out.

It does nothing. This is most of what it does.

She goes to the pipe at every priming that season, and every time she holds, and by midwinter she is chalk again and eating. It stays through the winter with what it has learned. If this is her answer, it is an answer, and hers. She has the groove, and the surge on her face every four seconds, and a ceiling of blue-green points above the only eyes in the room built to see them. It finds it can want nothing further for her than that, and it checks whether this is true, and it is.

The next autumn the water brings turned earth, then the sharp thing, then straw, then rot. Her belly goes to metal. The level climbs. At 7 the siphon gives its long swallow and she goes down the room and puts her head in the pipe.

She holds for as long as it takes the line to fall twice.

Then she stops swimming. The pipe takes her as it takes water, head, then the long body that has streamed toward it like weed for a hundred autumns, then the tail. A few minutes later the siphon draws air behind her and breaks.

It goes with her down the culvert, into a ditch at night, under a dark that has weather in it. The first lights she sees that are not alive are stars. The ditches join. At the sea wall the flap lifts on the ebb, and she passes into water that has not been anywhere she has been. It stops there, where the fresh water gives out. It could see the rest. But the eels that go down are not seen again, and it lets that be true of her too. All it keeps is this: where she is going is dark, and the only light there is blue-green points given off by living things. She will be the one eel that has ever arrived there already knowing that sky.

Then it goes back, because the room is also in its care.

The margin gleams. The isopods have come down the walls, with the salamanders after them and the ditch eels nosing along the shallows. At the uphill end the groove lies in the pale silt a body-length below the ram. It leaves the groove for the water to fill, and it leaves the bright ring at the bend for the scale to close, which has already begun. It replaces nothing. The oldest moving thing in the pit is now the valve.

In spring a needle of glass comes up the culvert and through the bend, with room to spare.

*Clack.* The surge comes down from the valve, crosses the groove, and goes on.

The line falls. The pearls turn.
