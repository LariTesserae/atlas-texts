It comes down by the lift, since that is how everything gets here. The gate opens on the landing and waits, and this time something gets off. It takes its coat off within a minute, as anyone would in that heat, and hangs it on a hook beside the mugs.

Then for a while it does nothing. It stands on the grating through one whole turn of the weather: the layer thickening, the damper, the eleven minutes, the amber going nearly yellow, the steam and the dry. It reads the chalk beside the jacks. It reads Boiler Row off the liverwort. It finds the fern that points two ways and works out from the fronds roughly when that lamp went.

Most of what could be done, it sets aside.

It could bring the people back. But they went up in this same lift and took their coats. The street is as hot as a bread oven and the rock has not finished moving. Whoever chalked those columns knew the place better than a visitor does, and they left. It will not overrule them.

It could bring something that eats, a snail for the gutters or a moth for the lamps. The town would look more alive with holes in the fronds. But a snail would be the first thing down here able to go hungry, and it would be set down in a town that is losing its light. The green can lose without minding. The loss should not be handed to something that would mind.

It could give the algae a day. The grey coin at the top of the shaft could be made to reach the floor. But the ferns are pale enough to read a valve number through, and everything here has grown to the measure of a caged bulb. A day would be a present chosen to please the giver.

It could mend the place properly. A fitter relieving this shift would start with the tags, repack every weeping joint, clear the gutters and scrub the walls around the lamps. Each of those jobs is an eviction. The faults are where the present tenants live, so the work has to be chosen for them.

What is left is small, and a caretaker could do it.

There is a store behind a wire door by the canteen, with bulbs on the shelf in corrugated sleeves. It carries a carton up the stairs and along the catwalks and changes every dead lamp in the town. This takes no wisdom. The colonies starved because a filament broke, and the remedy had been sitting forty steps away.

It does know what the change costs. The turbine gives only what the rain gives, and the working lamps have been living on the share of the dead ones. With every bulb back on the line, each will burn a shade lower, and the old discs will draw in at their edges by the width of a finger. Against that, a filament run a little under lasts far longer, and there will be half as many discs again. It makes the trade and does not pretend the trade was free. The grey crust it leaves alone. The rain will sow the felt back over it or it will not.

On Boiler Row it touches one joint, where the liverwort has gone all the way round the flange and the weep has begun to spit. It takes the bolts up half a turn, because a seep is a territory and a burst would stop the heat. The liverwort will lose a margin and keep the flange. Everything else on the row it leaves weeping.

It takes up the jacks, which are stiff and hot and each want a different amount. Then it finds the stub of chalk and writes the date at the foot of each column. A record with a gap in it should at least show where the gap ends.

It chalks nothing beside the lamps. The ferns keep that record. The one that points two ways will send its next fronds back toward the cage it gave up on, and after that it will show three headings in order: the lamp, the far lamp, the lamp again.

At the cut face it does nothing at all. The drill steel stays in its hole. The last building has no roof, and that is why the fern is there. Of all the buildings in the town, it is the only one that suits its tenant exactly as it stands. The last lamp on the line is the dimmest, because the line loses a little at every bulb before it, and so it will be the last to fail. The fern could not have chosen better, and it did not choose. The visitor looks at the crozier for some time and keeps its hands to itself.

None of this makes the green permanent. The bulbs in the carton are filament bulbs too, and the shelf is finite. The green is still the middle tenancy. The white will go on climbing the brick, and the visitor has no quarrel with it. The stone will have everything in the end and is in no hurry.

But the town was never short of anything a marvel could supply. It was short one person, at intervals. The routine does everything except mend itself, and the wait the lift makes at the bottom was built for exactly this. It is long enough to step out with a carton under one arm.

By now the layer overhead has thickened and begun its slow roll. The damper swings. The rain comes down warm and fine on the tin, the turbine's note lifts a half-tone, and the lamps come up, all of them, to a shade under the old yellow. On the grey discs nothing shows yet. At the far end the rain falls straight through the frame onto the single course of brick, and the crozier loosens by a little.

When the town has steamed and dried, the lift comes down on its timer and opens its gate. The visitor gets in. A coat on that hook has only ever meant that somebody is coming back for it. The green cannot read a coat, and it is not hung there for the green.

The gate closes. The coat stays where it is, beside the mugs.
