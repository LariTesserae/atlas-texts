It comes up the north side with the morning cloud, since that is how anything arrives here that isn't carried, and it goes through the fog-walls with the rest and leaves a little of itself on the stone. Then it walks the lane, keeping to the polished middle, and does the first thing anyone here would do. It lays a palm flat against a wall.

It does this at all seventy houses, warm and cold. It has laid its palm on a great many places, and most of them are wet to the lintel. It knows what asking feels like. Here the joints are dark and the faces are dry, which is the right amount of damp. The town is not asking.

It has a great deal it could give. A spring could open under the cistern row. Timber could take root on the lee flank. There could be iron, or a few words in a sleeping ear about the striking-stone. It turns each of these over the way a person here turns over a fallen stone, looking for the gap it came out of, and there is no gap. It could make one, but that would be starting something new.

It also knows what mortar does to a wall like these. The water that used to pass through stays in, and a wall that stood thirty generations by weight and fit bellies out in three. Given a spring, the fog-walls would stand a while, and then one morning nobody would reset a slab. The easy flame it considers longest. A quarrel here can last years. In the ninth year of one, a man whose fire has died in the night will take the pot two doors along to the person he isn't speaking to. He will be given a piece, carry it home, and go on not speaking. A flame anyone could strike would end that walk, and the power can't see what would then hold eleven households in one town. That need is the weight holding the wall together.

It doesn't mistake the place for painless. It has seen what is carried down the south side, and some of what is carried is small. It has heard which doors have a note that isn't sounded anymore. But the lane down is open, and so is the lane up. A woman came up it twenty years ago and kept her voice. Everyone here is here by not having left. The power can't improve on that arrangement, and it distrusts itself a little for having looked.

So it does what a neighbor does here. A man resets a slab badly, and someone redoes it at dusk without a word. That is the scale of it. Some winter a fever will break on the fourth night that might have gone to a fifth. Nobody will remark on it, since fevers do break. If anyone suspects, it will become one more thing murmured about for years and never settled, and the power can live with being a quarrel.

It could quiet the drain under the old man's worst house. It leaves the drain alone, because he has had fifty years out of that sound.

There is one thing more. In the store it finds the striking-stone, and it knows what a thing two or three people have been told about is worth on the morning it's needed, in a town with no tinder that stays dry. It can see, the way it sees weather, a night some generations on. There will be sickness in every house and ten hearths gray by dawn, the eleventh banked by a child who has only ever watched it done. That is the one fall the town cannot put back the same day. On that night it will sit by the eleventh hearth and see that the peat was pressed in well enough, and it will do nothing else. In the morning someone will push the ash aside and blow, and then walk the pot down the lane ten times. They will say the child banked it well, which will be nearly true, and the child will be careful ever after.

Tonight it goes to the upper lane and sits in the room where the slabs are ticking. The thing in the walls stops on its new run and looks straight at it, being used to that position itself, then goes on. The power is content to share the word.

It doesn't touch the ash. One doesn't uncover another household's fire. It only stays a while by the being that lives here, the red thing the size of a tuber, with no particular shape. It is older than most of what the power is asked to look after, and it has been kept the whole time by people who own what can be carried in two trips. The power has no advice for it. If anything, the power is the one who takes something away. A thing can be kept thirty generations by feeding it twice a day, covering it at night, and carrying it down the lane when a cold room asks. That is light enough to take in one trip, and it will be wanted in the wet places.

In the morning the cloud comes through and the first drip starts in the cisterns. One of the children kneels, pushes the ash aside, and blows. The fire is there, and the power had nothing to do with it.

It goes out the south side, the way the cloud goes, without circling. By midday the walls have dried to silver, with only the joints dark, and nothing shows that it was there. It will be through again tomorrow, since its path has not moved either.
