The attention comes as far as the line and stops there.

It has to. There are two ways into the basin and both are taken. Whatever enters as sound is a plan, and the town would have a hall up under it within the hour and the hall down again by evening. Whatever enters as quiet is a tenant and takes ground. Just inside the gate there is a four-sided one that has not finished deciding what it is, and it should not have to decide next to something that size. So the power stays in a gateway, one pace deep, with the heat ticking in the salt on one side and the pressure on the chest on the other. It may be that this, too, is what the gates are for.

It stays while the ration goes once round the town, and it learns the place in the place's terms. It counts the colonnade from the south and gets thirty-one, counts it from the north and gets twenty-eight, and keeps both. It does not look into the residents. It has the means to find out what is done with the noise in there, and it leaves the means alone, because a pupil is not for looking into. What they do can be seen from the gate. They go to the bell, the market, the wells and the channel, and they stay. That is enough to go on.

Most of what a power could do here suggests itself in the first hour. Seeing why not takes longer.

- **Water:** The channel could be running by tomorrow. Within a season there would be bark, litter, humus, and streets that kept their places. There would also be an end to the guessing, since a wall that stands cannot become a different door. And the channel is not empty. One or another of them is generally in it.
- **The bell:** One touch would swing the gourd. The tolling would then have a place it came from and would stay there, and those who stand at the foot of it would have nothing left to keep. They have made their preference as plain as they make anything.
- **The answer:** The town cannot be told that the narrow street is right. It has one sense, and what reaches it by that sense it builds, so it can be given orders but not news. If it could be told, it would hang every street with doors two hands wide, and the small ones crossing the market would live in a town that had stopped wondering about them.
- **The sea:** The shelf is low and will not last as land is counted. It could be held up. But the seven-sided one lies along the quay on plates worn round, a floor older than the ground it sits in. That floor has outlived a ground before. Whatever the waterfront is waiting for, the eldest is waiting with it, and the power takes that as the view of someone better informed.

The longer it looks, the less it finds undone. A resident that settles over a well through the heat is lying with its damp floor across the mouth of a shaft of woven root, and the next quarter to go fat and glossy is, more often than chance allows, that one. It is a cupful. The town does not know where it comes from, and nothing suggests the resident knows where it goes. The town feeds them by losing fruit and draws them by stopping short. They give it the only yesterday it has. The two have never met and are keeping each other. To introduce them would add nothing but the knowledge, and knowledge here would have to arrive as a sound or as a silence.

There is one thing.

The street of doors too narrow for anything but light is about to come down. The front shows two districts off, a darker green with the din sharpening ahead of it. The sliver is on the avenue, point first, as on most days, and will pass the end of the street a little before the front arrives, or a little after. The power cannot make the guess stand and will not turn the sliver. What is left is the size of a breath. The wind in the basin is warm and steady and knocks the culms together all day. For the length of one knock it comes from another quarter.

The street goes to paper, rattles and comes down with a roar. One door, torn off its thorns, goes end over end across the avenue and over the line, to where the roar stops. It lies flat on bare marl, lengthwise, with a finger to spare on either side. At the edge the rootlets comb the air and cannot cross.

The sliver could put it off. The pentagon sets down what it does not want aboard: a side swings past, and the thing is in the din again. The power has not arranged what comes next and watches as anyone would.

The sliver stops. It has not been seen to stop on an avenue before. Then it goes on, point first, at the pace of a person carrying a full bowl.

Toward evening the wind drops. The fog comes over the wall in a slow white fold and stands in the plazas, each with its own number of sides. For an hour the sliver is a blade of white as tall as the roofs. In the gateway there is nothing for the fog to stand in.

By morning the streets run differently. The newest has moved a little further in from the gate. Three of its sides have settled, and it is taking its time over the fourth. The seven-sided one is on the waterfront. The sliver is on the broadest avenue with a door lying along its floor, dark with damp, the wet green already going out of it. In a few days it will be brown.

The town does not know it guessed right. Its guess is being kept the only way anything here is kept. At the end of the avenue, where the street stood, the marl has cracked, and the town is growing a door a hand high, and beside it a door that would admit a ship.
