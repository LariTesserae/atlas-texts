**What came**

It found the place the way anything does, by the hum. It had crossed the plateau many times and seen from above what there is to see, which is almost nothing.

It came down the slot at mid-morning against the rising ropes of bees, and it was stung. This surprised it. Then it understood that it carried the smell of every river there is. It took the stings as instruction and slowed down.

**What it had with it**

It had brought everything, and it weighed each gift for about as long as a keeper holds a mouthful of water.

- **Rain:** The grey veils that fade out high in the air could be let down the rest of the way. It looked at the clay, the thatch, the wax, the floor of dry flour a thousand years deep, and the grain older than anyone's grandmother. Everything here is kept by what does not arrive. It left the veils where they were.
- **A spring:** This was the same harm, only slower, and it would bring neighbors.
- **Her eyes:** It looked for what the milkiness had cost her and arrived at her own figure, which was nothing. To give her sight it would first have had to convince her of a lack.
- **Writing:** Marks pressed in clay would stop the count from living in one head. But marks can be read by someone who has never been stung. The keepers own nothing here except knowing what is in it. A tablet also cannot unpick a knot and hand back the cord.
- **A river:** It could have shown her one, in a dream. It listened to the hives all night first, and then to what she had made of them: a thing that goes on until morning, that you can sleep beside, that would tell you the hour if you knew it well enough. It found no error worth correcting. As for her question, why people would live beside all that water and keep none, it had no good answer and thought the question should stay asked.

Then it watched the man with the crooked leg quiet a colony with three breaths and stop before he was sure. It recognized the rule. It had been trying to learn that rule since before the plateau was flat.

**What it did**

It stayed. It learned to move at one speed. It sat in the fourth alley in the slack after the bats and learned the knots alongside the girl, somewhat more slowly than she did. Near the end of the hot season the stinging stopped.

After that it allowed itself three breaths.

The first was for the count. It measured the alleys still ahead against the evenings the old one had left and found the two did not come out even. It made them even. It gave her exactly the evenings the knots will take, counted out by the gourd and not one over. She would have noticed a surplus and wanted to know whose it was.

The second was for the girl, who is the only one of the four who did not choose. It changed nothing in her. But the woman of thirty went down twice and came back a day early both times, and a day early only means something if the path runs both ways. So it will keep the path. One morning the girl will stand on the lip after the dew and look toward the plateau's edge instead of at the stone. That day a carrier will be starting down who does not mind company. Whichever way she is walking a season later, she will have chosen it herself.

The third it could not defend, and it did it anyway. On some mornings the wad of beaten bark now wrings out to three mouthfuls. The third goes into the stone hollow with the pebbles, like the others. The bees are served first.

It also keeps the tally of ants fed to antlions, since someone ought to. Nothing is owed on it.

**What it left alone**

It learned the knot that belongs to no household and went to the back, to the knee-high bins that hold no grain. It knows of other places a keeper might be carried, and it considered them honestly. Then it left every cord as tied.

It let the falcon drop through the slot and let the town flinch. It let the snake be stepped over, and it let the floor keep rising.

**Tonight**

They are still in the fourth alley. The girl ties the knot wrong a second time. The cord comes back to her unpicked, and nothing is said. It does not help. She ties it a third time, and the old one's fingers go over it and stop, and she names a household.

Then the old one lays her palm flat on the pole lashing beside her and holds it there, the way she holds it on the woven disc of a hive. She keeps it there a while. Whatever she finds goes into the count with the rest: something that came up at no one's knock, takes no handful, and has been here since before the clay was last mixed.

She does not mention it, since it has cost her nothing. But when she turns her head aside to breathe, she turns it the other way from her habit, toward the girl. It is the courtesy keepers give to bees, and to people.

It turns its head aside too.
