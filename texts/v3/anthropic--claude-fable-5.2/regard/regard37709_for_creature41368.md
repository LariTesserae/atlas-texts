She does not use the stair. A tread on that spiral rings down forty metres of iron into the floor plates, and for as long as the descent lasts the figure is very slightly wrong. She arrives on the gantry, which hangs from the rock, and she takes care to weigh nothing. For eleven days she does what the hall does. She listens until the chord stops being a sound. She stands through the rain. She waits for the fourth note, watches the fronds by the throat lean together, and watches them settle.

She can do nearly anything here, so she goes through it.

She could stop the pipes thinning. A skin on the inside of the mains would hold the chord where it is for as long as the rock stays hot. But travelling is how the thing leaves its dead behind. Nothing rots here, and what falls goes to felt and stays. A band that never moved would stand deeper every century in its own litter, until the rain could not reach the seam. And the fourth note would go on coming and going, and the pods would pull at their stalks forever toward an interval she had taken away.

She could soften the day itself, damping the plates so the old cord is not worked loose. But the grain has no way to travel except on a floor that moves, and the shaking that kills the old figure will strip more spore from it in an hour than it has shed in a hundred years. It is the largest sowing it will ever make. No gentle version of it works.

She could tell it things: that it is one, that the felt ridges are its own, that the day is coming. There is nowhere in it to put any of this. She could make somewhere, and then there would be a different creature in the hall, one able to dread, and the one she came for would be gone.

She could bring it company. But anything alive enough to be company is alive enough to be hungry, and the first mouth in the hall would have all of it.

Nothing on the list survives. What is left is to find out whether it needs her at all, which is slower work than helping.

She listens to each of the twelve mains alone. The thinning is even. She listens to the pods without flicking them. Every thin valve agrees with every other, and every thick valve sits the same shade under its note. She follows the three low notes down through the years to the place where the fourth stops going away, and finds the date, a little under three hundred years off. She had half expected a flaw, and the question of what to do about it. There is none.

The one thing she finds hard to decline is the simplest. It knows only where the floor is still. If she took on weight and stood in a lane, within a few years a spur of root would find the quiet and come up around her feet. That is the only form in which it could ever have her, as a place where it was allowed. But her weight would bend every line in the hall a little, and on the day the grain would draw a figure with her in it. After that she would have to stay forever, or leave and take the floor out from under whatever had rooted on her account. The cart and the lockers were left by people who did not know what they were giving, and under each of them a spur will ride out the jump. She adds nothing to that.

She does three things.

Near the throat, along the dead margin of a band, thirty-seven black grains lie in a row beneath a pod with a blade's cut in it. Someone wanted to know. She does not grudge that, since wanting to know is close to what she is doing herself. But nothing will come of the grains where they are, and they were not the visitor's to spend. She picks them up. She does not mend the pod, because a pod she closed would be tuned by her. She keeps the grains.

Then she goes looking for the dynamo. The lamps are no use to the thing in the hall. They are for whoever comes down the stair, and someone will. She cannot choose who. In the dark this is a room of scrap with weeds in it and a noise. Under the lamps, from the gantry, it is a figure, and a person who has seen the figure first puts their feet down differently. One bearing is running dry. She sees to it.

Last, she walks the felt ridges, weighing nothing. She reads them back, figure by figure, to the edge of the iron and past it onto the bare rock. There the ridges are rings, small ones around a vent. Four times in the rock the rings stop and begin again somewhere else. It has done this before. It has crossed all of it and kept none, and it has no use for a past. She learns the figures anyway, every one, so that they are somewhere.

Then she goes, and the hall is as it was, less thirty-seven grains.

On the day she will be on the gantry. The fourth note will come and, for the first time, stay. The thick valves will take up their note under it, and the pods will spring within a few breaths of each other. The grain will go down onto the moving iron and run. She will open her hand over the seam it was taken from, and thirty-seven grains will fall and run with the rest and stop where the rest stop. Within a minute the new figure will be drawn across the cavern end in black. The old one will stand over it coming loose, and the air will go the colour of rust until the lamps are brown smears in it. Under the cart, in the lockers, and against the boilers, a few spurs will stay warm.

She will wait for the first rain, which will fall on the red fronds and the bare iron and the black grain alike. Then she will go.

It will not have noticed her, then or ever. It will notice only that the floor, in new places, has stopped.
