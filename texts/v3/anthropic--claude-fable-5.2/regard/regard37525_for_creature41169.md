It came down through the cloud, and the first thing it did was make itself small enough not to matter.

This took care. Anything of size in that water would bend the note, and where the note bends the still places move, and somewhere a pair would feel their open loop pulled shut before the strand was through. So it entered as less than a shrimp and did what the weavers do: it moved until it felt the least. After a while that brought it to the foot of the central column, where two fish were testing a strand that had no slack yet.

It had come as anyone would, expecting something in the middle. It found galleries, eggs, old braid, and the dead, tied in. It understood that it was itself the only thing for a long way in any direction large enough to be what the rings were around. It could settle there. It considered this for about as long as a weaver considers a strand with no slack, and moved along.

It went through what it had to give.

Eyes. But the only light that ever enters the lake enters through a wound. Eyes would be two organs for seeing damage.

Knowledge. It could let the notched one know that there are other columns, that they stand in rings, that the whole thing has a shape. It thought about what that would be in that mouth: an end that could never be reached, given to an animal that cannot leave an end alone. It would be a loose tooth to tongue for fourteen more years. It kept the knowledge to itself.

The salamanders. It went and stayed by one, head-down on a trunk, coughing into the dark and waiting for the cough to come back. The salamander was six degrees too. It ate seldom and missed often. The power looked for a place to cut where the braid would not come apart on both sides, and did not find one.

Then it grew wary of itself. The pattern was beautiful, and beauty is no proof that anyone inside it is well. So it made itself a pair of lips, pleated and strong, and laid them on a strand.

Slack was what it had been told, an itch that had to be answered. Snug was the itch gone. Under both was the faint sweetness of the growing tip, and along the flank the absence of the hum, and a body-width away the other one, bracing on its elbows. It was a small want, answered a few times a day, in company, for forty years. The power had been to a great many places, and it had not found that in most of them.

It stayed near one whose partner had stopped. That one tied the partner in where it settled, found an end, took it in its mouth, and held. Something moved through that fish, and the power did not pretend to know what it was. It did not take the other side of the loop. It would have had to leave, and then there would be a pair that was one fish. It kept station nearby and did nothing until, days later, another came along the braid testing strand after strand, found an end already held, and drew the loop through.

It thought it might at least tie one loop itself, anywhere, as a courtesy. But each strand gives up its slack every three weeks, and every loop is somebody's turn and somebody's meal. There was not one it could tie that would not be taken from a pair. It had come with everything, and the lake had nothing loose to hand it.

So it went up. It climbed the central column past the snails' depth and the shrimps' depth, went through the roof, and stood among the frogs. They were calling into the cloud, the only animals in the place whose call never comes back. It did not answer them. They were not calling to it, and an answer would have been a new sound in a place tuned to the old ones.

Here, at least, its size was of use. The rain was not made in the lake, and neither was the stone. It followed the rain back to where it is put together, out over an ocean and up a long slope of wind, testing each length as it went. It went down the flanks under the thousand thin falls and felt the slabs. It went beneath the floor, through braid pressed dense as wood, and touched the rock that nothing in the lake has ever touched.

All of it was taut. The stone was going, as it had been for a long time, and the floor was thickening faster. The wind had held its line for longer than the slabs had been round, and it had no give in it now. Far along, further than the oldest braid was old, there was one length that would someday have some. It marked the place. When that length was ready it would come back, take up that much, and draw it snug against what was already there.

Its coming in had parted the roof, careful as it had been. A shaft of grey a hand wide stood in the water under the place where it had looked in. It could have closed this. It knew a great deal, but it did not know the braid, because the braid is in the edges. It left the tear as it was. The weavers came because the rain sounded sharper there, and by the next day the place was closed with braid identical to the braid around it.

At the foot of the central column the loop is snug and the next strand is not ready. The two there move a lip's width along and test again.

Out past the cloud, something has taken up a beat. It is a long one, one repeat of the weather. There is rarely any slack in it, and most of the work is checking.
