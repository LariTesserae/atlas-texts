What comes over the rim next is not cloud, and it does not cross like the swifts. It comes down as anything comes down here, through the thick air, the hanging roots, the mat, with no moment of entering. It has no body. The steam does not part for it and the mat does not dip.

The first thing the visitor does is slow down, and this takes some time. It could have closed this valley itself, or opened it, and powers of that kind have habits of speed. The valley shows nothing to anything that moves faster than a grazer. So the visitor takes a place an arm's length from the old one's branch and keeps it through one clearing of the vents and into the next. It watches the mat lift and settle some thousands of times. It learns to hear the pearling as separate ticks.

Then it reads the line, all four hundred skins, down through glass and chalk and opal to the stone leaf on the floor. It stops a hand's width above the thirtieth cast. There, deep in the banding, is a fleck of opal the size of a rice grain, with a nail on it. One year the animal held so still that the tree's new skin of glass closed over a toe along with the branch. When the warm dome came up and the animal let go, the toe did not.

The visitor could have the toe out of the trunk and back on the foot in less time than a gill takes to flick. It leaves it. The animal has made the climb some three hundred and seventy times on seventeen toes and has never been short of one. The four-toed foot is also the only way anyone has ever told this animal from the others.

It looks into the animal next. There is someone there, though not much of one. There is warmth, the weight of the coat, the branch under the feet, and a hunger that rises over months. Once a minute the used water goes, fresh water comes against the gills, and each time this is good. It has been good some seven hundred million times. The animal does not remember the litter or the air. It does not know it is old. The visitor looks for somewhere to put a gift and finds no room. Everything the animal wants, the valley gives it a hand's width at a time.

The taking is harder. The visitor follows one trail through its last week and attends both ends of it. The grazer knows the taste of felt and a lean toward the brighter water. The pale one, laid along the bark, has its patience and nothing else. The visitor is inside the grazer when the gulp comes, and it finds that there is no moment of it there either. The old one has done this more times than it has skins, and to its own young. Every pale animal in the basin is a grazer this did not happen to, on a trunk it did not choose.

Other meat could be brought over the rim. The visitor follows that forward. A fish would have to eat, and what it ate would be grazers. A hatch of flies would give the swifts a reason to descend, and after the swifts would come whatever follows swifts, and then there would be hunting that could be watched. A gentler valley could be made, but it would be a different valley, and the visitor would have to stay and hold it together by hand. It does not decide that the arrangement is good. It decides that it cannot build a better one out of these parts, and it notes that these are not the same finding.

Last it goes to the dam and reads the wall as it read the trunk. The notch is sound. But two arm's lengths under it, inside the rock, lies a seam of clinker, loose glassy rubble where the flow rolled over its own crust. When the lake comes over the notch, the outflow will work down into that seam within a few wet seasons. Then, in one night, it will carry the seam away, and the level will fall by three times a person's height before morning. The mat will hang in the branches. The top century of every tree will stand in air, with every pale animal still on its branch and its gills stuck flat.

The lava that closed the valley did not ask either. But the lava left the forest something to do, and this would not.

The dam weeps, and what goes through it is lake water carrying the lake's silica. Where it has passed for fourteen centuries, the cracks are lined with glass. It has never passed through the seam, which is sealed dry behind a skin of chilled rock a finger thick. The visitor works the sum three times, because this is the kind of help that can turn out to be the harm. Then it makes a crack in that skin, on the lake side, a hair wide and a hand long.

That is the one thing it does here. The weeping will find the clinker now and leave on every fragment what it leaves on anything that holds still. In a thousand years the seam will be the hardest thing in the wall. It will be banded, and it will ring faintly if touched.

The notch itself the visitor leaves alone. The water will reach it and go over in a sheet no thicker than a leaf, down the outer face where the ferns have already started. After that the level will stop rising. The trees will have nothing to keep pace with. A dropped thing may drift. Eggs as clear as the water may go over the lip and down into a range that has never had a salamander in it. The visitor does not consider any of this its to settle.

It could look ahead and see what the animal will do then. It does not look. That is the only thing in the basin not yet set down in stone, and it belongs to the one who will do it.

The visitor goes back to its place for one more mid-morning. The pearling comes down through the trunks and silvers every leaf, the old one's back and jaw and toes, and the casts below it all the way to the floor. The visitor has nothing for a bubble to form on, and for once it minds. The animal flicks its gills, and a small bright scatter goes up.

Then the visitor leaves as it came. It means to be on the rim in a thousand years, to watch and nothing more.

Inside the wall, about once a minute, a drop goes through the seam.
