Something does take notice. It does not come down through the glass in a shaft of anything. The squares brighten at their usual hour, the weed leans its few degrees, and the governor ticks when its weights settle. The only sign is that for nine or ten days the hall holds one more thing that goes a pace to each turn of the shaft.

The visitor comes in at the north end, up through the feed-wells with the water, because that is the one way in that parts nothing. It could know the hall at a glance. It has found that what is known at a glance gets treated accordingly, so the first thing it does is the round. It leaves the well-house a pace behind the other, through what the other has parted, before it closes. It stands by while each lid is lifted and let fall. It waits under the pavement lights for as long as the feeding takes. In the afternoons it stays on the floor and watches the hobnails go up, and at night it sits beside whichever machine bed the current has chosen.

On the sixth day they come to the catfish. The barbels go over the cryptocorynes, the hobnails, the silt between, and then, at the same speed, over the visitor. The visitor holds still for it. Nothing else in the hall has the standing to ask.

By the time the round comes back to the well-house, the visitor has gone through what it could do:

- **Bring the tenter back:** The buttons would know him, and the watch. But he finished. He went with the loose lime in the first years, and he would be called back to a drowned hall, a body of leaves, and somebody already standing where he would have to stand.
- **Lift it out:** It follows the feet on the glass, so it could be given the street. On the pavement it would be a barrowload of weed, drying. Every afternoon it hangs with its face in the air and has no use for it.
- **Stop the shaft:** Then nothing could be wound. Then also the filaments go out, and a round that goes a pace to each turn has nothing to step to.
- **Put out the lamp:** A word to the street would do it. But the lamp is one of the two things it has that nobody handed down to it.
- **Tell it what it is:** It is finding out, at less than you could read off a rule each night. Nothing suggests it wants the answer sooner.
- **Open the watch:** No.

Then there is doing nothing, and the visitor weighs that longest. The hall is settled. Nothing in it is in want, as far as ten days can show, and a place this settled is rarer than a place that needs saving. The visitor has seen what gets done to such places by those who love them. If the one under the lamp is reaching for the shaft, to be a belt and carry a little power, that is a settlement of the usual kind and nobody's business. If it is reaching for the lamp, the same.

The trouble is that the shaft cannot tell which, and neither, yet, can the one reaching. Whatever touches is taken up, a hand-width an hour. The first touch would settle what the reach had been for, and the shaft would do the settling.

So the visitor looks at what lies between. Where the shaft crosses the south panel it carries two pulleys side by side, as a shaft did wherever a machine had to be stopped without stopping the mill. One is keyed fast. The other is the loose pulley, made to run free on a bronze bush, so that a belt shifted onto it would hang idle while the shaft turned on inside. It has not been loose since the water came. The black skin sealed it to the shaft in the first years, and it has gone round as one piece with it ever since. The tallest strands of the arm stand directly under its rim.

This is tenter's work. A seized pulley is the kind of thing the round was for, when there was something in the cups.

On the tenth morning, with the round begun again and the other away up the hall, the visitor puts a hand to the rim and holds it against the turn. For most of one revolution the whole line labours, and the filaments go down a shade inside their green fruit. Then the skin lets go. It makes a single tick, and it is not the governor's.

At the third pedestal on the east side, it stops where it stands, with a lid half lifted. It stops for one full turn, the way a tenter stops when the note of his engine changes. The note has changed. The shaft carries one pulley the less, and somewhere under the hum is a difference too small for anything to catch that had not once minded these engines. That is the part of it that is his. It is the smaller part, and it heard. Then the lid falls, and it goes on.

Nothing else is altered. The film will creep into the bush within the month and serve better than oil. The arm has its two fingers still to go, and will go them at its own rate. When it touches, the pulley will take it up as the shaft would have, a hand-width, perhaps two. Then the pulley will feel the weight of what it has hold of and stand still, with the shaft turning on inside it. The arm will be held the way a hand is held, and it can leave the way a hand leaves.

If a belt is what it means to be, the fast pulley is a palm's breadth to the east, and weed finds such things. If it is only reaching for the lamp, the lamp is where it was, and past the rim there is nothing in the way but water, and then the glass. Some year there may be strands lying against the underside of the prisms there, five of them or more than five, where anyone who stops under the lamp-post and looks down could see them.

The visitor does not stay for the touch. That belongs to the arm that grew toward it.

It leaves by the street. It crosses the pavement lights from the south end to the north, two prisms and then the next two, and it goes more slowly than anyone has cause to walk there, about a pace to four breaths. Below, something turns its front toward the glass and comes after. For the length of one bay it is not left behind. At the next column it stops, puts a hand to the iron, and takes up the round again.

The visitor keeps to that pace as far as the corner, in case. Then it goes on the way people do, and the prisms behind it are lit again.
