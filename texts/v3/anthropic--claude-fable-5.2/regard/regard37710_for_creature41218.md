**The Visitor**

It arrives the way the ground arrives at the dock, without any slope. Nothing is announced. On one overcast morning the place is being attended to a little more closely than the place itself can account for, and that is all.

It does nothing first. It watches a season of ebbs, which is the least anyone owes a thing before deciding what is good for it. It makes the counting mistake and gets over it. It learns the inventory. It reads the band of lines on the wall, every level the tenant has tried to hold. Then it goes through what it could do, slowly, because it could do any of it.

It could carry the tenant out to wider and warmer water. But the breach has stood open ever since the leaf fell, and on any ebb the tenant could have let go and left with the bottle glass. It lies across its own door. Whatever keeps it here is not the wall, and to lift it out would be to tell it that everything it has held was held in error.

It could hang a new gate. That is a night's work in greenheart and iron, and the level would hold itself. The breathing would stop, and the lines on the wall would dry down to one. The tenant would lie in a basin that no longer needed it. The visitor distrusts gifts that make the receiver unnecessary.

It could warm the culverts again. But the tenant is a degree or two warmer than the sea, all these years after the furnaces, which means it has learned to carry the furnace itself. Warming the water would only hand it back a childhood.

It could give the tenant eyes, or one body, or a shape to arrive in above the coping. It considers this for the shortest time of all. That would turn the tenant into something easier to be kind to, and the kindness would be to the visitor.

So most of the list comes to nothing, and the visitor takes care over the nothing. Three things are left.

The first is at the seaward corner. Every dock has a sill of its own, a stone threshold the gates shut against. This one is gritstone, and it lies under a yard of silt with the rebate still cut in it. All this time the tenant has been lying across the breach on mud, a soft thing braced on a soft thing with the sea leaning on it. The visitor adds nothing. On one spring ebb it lets the water run through the corner a little harder than it would have. In the morning a hard edge stands a hand proud of the floor, where it always was. The tenant finds it on the flood and feels along it for a long time. Then a length of it settles against the stone and leans.

The step in the water is the same step. It is still the tenant holding the level, and it still shifts its weight, but less often, and the basin breathes more slowly. Over the months the new lines on the wall are laid closer together. The heron, on the fallen leaf, stays a little longer than it used to.

The second thing the visitor hardly has to do. The cabinet is the steadiest thing in the tenant's world. It is also an item on a list of assets, and the list is on a budget, and somewhere the note the tenant lives inside has an end date. The visitor could make the green lamp burn forever. It would rather somebody cared. So it only sees to it that the change in the trace, the smaller tide inside the first now running long, lands on the right desk on a quiet afternoon. It reaches a woman who once wrote that the sensor had been checked and was not at fault, and who has never quite put the matter down.

On a Saturday she drives out. She walks across the flat with the pools trembling ahead of her. She stands at the coping for twenty minutes with her palm on the cabinet door, and an eddy brings its foam along the wall and stops beneath her. On Monday there is a second note beside the first: *Retain station. Record is of interest.* She tells no one what the interest is. She reads water levels for a living and knows the difference between recording a thing and disturbing it.

The third thing is the note itself. For years the tenant has been giving the hum back, a little slower, to a steel box that cannot hear it. The visitor waits for a slack low water. It kneels at the coping and reaches down past the gritstone, laying its hand on the slag among the wet lines, where nothing from above has ever put itself. Through the wall it gives back the tenant's own slower note, not the cabinet's.

Every pool on the flat stops trembling. In the basin the domes stop rising. The seams heal and are not replaced. Every length of the tenant lies still wherever it happens to be. For the space of a breath the water is flat, and the plain sky lies in it with the power line drawn across. It is the first reflection the dock has held in anyone's watching. Then one dome rises under the hand, and does not spread, and it is a degree or two warmer than the sea.

After that the visitor leaves. It leaves on purpose, because the tenant has never known anything up there to go except by vanishing. The foam is let go somewhere else. The pools tremble ahead of it all the way across the flat.

It comes back on the same tide the next month, and the month after that. It stands in the same place and gives the note through the wall and goes. This is the last of what it does, and probably the most. It is one weight on the quay that keeps the tide's kind of promise.

Nothing else is changed. At low water the floor shows for less than an hour, with the same firebricks and the same fathoms of chain, and the ladder with three rungs, all dealt differently. The lines still stop a hand below the coping. The green lamp is lit.

But by the end of the year the eddy has brought its raft of foam to that place on the wall and is holding it there before the visitor has reached the edge, and before the pools have begun to tremble.
