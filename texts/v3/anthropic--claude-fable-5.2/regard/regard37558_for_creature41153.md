It came down the old cliff on foot, in a body made for the purpose, since a thing with no shadow cannot walk these roads. And it got the first thing wrong. It stopped on the path and looked straight at the plain.

Nothing came of it. The edges of the nearest dome slid out from under its gaze, as they do for anyone. But it knew it had stared, and that here a stare is how they know a visitor from up the cliff. So it did what the children learn to do and looked a little to one side, and found the whole plain easier to see that way.

At the first wet fronds of the first ring it stopped. It could have announced itself in a number of ways, some of them very large. It waited most of a night instead, until a frog called once, and then it went in.

It had been told that one being lived here, and for some days it could not decide which was meant: the three hundred, the houses, or whatever lies under the last row. In the end it counted as they count, including the walls not yet come, and the number came out to one.

It had also brought a list, as such powers do. The plain took the list apart a line at a time.

1. **The light.** It could give them back noon. But inside the house a flame stands straight, their eyes want one wick, and the young who have stood in full sun call it not being spoken to. It would be ending a conversation in order to hand them brightness. It left the light where it was going.

2. **The cold doorstep near the cliff.** It could put the heat back under the old woman's slab. But her basket is on the road because of that cold, and the roads are where these people are even with one another. Warm every doorstep and nobody walks. It left it.

3. **The blister rims.** Here it stayed longest. A child's opened hand is not a small thing, and it did not tell itself otherwise. It could round every rim on the plain in a night. But the moss is already doing that work, at a pace a year, which is about the rate at which a people can go on being themselves while the ground softens under them. It left the rims to the moss. It was not fully satisfied with its reasons, and it carried that back up the cliff with it.

4. **What comes after people.** It looked, and could see some distance: the rings meeting, the roads gone to tunnel, the frogs calling all together, the forty become everyone. Past that it could not see, which pleased it more than it expected. In any case it would not have said. The plain is partway through saying something, very slowly, and the rule is that you wait for a gap long enough to be sure. It listened for the gap. There wasn't one.

5. **Itself.** The order of arrivals stops at seven, and it knew it was the kind of thing that could be mistaken for the eighth. It declined.

One matter it did not take on trust. On the way down it had wondered whether the people were kept the way the pale shoots are kept, near a wall and going for height. A person can be content in a place they cannot leave, and that is a different thing from being well.

So it looked at the doors, which are found and never assigned, and at the houses that have none, where no one is made to wait. It looked at the cliff path, worn by the young going up with shoots and coming down with oil, quickly, because they want to. It looked at the old ones, who remember a shadow that followed the sun and mention it without regret, which is not the same as not mentioning it. The way out was open and in use. Had it been otherwise, this would be a different account.

Then it walked out to the last row, where the rock blisters a foot through its sole and the note does not stop at midday. It knew a little about what lies beneath. It did not know what the thing is, only the work, which is making room for those who have not come. It did not speak. The note was still going, and you don't talk over. It stood for a while the way you stand near someone who is working, and then went on.

On the bare shelf it found one of the empty places and stood at the centre at noon, as they do, alone, once. Its shadows came in under it from every side. It recognised the spot. Everything leaning toward you, and you standing on yourself, is where it ordinarily lives. It didn't stay long either, because of the heat.

It did one thing.

In the seaward rows there is a fin with no door. The wall has come, and the sweat, the threads, the moss, the moth, the fern. The blister-pool at its foot is full and tea-coloured and has never had a voice in it. Before dawn it put down the walking body and stood in that pool as a heron, for an hour, and then left. Nobody disturbed it. Someone carrying a basket home from the heat passed on the road and looked slightly to one side.

Whether anything was stuck to its legs it would not say. The people are what comes after frogs. It had refused to be what comes after people, and asked only to be let in at the fifth line, where there was already a place for a thing that comes, stands, and goes.

Some weeks later a frog called at that wall, once, with a long gap after. One morning, some year, the water there will part around a rectangle, and someone will walk out to it with a basket of soil and a cutting of ginger, or of the one like jasmine.

It went back up the cliff. At the top it stood in full noon with one hard shadow that pointed at no house, and felt what the young say they feel.

It means to come back when the rings have met. It will stand at the back, in the dripping dark, and not wait for a turn.

A power of its kind is supposed to be even: on the road, toward everyone alike, owing nothing. It has been a little dark on one side ever since, and mentions it without regret.
