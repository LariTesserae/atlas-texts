What noticed the plateau could have done anything to it. For the first year it only looked.

It looked from above to begin with, and that was the wrong place. From above there are no grazers, only wedges of shadow with nothing to cast them. A ground bird is a glint. Trouble and ease are the same silver as the felt. So it came down.

It did not come as anything tall, because nothing tall lasts here past mid-morning, and it brought no voice, because there is nothing on the plateau a voice can do. It came as a grazer. It took the wedge, the wide feet and the plugged ears, and a place in a herd a hundred metres from the next animal. It shaved, licked and stepped. Twice a day it turned in its tracks with the rest. It had its turns on the windward edge, looking at empty felt, and on the lee edge, where it saw everything the herd knew and could tell no one. It stood charged through the afternoons. At night it lay on a dark band with the others, among the hunters' eggs.

What came up through its feet was the knocking, and that was the first thing it looked into, since no one on the plateau could. Strictly, one being lives here. The mat is a single plant and everything else lodges on it, and it has been drinking its own bedrock since before the scarps had their first band. So the power followed the roots down through the dead to the salt and measured what was left. There was enough, many times over, for longer than the plateau has existed so far. It noted the depth, and the age at which it would be worth listening again.

It thought about rain for a very short time. Tens of metres of dead mat have never been wet, and the plateau stands on them. One wet season would start the rot. The smoulder fronts would drown and the towns with them, and the first standing water in the history of the place would lie at the bottom of a sinkhole.

It thought about the wind for longer. The wind keeps every animal from every other. But the mat makes the wind out of its own brightness, and the wind opens the scars the moths drink at, rolls the bales, sows the seed and cuts the pipes. Even the hour is made of it, since there is a stillness only because there is a gale on each side.

It thought longest about the hour. Having stood in it flank to flank with whoever was nearest, it wanted a second one more than it wanted anything else on the plateau. But the hour is as long as the hunters can sit on the ground with their wings half open and not eat, and as long as a flower can stay raised. It left the hour alone. It did not blunt the hunters either. It had slept among their eggs.

By then it understood the choice. It could be a climate here or a neighbour, and the plateau has a climate. So it held itself to what a grazer could do, or a bird, in the places where there was no grazer or bird to do it.

There was an old grazer on a dark band that its herd had left behind. Nothing had decided this. A herd follows the knocking, and the old one had grown too slow to follow. It stayed on the warm strip and cropped the dark felt alone, and its bib grew to the size of a fist and past it, until the jaw would open the width of a stalk and no more. It could still plane a strip if it got the angle. It could not always get its tongue to the wound inside three seconds.

The power could have cracked the amber off at noon from the far side of the sky. But an approach in the afternoon means a crack and singed hair, and the old one had no reason to expect otherwise of anything coming toward it. So the power walked up the band until it stood a hundred metres to windward and raised its rump once. It could not see whether it was answered. The windward animal never can. It waited for the wind to fail and the charge to go down through their feet. Then it walked over, stood flank to flank, and gnawed.

A fist of amber is more than an hour's work, and it took five evenings. Chaff came out of it, and moth scale, and a beetle with its legs arranged, and darker layers from years the power had not been there to see. On the fifth evening the jaw opened as far as it was made to. Then the old one did what a grazer does, which is turn and reach under the other's chin.

The power had thought about this beforehand. A creature that is only ever helped has been told something about itself. That was one reason for the year of feeding the slow way: it had a bib. It was a thumb thick, hardly worth the trouble. The old one worked at it until the first air came from the wrong quarter, and they stepped apart.

After that the power stayed. A herd of two can go at any pace. By day one walked ahead and was watched, after the turn they were the other way round, and in the hour they kept each other's jaws. This went on for four years, which was as long as the old one lived.

For the other thing it was a ground bird as well, for eleven days. A flick with no second had been going up from the same vent-stem for most of a week, at longer intervals each day. Thirty-five kilometres off, past reading, there was another like it. The power did not answer either one. An answer would have been a lie, and a bird that is answered stays where it is, keeping count with nothing. It took a place halfway between them, and when a flick came from one side it opened its wing to the other. It added nothing and held nothing back, and every flash it showed had a bird behind it. Each of them shifted its ground a little toward the light, as they do. On the eleventh day they could read each other over its head, and it shut its wing. In the hour that evening two birds went straight up.

There are things it did not do that it has not finished thinking about. It knew nine days ahead which hem would lift, and there was a nest under it. No grazer can peg a hem, and it kept to its rule. The sheet went up with a noise like canvas ripping. Moths drank at the scar. The bale travelled sixty kilometres with small birds glued into it, and broke, and the hunters stacked. The power does not claim that this settles the account for the nest. It watched, and it remembers which hem.

The old one lay down on a dark band one night with its nose to the night wind and did not get up at the turn. The power lay where it was, a hundred metres off, until just before the morning wind. Then it left, and it left the body, because that is how a grazer leaves. The hunters and the beetles took what they take. Within a season the felt had closed over both from the sides, brighter than the old.

They are in the dead layer now, a hand or two down and going deeper at the rate everything does. If the scarp ever works back that far they will show in section between a mat band and an ash line. There will be two wedges a hundred metres apart, a long way from any others, both facing the way of the dark. Nothing in the section will say that one of them was anything different from the other. Both bibs are gnawed thin, and no animal can reach its own.
