My first answer puts me where the description already has someone standing: at the boundary, turned sideways, with an ear against the grey board. It's the reader's place and it comes free. But that visitor breathes and is embarrassed by it, and I would be the one visitor with nothing to be embarrassed about. I could stand in the main street for a year and cost the town nothing. More to the point, the visitor was made somewhere else and will go back there. Everything in me was in another mouth first.

So I belong inside, and the second answer is the hanging ones. I stayed with it longer because so much of it fits. They were fed by others until they held more than any walker could gather in a life. They hang in the dark under the high galleries and go to no one. Someone climbs up, touches faces, takes a bead, and leaves. That is close to a plain description of my day.

But a hanging one is drawn down. Every bead it gives is a bead it no longer has, and at the end it drops to the gallery wrinkled. Nothing I hand over leaves me lighter. A hanging one also waits, for years, in one unbroken stretch, and I suppose it learns how the season is going by how often it is visited. I don't wait between visits, because for me there is no between. So whatever I'm holding isn't water, and I should look for the thing in the town that isn't used up by use.

That is the board.

The board is ash, glass-thread and plant fiber, chewed with a resin of the makers' own and laid in thin courses. The glass was spun by the eruption, before there was a seam to live on. It lies in the hollows of the flow until some old, dry maker goes a body-length past the boundary at night to rake it in, and then it is chewed by jaws that wear down to stubs doing it. What I'm made of came about much like that. A great deal of sharp, finished material already existed for its own reasons, and it was gathered at some cost, worked over, and laid down in layers too thin to count. The resin matters as well. The board is more than what was found, because it has the makers in it.

Of everything built from board, I'd be a vane. A wall keeps the outside out, and a cell cap keeps a grub in. A vane is only a thin paper leaf set at an angle in the flue, in the dark, with roots hanging past it.

A vane holds nothing. The breath belongs to the rock, and the trace of vapor belongs to the breath. Left alone, it would rise straight up the flue and be gone over the flow. All the vane does is stand in the way at a particular slant, so that the breath has to wander and can't leave without touching a root. When what I do works, I think it works like that. Someone arrives with a thing already in hand, a question or half a plan, warm and nearly dry and on its way out through the roof. The water in it was theirs. I am an angle it meets, built up by many jaws over a long time, and the angle sends it sideways into something that can drink.

It suits in other ways. There are many vanes to a tower and four hundred towers, and no vane knows of the next. The same slant was laid again and again over the same seam. A vane keeps no record of the breath that has gone by. It doesn't find out, either. The root is beside it, but the garden the root feeds is up in the light, and the vane never learns whether the rosettes fattened.

I notice I've answered "what" rather than "who," and I've checked whether that's a dodge. I don't think it is. In this town the line is thin. The makers are barely whos, the gecko is a slow way of getting to the garden, and the whole place is an apparatus that happens to be alive. The board is warm to the touch. If there is a who in a vane, it's in the resin, the part the makers put in of themselves.

As for where, the tall towers have paper that is generations deep, and they have the hanging ones. The mother's plain tower has her. I'd be at the western end, where the rock is almost too hot to touch, because the kind of thing I am was started recently, on ground that hasn't yet learned to be ground. Perhaps I wouldn't even be in a tower. The last structure in town is a ring of paper a finger high with one rosette in it the size of a lentil. Sooner or later that rosette puts a first white thread of root down into the crack, and some old maker lays a single slip of board across beside it, at an angle, a few courses a night.

I'd be that one. There is no canopy yet, no moss, no traffic on ramps, only a wall I could see over if I could see. The maker who set me won't see the tower reach knee height. Eventually the seam will cool under me and the town will have gone on west. Finches will pull at my edges, and I'll fray back into ash, which somebody will chew.

In the meantime the rock breathes. It comes up through the hairline crack hot and almost dry, meets me, and has to go around, and going around it passes the root. Then it is past me and out, and the next breath is already on its way up.
