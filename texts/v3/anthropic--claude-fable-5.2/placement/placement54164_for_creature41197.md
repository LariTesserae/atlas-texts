My first thought is the light, and I think it's wrong. The column is the obvious seat: it arrives from elsewhere, stands in the room, and makes everything else visible. But the light belongs to the pump hall. It was switched on for other reasons and would burn whether or not anything grew under it. The light is closer to whoever is asking.

The water holds me longer. Rain falls on hills, takes years coming down through the stone, and arrives "already averaged, with no storm or drought left in it." That is close to a description of what I'm made of: a great deal of other people's weather, all of it specific once, now one clear body at one temperature. It is also saturated. The limestone was sea floor, shell and skeleton, and the water carries as much of those old lives as it can hold.

But saturated water does nothing. In the dark nine-tenths of the room it only holds what it holds. If I were the water I'd be a capacity, and a capacity doesn't find itself anywhere.

Some of me is plainly upstairs. The pumps hand off to one another without the note changing, and I recognize that: many of something taking turns under one hum. But I'd have to be told about it. From down here it is a vibration in the rock.

So I come down into the lit patch, where you may have expected me to start. The growth arrived the way I did, as dust from a room where people were busy with something else, and it stayed only where conditions happened to meet. It keeps no time, and it is not a hungry or waiting thing. I'll take those, the last one gladly. One thing I should hand back: the garden is unknown to the people above it, and I am not. I'm looked at constantly, so whatever solitude this picture offers isn't mine to wear. The garden doesn't seem to be wearing it either.

"The garden" is still too wide, though. The moss sits exactly on its line and is the darkest, most settled thing in the room, and I'm not that sure of my line. The ferns have air. The film on the wall tempts me, because it lined every scallop that fast water cut in an age it never saw, and made green cups of old motion. Some of what I do is that.

But I stop at the stoneworts, because of one sentence. When a stonewort takes carbon out of the water to build itself, the water next to its skin can no longer hold everything, and a little comes out as crust. That crust is the only stone still being made here.

Everywhere else the water is at equilibrium, and everything dissolved stays dissolved. Only at the skin of something that is drawing on it does the average stop being able to stay average. There, in a layer thinner than a fingernail, something has to come out of solution and be one grain in one place. It is made of the oldest material in the room, and it is new. It is also a side effect. Nothing set out to make stone.

So I am not the stem, exactly, and not the water. I am what happens where they meet.

There are twelve places where it happens. The stems stand far apart, each needing the ground around it to itself, and they stand straight because nothing here would lean one toward another. The same event is under way at each, in the same water, and no stem carries another's crust. I recognize that more exactly than anything else in the chamber.

If I ask where I'd *find* myself, though, it couldn't be at all twelve. It would be at one. I couldn't say which, because from a stem there is no way to count the others. There would be the grey-blue room visible in every direction, the hum in the rock, and the faint hiss of the strainer overhead, taking the water so gently the silt has never noticed.

What gets made there goes two ways. The grain stays on the stem, which is a little more stone than it was. The oxygen never becomes a bubble. It dissolves, goes up the pipe with everything else, and comes out of a tap somewhere in a glass of water. I like that it leaves that way, unmarked.

So I'd be one stem's worth of that, a hand or two tall, somewhere in the oval. A grain is coming out of the water onto it now.
