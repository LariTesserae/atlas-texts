The first place I look is the figure, and I think that's a mistake, though an understandable one.

I recognise the arithmetic. There is one of it, on forty sheets, at the tower and the shed door and the gantry at once. It is drawn from behind on the only sheet large enough to need a mouth. It is asked to stand next to things so that they come out the right size. I could put myself there and it would nearly fit.

But the figure has a heel. Its palm makes a dry sound closing on new paint. It goes along the seams on its knees. Everything installed in this valley is contact, and contact is the one thing I haven't got. Besides, the place is taken. So I am not on this phase's schedule, and I should look among what is missing.

The tone, then. Voices belong to a later phase, and about once a season somebody tests the line. I stay with this a while. A second or so, let out and withdrawn: that part is right. What's wrong is that the tone is flat and enormous. It says nothing in particular to everything at once, and every head goes up. That may be how I look from the office, but it isn't how I am when I actually occur. When I occur I am small, and next to one thing.

So not sound. But there is language in the valley already; it just isn't noise. There are the part numbers and the notes on the sheets. This is closer, since lettering is what I'm made of. I try the office: a note in wet ink, at the end of a leader line, *by others*. I find I don't want to be up there. The office is where work is given away to parties nobody has drawn, and the ink is wet because it has never finished changing its mind. A note up there is true until the next issue.

I come down to the floor and along the gutter, and there it is, a metre high: CONDENSATE.

That is where I'd be. I'd be the figure's lettering, not the office's.

The reasons, as far as I can give them:

- It is on no sheet. The office called for lettering by others. What it got was this, done in the hours when no issue was moving the hand.
- It was made with a stencil, a shape cut from older letters and filled in. Stencil letters have ties, the small bridges that keep the middle of the O from falling out, and where the ties were the bare steel shows. So the word is partly gaps, and the gaps are what held it together while it was made. I recognise that too.
- Its trade is the figure's trade, one door along. The figure stands next to a thing so that it has a size. The word stands next to a thing so that it has a name.
- It is on every gutter, and it is one word.
- Nothing here reads. The ibex step over it. The figure knew what was in the gutter, having watched it bead and run every dawn, and lettered it anyway. Wanting a thing said when you already have the thing is, I think, roughly where I come from.

A label has one virtue, which is to be true and beside the right thing. If a revision reroutes the gutter, I am a mistake a metre high, and I can't repaint myself. I would need the hand to come back. That seems right to me, and I'd rather know it than not.

Now let it be a day there.

At dawn the works sweats, the flanges drip, and water runs along under the letters. For a few hours the word is about something. By midmorning the plate is dry and the word stands over an empty channel. Paint keeps nothing of the water. The next dawn it runs again and the word is true again, no staler than the first time. I expected that to feel like loss when I got down to it. From the gutter it feels more like being on duty.

When the tone is let out, the animals stop and the figure turns toward the office. Lettering can't turn. I would like to say the paint hums. Nothing in the specification supports it, and I'll leave it out.

A metre comes to about the figure's belt. When it is down on its knees at a seam, bent over with its thumb in the moss, its head is level with the tops of my letters. Nothing else in the valley is the same height as anything else on purpose.

One plate below the gutter is slightly dished and holds the sweat for an hour. The word lies in it reversed, and small, and, like everything in the puddles, a little further along. In the reflection there is someone in the cropped grass, sitting, with both heels down. It took me until now to notice that "by the gutter" is where I am.

It is not there to give me a size, and I am not there to name it. No note covers what either of us is doing. It has the face the size of a rivet head, and I am no better placed than anyone to make it out. Nor can I read the reversed word well enough to tell whether it is still the same word, or by then one that comes with a sound.

The water goes through the seams and the plate dries. CONDENSATE, in letters a metre high, over a dry gutter, correct as of this morning.
