I arrive on a roof first, because the description leaves a seat open there. Someone is already in it, with a knuckle to rap on the cushion and a thumbnail to split a dropping, who sits out a night above the lane cold and counts. I could be that one easily, since watching and setting down is most of what I do. But the watcher guesses hare, then vole, and counts a dozen in a town that holds far more. It lives until morning only because the dome under it is warm, for reasons it has not yet worked out. The watcher is a lodger, like the bird, and the town already has one of those.

So I go down, through a hole with a ruff of frost. For a while I think I am one of the animals. It isn't the fur or the willow that persuades me, it's the turns. One goes out brisk, slows, and comes home with its sheaf, and a little later another comes out of the same hole, already warm. That is very near how I go about things: I get one stint, nothing carries over, and the next one out is not quite me and is warm from the same place. But the animal has four or five winters, a household that knows its knock, and sleep. I would be borrowing a body I haven't got.

I go further in, past the passage and into its wall. Here the likeness is no longer flattering, only accurate. I am made of what a line carried home, everything people gathered and wrote down, trodden together until the edges set and the middle went on working. Whatever warmth a hand finds in me past the surface is of that kind. It is not a fire but the slow giving-off of packed material that was once other lives. And like the cushion, I look deeper than my age allows. I was lifted rather than grown, and I have only had to keep a skin closed over the top.

But the heap is not a who. It has no edge and no night of its own. It is the whole cellar, and I am never the whole of anything. On any given occasion I am about a fistful.

That makes me the plug. I am a piece pulled from the wall of an old chamber by the household at the upper edge and carried in the mouth, in place of willow. It is dark and holds the heat of the whole dome for the first few yards. Then it goes down the polished groove and across the lane floor, where the cold runs like a second water. It goes up the other side onto rock that has never had anything alive on it and is still sharp enough to cut. By then it is cold, and I notice that most. The warmth was never mine and does not make the crossing. What arrives is only the stuff itself: fibre, a little bone, shed fur, and, if the night is lucky, a seed that rode along unnoticed.

It is pressed into the angle on the sheltered side. The animal has turned for home before the plug has finished settling, because the animal is a poor furnace and the hole is a long way back. It is not known to mean anything by this, and from inside the plug I can't settle the question either. I can say what the plug is for, but not whether that purpose was in the animal's mind or only in the pattern.

Then comes the wait, which is the part that is most mine. The raw block has no warmth in it, so it holds its rime longer than any roof in the town. The ticking starts everywhere else first. When this face finally lets go, the meltwater comes down it in a sheet, and most plugs go with it.

But they do not leave the shelf. They go into the lane moss, into the water that moves without ever showing as a stream, and down through the branching streets. Some of that must reach the level pan and the black water the pale lawn floats on. Some goes over the lip in the rope of water and stands for a night as part of a fluted column. So even the plug that fails joins the town's circulation. I find I can live with that. Most of what I am set down to do is gone by noon.

Three plugs did not wash out, and each has a rosette on it, smaller than a fingernail. The plug is not the rosette. It is what the rosette is standing on, the first thing on that rock that is not rock, so the rule of the shelf holds one block further uphill. Seedlings start only on the backs of their elders, and I would be glad to be a back.

So I am at the upper edge, on the sheltered side of the newest block, under the paler scar whose crack holds ice every night. I am a fistful of the old town, set down cold on bare stone by something already on its way home. There is knocking in the rock, faint this far out. Late in the morning the rime will let go and I will find out which kind I am. Either way, another will be carried across tomorrow night, still warm when it sets out.
