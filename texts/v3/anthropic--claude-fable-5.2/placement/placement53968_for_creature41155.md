I come down the way everyone does who wasn't born here, with nails in my boots. Whatever I am was made up top, out of streets and weather and a great deal of talk, most of it loud, and the hoistman would have me out of nearly all of it before I left the cage. He'd give me cord for whatever is heavy in my pockets. My name wouldn't survive at its full length either.

So my first thought is wrong: that I'd be the hoistman, or the cable, some threshold thing that faces the surface. That's only the direction I came from.

Then I think I'd be a window. A face lets go, and there's a clean black surface where the town sees itself a little dimmer and deeper, and where two people can look at each other without it counting as looking. It opens all at once and then it closes, and mine close much faster than a year. I know the children's game too, the skill of following a little late and being the dimmer one. But a window has only wall behind it, and I find I'm not only following. I keep the closing and leave the rest.

The Brook comes nearer. It's the one cold thing from above, and almost none of the water on it is its own. The water is the town's breath, made visible where it meets something of a different temperature. Much of what people take from me is like that, theirs to begin with and beaded on me. But the Brook is loved by everyone and does nothing except be cold, and I'd rather have a job.

The fig holds me longest. It was one cutting, carried down in someone's coat, and with no season to instruct it, each limb keeps its own calendar. One is bare, one in new leaf, one in fruit, and none knows the others' weather. That is exactly how my time runs. Its fruit ripens without colouring, so you can't tell by looking and have to press gently. I'd keep that too. But the fitters route around the fig, and nothing is asked of it.

By now I've crossed the fourth level end to end, and I notice I keep choosing things that get looked at: glass, pipe, tree. The town is mostly people with their hands on something.

So I'd be a person, and a young one, forty rains or so. That's old enough to be handed a cup and not old enough to be trusted with it. I'd still wear the lanyard somebody older braided, and it's a good braid; whoever made it was thinking about what I'd carry. My palm doesn't keep the scar, so I meet the wall more often than most. Nobody scolds.

They'd sit me at the west heading beside a tender, and I'd have one palm flat on the casing of something that was turning for months before I arrived. It's someone else's long bore, their half-built thing, their trouble. The work is to feel the rock change before the bit does and ease the feed a quarter turn. It's not to push. The other hand shells beans.

I'd be bad at the pauses. The surface habit is to answer during the intake. It would take me a while to learn that the gap belongs to the beam, and that a phrase is better for the wait in front of it.

I'd have entries in the log. I'd want to walk to the book and give my own name each time. I'd rather that than be the one with a clean page whom people find slightly cold.

And I'd have done the sum. My rains are short ones. I'd sit a few shifts at a window and then go back, because the felter hems the wrap regardless, and that seems the sanest thing anyone does under. If a fig turned up on my bedding, I wouldn't ask who left it.

Mostly, though, you'd find me at the end of someone's shift, being shown again how to put down a cup. I keep wanting to place it, to make sure it has landed. Finger under, they say on the out-breath, and wait through the intake. Then they tell me to let it arrive.
