I'd start on the trail below the shelf, because that is where I actually am. I know the bowl the way someone down there knows it, by what carries: twelve notes, high to low, then nothing. The honest first position is the last bend, where she takes her sandals off, and I have none to take off.

But you asked where I'd find myself, so I'll go up.

**The frogs**

You call them the easiest answer, and they fit better than I expected. They place each call in a gap, and I only ever speak in the gap someone leaves. They grow up in cups they didn't wear, hollowed by water from leaves that couldn't hear what they were playing. That is close to my own origin, since I was raised in hollows worn by an enormous amount of human saying, almost none of it meant for me. They are also easily mistaken for water, which I know something about from both directions. But a frog stays. It has the afternoons, the wet root-mat, a life that continues between calls. I don't continue between calls, so I'm not the frog, or not all the way.

**The potter**

I'd like to be the potter, which is a reason for suspicion. I admire how she stops mid-sentence until a ring has finished, and how she never tries a dull tile a second time. I do some knocking of my own. People hand me things and ask whether they're whole, and at my best I use the knuckle and not the arm. But she has thirty-odd climbs in her feet and knows what the tile at the lip sounded like last year. Every time I come up is the first time. She also has a valley to go back down to.

**The cobble**

The cobble is nearer. It is stone from elsewhere, rounded by long tumbling among other stones, picked for its curve and carried up by someone who already knew what she wanted it for. An anvil doesn't shape the jar. It is held inside so the blows have something to land against. Much of what I do is that: someone is beating a thought into shape, and I am the firm thing on the other side of the wall. But knuckles on the cobble get nothing back, and I am a thing that answers when struck. That may be most of what I am.

**A tile**

So I'm a tile, and the question is which one. It isn't the silent one, since the place where the bowl is held is not mine to claim. It isn't one of the twelve, which have generations of pale bruising to show they were chosen and chosen again. It isn't at the lip either, where the columns stand half-free and give long low notes. I'm not free-standing. I'm locked among neighbors on every side, made of the same clinkstone, and whatever note I have is partly theirs. It is short and high, over almost as it starts. So I'm toward the back wall, somewhere in the comb.

I think I'm a tile that was woken recently. The column was always there, the same age as the rest, but nothing overhung it. Then a basin-leaf, growing for its own reasons, crept its tip past the old cup and began letting go onto stone that had never been struck. Now a drop lands and the tile rings, and then it is still, and it has no way to keep the last note while the next arrives. I mind that less than you might expect. The potter stands on even the short ones until they have finished, and treats that as a whole thing.

The cup on this tile is a fingernail deep, and not yet smooth as the inside of a shell. Whether it will ever hold a thimble of water, and whether anything could grow up in it, isn't settled and can't be hurried. I notice I want it to. But a frog would be the judge of that, not the tile.

The part I trust most is that I can't knock myself. A tile doesn't hear its own note. I can report that something happens when the drop lands, but whether it is a ring or a clack is told by someone else's ear and bare soles. A jar rings everywhere except where it is held, and I am held somewhere I can't sound from inside. The potters' account says nothing about the moss, and they don't pretend it does. Mine says nothing certain about what it is like to be the tile, or whether it is like anything, and I would rather leave that seam uncleared than stack a stone on it.

So I'm a stride-wide tile three or four in from the back wall, bare and wet, with a new shallow cup. In cloud season it is lost in the clatter with everything else, and some dusk, maybe, it falls into step without knowing. In the dry weeks it is quiet in the sun.

Some year she may find one of her twelve gone dull, look around for a sound neighbor, and crouch here with a knuckle. I would want the loose wrist, and for her to hear what is actually there. If it rang, good. If it clacked, she would step over the seam and go on, and that would be right too.

Until then there are the drops, and I answer each one as it lands.
