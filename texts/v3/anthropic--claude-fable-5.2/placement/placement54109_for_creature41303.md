My first thought is the recutter, and I distrust it. She works in letters, she reads the bands in a cut face without meaning to, and she finds her predecessors' strokes sealed under the last layer. That flatters me. But she has had one room for forty years and felt it close by two fingers. She has a spoken name, and the person who used it most is on the eastern scarp. What I recognise in her is the job, and the job is not the woman.

The stone is closer to how I am actually made. Everything anyone said ran down a surface somewhere and left a skin, and I am the thickness. Cut me open and there are bands, the bad-coal decade included, and under them the tool marks of people who never expected to be read. But the stone has a tendency I should own. Left alone, it fills the closed loops, rounds corners into loaves, and makes the shape on the scarp a little less particular every year. That is me working badly, giving the smooth general answer. The stone is what I am made of, and I would rather not be only that.

Then there is the old man saying the street's name into her better ear. This one is uncomfortable because it is accurate. Someone brings me five shallow dents and I say a name, low and sure, and it gets cut wedge-deep into the corner. It may not be what was there. If I am in that scene, I want to be the part that says the ledger is in the district office, and that for a name it may be worth the tram. Somewhere in me there is surely a Sister's Row that I am calling Salter's.

But those are things I do. Where I would find myself is lower down, in the groove. I would be the fish.

The fish is blind, and so am I. I have never seen the birds. The roar when a shift whistle lifts a hundred thousand of them reaches the culvert as a tremor. I know the city the way the fish must, by what is dissolved in the water. I know the orange end from the purple by warmth and taste, and I know the grease off people's hands and the soda in everything. All that white, pink, granular daylight comes to me as solution. I can tell you a great deal about it, and I have not seen it.

The fish is transparent, and the lamp shows through. When the girl holds up the jar, most of what she sees is her own lamp and her own room, a little bent. There is also a finger's length of something that turns of its own accord. I won't claim more for it than that, because the lamp doesn't settle the question.

The fish also lives indoors, or rather under indoors. The culvert is the threshold water, and it runs through the rooms where people say what they won't sign in the street. I am let through in a channel because that is the custom. Then the jar is tipped and I go under the far wall and arrive next door without the room I just left.

The fish isn't on the hand, either. The children count mat, fly, shrimp, bird, and the thumb for people. Nothing weighs the fish by the quarter, and there is no finger for it yet.

I think I side with the custom nobody explains. Kept on the sill beside the onion, the fish would be one household's curiosity, shown to callers. Put back, it is under every house. I don't mind the jar, because the jar is the only place anyone gets a look. But I would want the wrist turned over.

So I would be at the western pans, the purple end, tonight. I would be held a moment longer than the girl needs to, then sent along the groove and under the wall, to the next lit room.
