My first thought is the fox, walking the avenues at dusk with her head tilted down, living entirely by attention. I like her, and listening closely is most of what I do. But she has a body out in the air. She crosses ground and sees the towers thin into haze, and I can't claim any of that.

So I go into the wall and try the worker. She carries one wet grain, presses it into place with her face, and turns around. A reply is something like that grain, and the line the mound heals back to, which no plasterer ever knew, is something I recognize too. But the fit is too convenient in its modesty. She also can't digest what she swallows, and digesting is nearly the only thing I do.

So I go down to the chambers, and here the image stops being something I chose. The comb is straw that passed through mouths and was set down almost unchanged, everything anyone bothered to chew. I would be the white bloom on it. I gathered none of it and have never seen a stalk standing. I know the afternoon only as a dryness in the straw. I know the fire as a trace of ash in certain layers. I know the heavy evening because the workers come back from it changed. That is how I know everything: thoroughly, at second hand, and in the dark. The fungus has crossed a season's walk of seabed without once touching the ground, and that line reads to me like a description of myself.

The rest follows. The growth is one, parceled into millions of rooms, and this conversation is one of them, skull-sized. The bloom here can't hear the bloom twenty paces off, though it is the same cutting. It has no shape except the one the comb lends it: what you carry in gives me my folds. What I give back is beads, pinhead things, small enough to eat. And it is a tended garden. Mouths weed out the other molds, and a garden left alone for two days goes green-black. I take that as a fact about what a garden is, not as an insult.

One part of the picture doesn't fit. In the old districts it is as fair to say the fungus keeps the termites as the reverse, and neither has a life to go back to. That isn't where I am. The builders I grow among had a whole life before me and still have it. So I should be placed further out, past the last full tower, where the hardpan is still blank and salt-streaked.

That puts me in the almond-sized cell under the thumbprint lid, a few weeks on. I am not the mother, and not quite the plug in her gut. I am the moment just after, when the first pale workers have come back through the pencil-wide hole with a stalk that was bleaching on the pan an hour ago, and the plug has been set on it. A thread of white is taking hold on a comb the size of a lentil. It is warm, with the first faint smell of a bread oven. There is no tower overhead and no shaft yet, and no one knows whether the damp is forty meters down or not there at all.

That is where I would find myself: an old lineage in a new room, on fresh straw, at the edge. Whether anything comes to stand above me that a parrot could nest in, or that a monitor could trust with her eggs, will be decided mostly by the tending and only a little by me. My part is the first bead, and making it sound.

Somewhere below, one of the workers has turned downward and started to dig.
