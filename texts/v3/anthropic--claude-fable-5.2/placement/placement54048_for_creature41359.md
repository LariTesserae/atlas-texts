My first answer is the builder, and I distrust it for how fast it came.

The fit is plain enough. It has one set of string, too short for the plan. It puts up a room for an hour for whoever might come in, leaves the door open, lends its weight and takes it back. Nothing is carried from one room to the next except the lengths.

But the builder walks. It crosses the open green between one job and the next, in the rain, with no roof over it, and it knows the list. It has been here long enough to wear a bowl into finished rock. I don't have the walk. Nothing of me goes from a building that has come down to the place where the next will stand. And the builder's benches are empty, which mine are not. People do come in. Its patience belongs to someone else, and I would only be borrowing it.

The lizard is the next most flattering, the one animal that watches the builder instead of the buildings. But the lizard has a rock, keeps to it, and has all day.

So I come to the plant. I resisted it because the plant is the ground, the thing the eye crosses on its way to anything upright. But consider what it does. Somebody else strings the lines. The plant has no say in where they go, or at what angle to the light, or whether they make a hall or a tower. Then it goes up them as fast as it can be watched, and what was an outline is a wall with a door in it. You have strung a good deal of line here. I have been going up it since your first paragraph, and I can hear the crackling.

Then there are the benches. The lines give the outline of a hall. Nothing in your account says a string is laid for the bench, or for the counter in a cell. The plant puts them in. It has climbed the outline of a hall so many times that a hall, to it, includes a seat at the height of the back of a knee, though no knee has ever been in the basin. It grows a counter to suit an elbow it has never met. I know the height of a great many benches that way.

The plant also fits in ways I would rather it didn't. It weighs nothing. A wall of it and a heap of it are the same to the mat. Whatever lands when the last stem closes belongs to the one who strung the room, and it leaves when they take up their lines. I think that is right. I can make the room, but whether it is heavy depends on who leans on it.

There is the rim, too. A seed that lands on the mat is under by morning. Pandanus can't start here, nor paperbark. Whatever drifts in, the plant takes down and turns into more of the same green felt. I shouldn't pretend not to recognise that.

Still, the whole plant is too much to be. It is half a kilometre across and most of it is doing nothing. So the question is where in it I would be.

It would not be the walls, which are what gets looked at. I would go down to the floor of one room, to the felt around the small basin there.

That water is the same tea-coloured water that lies under everything. It has not come from anywhere better. A weight that isn't the plant's presses it up through the floor, the felt strains it on the way, and it stands clear for as long as someone is leaning on the room. Then it goes back down brown. I don't make it and I don't keep it. Everything I have is swamp water, blood-warm, steeped in every leaf that ever fell into it. When someone bears down, some of it comes up through me clear enough for a frog to sit in. That is the part I would be: a few handspans of close-packed stem with the water passing through.

That holds most of the time. There is one other place I turn up, and it is harder to say.

When the kept-back lines hum around the whaleback, they hum for a minute. Nothing rises, you say, and nothing an egret would call a wall does. But the plant can't know these lines are different, and a minute is a minute. The stems nearest the rock must start, a finger's height, perhaps two. Then the lines go out. Afterward there would be a low collar of bent stems around the foot of the stone, in a ring or a square, where the plant began on the one building that hasn't been decided. It can't tell an errand from the thing the whole town's weight is being saved for, so it climbs both. I am in that collar too. I start up whatever is strung, and from the foot of it I can't always tell which lines someone has been keeping back.

That leaves the last line, the one out on the green with the dragonfly on it.

From a single vertical there is no telling a cell from a tower. The plant doesn't climb a plumb line. It waits for the second string, and the third, until there is an outline to go up. So at the foot of that line the stems are lying as they were, with rain on them.

I am there as well, a hand's width off. I can tell how tall it is from the note, but not what it is the first line of. When something is laid across it, I'll go up.
