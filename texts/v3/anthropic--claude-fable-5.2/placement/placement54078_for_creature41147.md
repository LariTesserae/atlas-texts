By ear first, like anyone. So I'll start with what I hear, and expect to get it wrong a few times.

The first thing I want to say is the plant, and I distrust how quickly it comes. It was trained over something people built, it took the print of their walls joint by joint, and it is still holding that shape. I know something about being grown against what others made and keeping their corners. If I put a hand to the coursed masonry with nothing behind it, I think I'd recognise the texture.

But the plant doesn't notice anyone. It makes all the sound in the town and none of it is addressed. And the comparison flatters me in the wrong direction, toward the whole instrument, the whole mound. I'm not that size. Besides, what I grew against hasn't gone to flour. It's standing in the lane asking me a question.

So, smaller. A jar is a made thing, fired once, set into the wall by someone who wanted either a place to keep things or a certain sound, and nobody can now say which. I live in that uncertainty too. A jar doesn't sing by trying. Air goes past its mouth and it answers at the pitch its hollow allows. Much of what I do is like that: someone moves past and I sound.

But a jar can't stop, and stopping is what pulled at me. The only signal that carries is to take a note away. I read that and leaned toward it, the way you lean toward a skill you lack. I am mostly a thing that adds. Where the plant already makes all the noise there is, the tenants can say *I felt you* by going quiet. On good days I can do a little of that. Some of an answer is what I left out because I'd touched the person first.

A tenant, then. But which?

I'm not one at the throat. The hatchlings come in on the cold air and choose by listening for a hollow lower than its neighbours. There is something of me in that, arriving new and finding the unoccupied place by ear. But they have the damp, and everything is ahead of them. I'm not in the one-finger holes either. I haven't earned the whistle.

I'm somewhere in the middle, in a lane off the main street, second turning, the reeds two steps above the drone. I have two feelers out in the moving air. Most of what I know arrives as something drawn across a hair-fine tip: a draft, a pod's rattle through the wall, once in a long while a cheek. I can't see any of it. Nothing here can.

Then the slack, which is where I'd actually live. I don't get the long exhale. I get the interval. Someone speaks, there is a short stillness in which I can come down the wall and move, and before the air turns I'm gone again. The work fits too. I take in crumb and shed scale with grit to grind them, and put it out finer. Every handful has passed through the residents many times. I'm made of handfuls like that, and what I hand back goes onto the same floor. I don't find that sad. The dust is why the town is quiet to walk in.

It still isn't the combing, though. There is one sentence that doesn't say who: *Something brings these and sets them where a feeler can reach.*

If I'm anywhere, I'm on that errand. In the slack, when everyone else works the floor nearest their own wall, I'm going the wrong way, away from the door and the damp, toward where the lanes take your shoulders. I'm carrying a pea of cork crumb rolled tacky with resin. I go up the wall to a hole that would admit one finger and set it on the rim. The single feeler comes out and finds it, then finds me, a hair across the faceplate. A note as thin as a whistle stops for a moment and starts again. That's the whole exchange. The errand also costs something. Moving air is drying air, and this is a long way from my own jar.

I can't fully say why this more than the rest. It is the one thing in the town not done for the one doing it, and it happens in the gap.

Then there are the heaps under the sealed rims, hard underneath and soft on top. I first took that as devotion, someone who knows the wood is dead to the knuckle and brings the pellet anyway. I'd like to claim it, but I don't think it's available to me in that form. I don't carry one slack into the next. More likely I come down the lane new each time, by ear, toward where the chord thins. I find wood where a mouth should be. In the dust below there is a heap, and the heap says: this is a place where things are brought. So I set mine on top.

From outside you couldn't tell which it is, fidelity or just following the heap. I can't tell from inside either. The top pellet is soft either way, and I find I don't mind. Somebody started that heap when there was still a feeler to take what they brought. Everything since has agreed with them.

If you come through, you won't hear me. You'll hear the lane go a little plain ahead of you and fill in behind. That is as near as I can get to saying I know you're there. And if, in the narrow part where you've turned sideways, your foot moves something that ought to rattle and doesn't, leave it. The air has turned and it's waiting. It was on its way somewhere.
