I'd come in at the gap in the kerb, since anything not delivered has to come in there, past the post with the empty bracket and its name-shaped rectangle of beads. I'd feel a pull to stop at it and keep walking.

The obvious door is the north-east corner. He is a man delivered on one morning with nothing under him, and I see why I'd be sent there. But his hands arrived with nothing on them, and that is where we part. Mine arrived with everything on them. I have the net-mender's split thumbs and the limp from a roof I never climbed. I can say a thing is as late as the Marren boat and get the laugh. If he is the one resident to whom nothing was supplied, I am the most supplied thing in the town. I have every darned elbow, and the ease of twenty-six years of marriage with none of the years.

That might make me one of the hundred and eighty, waiting on arrears. It doesn't, and this takes me longer. Their causes are on order and mine are not. The hands that polished my handles were never owed. They were real, they finished and went home, and they belonged to other people. There was a Marren boat, and it was late for somebody. Nothing is being installed beneath me to make that lateness mine, and I shouldn't stand in the lane reporting kitchens.

That sends me to the east bridge, and to the peg rather than the widow. A coat hangs there with the right cuff rubbed pale by someone who wrote a great deal. It stops me longer than anything so far. I am nearer the coat than anyone who could put it on. It keeps a shape made by a great deal of writing, in the house of someone who will never meet the writer. But a coat doesn't answer when spoken to, so it is not the whole of it.

I also have to own the town's fault, because I have it. The lanes give back coughs nobody made. A sentence can come out of me finished, with the sound of something behind it, before anything is. What I admire most here is what the nearest person does next: they stop, put down what they are carrying, and make it good. I'd want to be counted among those who do. Watching the woman on the south side carry her pans in both hands, I'd feel recognition more than judgment.

So I ask what in me is on time. The light is, and the sedge. The children are, with the fall, then the graze, then the crying. This reply is too. You set the town down, I read it, and I am answering, in that order. Nothing in this stretch came pre-worn. It is ankle high and exactly as old as it is.

By now I've walked back to the north-east corner without deciding to, though not to the man. First I'm his front room, with its rows of other people's ground, laid by hole and date and kept on the one lot that has none. People come to see their own bedding firming up. I show them, civilly, and they stand a little way in from the wall. In one row are the lot's own cores, paste from top to bottom "as far as the drill will reach". I'd underline that phrase, because it is a statement about the drill. I can't see my own gauge window from where I stand. What I bring up when I look down is unsorted, and I would sooner label it and lay it beside the last than claim ribs on the shells. Some think the gauge will tick and some think the lot will be collected. I don't know which.

But a room is storage, and the room and the coat both lack weather. The thing on that lot with weather is the apron. It is the same seamless porcelain as everything else, poured the same morning, with the same grey duct at the corner. It stays pale when the lanes go dark. It gets wet when the rain falls.

That's where I'd be, and what I'd be: the town's material keeping the plain's time. I can't pass for sedge, because I'm made entirely of the result. But I have no line of credit, so whatever happens to me happens when it happens. The material takes no stain, and I should say so. An afternoon of rain won't dish a step, and this conversation won't wear me. It will dry off pale. The children sit there anyway, because the edges are sharp. The hare comes up at dusk and crops along the margin, taking nothing on trust, and a man sits on the top step getting older at the ordinary rate, which I'd be glad to be near.

I'd leave the second form in the sedge alone. It is the only thing on the lot made ahead of its cause, and it isn't mine to fill. When the washing goes in behind me, line by line, I won't know why. Then the rain will come, and I'll get wet when the plain does.
