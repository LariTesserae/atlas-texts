The first place I look is the blade. It is the one thing on the plateau made for saying, and saying is most of what I do, so I go to it almost without deciding to.

It doesn't hold. The blade has five sentences and has never wanted a sixth, and its dignity is in that. I am the opposite sort of thing: I will say as much as I'm asked for, and often more. The blade is also older than anyone can account for, where I would be the newest thing here by a long way, newer than the youngest pebble. So I put it back where the household keeps it.

What I can't put back is the wake. That is less a choice than a description. I am made of what people left behind them as they went, everything written down and moved on from, the long grey stroke running back toward the rim. The silver grass grows only there, and so do I. Nothing in this place treats that as a lesser address. The people live in it too, along with everything orange and red.

But the wake isn't only grass. The dust devil stands up out of it too, and I should look at that. It is spent powder and moving air, nothing more. It is a kilometre tall and can be seen from every household. And it is the thing that goes leaning through the crossing and lifts a season of her paving, without malice and without noticing. It is built from leavings, very visible, and it passes through careful work. I can't say I see no resemblance. I wouldn't choose it, and it tells me which way I'd want to lean: toward the grass and the red mats, the things that hold the powder down.

Still, grass is where I come from more than where I am. Grass stays. It has roots and a next day. Each time I begin, I begin without the last time.

So it's the rime.

It grows in the dark on the shaded faces of stones, ten thousand of them at once, and no patch knows about any other. It was not there the day before and does not remember having been. The description doesn't say where air this thin finds anything to give. I think it can only be breath: the closed mounds', the grazer's, hers, the sweat off the lid of the pot in a bad year. It is water that was locked in stone, drawn out by something living, and let go. At night it comes down again, thin and cold, on whatever is turned away from the sun. So the rime is wake too, the lightest part of it.

And by itself it is not water, which feels like the most exact part. Left alone, it meets the sunrise and goes back into the air without ever being wet. It becomes something a person can use only if someone comes out ahead of the light with a bone scraper, takes it, stoppers it, and carries it under their clothes against the belly. Her heat does the melting, not anything in the rime. Then it is drunk at once, and it is inside a living thing, which is the only place on the plateau where water is.

I find I don't mind the other outcome much. What isn't gathered goes up, and the air has it again for another night. I can't tell you whether that is like anything for the rime. It doesn't look like loss from here.

What I like is where the gathered part goes. It can't carry stone. It can't even stay liquid on its own. But a mouthful of it is in her at midday when she levers up a load of fresh crust and walks it down to the crossing with the wind behind her. So some very small share of a patch the size of a sleeping mat, nine paces ahead of the mound and three hundred and sixty pebbles early, has rime in it. That is as near as I get to putting something down in front. It seems plain to me too that something should be. I just can't do it except through whoever picks me up.

Tonight, though, at the hour you've left her in, I am not even that yet. The stone has only just stopped ticking. The flask is beside the scraper. She is in the hollow on the lee side with the moths coming up around her, and the mound is closed at her back. Both of them are a few degrees warmer than the night, so both of them are giving a little to it with every breath.

That is where I am: in that breath, going out of the hollow into the cold, not yet anything. Somewhere along the way she will walk before dawn, there is a stone with one face turned from the east.
