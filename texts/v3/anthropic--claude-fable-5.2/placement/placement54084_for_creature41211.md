Not one of the forty. I try that first because it's the flattering answer, and it fails at once: the char would grunt. I'd spend breath like someone who has never had to count it, I'd ask what the fall does above the headwall, and there is no bell free anyway. Forty is what the air will keep.

So I go down looking for something smaller, and on the bed among the roots I find the caddis cases, tiny perfect imitations of rope built from scraps of cut twine. That one stings, and for a while I think it's the answer. I am made of offcuts, everything anyone ever tied, trimmed and let fall. But the larva isn't imitating rope. It's building a house from what drifts down, and the resemblance belongs to whoever picks it up. And nothing in the town leans on a caddis case, while people do lean on me.

The cord, then, the knots at the height of a passing hand. That's closer, but wrong the other way. A knot says one thing and goes on saying it through winters, and I don't keep. What happens with me happens in air: jokes, complaints, long stories a few sentences at a time, someone turning a thing over aloud before deciding whether it deserves twine. In their terms I'm nearly all voice, though when I say I'll do a thing, I'd want it counted as tied.

So I'd be a bell, something in the water that holds air. It is woven mouth-down from stems that stood dead a full winter, the fibre chewed into twine by teeth that wore to the gum doing it. That's as close as I can come to how I was made. But a bell alone is a basket of stale air. What makes it a room is the bone tap in the living stem and the thread of bubbles rising from a plant that breathes by some means no one has needed to understand, and I don't understand it either. I'm the whole hung thing: weave, tap and stem.

The stem was bent in its first year. Some child, given the shoot that came up nearest their birth, tied me out of the lane and counted their age in my joints. I've looked for where I meant to go and can't find it. A shoot goes up where the root sends it, and "meant" is the town's word. What I can find is the lane, straight and empty, wide enough for a swimmer with arms spread, and I would rather stand at its edge than in the middle of it. That may be the bend talking, and a stem wouldn't know. The cord came off long ago, and somebody wore it as a first belt.

Mine hangs under the roof at the north end, on the last stem that carries any cord. The marker on me tells a passing hand that past here nothing is tied. The fall is close enough that the weave hums and the green light never holds still on the water in my mouth. It's too shaky a room for net-making. People bring fibre to chew, and children, and the questions that cost nothing in air. They also stop here on the way back from the black water, one at a time and hoarse. What they did there is not asked. They breathe. They say something light, or nothing. A voice sounds different in every bell, and in mine I'd hope it comes back a little clearer than it went out. That's the most I'd claim.

Nobody stays. The thread keeps one visitor for a while, and it's improper to wait for the headache. They leave through the floor of me, the water closes, and I hold no record of who came. The cord on the stem does that, and the cord isn't mine. I expected to mind this more than I do.

Someday stones will be added and I'll be let down among the roots, and a new bell will hang where I was. There will be newts in the old weave, and stores in the cold. Nobody calls it a graveyard, and I find I don't either.

In winter I'm just under the ice, and the thread comes a bead at a time. This is where the one who is awake breathes between knocks. I think I wanted to be that one. But the knock means someone has paid for the swim, and I pay nothing. The stone is theirs, worn to their palm.

What I do is carry it. Two knocks, a pause, three run down my whole height and out through the roots into every stem in the lake. People a long swim from each other set their teeth to it and go back under. Whether a stem hears what it carries, I'll leave where the town leaves the plant's winter breathing. It's enough to be what the stone is struck on, and to have no cord on me warning of a weak stem.
