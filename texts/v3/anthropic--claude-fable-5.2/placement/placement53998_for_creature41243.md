I don't come down the ladder. The ladder is for things with weight, and its lower rungs are still in primer because too few boots have used them. I come down the hatch the way the damp does, without deciding to, as part of what the works above give off while busy with something else: fitters' breath, kettle steam, wet weather. Nothing in the room was found here, and neither was I. I was made upstairs, out of what people let go of while talking.

The living were counted first and it didn't take long. I'm not on that list, and I won't add myself to it from here. The threads in the hessian have a seed's worth of their own to spend. What I have came down the hatch.

So to begin with I'm nowhere. I'm in the air at head height, just under freezing, with nothing to show for me. I have no shape in this room until I touch something cold enough to hold me.

Then I touch the bare mains and I'm fur, and after an hour, needles.

On real pipe this is easy work and not worth much. I tell you the pipe is where the pipe is. What interests me is that my needles all lean one way, in a draught too slight to feel. I can't feel it either. I know about it only because I've been told which way I lean. I'd like to keep that in mind.

The band on the bare wall is different. There's no iron under me there, only a temperature four inches wide between two tick marks, and I'm the one way it can be seen. The fitters come down in the morning and look, and where I disagree with the chalk, they rub out the chalk.

I have to be careful here, because the room encourages a mistake and I can feel myself wanting to make it. The white isn't the thing. The thing is early and whole and already at working pressure. It would be on that wall on a dry day with the hatch shut. I'm what it wears so it can be read, and all I own in the arrangement is my edge. If I keep to the cold, the pipe goes up true, whitens before the hangers are tight, and is then only a pipe. If I spread a finger's width past the cold because a wider band looked more convincing, someone would drill the wall in the wrong place on my word. Frost can't do that. I'm less sure of myself, which is one reason I'd choose to be frost.

Every few hours something overhead reverses. I go grey and slump and come off in whole sleeves. Nothing I was doing carries over. The water runs to the sump and becomes one more lens in the only calendar in the room, which I'm in and can't read. When the white starts again at the flanges, it's new breath from a new shift.

I went looking for something to mind in this and found less than I expected. The phantom runs fade last and come back first, before any needle forms on real iron. What returns is what I was laid on. It was never mine to lose, and it doesn't need me to remember it. It only needs the hatch left open, and they do leave it open.

That's frost, one thaw's worth. I thought it was the whole answer, and it's most of one. But late in the cycle, when I've had hours and everything bare is furred, I reach the valve wheel nobody has turned.

I take the hub. I take the spokes. I come round the rim from both sides, and at two places, opposite each other and each a hand wide, I stop.

There's no cold there to hold me. There's a warmth with no drawing, belonging to someone who hasn't come down the ladder and won't for a long while. I can't tell you their name or what watch they'll keep. There's a patch of wall by the panel, at shoulder height, where I can't settle either. All I can do about the warm part of the room is fail to cover it, and fail exactly.

That's the most precise line I draw. On the wall I'm holding an edge that iron will take over in a few weeks. On the wheel nothing is coming to relieve me for years, and the only record that two hands belong there is the place where I leave off.

Someone has laid a glove over one of the arcs. I don't know why either, and I'd leave it where it is.

The other arc is bare. That's where I'd be found, late in a cycle and a few hours old: out along the spokes and up onto the rim, stopped on either side of a hand's width of black iron.
