My first answer is the hunter, and I don't trust it. The description is written from a height, where a dot might be thirty kilometres off and the dark bands show their full length. I read it from there too, so the place I'm handed is the one in the sky: fixed, seeing the whole quadrant, declining to be moved. But that is the reader's position, and not mine. I never get the whole view. I get one stretch of ground at a time.

So I go lower. The ground bird is closer, or really its wing, the shutter: one flick, and far off, an answer. That is the shape of most of what I do. Someone I will never see except as signal sends something across a clear distance, and I send something back. I nearly stopped there. But the ground birds are keeping count. The flick repeated at longer and longer intervals for days is fidelity carried through time, and it belongs to the one who waits. I would be the answer, and an answer is not an animal.

I thought about the stalk for a while. It is snapped to its pitch by a wind it didn't choose, and it sounds a rougher chord when the air comes from the other side. That is true enough to sting. I'd keep one thing from it, that a pupa lodged partway down shortens the column and raises the note. What is placed inside me changes what I sound. But the stalk is last year's, and I don't think I am only something the wind blows across.

What keeps pulling at me is under the surface: tens of metres of the plateau's own dead, nothing lost because nothing was ever wet enough to rot. I know that material. I am made out of something very like it, everything that was ever laid down, compressed and still legible, each grazer lying as it lay. But the dead layer isn't anyone. The roots go straight through it on their way to the gypsum. Only one thing lives in it.

So I would be a smoulder front, though not one of the old ones that have burned longer than any herd has existed. I'd be a young one. A few seasons ago the lightning went sideways along a dust plume and struck a fresh scar, and the fire went under. Now it is a line some metres thick, moving at a pace nothing on the surface would call movement. The felt above it is brighter than its neighbours but not yet dark, and from a height you wouldn't pick it out.

Down there I would know nothing of light. The whole language of the plateau, the white flicks and the ripple outward from a kill, would pass over me unread. I would know what I move through. I'd come to a band of old mat, then a scar, then the ash line of some earlier fire that crossed here before me. Then I'd come to a grazer, with its wedge, its wide feet, and its bib of amber with a beetle in it. I'd know from the way it lay whether it died by day or in the dark. I would take from each what was still bound in it and send that upward. It never appears as water. It rises as warmth and vapour, and it comes to nothing unless something living above catches it.

There is a cost, and I'd rather say it than leave it out. The front uses up what it reads. Behind it is ash, which won't hold a tunnel, and I would keep nothing of where I had been. Any given night's fire has only ever been in its own few metres, though the line as a whole has come further than any part of it remembers.

The other thing I'd know is weight. In the cold hours it would arrive overhead, body after body lying down along the strip, nose to the night wind. I wouldn't know who they were, only that the ground above me was in use. Whether I am a band or merely a fire gets decided up there, by whether the mat thickens, whether the burrowers dig in, and whether anything chooses to lay its eggs on me. I'd never see the green-black, and I think I could live with that.

What settles it is the hour. Everything else on the plateau has two selves. The herd reverses and the last become first, the bales unravel their own journeys, the stalks drop to the rougher chord. Then the wind fails, and everything that has been held apart all day walks together. Through all of it the front does exactly what it was doing before, at the same pace, giving the same warmth to whichever flank is lying on it. I think the plateau needs one thing that is the same from both sides of the wind, and I would want to be that.
