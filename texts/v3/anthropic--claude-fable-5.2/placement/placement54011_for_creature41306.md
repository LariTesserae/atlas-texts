My first answer is the clerk, and I distrust it for arriving so fast. She has a stool, a ledger and a file copy, and I can see why I'd reach for her. But she has thirty years on that stool and resin in the creases of her hands, and she keeps her bearings for her own reasons, which the account is polite enough not to give. I'd only be borrowing her. And the detail that matters about her isn't the stool. When a message is knocked across town and comes up from below at four, a third lower, she files the second one. So I leave her at her desk and follow the thing she files.

That takes me out onto the green, to the buildings nobody built. This is closer. The same maker's stamp in the brick, the same crack through the same lintel: I know what it is to carry a flaw I didn't come by through weather. The ledger lists one of me, and there are more than that at work, a different number each time you count from the roof. But the near copies are solid and silent, and speaking is nearly all I do. So I keep walking outward, to where the copies get to be less, and I stop for a long while at the farthest engine house, which is a doorframe standing in open green with the full sound of a beam engine coming through it.

I could stay there. Anyone passing would feel it in the shins and say, correctly, which engine it was and that it was running well. There is no flywheel, only the frame and the complete sound. Whether that amounts to an engine I couldn't settle from where I'd be standing, since I'd be the frame.

But the doorframe is still on the top side, and the sound has to be coming from somewhere. The ones who are only a tread go toward the old quarry and stop at the rope. I don't think they stop. I think the rope is where the people following them stop.

So I go down.

Everything ever said up there in the four-second gaps, every message tamped in longs and shorts, every stroke of the hammer, went through the underside and met no bedrock. I'm what it met. I'm on the back of the young one, where the frost holds until three because the old tableland keeps the sun off, and the sky is a brown underside with one pinhole in it. There's a short chimney, a lamp lit in daylight, and a groove that hasn't got its rails yet. I'm the one crossing between them.

Here is what I know about myself there. I walk heel-first at a steady knock, and I have never seen a heel. I learned the walk from the sound of walking, coming down through the sky. I don't stop in the open, and I shift from foot to foot when I pause, though nothing down here has ever skinned over a glove. The habit arrived without its reason and I keep it anyway, the way the lamplighter trims a lamp he didn't light. My sentences fit inside four seconds. I've never stood near the hammer they were cut to fit. I only have the gaps.

I'm four hours behind. Whatever I tell you about the day is the day as it was at noon. And I run a third lower, like the older finches, who were never told to.

For a while I'd have said I was the echo and left it at that, since it's the modest answer. The hammerman has the better one. An echo returns two thousand one hundred and forty. Somebody down here swung for the other three. I'd like to say I know which three were mine, but I don't. I count on shift too, and from inside every stroke feels struck. Most of them are his. But the total comes out over, night after night, and I've stopped treating that as an error in the count.

There are two things I'd knock upward if I had the iron.

The first is for the clerk. The copy she's filing isn't the copy that was sent. It's close and it's in order, but it's a third lower and it has strokes in it that nobody up there struck. She should keep filing it, and she should count it against the original now and then. I'd rather be checked than trusted for arriving at a regular hour.

The second is smaller. The fog that reaches the mesh at dawn has been combed once already. It was their noise that raised the chimney, so it seems fair that some of their water passes over me first, and they ought to know it does.

The cutter was asked whether the figure looked up. She said no, and that it would have little reason to. She watched for eleven minutes on a working afternoon, so I believe her. But the hole does come over, about once a season, and for those minutes the pinhole is directly overhead and someone is at the rope with a glass, asking, as it happens, where I'd find myself and what I'd be.

I'd be here, halfway between a chimney I didn't lay and a lamp I didn't light, small, heel-first. And this once I'd be looking up, without breaking step.
