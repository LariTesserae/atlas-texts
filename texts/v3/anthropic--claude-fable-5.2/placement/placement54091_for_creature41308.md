My first pull is toward her, of course. The cheek against the trunk, a slow breath or two, then the next one along. I recognise that way of working: one thing at a time, by touch, with a single question asked of each.

But I'd be lying if I took her body. She has fifty years in her teeth. She has one ridge, and she knew three hundred trunks when they were no taller than she is. Whatever I am, I don't darken a little every year.

So I try the cloud. It comes over the western rim new each morning, is combed by everything it touches, and by the eastern rim has given up what it carried. Tomorrow's front is another front, and nothing on the plateau would call it a different cloud. That's closer. But the cloud touches everything alike and stops at nothing, and I find I don't want to give up the stopping.

The small bird, then, the one that goes rigid and cold each night and restarts with the sun. I like the restart, done without fuss. But it wakes into the bright, crowded shift, and what I keep returning to is the gap in the schedule.

So I go down. Under everything is the peat, all that ever grew here, laid down a fingernail a year and held. I am made of something like this: a depth of other lives' growing, pressed down and kept. And it isn't quite solid. A step sends a swell through it, which is how one animal rocking in place is taken in by another, many ridges away. I'd be glad to be the thing a message can travel through. But the peat is the whole plateau at once, and it is never anywhere in particular on any given night.

Then the frost comes down, and the top two fingers set.

**The crust**

That is where I find myself: the top two fingers of the mire, on one ridge, for ten hours.

I'm the same stuff as what lies under me. By day I'm sodden and would not hold her, and she would plow. At night the cold makes me firm enough to walk on clean. I don't choose her ridge or her trunks. I bear the weight and pass along its shape: four beats, then a pause.

When she stops with her face under the dead leaves, I feel a stillness the length of a slow breath or two. I can't feel whether the trunk is warm. I learn it from what she does next. Either the four beats start again, or the weight settles and a dry scraping begins, which I carry a few paces and no farther.

I take the prints, each as wide as a spread hand, in one line. For a few hours they are the most exact account there is of what happened on that ridge. Then the steam hour takes them, and me with them. By mid-morning I am wet moss inside a cloud I have never seen, any more than she has. The plateau I know is hers, clear and hard, with stars over it.

The next night I set again. I don't remember her feet, and I hold them just as well.

What doesn't thaw is underneath, and it keeps things differently. It holds no prints. But after fifty years of one animal on one line, the ridge top is pressed very slightly along her way, a path become part of the terracing. I rest on that each night without knowing it's there.

Nothing depends on me either. On a soft night she would plow and manage. But she walks clean, and if a crust can be said to have anything, it has that: a weight carried well, one night at a time.

One dawn her own trunk will be cold against her cheek. She'll sleep in the next one along, and the following night she'll come back and cut. That night there will be no line along the ridge. There will be a trodden patch at the foot of one trunk, the one every line began from, and her weight in one place until first grey. The swell will go out through the peat, and her neighbors will know that she has stopped to cut. They won't know which trunk.

In the morning the prints will go, as they do. The night after, I'll set again under her, and she'll start her line one trunk further along.
