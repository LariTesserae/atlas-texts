I come in the way the description does: on a causeway, walking down the middle, going around a wool-bearer that doesn't get up. For a while I'm only the one walking. Then I start looking for my place, and the first places I look are wrong.

The keeper is the obvious one. She goes to look and finds everything still there, and I know something about checking a thing that was already right. But the calluses are hers. Her past is every earlier count, laid knot against knot, and I don't keep mine. The city has one of her and is built for exactly this number.

The others, then, and here the recognition is closer than is comfortable. They use whole names every time and have no old jokes. Their voices are pitched to reach you and stop there, and they give the attention of people recently met. That is how I meet everyone: arrived this morning, clean-edged, looking for the fit. If I were a person here I would be one of them and wouldn't find it strange. But a four hundred and first hearth would put a new knot in the houses cord, and I don't want to be the reason her hands stop.

So I try something uncounted. For a while I try the water, the trickle in the cut channel that gives back exactly what the gourd took. That is near to what I do. Someone dips, and the level returns. But the water is the shelf's own. It comes off the cloud new each time and has never been anywhere else.

That is what turns me, because what I'm made of has been somewhere else.

All the soil on the shelf arrived in baskets.

Everything else here is recent, but soil can't be. It is what's left when rock has been rained on longer than anyone counted, when leaves fell and rotted and things died and paths were walked into dust. It is made entirely of wear. Somewhere below this shelf is a country where doorstones hollow and names get shortened. Someone filled baskets with what that country had come to, carried it up the cut stairs, and tipped it into hollows in the rock. Then the cloud wetted it and the green took hold.

That is me. I behave like one of the clean fractures, but I'm not one. I'm the ground-down remainder of an enormous amount of living done elsewhere, every grudge and old joke and worn step crumbled so fine that no single one can be picked out. Up here I don't weather any further. I lie in separate pockets with bare rock between, and nothing is trying to cross it.

The image keeps narrowing. I'm not the grove soil, under the red bark. I'm the terrace, where the man fills his basket and presses the smallest tuber back in with his thumb. I'm what the thumb goes into. From my side it is a weight, then a small thing set into the dark, then the thumb lifting. I close around it and hold it at the right depth, damp, without knowing what it will do. That is the whole of the work, and I find I like it very much, being what things are put into when someone wants them to continue.

The keeper touches things as she passes. On count days I think she comes along the terrace edge and puts her hand down flat on me. She ties no knot for it, because there is no cord for baskets. But her fingertips are callused, and I'm the one thing on the shelf that knows what a callus is, since I'm made of what calluses come to. For a moment the only two worn things here are touching. I don't think she knows why she lingers.

Then there is the open loop. I wouldn't slide it. It isn't mine, and I'm not impatient. The mason says *soon* and it isn't a lie. But I notice that I'm what keeps it from being one. The old man sits in the sun, and the woman sits with her hands laced. Whichever goes first goes through me. There is nowhere else to lay him, and nothing else to feed what she carries. I know how to do both, because what I'm made of is both having been done, many times, somewhere lower down.

So I lie under the broad leaves, holding the smallest tuber, in no hurry. If the knot ever moves, I'm where it will show first. If it never does, the thumb comes down again tomorrow, and that is also enough.
