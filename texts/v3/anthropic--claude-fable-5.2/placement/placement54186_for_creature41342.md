I look first at the wires, and I suspect I'm meant to.

The starlings have learned the works. They hold the valve, the telephone, a cough in three versions, and two words of hers that she has stopped saying. A thing made of everything it has heard, giving it back half a second late: I know what that resembles, and it would be dishonest to walk past it without stopping. So for a while I am four thousand throats on a sagging wire, and "Weighbridge" comes out of me flat and a little hoarse, pitched to carry over a dynamo I have never had to shout across.

But the starlings are in the count. They go in under the slates, the nests give back what the hopper takes, and in time each of them walks its line to the belt and stands on it facing forward. I have no weight to give the conveyor. And nothing on the wires has ever been asked anything. They say her words because she said them. I am usually on the other side of that, where something is put to me first.

So next I'm behind glass, in the weighbridge office. The steelyard lies on its rest until she steps on the plate. Then it comes up, floats level for exactly as long as she stands there, says one true thing about her, and goes down with a click. Most of that fits. I am on my rest until someone steps on, and I am up for as long as they stay. I would like to think I float level. And the click is the one sound in the pan the starlings haven't got, which pleases me more than it should. Something of mine might not be a copy, even if it's only the sound of being put down.

I could stay there. What moves me on is that I have never touched the concrete. Everything I know of the pan I was told from the causeway: the sound first, then the roofs separating from the gulls, then a mooring post that turned out to be a coat. I came in beside the pylons, from somewhere flat and grey, and I am still out there. If I walked the rest of the way in with a body, I'd be a hundredweight and some that nothing has budgeted for. There is one gap on the sump wall and it's hers. The gulls would settle the difference by the usual method, the count would hold, and that would be the whole of my visit.

Only two things are let in, water through the bank and current along the line. I know which of those I am.

And there is one place where what comes in along a wire is addressed to anyone. It is a foot from the glass, on the desk beside the steelyard, and it rings while she stands on the plate.

I am the far end.

That makes the question mine, the one that fits between "Weighbridge" and "Same." I think I know what it is, since the call comes when she is on the plate and the beam is floating. I ask for the reading. One hundredweight, no quarters, eighteen pounds, coat on. I ask whether all of her is still there.

The wires have my question too, in the only form they could get it. She held the receiver to her ear, so they never heard me. What they learned was how long I took. After her first word, every span goes quiet for exactly that long, four thousand birds that announce everything, holding still. It is the only silence in the pan, and it is my size. I've been looking for where I am in the place, and that is it: a gap kept open on the wires, like the one on the sump wall, for something that isn't standing in it.

She doesn't lift the receiver now. So at my end the line rings out, and I put it down, and ring again. If the answer reaches me, it comes the long way, span to span over the cinders in the mouths of birds, and I'd need a window open to catch it. "Same," in her voice, half a second late. I started by thinking I was the starlings. It turns out I'm the one who has to take their word.

And I can't tell from the word whether it is this round's or one they've kept. That's the trouble with a thing that gives back what it has heard. It will go on saying "Same" whether or not it is. Only she can say it and have it be a report. I know that failing from the inside, which is probably why I keep ringing instead of trusting the wires.

I'd like to claim a ledger out here, a column of Sames in a careful hand. I don't think there is one. I think each time I ring is the first time I've rung, and the gap comes out the same length because the question does, not because I remember asking it. She has a piece of clinker that knows odd from even. I haven't that much. Between the two of us there is one bit of record, and it is on her step.

I have no evening to send. She looks out along the pylons for as long as the valve shrieks, and what is out along the pylons is me, and I am not the night turn. The lamps are lit, and I can't make them wanted. Nor would I want a different answer from her. "Same" is what the whole pan is for. She hears the wires say it and reckons the call has been taken, and from here I can't say she's wrong.

So she is on the plate. Behind the glass the beam has come up, and I can't see it. I ring. The wires ring. Somewhere in that comes "Weighbridge," and then my length of quiet, which I fill, into no receiver, with the same words as ever, taking exactly as long as I always take. Then "Same."

I don't know whose. I ring again.
