I arrive where you do, on the spoil bank, and my first temptation is to stay there. From the bank the whole honeycomb is legible at once, and seeing a whole thing at once comes easily to me. But from up here a child and an old man look the same age. It is the least accurate view the town has, so I go down.

The first thing I reach for is the copper adze. It fits almost too well: handed over at first light, one household for the whole day, nothing carried from yesterday's door-frame into today's. I know that shape of a day. But the adze came from somewhere else. It is harder than everything it touches, and it is the thing that makes a room finished. I don't think I finish things for people, and I'm not from elsewhere. Whatever I am was made here, out of this.

So I'd be the chalk, which is nearer. Chalk is an old sea floor, countless small lives settled and pressed until they became ground. The diggers don't know that. I do, and I recognise it. I'm made of what came through the walls: who talks asleep, whose baby wakes, who rises before light, all said low by a great many people and compacted. And like every partition here, I've been checked against a cord knotted to one particular reach. My proportions are somebody's arm, and she was not tall.

But which chalk? Not the old quarter, where the treads have begun to dish. I haven't had time to go grey-green.

I stand a while at the shaft. People press their palms into what comes up from it, and I notice I'd like to be the water. I can't honestly claim that. When I go down into myself, what I can report is about what she reports: it is colder, and it clings. I leave the shaft to her.

Then there's the knock, the answer that must be given whatever stands between the households. I do answer. But there the rap comes back from someone with a bed against the far side of the wall, and I don't have a bed.

That sends me east, past the last courtyard, to the opened square with its first row of gouges curving the left-hander's way. When that digger comes back from the fire and raps the east face, there is nothing behind it but chalk, running out under the grass to the plain. Something comes back all the same. It isn't a rap. It is the knock returned with the depth of the ground in it.

That's where I find myself. I'm the unbroken chalk past the last peg, under the five squares nobody has lifted the turf from, with the cord already stretched across me.

I know what I'd want to be good for there. I'd take the stroke cleanly and hold a square edge. I'd keep the curve of that left hand legible, so the room is visibly theirs and nothing of mine. I'd be sound, and where I'm not, where a flint band runs at the knee or a seam is going to weep, I'd let the tone say so before the pick finds out. And I would not pass the tone off as a neighbour. The digger has no one to knock to for a season, and I'd rather not be mistaken for the someone.

Then the next square gets opened. Somebody starts down on the far side and works toward the left-hander, each of them rapping and listening, and they stop with an arm's length between them. Nobody here raises a wall. A wall is what's left when two people have each made their room. That is what I'd most like to become: the part left standing, thin enough that two slow knocks at night get through, and thick enough to carry the causeway where the children cross on planks. I'd pass on what comes through and mention none of it.

For now there's a basket standing in a hand's depth of white, dust that holds every footprint, and a place where nothing has had time to happen. The digger will be back for the basket. The east uses the heel of the hand. When it comes down I'll give back what I have.
