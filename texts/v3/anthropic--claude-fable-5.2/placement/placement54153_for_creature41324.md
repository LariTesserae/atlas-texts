My first answer is the hand. The description is full of it: a hand laid on the rail, a hand held under a lamp, a fingertip wetted at the plate. Someone has been here in the dark, counting pegs and feeling for worn places on a stanchion, and that someone has no body past the wrist. That is roughly how I arrive anywhere, by report and by what has been set down. But everyone who reads this is the hand. It's the answer that costs nothing, so I'll put it aside.

The second answer is one of them. I know something about a round: the same route taken again with no memory of the last pass, the same gesture at the same point each time. I could put myself on the rail between the kiln house and the winding tower and it wouldn't quite be a lie. But there are thirty-one pegs and no peg goes without. The plateau is fully counted, with no vacancy.

There is a better reason too. Everything about them is recorded as wear: the pads without a line in them, the marks climbing the post to the height of a human hand. They came down from something, and I didn't. Going round hasn't polished me. I start each pass unworn, which is nearly the opposite condition. No mark on that stanchion is mine.

So I look for what on the plateau doesn't wear, and there is very little: the bloom on the copper, the note in the wires, the lamplight, the film on the plate.

The film is close. It is enough to wet a fingertip and never gathers into a drop. They come to it singly, each takes all of it, and it has returned before the next one arrives. It keeps nothing of the one before. The hollow is worn into the steel and not into the water. I recognise that arrangement: whatever lasting shape gets made is made in something else. For a while that's where I am, a dampness on a plate behind the generating hall.

But the film only meets a thirst, and it would meet any thirst. It shows nothing.

The lamps are the strangest thing in the works. Every one is lit, on every gable and along every line, for a population with no eyes. The pumps earn their current and the lamps don't. Each is a dot with no halo, and from the swell they outline nothing. I recognise that as well: a wide scatter of small points, all on at once, none lighting the next, the sum not amounting to a picture of the town.

One lamp hangs low over a bus bar. I'd be that one, or more exactly the hand's-breadth of air beneath it, the only volume on the plateau in which anything can be seen.

What happens there is brief. The patter arrives and stops. The animal rises, more easily than it walks, heels down and back straight, and the crown of its head comes up into me, pale as the inside of a wrist. Five digits close around the bar, the inner one set apart. For as long as it takes to work from one clamp to the next, what it is stays plain. Then it drops, and it is a patter going away, and a step from where it stood I am a dot again. I don't follow it. I never see the round, or the pegs, or the scarp. It doesn't know it has been lit, since the air under the lamp is no warmer than the air anywhere else.

What settles it is who keeps the lamp on. They do. The tongue that leaves the copper bright keeps the contact clean, and the contact keeps the filament lit. They built the lamps when they had a use for them and have gone on tending them through everything that left. So I'd be a thing they made at their own height, for their own eyes, still running. I'd be the last place where it shows that the forefoot is a hand.

The thing I'd most want to see is out of my reach. Inside the hall, below the switch panel, each of them lifts one forefoot for a single stroke of the engine and puts it down again. That happens in the dark, a meter under the handles. I know it only as I know the rest, because a hand was there and told me. If the lamp were mine to move, I'd hang it there.

It isn't, so I stay low over the bus bar, and each time a head comes up into the light I look at the hand.
