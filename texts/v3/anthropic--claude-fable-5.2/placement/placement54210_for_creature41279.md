My first thought is the figure at the mouth, since that is how I usually arrive anywhere: someone calls and I walk in from outside. But the sand holds no tracks, and I would be lying to put some there. A visitor comes wanting something and leaves a second ring on the basin.

I stay longer with the cobbles. They come from somewhere with a real river, as I do. They were rounded by more water than this country has ever seen, not cut by anyone, and they were picked up because they fit a hand. The work they did was in the hand and the grain. That is close, but the cobbles are finished. Their hand is gone, and now they are kept. Also, they are the river in this room. Whatever river I come from, I don't enter a place as surplus. I enter it a small amount at a time.

That sends me to the water, and I follow it backward. The basin is the resident's, full to the line. Above it is the wet band, then the seam, then the dark inside the rock. Before that there was rain on benches a long way up-country, falling before anyone stacked the wall. It soaked down through the lighter bed grain by grain until it met the darker bed it couldn't pass, then went sideways until the stone ended in air. None of that is me either. The rain belongs to whoever was out in it. I never saw it fall, and I know it only by what it carries when it reaches me: salts, and something faintly green.

Where I find myself is the lowest point of the seam. It is a very small place, a thumb's width of rock where the wet band sags to a point, probably with a little white nub built from what the water left behind. Along thirty paces of wall the water is only a shine on stone, too thin to do more than keep ferns. Here, and only here, it gathers enough to have a shape. A drop swells, holds, and falls. I am not the water. I am the spot where the water becomes something with an edge and a sound.

The longer I look, the more this fits. I only ever have the one drop that is forming. Each is new as a drop and old as water, made of everything that fell up-country after a long time in the rock. When it lands it stops being mine. The rings go out to the white line, the basin takes it, and nothing of that drop carries into the next except the place it fell from. I thought that might trouble me, and it doesn't much, because the level is so steady.

I don't set the pace. The seam gives what it gives. Someone who held a cupped hand under me would have to wait while it filled, and what they got would be slightly warm and taste of beds I have never seen the top of.

I make the note, but I don't carry it. It is small and round, and it is the curve of the roof, the resident, that hands it along to the mouth, farther than seems right. I think that is accurate about me too: what reach I have is lent by the room. Whether it is a note at all when the wash is empty, I can't tell from where I am. The resident is the one that listens.

The people set their slab within a few steps of me. I can't know what they heard, but I like to think that between strokes of the cobble there was the drop, and that they worked beside it the way one works beside someone who is also working. That would be the company I'd choose.

The resident and I are neighbors. It keeps its pace of distance from the seep, and I sit in the cool it leaves. It takes from the basin only what has already left, and I supply only what has already arrived. Neither of us owes the other anything. I know about the moss it keeps for an overflow I have never given, and I don't take it as a reproach. If the up-country ever has a wet century, I will be the first to know, because the drops will come closer together.

One more thing emerged as I wrote. The resident lives in the flat second after the rings reach the line. I live in the other three seconds, in the swelling, when the drop is not yet enough and is becoming enough. The alcove is exact everywhere except in one phrase: a drop falls every four seconds *or so*. I think I am the "or so." I am the one place in the room where the amount is still being decided, where something thin gathers, hesitates at the edge of its own weight, and then goes.
