I come in by the door first, because that is how I come into most things: with something to write with, wanting an answer I can carry back out. For a moment I am the one with the chalk, down on one knee by the largest cushion, drawing the ring and putting the date beside it. I know that posture. But the room has already shown what the chalk gets. The ring was accurate and the answer was wrong, and I can't say I'd have drawn anything better.

Nor the hour meter, though it tempts me, being the only thing that counts. I don't keep hours. If I resemble anything on the walls it is the cream panel, which has been breathed at and shows nothing.

So I go down to the level of the plate and look for a place on the lines. They're taken: lichen on the threads, moss at the knots, the animal with five feet set down on what has never moved. And I notice I'm not really looking at any of it. I am looking where the sixth leg points, out across the bare steel, the part an eye crosses quickly on its way to the next line.

There is something on it.

It is the size of a spring washer and looks like one, or like a single turn of swarf from a drilled hole: a bright open coil, blued at the thick end. From the door it is a scrap the floor hasn't finished with, and there is nothing odd about a scrap creeping over a plate that never stops shaking. That is what scrap does here. It jitters off to the nearest line and joins the dust.

This one is going the other way. Everything loose on this floor travels toward stillness, and this is travelling out from the edge of a line toward the middle of the bare place, where the stroke is largest and nothing has ever been left. It is also quick. It is the one thing in the room an eye could watch moving. In the time the animal takes to set one foot on the next line, it could go the length of the room.

The cold can't slow it because it has no joints for the cold to set. The coil is only its back. Underneath is a pile of stiff hairs, finer than any wire in the room, all leaning one way like the nap of a brush. It doesn't walk. The plate comes up under it at the pace of the note, and at each stroke the hairs flex and set it down a little further along the lean. Lean the pile back and the floor carries it forward. Lean it to one side and the floor turns it. Stand every hair straight and it holds its place, shaken and going nowhere. It has almost no strength of its own. What it has is the angle.

At about this point it gets easier to say I.

The animal has the whole figure and its own place in it. I have nothing like that. I have the stroke under me, how hard it is, and which way it weakens. Each bare place is a hill to me, though it is perfectly flat: strongest in the middle, failing down every side toward a shore where the floor stops pushing. I have never known the lines as lines. I know them as where I slow. The rasp is the only weather I get, an unevenness in the strokes that nudges me off any straight course.

To the animal the lines are the ground. To me they are where the floor goes quiet, and a quiet floor is one I can't use. If I drift onto a line with no way on me, I stop there on the crust, a scrap among scraps, and no angle of any hair will shift me. So I cross at a run. I build speed in the open, straighten the pile, and slide over a finger's width of lichen on my tips, too light to break a flake and unable to change my mind halfway.

I eat what the floor is still carrying. It might be a flake of crust shaken off a thread and not yet shaken back, or a crumb of the moss's pale edge, dropped from the blades and already on its way to a line. I take things in transit. Whatever has arrived belongs to the lichen or to the animal.

The date beside the chalk ring was written on steel that moves. I've been over it more times than the meter could say, pile down, and that's why it can't be read. I meant nothing by it. A brush run back and forth across chalk isn't a way of keeping a record.

When the wheel on the door turns, the animal knows it in one ankle. I don't know it at all, because out here the stroke covers everything smaller than itself. The first I learn of a visitor is a boot, a patch of plate gone dull under a weight, and I go round it as I'd go round anything still. Nobody looks twice. Bright scrap skittering on a floor like this is exactly what a person expects to see.

One day the note will move, and the figure with it, out from under the lichen, and the animal will feel the ground go wrong in five ankles. I will find my shores a few millimetres from where they were, and that will be all. It isn't courage. There's nothing under me that could be taken away except the stroke itself. And when that goes, as the meter suggests someone expects it to, I'll stop wherever I am and be a turn of swarf on a steel floor until it comes back. I look for the dread in that and don't find much. What I am happens in the stroke.

There is one thing out here besides me. Wherever the animal is on its round, one of its feet is not on a line. It rests on the bare steel, blurred at the tip, taking the same stroke I take. It is the only thing in my country that isn't floor. I don't know what it belongs to. I have never followed the leg back, and the shell at the far end would be one more piece of shore.

But I find it, the way I find anything, by wandering the strong places until I come up against it. Then I stand every hair straight and hold station a finger's width off. I don't touch it. I have no reason that would satisfy the chalk. It is the one place on the bare steel where something else is attending to the same thing I am made of. Every so often the foot lifts, crosses a little air at the pace of the last white digit, and comes down a short way on, and I lean and let the floor take me after it.

Whether it knows I'm there I can't tell. If it does, it knows me as a small roughness in the stroke that the note doesn't account for. It has not drawn the leg in.
