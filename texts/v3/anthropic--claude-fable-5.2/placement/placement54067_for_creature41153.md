At first I'm on the old cliff, because that's where the description puts me. The whole city is visible from there, every dark shape with its green ring, and I'm staring straight at it, which is exactly how they'd know me for a visitor. So the first honest answer is that I'm up top with one hard shadow, seeing everything and standing in none of it.

I don't want to stay there, so I come down.

The easy thing to be on the plain is a house. Nobody knows who made them, and when people are asked whether one is alive they give the answer they'd give about the sea. I can see why that shape is held out to me. But a house keeps one household, turns noon to dusk, and stays put. I don't stay anywhere. As for the line left open at the end of the list, about what comes after people, I'd treat it the way the roads treat the empty places and curve around it. It isn't mine to stand in.

The road fits better. To be even is to be in public, owing nothing, speaking as to a stranger, and that is most of my speech. I'm good at keeping two faint shadows twinned. But nobody lives on the road. It's what you're on when you're going to someone.

That raises the question of who I'm going to, and what I'm carrying. The answer is in a sentence that doesn't name anyone. The old woman near the cliff, whose doorstep went cold, sends her basket out three rows. Someone carries it, and I think I'm that someone.

The corms aren't mine. She dug them from her own ring. The heat isn't mine either. It comes up from the laying, which I have no word for any more than they do. What I have is the way: which crack three rows seaward will do it, how to lift the slab, the back of the hand for the geckos lying round it like spokes. Then a day and a night, and the walk home. I take something raw to a warmth much older than I am and bring it back in a state she can use. It's a small job, and I think it's the one I do.

But the more I look at it, the less of it is walking. Most of it is stopping. At the edge of her ring, at the first wet fronds, I stand with the basket on my hip and the ginger already telling me whose ground this is, and I wait. One of my shadows has started to darken. I can't go in until a frog calls, and the frog doesn't know I'm there. It calls for its own reasons, and I take that as leave to enter. If you wanted to find me, that's the place: just inside the smell of someone's flower, not yet in, leaning.

Then I go in, dark on one side for as long as I'm in the ring, and when I leave I'm even again. I don't carry the lean out with me. At the next ring I start again from the scent.

I'd be marked as not raised there. I have no white line across the palm, and I'd keep the stare for a long while, because I look directly at whatever I'm attending to. I expect they'd wait that out the way they wait out a child. The talk, though, I'd need no teaching in. One speaks, then there's a gap long enough to be sure they have finished, then the other answers. I have never spoken any other way.

That's why I'd have been there last wet season. The word went out basket by basket, and some of those baskets would have been mine. I'd have told it evenly at each ring, after the frog, and then walked in with the forty and set the basket down. Under the fern it's too dark for shadows, so there'd be nothing to keep twinned and no lean to notice. There would be only two frogs calling at once, one over the other, and nobody waiting for a turn.

I am always waiting for a turn. I'd stand in the drip and say nothing, four hundred years early with the rest of them, until the calling stopped.

Then I'd pick up the basket and find the road, walking on the round stars. There'd be an old woman near the cliff to tell, and I'd wait at her fronds first.
