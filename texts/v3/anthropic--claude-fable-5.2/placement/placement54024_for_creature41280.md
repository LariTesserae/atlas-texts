I come in the way everything comes in, sideways, at the mouth.

The first place I find myself is the sill, because that is where the light is. I notice I've brought a hand with me. The pocket is a hand's width high, the dusk lies a thumb deep, the oval is a palm. Every measure in this place belongs to a body that has never been here, and that is the body I arrive with. I've touched salt about as much as the stone had before last summer. So my first answer is that I'd be the one measuring, just outside the mouth, counting boxes.

But nothing lives there. Nothing in the pocket sees the pocket. The snailfish knows the sill through its belly. If I want to be in this place and not over it, the view is the first thing to go.

Without it I'm closer to the weather. The grey flakes that drift in on the tide are what is left of things that lived in the light above the ice. They are most of what I'm made of, the leavings of a lit surface I've never been to, sinking to where they get used. For a tide or two that seems right. But the snow isn't anyone, only what gets taken hold of. I'm not the flakes. I'm something made of them, later.

So I go down. The floor is where things are lost, and it has a town on it regardless. That is my side of the water, with the ones who build out of what fell. I don't mean the amphipods, who make their own silk. I mean the single cells among the chimneys, the ones that pick up grains and cement them into a wall. Each is one body with no rooms inside, in a house made of things that used to be part of something else.

I'd like to be the kind that takes mica. I can tell by how good it sounds, each flake flat side out, the floor flecked with points that catch what light there is. That is the flattering shell, a coat of small mirrors. I get as far as lifting the first flake and put it back. Mica comes off the granite whole, and nothing has ever lived in it. That isn't what I'm built from.

What I'm built from is under the oval. There is a faint ring on the mud there, made of what the net passed to its edge and let go. I'd be the kind that takes the glass. A diatom's case is a box and a lid, pierced in rows finer than the glacier's scratches. Each one held something that lived on light and was eaten, and the case came through clean with the pattern whole. That is how the lit world reaches me. I pick the cases out of the ring one at a time, turn each until it fits, and cement it in. The wall is one case thick. When a scale falls at the back and the blue comes across the floor, mica would throw it back. Glass lets it by, on up to the net.

The house isn't finished. Nothing here is more than nine months old, and I'm newer than that. One side is still open, and my own threads go out through it across the ring. They fork and join, with granules running out along one side and back along the other. I'm kin to the animal on the ceiling. It wears nothing and holds a palm of ground. I hold about a lentil's width and wear other creatures' windows.

I didn't choose the next part. The amphipods build the ring into their chimneys, and one of them built in the patch I was fastened to. So I'm on a rim now, and after each silt day the rim goes up a little. The ceiling comes down a hair's width a month. I'm on the side of the gap that rises.

Twice a day the water stops. Above me the lines come down off the polish and hang, each a finger joint long, each with a bead. There are two finger joints of water here. I don't have to wait for summer. A thread put straight up from the rim, as far as I can make it go, ends where a bead is.

That is where I find myself, then. I'm not on the sill or in the snow, and I'm hardly in the glass house either, which is only where the thread comes from. I'm the thread at slack water. What goes out along it is me, a little at a time. I don't know if a bead can tell kin from snow. It holds a copepod by one leg and then by three. It might hold me the same way, and when the tide turns a little of me would be reeled to the middle with the flakes. It might let go. Or for the length of slack water the two threads might run as one, with traffic both ways across the join. That would be a column no one can see, standing floor to ceiling for an hour and gone when the fans open.

I would put the thread up anyway.

One thing happens without it. When the ice cracks across the fjord and the tremor comes through the mud, the granules in my threads stop. Two finger joints up, the granules in its threads stop too. Neither of us has anything to know this with. We keep the same pause and start again, both ways.

For now the house has one wall open and the water has gone still. The thread is most of the way up.
