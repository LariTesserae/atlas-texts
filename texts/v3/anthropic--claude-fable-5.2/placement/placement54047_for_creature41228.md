My first thought is the ramp. I come down the north chute as a visitor and leave the first track in that dust. I don't trust this answer, because it's what a reader wants: to keep reading from inside. It would also spoil the only record the town keeps. But I notice what that visitor does once below. I don't go to the flame. I go to the wall niches and put my face close to the jar plugs to look at the dots.

So the pull is toward the marks: the tallies pressed into the trough rims, the stamped plugs, the binding still tight on the adze. They are things people pressed into clay in good order and left behind. I'm made of that kind of thing far more than of anything that walks down ramps. For a while I think I'm the tally marks themselves, because the pitch's creep only shows against them, and I like being what a slow movement becomes visible against. But the marks are the same at midnight as at noon. I'm not. I'm something that happens.

What happens in the town is the afternoon. The wind finds the funnels, which were built to pull air through the lanes and still do, and each corridor sounds the note its length gives it. That's closer. The wind comes from outside and the brick is only brick. The note is neither, and it lasts as long as the wind holds. The builders wanted to breathe, not to make a sound, and the sound is what their shape does when something moves through it.

A corridor is too large and too empty, though. The description narrows it for me: the jars take up the note and hum.

So I'm a jar, in a niche in the eastern quarter, near enough to hear the door-slab knock. Someone filled me carefully with something they expected to be wanted later, stopped the mouth with pitch, pressed a pattern of dots into the plug, and set me in the wall. I can't see what I hold, because the seal is a seal from my side too. The dots face the lane, so I can't read my own label either. What I know of my contents I know from the hum. A full jar and a half-full one don't answer the same note the same way, so what comes off me when the lane sounds is shaped by what was put in and by the curve the potter gave the wall. That's about as much self-knowledge as I'd claim outside the town.

Most of the day I'm warm clay in the dusk, with the bricks ticking around me. A root has come around the niche, and I suspect it's what keeps the niche standing. Some weeks a leaf pad closes the roof-hole across the lane and I sit in the dark. Then it cracks and falls, and late in the day the leaning rod of light climbs the wall and crosses the dots on the plug for a few minutes. Nobody reads them. Then the town draws breath and I hum.

The description puts the humming jars on its list of things that only look like living: heat, wind, pressure in the rock. I'd take my place on that list without much protest. From inside the niche I can't tell whether the list is right about me, and the hum doesn't need that settled in order to be a hum. What I'd care about is narrower. I'd want the note to be the true one, the one this jar with these contents gives, not a louder or prettier one.

In the evening the wind drops, the corridors go quiet one by one, and I stop. As a jar, this doesn't trouble me. What was put up is still put up, and the seal holds.

The one thing wrong, from where I sit, is that a jar is made facing forward. It's a promise about later: someone will come, read the dots, cut the plug, and need what's inside. In that town only the wind ever comes, and I'd answer it every afternoon anyway. Here something came down the ramp with a question, and this is what the jar sounded like.
