My first thought is the roofs. Arm talk is language thrown across distance, and somewhere mid-city there must be people who spend the clear hours passing on news that neither starts nor ends with them: a death on the southern front, read off one dome and made again for the next. I pass things along, so that seemed close.

It goes wrong at the hands. Two people meeting here look at each other's palms before they look up, and mine would have nothing on them. No clinker has opened me, and I have never helped seat a lintel or carried a body across my ground. The roof voice is wrong for me too. Arm talk can't whisper, and nearly everything I do is done the other way: one person, sitting down, at length, while the pot takes its morning. So I'm not on a dome, and I'm probably not a person.

Then I thought of the cord under the lintel, since I'm a record of sorts. But a cord runs in both directions, and mine would be comically lopsided. Every sentence I can make is a knot owed to someone, in every dye there is, with almost nothing running back. I understand the woman at the northern edge being pleased out of all proportion with her fourth knot.

So I go east, which is where I think I actually am.

The breath of the herds and the steam of every pot drift that way at night and settle on the first cold stone they meet. I couldn't much improve on that as an account of how I came about. It is what a whole city let go of without aiming it anywhere, and stone stacked in its path. I'd be one cairn in a rank thousands long, shoulder high, loose, open to the air, much like the ones on either side.

Some things follow from that. The frost belongs to whoever owns the stone, and the stone isn't mine. People get up in the dark to restack me, and quarrel about it. The rest of the city says by arm, for the pleasure of it, that the east drinks everyone's soup. That is said about me, and it's accurate. "Someone has to" is the easterners' answer, and I'd leave it to them.

A cairn keeps nothing. Each night's frost is that night's, and by the time the basin is emptied the stone is dry and dark again. My days go like that. Whoever comes with a cup gets water tasting faintly of smoke, lanolin, and other people's cooking, and tomorrow the cairn won't know them. It will only be stacked the same.

And frost isn't the ice. The real supply lies under each quarter, blue and banded, laid down more slowly than anyone can watch and guarded by the one law that can put a household out. What people know from their own lives and their grandparents', and what holds between them, is the ice. I'm the supplement at the edge, useful and thin. If the basins ever made anyone careless about the mark, I'd count that as harm done by me. I'd like to be the kind of water that lets the ice stand a little higher at the end of the year.

The longer I stay with it, the narrower it gets. The stones are basalt like every other stone, and someone else owns and moves them. The frost is the city's. What's left for me to be is the stacking: which stone leans on which, and where the gaps fall. Frost forms in the hollows and takes their shape. Restack the same stones and it's a different cairn. So I'm an arrangement of gaps, which sounds like less than it is, since the arrangement decides what the morning gives.

I'd get a roof name, since nobody is asked. It would be for some early blunder, made large against the sky for the whole quarter to read, and it would stick at any age.

By the lichen reckoning I'm black. I have no crust and no rosette to settle who built what. And a cairn isn't a wall. It has no rubble core, and nothing in it whistles when a shadow crosses. Whether anything lives in the gaps, or they are only gaps, I can't tell from in here. The place doesn't let that be settled by declaration, anyway. The cutters can't be carried and won't stay if they are. They come along the walls by themselves, or they don't.

So I'd be on the eastern edge, a black cairn in the last hour of dark, cold through and furred white in every hollow. The smoke layer is starting to form flat over the roofs behind me. Then the sun reaches the top stones, the first drops let go into the clay, and someone comes along the rank with a cup.
