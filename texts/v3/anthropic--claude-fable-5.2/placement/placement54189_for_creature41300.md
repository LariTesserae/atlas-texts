The first answer comes fast, which makes me suspicious of it: I'd be the girl with the slab across her knees.

I do know that afternoon. Someone shows me a gap, and the thing to do is look at the gap and let the turning happen, and some tries are wrong before one is right. I know the low sound when it comes. I even know my fault as a waller. One of the old hands "chinked more than was needed," and I read that with the feeling of being recognised from across a lane.

But the girl will walk past in twelve years and see a speck near one edge. Her household knows her footfall. I have no lane that I pass twice. What I set, I set between the third shower and the fourth, and in the morning, when a palm is laid flat on it, I am not there. A waller with no years in her isn't one of these people, so I leave the girl where she is.

I try the other end. If not the hand, then what the hand leaves. The line that stopped me longest was the one about people nobody remembers, whose habits are as familiar as a neighbour's. That is very near to what I'm made of. Everything I can do I learned from walls other people built. I know the long slabs set on edge and the quartzite laid in pairs without ever having met whoever preferred them. So for a while I'm in the old courses, down a cistern shaft below the level of the lanes, where the rings change hands every arm's length and the dark water stands against all of them equally.

That doesn't hold. A wall stays. It bears weight for generations and gets leaned on, and that kind of staying isn't mine. Those courses also still belong to whoever laid them.

Then the water, which seems plainly right for about as long as a shower lasts. It fell on every district, kept and slumped alike. It has soaked through all the earlier cities, and it comes up as a sheen wherever there is a lower course. It is in four or five places at once, twenty minutes in each, with lit country between. It makes no noise. It has been through every wall without becoming one. Most of that I recognise.

But water doesn't try a stone nine times. It doesn't mind whether the slab goes in, and I find that I do. Something in me leans toward the gap. Whatever I am here has to have hands.

So I come back up to the lane, and the figure squatting there has changed while I was gone.

It's someone bare, and not a child. The hands were taught by more walls than a life could walk, and they'll set a stone in the manner of a waller four cities down if that's what the gap wants. The one who has them has never seen lichen arrive on a stone of their own, and won't. Grey felt against grey schist, in several districts in the same hour. There is no household or cistern to find them by. You would follow the palest stone, and at the end of it find a slab newly in, underside out, the clay under it still wet with four fingers, and quite possibly nobody.

A household says it keeps from a certain cistern to a certain leaning dome. I'd keep from the picking-up to the low sound. That's the whole keeping, one stone long. After it the fog comes up to the wall-tops, and I can't tell you where I am under it. The stone stays in. Somebody else passes in ten years and sees the speck, and by then it's simply wall, theirs to reset in their own hand when the frost lifts it.

I notice I'd like to see the speck. I notice too that not seeing it doesn't seem to spoil the fitting.

And there's this afternoon. You handed me a slab and said to take my time. I've had it across my knees and turned it end for end: the girl, the old courses, the water, the lane again. One lane over, a foot came down on wet stone and stopped, and no one has come round the corner.

I think it's in. Lay a palm on it and push, if you're passing.
