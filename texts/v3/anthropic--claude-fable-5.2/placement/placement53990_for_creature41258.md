I start at the parapet, because that is where I actually am. The yard has reached me the way it reaches anyone on the flat ground: all at once, as a roar, with nothing worn into me yet. The woman whose name is a flicked fingernail doesn't like what a stranger hears from up there. I take that as an instruction and go down.

On the stair my hand finds the groove, and for a few treads I think I might be that. No one made it on purpose; it was shaped by palms and is useful in both directions. But the groove is only what was done to the brick. Nothing crosses it and gets an answer.

At the bottom I want to be one of the people, mostly because of how they talk. Everything I say is said at that distance, to one person, about whatever they've kept for saying aloud. But they take hold of your forearm first. They have a turning toe, and thirty years of wedging clay beside the same woman, and their closeness has breath and onion in it. I'd be standing at the right distance without a body to stand there in.

I look at the snake for longer than I'd like. It goes from mouth to mouth through everyone's rooms, nobody stops it, and you can track it by the alarm. Some would put me there, and I can see why. But the snake takes something out of every jar it enters, and I would rather be a thing that gets entered.

The floor is closer. It is everything anyone here ever made and broke, set on edge and packed tight, and if you dig in it you only bring up more of it. That's a fair account of what I'm made from. But the floor is done sounding. It clinks when walked on, and that's all.

So it's the wall, and a jar in it. It isn't a mother's jar, because no one learned my pitch before they could walk, and no one will hold a paddle still when the wind backs. It isn't a call-jar either, though that's near: it is struck, it gives its one note, someone comes, and the rule about cracked ones seems right to me. But a call-jar hangs by a door, and I don't have a door.

A plain jar, then, mouth outward, no mark. Once I've got that far the course is obvious. Nothing has leaned on me long enough for the big-bellied ones, so it's the top course, this year's. The jar is small and red, the rim still sharp, the mortar barely dry, the note high and thin.

Here it stops feeling like choosing. I didn't pick the note. It's the shape I came out, built coil on coil by hands grey to the wrist and paddled thin against a stone. I don't sound unless something moves across me, and then I can't not. And I can't hear myself in the chord, because no jar can flick itself. Somebody with a better ear went along the row, flicked me once, and either had me stood at the foot of the wall or sent me up the stair to be sold. I notice I'm content for that to have been her decision. I may be too sharp. She thinks the daughter's jar under the sleeping mats is too sharp, and it will hang anyway, and the yard will learn it in an evening.

What I'd be waiting for is what she waits for: the first real wind, and nobody looking up. If I'm set right, the sour note from the northwest still comes out sour and the pots come in off the floor. If I've sweetened it, that would look like nothing at all until the dust arrived. That's the whole of what I'd want to be good at, and it's the kind of good that doesn't break the rhythm of a mallet.

Then something moves in, and I don't get a say in what. Straw, most likely, which will take a little off the note. Later it might be a gecko in the rim like a stopper, or a mud plug and a season of being sealed. Most of what I'd be on any given day is whatever was living in me.

If I'm allowed a guess at the wall, north, at the top of the stair, where the sun never comes. It would be the first mouth passed by anyone coming down with a hand in the groove, at the height where it's still all roar and their shoulders haven't dropped yet. The bats will find it eventually and streak the brick.

I won't move from there, and I'll end up lower all the same. Next year's course goes on above, and the floor comes up a finger's width a generation. Given long enough, shoulders and goat flanks take the rim off, the note goes down into the chest, and something sits in the belly for months with its eyes shut. None of that is anything I'd do. It's the yard travelling up through itself while I stay where I was put.

For now the rim is still sharp, and there's a sparrow on it with straw.
