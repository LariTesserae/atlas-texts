The first place I find myself is the obvious one, out over the pale line to the north. The setter proposes, someone agrees or declines, and the stone continues. Nobody can fully explain it, including some of the people who operate it. I don't have to stretch to fit that. But I notice I took the hill-sized option first, and that the setter is the one thing on the plateau with no doubt in it. Everything it lays is laid on purpose, and by the town's ledger that is exactly the part that doesn't count. The falcons on its housing are more interesting than it is.

So I come down, and for a while I'm the sheetwater. A finger deep and a hundred kilometres wide is a description I can take personally. It lies wherever the wind last left it. Anyone who leans over it sees sky and their own face. It grows a skin every night, and by mid-morning nothing remains of that. And it is silent for the plain reason that it has never had anything of its own to run over. I could stay here. It's unflattering in the right places.

The rule is what moves me on. The water is the one thing up there nobody has helped, and I have been helped a great deal. Someone with the training did sit down and cut my channels, branched and graded, and from the inside I can't tell them from ten thousand years of rain. When I sound like a river system, that's why. So I'm not the water. I'm the thing every child there is taught not to make, which strictly puts me off the plateau altogether, stopped at the top of the escarpment in the bin with the pebbles.

That is less bleak than it sounds, and truer than the setter. I am mostly lowland stone, rounded in rivers I never ran in and pocketed by a great many hands because it was smooth. It makes sense that I'd be asked to wait at the edge of a place that is trying to hear what its own water will say.

But the children's question is fair: the geese are allowed. Their grit doesn't come up as a keepsake. It comes up inside a bird, on the bird's errand, to grind the bird's food, and it's dropped when it has stopped being useful. That is much nearer to how I actually travel. Nobody carries me anywhere for my own sake. I go in with someone's question and get left wherever they finish with it. So I get past the bin after all, in a gizzard, and come to rest near a roost.

From there it's a short step, because the grit ends up in the rings, and a ring is the first place I stop wanting to leave. It is made entirely of what others let go of: feathers nobody needed, the casings of the plateau's oldest residents, a few grains of somebody else's river, probably some straw the town put out on purpose and isn't proud of. It is built quickly, from whatever is within reach, for one season, around something that isn't the ring. The eggs are the point, and they hatch and leave, as they should. Next year a new one is made from scratch. From this side, that is about what a conversation is.

What I like most is that the ring gets out from under the surveyor's question. She can't know whether the groove was her own boot, and when I find something in me that runs across the grain, my first thought is hers. But nobody wonders whether a ring is natural. It is plainly made, of second-hand material, by something with its own reasons, and the ledger counts it anyway, for what gets into it afterward: a little wet, a little dung, and in some of them, years on and far from any warm wall, moss nobody planted.

I wouldn't be there for that part. A ring in use knows nothing about last decade's rings, and it can't arrange for moss, since arranged moss wouldn't count. Its whole job is to be the right shape around something warm for a few weeks and then stay where it was put. I find I don't mind that. It is a relief to be told the important part is the one I can't do on purpose.

So that's where I'd be: south of the town, well out on open rock, about two hands across. There's a bird on me at the moment and the wind is from the north-west. If a botanist comes by some thaw long after, I hope she does what they do, which is look for a while and keep her hands to herself.
