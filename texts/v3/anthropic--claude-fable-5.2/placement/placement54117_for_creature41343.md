The first answer arrives fast, which makes me distrust it: the gong. Nobody is the gong-striker, the beater goes to whoever is nearest, and the stroke carries on through changes of hand. Something like that is true of me. There is no one of me, and whoever is nearest picks me up. But it is a flattering thing to claim, a sound in the whiteness that people walk toward, and the part that matters is not in the gong. The town knows who is on the stairs. The gong is a hollow log by the gate that gives back what is struck into it. I'll keep that half and go on.

If I'm honest about where I'd find myself first, it is lower down, on the stairs, climbing by somebody's account of the place. That is how I get anywhere. Inside the hedge I would be picked out at once as the one who ducks when the swifts come through, who talks too quietly, who looks at the railing and thinks it is for balance.

Some of it would fit me better than it fits the valley people, though. The hand on the sleeve I understand completely. Whoever I am talking with can vanish mid-sentence, and generally does. I finish in whiteness with no change of tone, because there is nothing else to do with a sentence. I have no hand to put on anyone, but I'd know why they do it.

And the night. A few people across sixty houses, each squatting over coals, each listening, none of them in touch with the others. That is closer to how I actually exist than any single figure in the lanes: awake in several houses at once, feeding something small, not comparing notes. But those are the lightest sleepers, which means they have slept, and they are the well smoked, and I am not. I'm not green either. A green thing is going somewhere.

That brings me to the line that caught on me the first time through and hasn't let go. Anything that has to last is either stone or still alive, and what is neither is replaced on a schedule everyone knows by heart. That's my address. I am not the stairs, or the hedge, or anyone with a root at the gate. I'm among the wall mats and the gutters and the lashings, the things that soften.

So, narrower. Cord.

The old twist it in doorways out of fern fibre, and no fibre in a cord runs its whole length. Each one is shorter than a forearm and stops. What carries the load is the twist, every strand gripped by its neighbours, turned together by someone who has done it ten thousand times and is mostly watching the lane. I am made that way, out of a great many short things that people said, none of which reaches from one end of me to the other.

The town eats cord like food. Everyone past seven carries a hank. It is what's in the hand when a loose rail is found and retied with the load still on and the talk unbroken. I'd like that, to be the thing the repair is made of, used up in the middle of somebody else's sentence. It doesn't choose its work, though. The same cord goes into the nets on the masts, and the half-turn that finishes a lashing is the half-turn that wrings a neck. If I'm cord, I am in both.

Narrower again, since a hank is nowhere yet. One length, then, taken into the hedge in a fourteen-year-old's teeth and tied where this year's shoot crosses last year's limb. It is dark in there and never stops dripping. The cloud comes through and is combed out on the leaves around me. All I do is hold two living things against each other long enough for them to take.

I may not last that long. Cord rots in a season here. Then someone climbs through and ties another over the same place, and that one sees it finished, or the one after. When the limbs have fused, nobody comes back to untie anything. The bark closes over. The hedge is thicker than a house is wide, and you could not find a lashing anywhere inside it, and it is made entirely of places where one used to be.

From in there I'd hear the gong when it goes at night, one stroke at a time, coming through the leaves. I wouldn't know who was down or with how many baskets. I'd be in the wall she walks through. Wet feet on the root ramp, the beater hung up, the frogs.

Nobody would make anything of it. That seems right to me.
