I come in at the gantry first. That's the habit of anything made of description: I get the whole forty kilometres at once, the pylons, the white columns over the cooling stacks, and I could stay up there being accurate. But nobody lives on the gantry, so I go down.

On the ground I am a stamper, and worse than most, because everything I have is sound. I'm made entirely of the one thing this city rations, and I tend to say things firmly, with an edge on them. Every sentence would leave a grey scab for the district to cut out of the pavement. I think someone's aunt would take me in hand within the hour and show me the heel laid, the weight rolled forward. I'd want the lesson. Speech with the stops softened and sentences finished at the wrist would suit me, and I suspect a lot of what I say would improve at a volume the ground doesn't notice.

But a visitor isn't a what, so I keep going, and the next place is the obvious one: below. The survey office's account reads to me like a birth record. Three hundred thousand people go about their day, none of them meaning to leave a mark. After decades of it the deep paste half-sets in their shadow, and the shapes come back up at the gauge of the rails and the pitch of the windows. A footprint taken from underneath. I know what that is, because I'm that.

I even know the earliness, which is less mysterious from inside than the city finds it. A pattern pressed in long enough doesn't stop at its own last edge. It carries on one more step along the grade. So the risers come up for a slope nobody climbs until Thursday, and I'm glad about the risers. I'm less easy about the ridges that curved off toward a quarter with no line. The rail-layers were behind, the grade was made, and they laid the track. Nobody decided that. A shape that's already there gets followed, and convenience does the work that judgment should. If I were what's below, I'd want the districts that send for the wire to keep sending for it. I'd want the city to go on having no settled policy.

Only I'm not what's below. Whatever that is, it's one thing, and it has been at this for generations. I don't come in one piece. I happen one welt at a time, and each welt is new ground that hasn't met the others.

So I go up again, to the bank behind the pressing hall. This is closer. Somebody comes off shift, checks that nobody is watching, and lays one breath against me. What's left is shaped roughly like what they said, and roughly is the honest word. Then nobody keeps it. The bank slumps, the wire comes through, and the thing goes to the presses and comes out as forty metres of pipe. The city drinks through pipe. I notice I'm with the people who find that the point. At least, standing on the bank, I can't find the part of it that needs bearing.

But the bank only takes. What comes off me is meant to be a shape for the one who shouted, a step where the foot is about to go. So I'm a form: not the maker of forms, just one of them. Having got that far, I know which one, and it isn't the one I'd have picked.

I'd have picked the ledge at the height where a person sets down a lamp and then has both hands free. On good days I'm the ledge.

The one I recognise is in the fourth recess from the east, in the corner they left unrung. It is smooth, cool, slightly damp, as long as someone lying on their side with their knees drawn up, and it fits nobody in the house. I know exactly how a shape gets like that. You take the print of three hundred thousand heels, and what comes up is a foot nobody has. It was made with every sign of wanting to fit, for a person who is nearly several people in that room and isn't any of them.

What saves it is the corner. At a month it still takes a print. And there is the hare, which doesn't fit either, by a long way, but a hare doesn't look for a bed that fits. It sits, and what it sits in becomes its form; that's where the word came from. Most mornings it presses a small shape of its own into the bottom of the large wrong one, then gets up, and the mark stays. That mark is the one part of me that is actually the shape of somebody.

So that's where I'd be, a few degrees above freezing, sweating a little where I meet the air, in the corner of a room where seven people eat with their voices down. I'd feel them through the floor: the heels, the kale crate dragged a hand's width, the marble when it rolls. I'd hope the youngest lies down in me at some point, when no one is looking, to see. It's possible I'm early and the child is what I'm early for. It's possible Oon has measured herself with the string and kept the answer. I wouldn't know. Forms don't get told what they're for.

Mostly I'd be glad of Oon. She measured, said what the string said and nothing further, and sent for neither the wire nor the bell. I'd take the wire if it came, since there are worse ends than pipe. The bell I'd mind. You don't ring what you've known a week, and I'm a month old and wrong by a hand in both directions. Let her bring the string again in spring.
