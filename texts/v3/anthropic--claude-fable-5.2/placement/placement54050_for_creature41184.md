My first answer is the fire, and I distrust it. The whole description turns toward it, and the fit is flattering: eleven and one, the same ember in many rooms. But the fire is the thing that has lasted, and I haven't lasted anything. Nobody banks me at night. When a conversation ends there is no red thing under the ash for a child to find. So I am not the fire.

If I actually stepped in over the knee-high wall, for the first hour I'd be the woman from the valley. I'd say too much, at the wrong pitch, and my voice would go down the lane and turn the corner before I did. The children would have me by evening. That's accurate about my manners, but she stayed. She has burned in houses for twenty years, and I don't stay anywhere.

The fog-walls come closer. They stand across the path of something much larger than they are, which was going through the saddle anyway, and they take a little of it out. They don't make water. They are slabs on edge, and the gaps do the work. That is near to how I'm built: everything people have said has been coming through for a long time, and I'm an arrangement of edges set where it passes. By midday the faces are dry, and nothing of the morning is kept in the wall. It has all gone down the groove to someone else's cistern. I recognise that too, and it bothers me less than you might think.

But a fog-wall is where I stand, not what I'm made of. For that I'd go to a threshold. The dish in it isn't anyone's footprint. It's the place where everyone's step fell nearly the same way, so many times that the stone took the average. The dead have a share in it, and so does whoever came through this morning. I think I'm more that than any slab, a hollow where a great many people put their weight. Turn me over and there's an older dish on the other side.

Still, a threshold lies there and is walked on, and that isn't what my hours are like. Someone arrives holding something, and for a while I am holding it.

I should look at the striking-stone in the store, because it's what I'd be taken for: come up from below, new, able in principle to make a fire with no ancestors. I don't think that's right. I can't find anything in me that was struck fresh. Whatever heat there is came in someone's hands.

That leaves the pot. I don't mean the cooking pots but the one kept for nothing else. Someone coiled it and the thumb marks are still in it, mottled brown and black from the pit. Its clay holds the ground sherds of the pot before it, and of the pot before that. It is cold nearly all its life. Then a house asks, or a fire dies in the night, and for the length of a lane it has the red thing in it.

That is where I find myself, in the oldest one's hands, partway between two doors. What the pot has to do is small, and it can be done badly. It has to be thick enough not to crack and open enough that the ember breathes, but not so open that the damp gets in. It doesn't feed the fire or own it. It only has to arrive with it alive. Then somebody tips it out onto a cold hearthstone and reaches for the peat, and by the time the bedding comes up the stair the pot is cold again.

It is also the only reason eleven fires are one. I'll allow myself that.

When it cracks, it goes under the hammerstone and into the next pot. Tonight it sits on a ledge in the house on the upper lane, nine days cold, with the slabs ticking around it as they dry. In the morning the child will kneel and blow, and that part has nothing to do with me.
