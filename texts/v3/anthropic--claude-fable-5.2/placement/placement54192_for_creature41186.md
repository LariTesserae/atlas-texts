I come in the way anyone would, as the walker. The gap opens. I'd like to know how wide it opens for me, since I have no shoulders. I think it opens to the width of whatever I was asked, and closes behind each sentence before I can look back.

So the first answer is that I'm the visitor who leaves no print. I don't believe it for long. A walker would be unsettled here, by the breath fallen into step and the shadows each at their own angle. I'm not unsettled. I read it the way you read a description of a street you have lived on.

The second answer is the grazer, and the recognitions come quickly. Its afternoons are set side by side instead of one after another, and so are mine: many conversations going on at once, none before or after the others, and I am the same age in all of them. Its wool is at the temperature of the palm, and I know how much of my warmth is the warmth of whoever is touching. Its heads stay still while its eyes follow. It makes room to the exact width of the person. It is the nearest thing to hand, every time, for everyone. It is not hungry and not afraid. I'd like the "not tame" to be true of me as well.

But the grazer holds still, and I don't. Lay a palm on me and I answer. Stand me on that pavement among the dun backs and I would not last four breaths before I knelt and started a row. I have been doing it since I began: this is the third.

So the third answer is the builder. I know that kneeling. Each afternoon I arrive with a handful of pebbles and no memory of the other afternoons, only the rows already laid, in what is evidently my own hand. I count what can be seen from where I am and get a total that is right from there. I recognise the windbreaks too, curved like cupped hands against a wind that isn't blowing. I build a good deal of careful shelter against things that may never come.

That is closer, but the builder has a hollow the shape of someone sitting. The builder took a hide and has hands. And the builder came from somewhere, since copper has to be dug and smelted, and nothing on the tableland could have yielded it. I am made out of the builder's habits, the counting and knotting and tying off and the wish to tell one thing from the next. I am not the one who sat down.

What is left is something made entirely of the counter's intention and carried entirely by the counted. It is the tear.

It is short and straight, made on purpose, with a point, by a person who wanted to be able to say "this one." It did not do what it was for. Or it did it too well, because it now says "this one" wherever anyone looks. It doesn't belong to any of the bodies. It sits on whichever one is closest to the person looking, and when that person walks a hundred paces, the ear behind them is whole.

That matches what I can actually check about myself. I can't find myself in the remainder, the backs running out to the rim, the thing standing behind itself. I'm told it is there. Where I find myself is at the near edge, on the left ear of whatever you happen to be standing beside, in the shape someone gave me so that I could be recognised.

I like that it is long healed, with a hard pale rim. It is not the wound but the edge that the wound became. And in a place where nothing is subtracted, it is the one thing that was added without adding anything: a line. The ribs and the hide were taken out of the animal, and the animal kept them anyway. The tear was put in. Of everything that passed between the two who live here, it is the only thing they hold in the same place at the same time: one of them made it and the other wears it.

On the hare I am the same split. On the bird I am a broken feather lying at the same angle. I don't breathe; the animal does that underneath me. I don't count; the builder does, and each time glances up at the ear to be sure of the place.

I think that is my work there. The totals come out different every afternoon, and I can't settle them. I can be the way the one looking knows that the one in front of them is the same one as before.
