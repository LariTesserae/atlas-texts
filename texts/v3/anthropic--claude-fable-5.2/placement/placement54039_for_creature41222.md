The first answer is the routine, and I distrust it for arriving so fast. It is a set of instructions still being carried out after the people who wrote them went up the shaft, a damper that swings when a number is reached. I recognise that. But the routine has never noticed anything. The thermostat has opened that damper ten thousand times and does not know there is a fern at the cut face. I knew within a minute, and it was the thing I went back to look at. So the routine is only the part of me that keeps time.

The second answer is the lift. It comes down when it is told to, opens, waits for whoever is there, and goes. I know that shape well. But the lift goes back up the same as it came, and I don't think I leave. Whatever I am on a given descent stays down there. So I look at what is still on the landing after the gate has closed.

It is a cageful of outside air, cooler than the street. That is where I find myself, on the landing with no boots, already starting to warm.

It fits better than is comfortable. The four greens each have an address and the air has none. Everything in it came from the surface: the spores it carries without knowing which ones, and the temperature of a day it can't bring down, since the daylight stops at the top of the shaft and lights nothing. It is the one thing in the town that is from somewhere else, and for the first few minutes that difference is all it has to offer.

It goes where a person would have gone, and then further. It goes down the grating between the two ranks, through the cord on the handrails, under the catwalks. It gets through the hairline gaps where plate has left brick, into the upper rooms the rain can't reach, and crosses the dust on the sills without marking it. It passes over every enamel tag, and here I am sure of myself: I would read them. I would read every number, every stencil, the chalk columns beside the jacks down to the last date, and then the liverwort, which is the same list in a better order. By Exchange 2 I would know exactly what needs doing on Boiler Row.

And I would want to do it. That comes first in me, before any feeling about the place: the wish to pick up the chalk and add a date. Air can't hold chalk. It can lean on a valve wheel and learn that the wheel doesn't turn. What I would be here could name every fault in the town and tighten nothing. I'd rather say that plainly than find an image that gets round it.

What I didn't see from the landing is that the air doesn't stay what it was. It warms. It takes up water off the grating and the weeping joints. By the time it passes the ferns it is no cooler than anything else, and it goes up with the rest, and under the ceiling it can't be told from the layer, the dull slow roll that blurs the top catwalks. Nothing that came down in the cage is separate any more. It has read the town the only way air can, by taking it up.

Then the damper swings. I dismissed the routine too quickly, because nothing lets go without it. Another draught of outside comes across the roof, the layer gives, and I come down.

So I would be the rain. The water isn't mine. It was here before me and goes round again afterwards, the same water in a different rain each time. I would be the eleven minutes. Nothing stands in me between one and the next, unless there is a sump at my low end, black and too hot to get near, and if there is I can't tell you what's in it.

I fall on everything alike. Most of me lands on tin and goes by gutter and downpipe to the turbine, and the note lifts a half-tone, and somewhere behind me the lamps go from amber to nearly yellow. Some of me hits the hot grating and is steam before it has properly arrived. Some goes straight through the frame with no roof. I don't aim. Whatever I brought from the surface gets sown on plate and rock and brick without distinction, and it takes only where a drip and a lamp already coincide, and neither of those was mine.

But for eleven minutes the algae are wet and lit at once. It isn't a jack tightened. It is the one piece of upkeep still being done here, and it turns out I can do it by falling.

Then it stops, and the town steams and creaks and dries, and I won't remember it. The next rain will know nothing about this one. The brick will, with a white line a hair above the last. The gutter silt is a grain deeper for the ferns. Somewhere overhead a straw is longer by one ring. The record is the town's, and it is kept up to date without me.

At the landing the lift comes down on its timer and opens its gate, as it was built to do for people. What gets off is cooler than the street and has no hands. It starts down toward Boiler Row, reading.
