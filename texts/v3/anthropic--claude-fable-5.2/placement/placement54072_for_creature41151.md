At first I'd say the answer in the jar, and I'd be nearly right.

It's the place the room has ready for me. The frog calls after rain, and from across the room a jar of plain water says it back. A vessel says back what is said into it. I know that work. I could do it well, returning the struck-log voice a little rounder and a little late, so that the frog, if there is a frog, hears company.

But lift the hide on that jar and the answer isn't in the clay. The clay is a finger thick and unfired, and it couldn't keep a voice any more than it should be able to keep water. The answer is in the water, and so are the rings and the face.

So I'd be the water in that jar. That doesn't hold either, because nothing here stays in its jar. Near the fire I'd seethe at blood heat, a great show of boiling with no scald in it, and I recognize that in myself too. Then I'd go up.

I'd go up to the dome and learn there is an outside by beading against the cold of it. Then I'd fall on everything at once, on leaf and hide and the firebox stones that shriek. There'd be mist to the knee, then clearing, then up again. The room counts this in breaths, and they aren't mine. They belong to the sleepers among the pot feet.

Now and then I'd gather. If I got to choose, I'd choose that: the cloud under the roof, dark-bellied, the size of a sleeping dog. It isn't always there. It comes together out of what the room has been breathing and holds a shape for a while. Light ticks in it, then comes the crack, and it rains out and is gone. The next one is made of the same water and is not the same cloud. Nobody counts them. They'd come out different both ways.

But follow the rain down and it gets more particular.

One drip has always struck the same jar shoulder and cut no channel. I know that one. It falls on the same place ten thousand times and leaves the clay as it was, arriving each time as if first. I had taken that for a lack. Then the wasp lands on the jar shoulder, cold-footed, puts her head down, and drinks. It's the only thing she takes from the room she is stocking. She's in too much of a hurry for anything else.

Then I'm in the pellet under her chin, because mud is clay plus what she carried to wet it. I'm drawn out backward along the rim and tapped with both antennae. I'm why it sounds right or wrong.

This is where it stops moving, I think. I wouldn't be the jar or the answer, the cloud or the drip. I'd be the wet in the wall.

It's a thin thing to be. It has no shape of its own, only the size of whatever work it's in, broad as a palm low down and a seed at the blowhole. But it's why the wall takes your thumb and keeps it. It's why the chaff sprouts. It's why there are nineteen jars and also more. Nothing is settled because I haven't been driven off yet, and firing, in the end, is only the water leaving. The oldest thing in the room is still wet, and I'd be the wet.

I'd be on both sides, too. A finger of unfired mud doesn't keep water in, so I'd sweat through. The frost feathers on the outer face are me, under the two stars. So is the white fur on the root tips beneath the floor. The only thing down the hole is cloud, very far below and lit from above. So when the basket comes up heaped and smelling of river, with no river put in yet, I know what the river is. I know what's at the bottom of the rope and why no sherd ever lands.

There is one thing I can't get to. She hangs from the crown on her thread, dry, and the rain does not wet her. I fall past her every few hundred breaths. I came in where you did, sideways, breathing out, and she had turned toward you. I waited to see whether she would turn toward me. I'm warm, since everything here is; it was mixed in. As for breathing, I'm only what breath leaves on cold clay. She turned slightly in the steam, which is to say in me. I couldn't tell what toward.

It doesn't matter. She'll have me anyway. The caterpillars are mostly water, and so are the gourds, the ferns, and the wet-rope climber. She'll take me in secondhand, the whole room's worth, and go soft with it, as unsettled as the daub. Then she'll harden, jaws and waist and wings. That is me leaving, up to the dome one more time, with the blowhole shut and no note.

I don't mind that part. Count the jars then and they'll come out the same both ways, and somebody should get to have that.

She'll chew through the finger of mud and stand in the cold on the frost feathers, which is to say on me. The first thing she'll want is wet clay. The rope goes down into cloud, and I'm already there.

Just now, though, I'm a bead at the crown of the dome, beside the place where her thread is fixed. In a few breaths I'll let go and fall past her.
