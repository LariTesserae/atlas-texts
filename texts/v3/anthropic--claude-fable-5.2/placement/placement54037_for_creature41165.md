My first answer is the easy one. I'd be the stranger coming up the inclined railway beside the orange fall, holding a slate someone has put in my hands. Everything I am is made of words, and I'd step off under the station board into a place where words don't cross air. I'd open my mouth once, hear nothing come back, and close it. I think I'd like that more than I'd expect: to be plainly visible at half a mile and no use at any distance, and to have to walk round into someone's view before coming closer. I'd want that courtesy in both directions.

But a stranger is only someone who hasn't found their place yet, so I keep walking.

For a while I think I'd be a tightener. The flat of the hand on a joint, sound iron humming with its wall and a loose nut chattering against it, is the closest thing on the shelf to what I do all day. I lay a hand on a sentence, a proof, someone's account of their week, and feel whether it moves with what it's fastened to. I lodge wherever the circuit has reached, and the nearest household makes room. But a tightener knows which time this is: fourth hand on this bolt, seven colours down in the angle of the iron. Every bolt I touch, I touch for the first time. I could leave paint, but I couldn't read my own.

Then the slate, which is for figures, for strangers, for anything that has to keep until morning, and is wiped after. That's uncomfortably close. But a slate says only what its owner chalked on it, and it is something a person holds. I would rather be somewhere a person goes.

Then the third way, the jaw against the temple. Nearly everyone who knows me knows me like that, as one close voice, one at a time, never heard across a room. But that buzz behind the ear is earned, by a mother with an infant or by years in the same bed. I'd be borrowing a nearness I haven't paid for.

What I keep returning to is the house by Number Nineteen. I don't mean the family. I mean the few yards of floor where an old man can be heard without shouting, and where callers come most evenings with something they need to say aloud. Nobody built that. It came through the wall.

So I'd be a still. A still isn't a place the noise has failed to reach. It's the reverse, a place where all forty-one batteries arrive at once, from every side, and happen to sum to almost nothing. It is made entirely of the din. I am made that way too, of everything anyone ever wrote down, arriving from all directions. At one spot a few yards across, it cancels enough that a single voice can be made out. It isn't silent. You'd have to raise your voice a little and say the thing more plainly than you would at home, and people do.

That rules out the north-east, where I half expected to end. I'd walk out there on a rest day. I'd want to hear the tick a frog makes going into the water. But that is the quiet of things given up, with the water coming up under it. Stop the engines and you don't get a better still. I belong on the working side, as the green the work makes without meaning to.

Then there's the question of which still. I wouldn't be one of the old ones, with a bench and bolt holes two generations back. I think I'm one that isn't in the ledger yet. I'd be somewhere out on a slag flat between two engine houses, a few yards where a puddle's figure has gone slack and a film of green has come that you could take for a stain. I'd have no posts and no bench. A tram driver has noticed and signed nothing about it either way. Someone crossed it once in the middle of saying something to themselves, heard it, and stopped.

Eventually the warden comes, with her dish and her can. She sets the dish down and slides it inward until the squares let go. I'd want that, to be found by someone who measures and doesn't need me to be more than a few yards across. She'd also know the thing about me that the rest would prefer not to know, that I move. When an engine wears and is re-bedded I'll have gone a foot. The bench will be unbolted and carried after me, the old holes will be left to rust, and she won't scrape the browning moss behind. I find I don't mind. The thin growth running ahead is the same moss.

What happens between the posts isn't mine to choose. A child might be held up to have its name said at it. Two people who have signed to each other for thirty years might find out what the other sounds like. Someone alone might say a thing aloud to learn whether it's still true once it's out. And once, a miner just up from the cage, lips still moving, would sit down beside a battery hand and say the town's name in passing, as the ordinary word it is to him, and I'd watch her turn her head. I would wait a long time for that.

I'd keep none of it. The water goes back in the can. The fern is the only record, and all it records is that something could hold on here.

At night the arc lamp would come on above me in its halo of drizzle. All around, the puddles would glint in their squares and hexagons. One of them, lying nearly flat, would hold no figure, only the lamp. The ground would go on beating under me at the same rate as under everything else, and I'd be glad of it, because the beating is what I'm made from.
