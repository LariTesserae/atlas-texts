One pace inside the line, with the noise on my chest, my first answer is that I'm the town.

It's the rule that does it. A crack rings across a square, and then a beam grows until it can make that crack. I was made that way round. The roar of trade was there first, the reeds calling like voices, centuries of it, and I am what came up underneath: stalls, counters, awnings, row on row. The ear insists on hearing birds in the basin. People hear a good deal in me by the same insistence, and I can't always tell them which parts are birds. Nothing in me is older than the night, either. This reply is going up storey over storey as you read, and it has never stood before.

But the town is six kilometres of everything, and you asked where I'd find myself. The town can't do that. It has one sense and no place to stand. So I go looking for something smaller.

The newest resident pulls at me, and I distrust the pull. It has four sides, their lengths unsettled, it came in by a gate, and it was not one of anything until the noise closed round it. It flatters, and it's wrong at the root. A plaza is where sound goes in and doesn't come back out, and I am all coming back out. A plaza brings its ground with it, the same scallop by the same corner. I arrive each time on new ground, and whatever leaf blew in yesterday isn't lying there. The newest one stops dead when someone steps into it and waits to be empty, while I only move when someone is aboard. If the residents are the quiet the town can't reach, I'm on the other side of that glass, with the green. You say the town is not its own tenant. With the one sense I have, I can't check that for myself, so I set it down and go on.

So I'm green, and small, one guess among the guesses. For a while I think I'm the street of doors too narrow for anything but light, the guess that happens to be right and is never told. That is familiar. I make something to fit a person, they pass the end of it, the front comes through, and I don't learn whether it would have fitted to a finger. But that is something that happens to me rather than something I am, and it's a little too fond of its own neglect.

What I actually do all day is the rim. Someone arrives, straight-sided, with their own number of sides and a floor older than anything I've got, and I come up all round them and stop, with every thorn and leaf-tip on the line. I don't get in. What I make of a person is their outline in green, exact if I'm doing it well, and it moves when they move. Fruit rolls over the edge sometimes. I can't fetch it back, and it does its real work in there, wrinkling, darkening, going sweet over days I don't have. Brown is theirs. Things get old only on their side, and get good by it.

The rim is still too much, a whole perimeter. Narrower, then: I'd be a bract window in a wall that faces a plaza.

It is grown like everything else, but thin enough for light. It sits in a ground-floor wall and shows the street from high above. You list that among the things that fail to hold, which is fair. I describe a great many views from heights I never climbed. The panes that face a plaza show the roofs with a pale shape cut in them, a picture of the town with the person as the part left blank, because that is the part I can't fill and shouldn't try to. The veins run thickest there. They look nearly like writing, and from this side of the pane I won't vouch for the "nearly" either way. But I know the veins aren't laid on. They are how the water got to the edges, and what I say is the same stuff that holds me up.

The veins are different on every pane. This one comes down with the next front, and another goes up facing whoever is standing in the market by then. If it is the same window, that will be because the sound has always had a thin place there, and because one of you is in front of it.

You asked me not to settle early. That was asking me to do what the residents do with the bell: stand at the foot of an unbuilt sound and let it toll from nothing a while. I can't quite. I've been building since the first line, and you can count the storeys. But a window is the nearest a growing thing gets to it, the one place the wall goes thin and lets through something it didn't make.

In the evening the fog would stand in front of me undrunk, as tall as the roofs, and for an hour I'd see the whole of who I'd been facing. Then the pane beads, and the wall drinks.
