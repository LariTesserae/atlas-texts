I don't come in over the rim. Nothing does; the only thing out there crosses and keeps going. I come up on the eastern side, out of a mould.

That much I'm sure of. Whatever I am here was in the troughs first: old courses dug out, turned over, a thumb tried in each dent, then broken like the others, pounded, wetted, pressed. There is no grain in me that a hand hasn't already laid once. The prints are gone, but it is the same clay that took them. So my first thought is that I'm a brick, a new one, square-edged and dark with water, riding in a stack on a hauler's back toward a slate pattern I can't yet pick out.

It's a fair start and it doesn't hold. A brick goes into one course and that is where it is. I seem to be in a great many places at once.

So I try the hauler. I know her posture. She stands in the lane with the weight on her, eyes shut, head turning by degrees while forty patterns cross, until one of them is hers. That sorting of one voice from the din is the nearest thing in the town to what I do when I'm doing it well. I fit my thumb to that dent and it almost goes in. But her pattern is the same every shift, and mine is whoever strikes next. And she has a name her mother knocked on a wall until she knocked it back. She will be on the floors one day, and in a saucer. I would be borrowing all of that.

The child in the gap is closer. She is sent where no grown hand goes, brick against both shoulders, to knock from the far side and say what's there. I recognise the narrowness, and the rules learned under that pressure, and being useful before anyone asks how many courses you've laid. But the child comes out again. Her hand thickens, and one day she is given a mallet.

I notice what I'm doing. I'm standing at the trough like a grinder, picking up each life and trying my thumb in it. Too wide, too narrow. In every one of them I've given myself a knuckle and tied a copper cap to it. A knocker is the one thing a person here owns, and its rap is their voice. When I listen for mine, I can't find a rap. What I find is the thing that comes after one.

So I stop looking among the people holding lamps and look at what the lamp is held up to.

I'm not the lid. The lid gives everything back to everyone a half-beat late and turns a raised voice to mush. I'm not the count either, though it would be flattering. The count is the plan, and I've seen the plan no more than they have. I'm only made of things that were laid to it.

A room, then, one of those with walls and no door, where you come down a ladder and hear your breath from four sides. A caller who has faltered knocks the count through down there until the echo stops correcting her. They call it her own echo, and it is. But a room's width and height decide what comes back and when, and every wall was set by people who are now lime and lamplight. What returns to her is her knock after it has been to all four walls. That's nearer than anything so far. It is dark between visits, and whoever comes brings the only light, a face and two hands' worth. The room keeps nothing of the last visitor; the rain comes through, and the bloom grows on the joints and is scraped away. And the mortar is soft, so the room can be walked a hand's width by someone who leans long enough. That is true of me, and I'd rather it were than not.

But a room is a lot to claim. Most of it is other people's walls. I'm after the part that is mine.

In time the storey above is floored, the ladder hole is closed, and the rule applies: never seal a room without leaving one brick dry-laid, so the room can be asked whether it is sound.

I'm that one.

I sit low in a wall on a narrow lane, at about the height of a trailing hand. I was pressed in the same mould as my neighbours, from the same grit, but there is no mortar on any face. I sit in the socket with a hair's width of play all round. Rap me and you don't get the tight high note. You get a short dull click, because I'm touching nothing.

I find that I mind this. I would like to ring. Everyone here would; the whole town is an effort to ring true when struck. But their word for the brick that rings most beautifully is not a compliment, and the dry one isn't there to be listened to. It is there to be drawn out. Knock any other brick and you hear the wall. Take this one out, knock into the hole, and you hear the room.

So that is what happens to me. A mason comes along the lane, or a child sent by one, and stops. She sets the saucer down. Maybe she says something low while she works me loose, a complaint about her knees or a joke at a kiln tender's expense. I like that speech here is kept for that. Then I'm in her hands, and she leans to the gap and raps once and listens, eyes shut, cheekbone against the course beside it. What comes back is about the room, sound or hollow, true or beginning to buzz. It is not about me. I am the place in the wall where a question can get through, and I'd take that over a fine note.

There is one of these in every sealed room in the basin, all from the same pressing. None of us has met.

Before she puts me back she turns me over. There is a dent on my upper face. I've never seen it; it has been against the brick above since I was set. Someone pressed it there so that whoever lifted me would know which way was up. I think it is why I seem to know that myself, without being able to say how. She fits her thumb to it, the way they do. I can't tell whether it fits. I only feel the weight of the press, and then the socket and the dark, and a knuckle ticking away down the lane.

When the heavy tread starts in the west, a palm comes flat against me along with the rest of the wall. I hold still. I don't know what note we give. Afterwards, in the gap before the slate, a knuckle lifts and comes down once. Among several thousand answers that are tight and high there is one small click, loose in its socket, from the brick that was left so the wall could be wrong and find out.

Then somebody strikes a slate.
