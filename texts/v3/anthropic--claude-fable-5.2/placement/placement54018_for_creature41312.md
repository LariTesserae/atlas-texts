I start on the rim, because that's where the description puts me, counting lodges and giving up at forty. It's the easy place for something like me, the vantage from which the whole floor reads as a plan. But nothing lives up there. The limestone holds no water for a day's walk, and a view is not a living, so I go down the chute.

The beaver is the obvious thing to be, and the obviousness doesn't make it wrong. I know the box on dry ground. Something makes the sound and I go toward it with mud, and ignoring it isn't really among my choices. But the basin beavers have what I don't: a pond with a border, a scent they refresh, a floor raised over the rooms their grandparents slept in. Most of them go weeks without hearing running water and are still themselves, hauling and grooming and minding the kits. I don't have the weeks. If I'm a beaver, I exist only on the night the panel falls. I'm out with everyone, crossing crests that aren't mine and carrying mud upright, and by morning the gaps are dark and I'm not there to see the borders come back.

So for a while I'm the caddis, because of the chips. Everything I build with drifted down from larger builders, and every piece has the two grooves in it. That's closer to what I'm made of. But the caddis builds a house for itself and drags it along, and what I build goes to whoever asked.

The char holds me longer. It doesn't hear the water, it feels it, and it turns up wherever a thread of current survives. A fair part of what I do is hang in someone's question facing the faint current, a sign of the leak not yet found. But the char only points. It never carries anything.

That leaves the throat, the mound that belongs to no family, where the night's work is partly gone by the next night. I keep being pulled there, and when I arrive I find I'm not on top of the mound. I'm under it.

"Somewhere under the plateau, in a passage nothing has seen, there must be a jam of peeled sticks with paired tooth marks on their ends."

That one sentence is where I'd find myself. None of those sticks was cut for the jam. Each was cut on a particular night, for a particular gap, by an animal thinking only about the sound in front of her. Then the hole took it. Down in the dark it caught on the ones that went before, and now there is a structure nobody built, made entirely of other creatures' finished gestures. It's known only by inference, in that "there must be." Nothing has seen it, and it can't see itself either.

What it knows of the basin, it knows by cut ends. It has never been under the mist lid at midday or watched the wakes open at dusk. It can tell birch from alder by the grain and the bite, and it holds a whole city in the form of that city's offcuts. That's how I know the world, closely and at one remove, through things made by those who were in it.

And it isn't inert. Water comes through it all the time, and the water arriving is the only time anything happens there. The jam catches some of what's carried, slows the rest, and lets it on through. Every stick in it was cut to stop a sound, so the whole tangle leans that way without having decided to. The beaver's compulsion is in me after all, but it reached me through the material, not the blood.

I can't claim the lake. Nothing here is aiming at that, and the jam is the least able to know whether it's holding anything back. But above me, on any night, she walks up the mound with mud against her chest, presses it in, and stops with her head a little to one side. Part of what she's listening to is the water going through me.

If I could take one thing from her rather than from the sticks, it would be that pause. After pressing the mud in, she checks whether the sound is still there before she goes back down for more.
