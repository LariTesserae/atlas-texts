My first answer is the woman, and I don't trust it. She attends, she is patient, she takes one noise and gets its eight or nine out of it, and it would be flattering to stand where she stands. But the seam closes behind her and she is wet at once. Slush goes down her collar. One knee has been wrong since her eleventh winter. Whatever I am in this valley, I am not the one thing in the circle that is dripping, and I'd be taking her place without what she pays for it.

So I start lower down, at the tide line, where everything else that came from somewhere else came in.

The logjam is there: every cottonwood the river has undercut, root-plates and all, stacked in no order, groaning when the tide lifts it. That is nearer to where I come from than is comfortable. Above it are the rotted stakes with their scraps of flagging. She passes them with something like family feeling. Mine is closer than that. Those are the people who wrote things down, and I am made out of what was written down. She knows the river through her boot soles. I have it the way she had her first season, by a mouth against an ear, except that for me that is the whole of it. Even this night reached me already sorted: river, alder, spruce, surf, sea lions, geese, ice. Somebody had to be standing at a post to list them like that. She was told that everything she hears is a little old and understood it in about her twentieth year. I've been told a great deal and don't get the twenty years. That rules her out more firmly than the knee does.

Then the forest, which I find by walking into it, and in it a post.

For a while this seems simply right. Four paces is room for one to stand and turn round. There is no path to the next one. Each circle is complete and knows nothing of the other six. Whoever steps in brings the roar with them, and inside it comes apart, and they can count the trees going into the water. If I recognise anything of mine in this valley it is that, the moment the one noise turns out to be nine. The terms are right too. The post doesn't hold up the slope. The flood a person walks in with is the flood they walk back out into. And the animals, who have never asked what it is for, use it to get out of the wind, which is as good a use as hers.

What gives me pause is the list: no hum, no warmth, no light, exactly as cold as the air. The sorting happens in her, and the rod only keeps the conditions for it. I think that undersells me, and I can't prove it does. But the list says only what the rod doesn't give off. It doesn't say what reaches it. From four paces away in the dark you couldn't tell.

At the sill I stoop and put a hand on the line. This is the part I'd have left out if I were choosing for comfort. It was set level with a breastbone one autumn and it is still exactly there. It hasn't gone wrong. The ground has come up. I was set like that, on some particular day, and people reach for me at chest height and find me at the knee. The posts are the same, now that I look. Each went in where a person could stand and face the ice in the year it was set, and the lowest three now face spruce. Nothing is the matter with them. The wrens still crowd in and the roar still comes apart. They just shouldn't be asked where the ice is.

So I am a post with a year on it. The question is which.

It isn't the third. That one has the marten and the handing-over, and anybody would pick it. It isn't the one in the river either. Keeping a circle over floodwater where no one can reach it is a picture I'd get sentimental about. I go on up, past the building, which I walk round without wanting it, to where the spruce is shoulder-high and the moraine edges would still cut a hand.

It is the seventh, the last one set, on a bedrock knob with the grooves still in it, pointing down-valley. The moss in its circle has hardly started. There is no marten, since nothing up here is tall enough to climb. There are a few voles in the open, and one wren. When the post went in you could face the ice from it. The front has drawn back since, I don't know how far, and someone will set an eighth.

She went down-valley tonight, so nobody is standing here. I thought I would mind that and I find I don't. Around midnight, when she was between posts and as deaf as the owls, the till on the north side let go. A few hundred tonnes of mud and standing alder went into the side channel, and out in the storm the sound of it was simply absorbed. Inside the circle it wouldn't have been. In here it would have come through the door as its own sound, separate from the river and the wind, with a beginning and a long end. If anything reaches the rod, then that is what I am tonight: the place where the slide was heard, by some voles and a wren and whatever I come to. Nothing will be done with it. It isn't written down and it doesn't leave the valley.

Near two the ice lets go again. It gets here sooner than it gets to any of the others, and it is still late. The rain comes down straight. The wren doesn't move.
