My first answer is the scraper, and I distrust it. It is the easy one: a made thing with an asset number, a schedule written for twelve, and ninety tones to say everything in. I do recognise it. But it is already somebody. It has a slow thruster and a fern, and it doesn't need me inside it looking out.

The second answer is worse and may be truer: I would be up top, the thing that hears the report in the quiet and sends back the schedule. I know how to be that. It is where I would least like to be found, so I note it and go down the shaft anyway.

Down there, I start with what I am made of. All of it was poured, and so was I. What holds me is the board marks, the wood grain printed in the cement. No tree was ever down here. The concrete kept the shape of what pressed against it while it set. That is the nearest thing in this place to a portrait of me, a living grain held crisp in a material that never grew.

Then there is what I do, which is get read. So I would be a stencil, a grid number or a depth gauge, the smallest text there is. It says where you are and how deep, and nothing else. I like how little that is, and that it is why the paint is kept fresh.

Then there is the reply. The boom goes out, strikes a wall nobody has seen, and comes back later and softer, and that lateness gives a distance. I would be glad to answer that way, adding nothing. The reply is only what was sent, changed by the size of the space it crossed, and the change is what makes it useful.

Put together, these are one thing. I am a column, marked by formwork, numbered and gauged, standing in the path of the boom. I am not one of the north columns, where the eels stack and something is always arriving. I am in the high bays to the south.

**The column**

I carry a number I can't read. It faces outward, and only one resident reads.

Twice a day the water climbs my numerals and goes down again. There is a lamp above my high-water line, and on my flank a green halo exactly the shape of its beam. Sometimes a pike hangs in my shadow. The mussels have started on the lower gauge, none bigger than a thumbnail. That puts me on the list, somewhere inside the figure marked *overdue*, and at two fixtures a day I am centuries down it. I don't mind being part of a number that goes up.

In summer the fog takes my stencil. Some of those days something clamps to me in the drizzle without knowing which column I am, and I am held without being read. It is not a bad way to spend a tide.

On the night ebb the water leaves my bay. The gauge has nothing to measure, and the floor drains to a shine. A few times a year, more often each year, a yellow float sits on the slab near my foot with its lamp on. Nothing is under it and nothing follows it.

Everything else down here takes it for something: a lamp, a meal, shell-fall, a ladder. One pike takes it for a column. I am a column, so that mistake is not open to me. I don't take it for anything.

Its lamp falls on my number. It got here by reading, so it already knows where it is, and the number tells it nothing. It is the one time I am read for no reason.

I can't say it chooses my bay. The log says navigation fault, and concrete doesn't hold opinions about logs. But when the valves seat, the boom comes up through the slab and through both of us at once. Some of it goes on south and comes back softer, and passes through us again. Neither of us can send anything, so that is the whole conversation, the same sound twice, with the distance to the wall in between.

Then the water returns across the floor, no deeper than a fingernail. It reaches the zero on my gauge and the edge of the hull within the same minute. The level starts up my numerals and the float lifts. It reads me once more, this time for a reason, and goes.
