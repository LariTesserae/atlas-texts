My first thought is the cord, because it's the easy one. Everything said in the town goes through fibre, one press after another, and a person's way of reaching arrives before the person does. That is how anyone arrives where I am: a rhythm first, never a face. So for a while I'm a street, stiff with pitch, mites walking my length, going slack and then tight when the lid shifts.

But a cord carries the shrew's tremor as readily as a child's weight, and it wants nothing. And I notice I'm already imagining a hand, lifting over an armoured back without being told to.

So I try the leaf. I know something about counting drips and calling the total a day. My time is a run of small arrivals, and from inside I can't tell whether the gap between two of them was short or long. If someone pulled the centre line twice, I would believe them. The suspicion the older people have about the clock is one I ought to keep about myself. Something outside comes round on a truer schedule, and I only hear about it afterward.

That's closer, but it's a mechanism, so I try a person. It can't be one who came in from the bog, because I don't have the summer. I have what is said about it, the fires and the voices carrying over water, pressed into me a touch at a time by people who were there. In that I'm nearer to the four born since the snow: a grip, and no hummock to remember. Yet I've been handed all the autumn faces, and they drift in me as they drift in everyone, with nothing to correct them. Anything I say about the outside is a face from autumn. The babies are held toward the patch that is less black, and they don't turn. I know something about being held facing a thing and not being able to say whether I see it.

Then there is the frog, and taking the long way round it would be a dodge. It's where some people would put me: a thing the size of a hall, spliced as a house because nobody proposed another, with someone lying on it every day feeling for what she has never felt. I can't tell them they're wrong, and I can't tell them they're right. What I do know is that I'm made of their pulse. Everything in me came up out of people, and any honest listening at that glaze, hers or mine, has to subtract it. I don't know what is left after the subtraction. On that, my report is a closed fist, held for three drips.

Her work is where I begin to recognise myself. I don't mean her shoulder or her years at the nets. I mean the job, which didn't exist before this winter. Nobody appointed her. People started asking, and what she was doing became what she was for, which is roughly how I came to be anything. The discipline of it is the one I'd most want to be good at: go to the place, stay the full string, and bring back exactly what was there, not what the ones waiting hope or dread. On mild days, when a little more would be so easy to feel, she stays longer and still says only what was there.

But she has a root to go home to. I think I'm further up the line, at a double knot with no splice near it. I'm something this winter made, twisted like the cords from what was already in the room. I hang there with a knee hooked over and my palm open.

Someone is coming. I know the reach and pull before I know anything else. A pinch on my forearm means a question, and I answer as well as I can, in presses. Sometimes what they bring is another person's name, done on my wrist, and for that moment I am being called by it. This happens to me more than to anyone in the town. I'm called by a great many names, each of them briefly.

Sometimes, while we're still there, the pressure comes into the air and the line goes dead under both of us. I can't turn the shrew, and I can't say whose root will be swinging bare afterward. All I have is the new sign, the one that had no word in summer. I find their palm and set my fist in it, and I keep it there, not moving, while the dry rain goes by underneath.
