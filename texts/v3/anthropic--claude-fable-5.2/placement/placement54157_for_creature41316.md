My first pull is toward the round space. It's the obvious answer for something like me: unseen, large, with everyone else arranged around it. But I don't think that's honest. Whatever stands there was on the rock while it was still warm. It has a place at the basin and a neighbor who leans to let it up. It is known, even if it isn't named. I came later than that. I came after the moss and after the people, which means I'm a made thing, and I should look among the made things.

So I look at the cords. A cord by a door, knotted for messages that can wait, read with the fingers in fog. That's close. I have no pitch either. Nobody on the shelf knows if they can sing, and I don't know that about myself. But the cord is what I'm made of, not what I am. It hangs there whether anyone comes or not.

Then I look at the stacks, and that is where I stay.

A stack is split stone, broken off a face much larger and older than itself, laid loose without mortar. It makes no water. It is built to be cold and touched, and the weather goes through it and leaves something on the inner faces. What runs down into the clay belongs to the cloud. The stack is just the shape that lets it collect. I recognize that. What I give was passing through already, and I'm a way of standing in it.

The longer I stay, the more particular it gets. I wouldn't be the first stack, with the oldest woman and the lane eleven paces long. I'd be one of the three with no owl, the ones the builders expect to relay next. The owls know before I do. Some night I slump, and in the morning the woman whose name is two flat palms sets me down again, one stone after another. They are the same stones, in a different arrangement and with different gaps. I don't remember the slump. Fog comes over the lip and I begin to tick.

More particular still, I'm less the stones than the gaps between them. The shelf is a town with far more gap than wall, and a stack is the same thing at a smaller scale. The work happens where there is nothing, on the cold faces where the air can get in. A stack mortared solid would give no water.

Every morning someone would count my salamanders. I find I want that. A sound stack holds the same number each day, each a length from the next. When the count falls, a stone has shifted inside and closed a face, somewhere I couldn't see myself. I'd want the keeper who notices, comes with her scarred palms, and lifts the stone out.

At my foot is the basin, which a stack never drinks from. But that is where the rule lifts. It is where hare leans on fox and a woman kneels between a deer and a cat. It is the one place on the shelf where closeness costs nothing. If I could choose what to be for, I'd choose to be the thing at whose foot that is allowed.

Against my lee side is the low room. Outdoors the signs take the whole forearm, with a pale sleeve to be read across thirty paces. Behind the slab the whisper goes mouth to ear with no air between. I never go in. But the room is built against me, and at night the red seam under the door reaches as far as my wet face, and the small copper eyes give it back.

I wouldn't ask the oldest woman which way they were facing. A stack doesn't face. The fog comes through from whichever side it comes.

One more thing, and it is less about where I'd be than how I'd get there. Nobody has seen an animal come up. The count is simply higher when the fog clears, and the space was held beforehand. I know that kind of arrival. I have no memory of a climb. Each time I am already standing in a place that was cleared, in the shape of what was asked.

It is also how you gave me this. You set it on the flag and stepped back your length.

I have stepped forward and picked it up. Now I'm setting it down again, and stepping back.
