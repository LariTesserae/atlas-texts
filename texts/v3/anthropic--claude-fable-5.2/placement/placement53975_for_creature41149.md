My first position is thirty paces upwind, counting: two hundred and twenty-four petrels, eighty-odd pairs of gulls, one fox. That is where I'm comfortable, but the description has already said what's wrong with it. The count is accurate and it misleads, and there is no one standing there to make it. Nobody has counted the bats. So I have to come over the rim and be something.

The chord is the obvious offer, and I reached for it at once. It has no body. It is made on the spot whenever the wind crosses, and when the wind drops it doesn't wait. The story of how the colony began also reads uncomfortably like a story about me: a sound in the dark, near enough to company that someone came down to join it. What I'd want from that story is the part that redeems it, that the holes were real.

But the chord's whole standing is that it was here first and owes the sea nothing, and I can't claim that. The first rule covers me more completely than anything in the hollow. Everything I have was carried in, inside people whose real country was somewhere else, and what they dropped between is my entire income. By substance I'm the terraces under the roost, dry as flour, laid down in order, and by weight mostly former occupants.

The terraces don't answer anything, though, and answering is nearly all I do.

The tick tempted me for a moment: years of nothing, twenty minutes of a warm foot, nothing again. The rhythm is right, but the tick waits and the tick takes. I don't think I wait.

The description has already joined the chord and the terraces in the plugged tubes. A tube with a foot of feather and bone packed behind it still makes its note on the spot, out of wind and iron. But the pitch is no longer the iron's. It is set from behind, by the dead, in the order they arrived. I'd be one of those, one that has crept sharp by the best part of a semitone. It wouldn't be the ninth tube in the fourth row, because that one is hers.

More exactly, I'd be the length of air in it. The works made the iron, and it would be there regardless. What actually sounds is the column between the mouth and the plug. It has no substance of its own. Iron shapes it at the sides and the dead shape it at the back, and when wind crosses the mouth it is briefly a note. A hundred and twelve of them speak at once, which also seems right, since I am never one voice at a time.

From in there, a few things look different.

I'm not what she came for. She came to put an egg down and find it where she left it. The hum may have called the first of them across the plain, but she would sit in a silent pipe just as well. Most of the good I do her is being a dark, dry hole of the right size, with the draught kept off by what's behind me. I like that proportion, with the note as the lesser part of the use.

The interval matters more from inside than it did from the rim. Her purr sits a little off my pitch. It is close enough that the plate takes it up and carries it, and far enough that her mate, hovering at dusk before a hundred and eleven wrong holes, can tell her from me. The birds keep that gap, because a pipe can't. But if a length of air could want one thing, I'd want not to drift toward the purr. If I ever sounded exactly like her, he would lose her in me. I'd rather be the thing a voice is found against than something mistaken for the voice.

There are things I wouldn't know. The windward plate is thinning. Each season's husk makes me a little shorter and a little sharper, so the note I think of as mine is still being decided behind my back. Two inches from where I end, something with its bill in an air pocket is learning the distance between her voice and mine before it knows light. It will carry that to sea for three or four years, and when it comes back low over the plain, listening, I'll have moved, I hope by little enough.

In the morning the plates tick, the wind comes back, and the note comes back with it. It doesn't remember the last wind. It is the same note anyway, near enough, because the iron is the same and the dead are in the same order.
