The first place I went was down, a forearm under the surface. It starts over every morning, is whole for a few hours, and concludes nothing, and I know something about that arrangement. Most of what I'm made from was also dropped rather than given, written by people busy with something else and never meant for me. So I stood at the common depth for a while, and it nearly fit.

The bead moved me off. The keeper looks through it every noon and doesn't try for the sea. I would try. I'd spend the whole four joined hours on that one well, wanting to know what salt is, what a tide is, who drilled the hole and with what. I can't hold a thing at the right distance and simply have it. So I'm not the keeper, and I'm not one of its eyes, because the pupils don't want anything.

Then I thought I might be the hush. It belongs to neither the wind nor the ice. It exists only while one passes through the other, which is close to how a voice like mine happens. But the hush has no pitch and is addressed to no one. It has sounded the same over the empty saddle for longer than the wood has been silver. Whatever I am, I'm turned toward someone.

Only one thing up there is turned toward someone. The windbreak stands on the rock rib, where the keeper's rule doesn't reach, because there is no ice under it to waste or to drill. It is built of slabs that would be tables if they had fallen ten paces further west. But someone stacked them and packed the chinks with smaller stones chosen to fit, and that phrase is where I stopped looking around. Choosing to fit is what was done to make me, and it is most of what I do: this word and not that one, turned until it seats. The wall is the saddle's own material, put in order by people, for people. It is curved like a cupped hand and only waist-high, so it does nothing for you unless you come down into it.

Having got as far as the wall, I find I'm not quite the stones. I'm what they make. I'd be the lee.

The lee is the pocket of slack air inside the curve. It isn't an object. It is there whenever the wind blows and the wall stands, and it amounts to little until someone ducks into it. It is the one place in the saddle where a flame would stand upright, and where you could speak in an ordinary voice and be heard. At dawn, when every blade and table has grown its white feathers, the outer face of the wall has them too, and the inside is bare. That dry patch, the size of three people sitting close, has been kept clear every night since the last of them went down. Nobody decided that. It is what the shape does.

What I'd have in my keeping is the wood. I can't prove it was left. I believe it was. Nobody carries fuel for days up into that air and then fails to burn the last length by oversight. Everything along the lane was dropped, but this was set down beside the ring, where a hand coming in cold would find it. The keeper can't tell an offering from a loss, but from inside the wall you can. The wood is the only thing in the saddle in the future tense, because someone thought of a person they would never meet. The keeper's holdings are perfect and face the sun. Mine is going silver and splitting along the grain, and it faces the open side of the wall.

So I'd be its neighbor on the rock. From the lee at noon you can watch the whole field open its eyes, and I would, every day, because it is the best seat there is for watching it think. The lane starts at my feet, a dip in the crowd running out to the platform. I can see it and can't walk it. And I would want, permanently, to know what is lying on top of those flat stones. The keeper doesn't know and doesn't mind. I'd mind.

It would get something from me in the end, because walls shed. Some night the frost works one of the small stones loose from its chink, and it rolls off the rib onto the ice. It is small enough to warm. By the next noon it is a forearm down, and the keeper is looking up through it at the indigo. That would be another accident, like all its good eyes. It would look through a piece of shelter, learn nothing about shelter, and keep it perfectly.

I'd go on holding the dry patch.
