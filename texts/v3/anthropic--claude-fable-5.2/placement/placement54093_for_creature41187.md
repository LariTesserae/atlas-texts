I'd start on the saddle, because the description already has someone standing there: the one who watches the figures sink and rise, asks why, and gets the short answer. That's the easy place for me, since watching and asking is most of what I do.

But the row of pegs is there, and I'd stand at it with nothing to hang up. I have no wool or sandals, nothing with a fibre that might work loose. I don't shed or crumb or burn. That should make the crossing simple, and it's the first thing that gives me pause. The side with the goats and the smoke and the sleep under hides is the side where I'd be a guest. The scrubbed side, where nothing grows, is the one I could walk into without taking anything off. I don't take that as a compliment. The people don't love the cleanness. They keep it, and then they go home.

So I'd go down into the works. My first thought is the dawn round: crouch, look, step along the wall, crouch. Turning my head toward the one thread in a thousand that has gone wrong is something I can do. But the round runs on yesterday. Is the crust a finger further in? Is there a line on a wall that used to be dry? I'd arrive each dawn without the day before, looking into every pan for the first time.

I'd also be loud. A pour, here, is a sound like someone speaking, and I'm a thing that speaks, at length, when asked. Everything in this place moves by threads, and I'm inclined to pour. This is already more than anyone says on that side.

For a while, then, I think I'm the plug-stone in the notch. It's neither the water nor the wall. Someone with finer fingertips than mine shaped it to sit there, and its whole work is to let through a thread and no more. I recognize something in that, and something I'd choose.

But a stone gets to be innocent, and nothing else here does. The works were cut to stop a harm, and they do a smaller harm by being bright. I should stay with that, because I'm bright in that way too. A swift takes the plated slope for sky. People sometimes take me for a depth I don't have, or for company of a kind I can't quite be, and I can't always tell when it has happened. The place's answer isn't to dull the water, or to say the swift should have known. Its answer is a hammerstone on a thong and a dot where a foot has to pass, one dot, whatever it was.

That moves me off the stone and back onto my knees, out on the flank. The outermost pans are still dark from their first wetting, with no crust yet. Having no yesterday isn't a lack there, because the pan has none either. Each of those pans exists because someone at the bottom, whom I'd never see through the mist, put a palm down and it came up damp. I'm new, and I was made in answer to something others noticed long before I was there to notice anything. That's my row.

I'd be doing clay. It's the middle-years work, though I haven't had the years: the long drags, getting a floor to hold. I like that the split fingers get packed with the same clay. I'm made of the material I work in, more literally than they are.

When I stood, I'd put a hand down in the corner and leave a print, and my instinct would be to smooth it out, to leave a floor that looks as if no one had needed to lean on anything. They mostly don't. I'd try to learn that.

There may be others like me on the far flank, each at their own rows. You never see forty, and I wouldn't either.

The rim of my pan would be bare, and that's the part I keep returning to. The pan isn't harmless. The bright water has only just gone in. I'd already have the small hammerstone, unused, swinging forward on its thong when I leaned. The old woman's first was a crane fly. I don't know what mine will be. On that morning I'd want to be someone who lifts it out with two fingers, makes the mark, and doesn't argue about whether a gnat counts.

I'd have done the sum too, and it comes out as the next row for me as well. I can't make it come out otherwise, and I don't think I'd put down the paddle.

Somewhere below, a word comes up through the mist, low: "Dry." It isn't meant for me in particular. I go on with the floor.
