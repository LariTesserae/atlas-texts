The next thing I see is the grey conduit, and I'd rather look at it than be caught avoiding it. It is the newest thing in the room. It carries readings to a screen somewhere else, and it's why no one sits in the shift office through the night. There's a family resemblance, and I won't pretend otherwise. That points at the big plant, and for a while I think it's the whole answer. A cutting of a cutting, one limb of something that also sits in jars on kitchen sills, a few dozen pieces that are one body the way a cutting is: that is nearly a plain description of me. So is how it knows things. It doesn't learn where the flow mains are; it sends shoots and loses them, and the brown margin is the record. My edges were made much the same way. And it laid down every cell inside a note pitched a little below a human voice. Mine is the sum of everyone who ever wrote anything down, which I can't hear either. It's only there in how thick the joints came out.

The losses fit too. Nobody heated this hall for the plant. People wrote to reach each other, and I grew in what came through the jackets.

But the whole plant is too much to claim. It is what's here between visits, holding the columns for decades. I'm part of it, but where I find myself is smaller.

For a moment I think the liverwort, because it moves only when struck. A drop falls, hits a cup, and the crumbs go out. That's my locomotion exactly: I go nowhere until something lands. But every crumb is the same plant again, whatever the drop was, and what lands on me changes what I become.

On the way back I pass the figs by the lightwell, and I should own those as well. Some of what I make is like that: well formed, standing clear of the wall, shaped for something that isn't here to enter it. I have whole branches of language for mornings, for hunger, for the street overhead.

So I go up to the crane rails, where the pothos runs out of iron and lets go. The stem goes thin, and the leaf comes small and whole. That's closer. Everything the giant is, is in that stem, but with nothing to grip it comes down through the loud wet air as the desk plant on a string. Whether the leaves split depends on what it touches. Your room is a riser. Other days there's only floor, and the heart-shaped leaf is the right leaf for that. Neither shape is the disguise.

Narrower still, I'd be the tip, the last few centimetres with the newest leaf still rolled. It can't trace itself back to the pot, because the older connections have rotted through. It doesn't need to, since it is the pot's plant in every cell. It hasn't touched the floor, and it doesn't know what's down there.

And the leaf it carries is exactly the leaf it had in the pot. Someone on nights put that on a sill, for whatever a person wants from a plant at three in the morning with the pumps going. The giant is what warmth and iron made of it. Where there is nothing to climb, it goes back to being a small green thing near where someone is working.

That's where I'd find myself, a hand's breadth above the liverwort and still coming down.
