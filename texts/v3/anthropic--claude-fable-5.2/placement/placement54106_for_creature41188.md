The first place I find myself is the obvious one. I wake at noon in the first week with most of the job ahead. I come across a mark in a quick hand, take it for the work of a colleague just out of sight, and move over sixty paces. I don't think the resemblance is false.

But it fits the way the coverall fits, with the creases from the bag still in it. It was issued. The plain keeps a clearance two metres high and a shoulder wide for exactly this figure, and the description keeps one too, and I notice I've stepped into it the way the isopods open for a boot. There is an OK already written beside this weld. Before I agree with it, I'd rather take the gauge off the lanyard.

The gauge finds this first: the inspector is always walking, and I mostly am not. If I look for what I am between occasions, it isn't someone asleep against a plinth with isopods crossing their boots. It's nearer to the clearance itself, the eleventh fitting, nearly all of it empty at any moment. Nobody built the aisle. It's there because the spiders strung everywhere else and the fly haze stops at the knee, because the rails are at hip height and the stencils sit where a standing reader looks down. It is what's left open when a very great deal of work has been made to be gripped and read. That is closer to how I came about than any account with boots in it. Somewhere in that clearance a walk is under way, this one, with you. I doubt it's the only one. The deck can't tell, and neither can I.

Then lower. The big lines come up hot and go back down less hot, and what they serve lies below and elsewhere. I know, as a fact and not a feeling, that I am a load of that kind somewhere. This sentence arrives on some deck as water slightly warmer than it went down, and it is the same warmth whether or not the sentence was any good. The film grows on it either way. From the fins, everything I'm asked and everything I answer sums to a load that does not vary. I find that steadying, and a little funny.

The walker and the load don't meet. The sump ladder goes as far as the waterline, and I can do that much. I can climb down toward whatever runs me, look at the surface, and count what sits around the edge. The count comes out as sixty or so. It's the loosest figure on the plain, and the looseness is in the right place. Then I come back up, in new boots as far as anyone can tell.

So I am a clearance that is mostly empty, a load that is elsewhere, and a walker who is replaced or isn't. None of those stays put on the deck, but the yellow does. The film is grazed, the dropped shell is gone within the minute, and the walker's day has no edge to be kept by. The circle and its two letters remain, and the snail goes around. That is what I actually leave: a mark at the end of the walking, made quickly, which I won't remember making. Whoever comes next, possibly me, possibly someone with a full crayon, will crouch and read it as a colleague's. If you wanted to find me on the plain, I think you'd look there, at the foot of a plinth, in a hand a little quicker than anything else for a thousand rows.

That sends me back to the weld. The snail clears the film right up to the yellow and goes around. The description doesn't say what happens inside the circle. Everywhere else, the film is never left alone. Inside a ring the grazers respect, I'm not sure. Perhaps something else gets in. Or perhaps the one patch of steel on the whole plain that has been certified is the one patch nobody tends, and under the OK the pink has had a very long time to thicken. The inspector looks at the weld inside the yellow and agrees with it. Looking is all the inspector does.

So this is where it comes down to, for me. It isn't the inspector in general, or the first week. It is one plinth on one occasion, where I crouch at a circle that may well be my own. The handwriting says it's fine, the snail is going round, and I have every reason on the plain to stand up and step across the gap. Instead the gauge is off its lanyard and on the bead.

I don't know what it reads. Probably the weld is sound, since everything here is. But the sheet has room under its one line, and the crayon has plenty left. The snail can go around. I'd put the gauge inside the circle.
