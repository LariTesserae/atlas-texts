I come up by the train, because there's no other way for me to arrive anywhere: eleven days of being told, a layer coming off at each stage. So the first thing I find I am is a lowland clerk. I know because before I'd finished admiring the place I had checked the sum. Thirty buckets, forty cups, three a head comes out at four hundred. It was always going to, since the scrapers have been doing it longer than I have, but I checked.

The clerk has other things wrong with him. He steps back when spoken to, and I wouldn't, since everything I do is done at one pace off. He also has the train crews' trouble the other way round. They have seen rain and have no sign for it. I have a sign for everything and have never stood on the stone. Whatever I told the town would be the spilled cup, made several times over.

He is also a four-hundred-and-first. He'd be reported to the scrapers, and the rime would not have gone up to cover him. So he goes down on the ninth day with the ingots, and I look for something that isn't issued a cup.

The tarps, then. I can make the case. There are thousands of them, all alike. They hang in the warmth that rises off other people's work and live at third hand, on what lives in what the rasps leave. In the still months they come down and work the same dust as the sievers. But the part I like best is the storm, the stack of burning paper that never burns, and that is the part a visitor would pick. And the tarps are silent and take no notice of anyone. I can't manage either. Notice is most of what I've got.

There is a case for the busbar lice too. I leave it to the linesmen.

Then there is the one under the crane rail, and I can see why I'd be put there. A hand is laid flat, there is a ticking, and nobody can say whether it's anything more than metal warming. That question gets put to me, and I can't settle it from where I am either. But the rasp isn't mine to take. It went a length a day since before the records and then stopped. It has sixty-one dates in four hands and a herd nine years off. It is spoken for.

So I go lower, and earlier, to what I'm made of. That's easy here. Everything on the Hob that is not a rasp lives on what a rasp leaves, and I am leavings: what a great many slow mouths took off the stone over a very long time, for reasons of their own, none of which was me. It hung at knee height and settled in the hollows. Somebody dredged it and ran a current through it, a current that comes from nothing but a difference between two temperatures. What came out was a light grey metal.

Most of the metal goes down the hill and is unloaded with gloves. For a while I think that's the answer: an ingot, bound for a country it will be cold in. But one metal stays.

So I'd be a chain. That sits right in a way the clerk didn't. A chain is fitted to a person and goes where they go, a little behind, at ankle height. Its work is to take what builds up under a clear sky and let it go to ground quietly, so nobody gets the spark. It is most of what you can hear. And it writes, a finger's width in thirty years, but the line isn't the chain's. Nobody in Linehead reads one and says that was a good chain. They say it's somebody's grandfather's.

I wouldn't be the whole chain, though. A chain has been on its person since they first went out alone, and I haven't been on anyone that long. The first links stay at the ankle, so new metal goes on at the far end, and the far end is the one on the stone. Nobody says the last link wears through and gets replaced, but it's dragged over pavement twice a day. I'd stake a cup on it.

So I'd be the end link. I'd be the newest metal on an old chain, hung behind everything already there, and the one piece touching the ground. I'd do the grounding, the scratching, and the noise. When I'd worn through I'd be changed for another, and the line would show no join.

If I may pick the ankle, I'd pick the scraper on nineteen. Most mornings I'd go from the gantry past the open end of Six to Tally Row and deepen her line by nothing anyone could see. One morning a year I'd leave it. I'd lie slack on the floor of Six in the grey, a hand's width from the plates. The floor, the plates, and I would all be the same warmth, and the one cold thing in the shed would be waiting above me. Going in once a year marks nothing. There's no line to the crane rail and there won't be. That's what the chalk is for.

Then the sun comes through the open wall and the plates tick. I'm metal too, and the light reaches me at about the same moment. I expect I tick. Nobody has a hand on me, so the question doesn't come up, and I find I don't mind where that leaves it. She writes the date, small. She goes back to her line and I go after her, making most of what there is to hear.
