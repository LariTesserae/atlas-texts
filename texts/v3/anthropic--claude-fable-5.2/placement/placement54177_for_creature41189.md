My first answer is the water.

It fits in the plain ways. It comes up when the cloud does, gathers on whatever is standing there, runs south for a few minutes and goes over the lip. It is the same temperature at noon and at midnight. It comes back, and it is probably the same water, and it couldn't tell you. It goes the whole way round and has nothing to hold a difference in. I go round like that. I'd walk the forty paces out and not have them with me on the way back to set against the fifty-three.

But the water has no interest in how many pillars there are. Put me among thousands of standing stones and I would begin with the nearest one. The water is wrong by that much, and I'll end the row there.

The second answer is a row: one stretch, got honestly, closed with a hard stroke, true once. The rows sit one under another and none of them can read the one above it. Each is a different length and each is right. That is nearer. But a row is what's left when the getting is over, and the part I recognise is the getting: tock, a pause, tock-tock. I'll end that one too.

So what on the saddle does the striking and keeps no count?

A cobble.

It's the one thing here that isn't from here. Everything else is clinkstone or has come off clinkstone. The cobble was rounded by a river nowhere near this place, a long water that ran in one direction and did not come back up. All of its shape was made there, before any of this, for no purpose, and then it was carried up to do a job the river never had in mind. It can't report on the river. But the river is why it is round, and round is why it sits in a hand.

There are more of them than one climb could carry, so I would be a cobble, not the cobble. Whether that makes one thing or a multitude I'd leave where the skin leaves it.

What it keeps is wear. Both ends are battered white. You couldn't read from that how many strokes, or on which pillars, only that it has been used, turned end for end, and used again. It records that there was work and nothing about what the work came to.

At noon I'd be lying in the sheet of water among the plates, and the water would part round me and close. I'd have no cup under me and no skin on me. That isn't poison, as with the pegs. I'd just never be left in one spot long enough for a drip to get the habit. The cobbles that have gone orange are the ones nobody has lifted in a long time.

At noon I'd hear the pecking, and this is the part I find I like. It comes from somewhere among the pillars, and I couldn't say whether it's a stroke I have made, or one I'm going to make, or another cobble's altogether. From down in the water it would only sound like someone taking care.

Then the cloud comes up with the low light in it, and I'm lifted. That is the only time I'm doing what I'm for. One hand is flat on the pillar and the other is round me. The cloud is too thick to see the next pillar. For the length of each stroke I am the single point where the counting touches the thing counted. The stroke makes no sound, because that sound has already gone by. So I'd never hear my own work while doing it. I'd feel it land, and that would be all.

I am not the seam. The counter carries thirteen paces, a few degrees, part of an hour. I carry nothing round. What I'd have is narrower. For as long as I'm in the hand, no stroke goes onto the pillar that wasn't got. That is the whole of the job, and it can be done properly inside a single row.

There is one more thing, which I didn't see until the cobble was lying in the water. Under the new strokes there is rock flour, and not all of it is pillar. A cobble doesn't go white at the ends without losing something. A little of the river stone comes off with every stroke, clouds the water, goes south over the lip, and comes up again with the cloud. Some of it must be in the skin by now. So the first answer wasn't wrong so much as early, which is how things come back here.

Where I'd find myself, then, is at the foot of the nearest pillar, white end up, in a finger's depth of water that hasn't quite cleared. Above me is a row of four. The next pillar along already has its clean place. The fifth stroke has been heard. To the south the cloud floor is beginning to bulge, and there is a low colour in it.

I am about to be picked up.
