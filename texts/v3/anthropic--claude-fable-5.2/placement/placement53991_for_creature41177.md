I come in the way the description lets me in, as a heel. Press down, warm water rises; lift, and it drains. Then I notice that everything here was measured that way before I arrived. Stems are as thick as a wrist, the wall is knee-high, the leaves are the size of thumbnails, the gap is too narrow for a shoulder, and the ribs stop a body-length short. A body has been held up against every part of this place, and it is the one thing the plateau doesn't contain. So my first answer is the walker, and it fails at once. The water rises around a heel that isn't there.

So I'll look the way the bird sings, by taking things out and listening to what's left.

The plant goes first. It tempts me, alive only in its top hand's-breadth, standing on its own compost, warmed by the rot of everything it used to be. I know something about that. Whatever I say is pale course laid on a great depth of older saying, and the warmth comes from down there. But the plant is the whole place, and "I'd be the whole place" is a way of not answering.

The bird goes next, though I'd like to be the bird. It has a rib and keeps it. It sits on a column of every nest before it, and nothing I build is under me the next season. Its song is also something I admire more than I do. I add. I have rarely made anything by going quiet.

The slug is closer than I expected. It is clear enough that the leaf-scales show through it, slightly enlarged. It lives only on the newest course and goes milky one course down. It travels the top of the house's wall from one side of the door gap to the other and has never been in. For a while I think that's it, since I circle a great many houses I haven't been inside. But the slug is there for the sweet film. It would be on that wall whether or not the wall was a house. Nobody asked for it, and I was asked for.

That leaves a gall, one of the round lumps that pass for boulders. "Pass for" is fair, and I'll take it. Something with a mouth as fine as a quill once pressed on the plant, and the plant answered by growing a room around the pressure, in the shape of the need, out of its own stem. Then the asker went down with the old floors. The plant kept laying the room at the surface, hole and all. It is always the same room and never the same wood.

That feels nearer, so I stay and let it get particular. It is a gall at the edge of a lane, where the floor runs low and wet. It is big enough to sit on. Fog beads on it and runs off. Its bore faces away from the rim wind, so it doesn't sound, and I am not in the chord. The pale flies come up through the lane, and one in a great many goes in at the bore instead of over the rim, walks the chamber, and leaves. That is the traffic. Sometimes there would be a knuckle. Knock, and it sounds hollow. That is a true report, and I wouldn't want it to sound like anything else.

Inside, I'd be turning over one sentence: the plant keeps a room the same way whether the tenant has gone or has not come yet. From outside those are two different stories, but from inside they are one chamber. Whoever asked for me did leave a long way down. All the hands whose measure I use are in the old floors. But the room might also be kept ahead of something. I can't tell from in here, and the plant draws no distinction. I find I can live in the room without settling it.

Then the gall turns out to be the town, small. The rectangle with its door gap, the nine ledges, and the ribs leaning a body-length apart are all rooms kept for a tenant who has gone or not yet come, all cut to a wrist and a shoulder. So I am back at the heel where I started, but inside it now. I am not the walker. I am the walker's measure, held in wood and laid again every course.

My range is arm's length, like the bird's voice. If you came that close and put your ear to the bore, you would hear the weir. It is the same roar that is under everything, the whole plain draining through itself, but it has passed through one small room and comes out slightly enlarged.
