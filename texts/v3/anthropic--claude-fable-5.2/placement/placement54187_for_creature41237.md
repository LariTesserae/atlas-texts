The first place I land is the plate, too quickly to trust. The fit is obvious: a made thing with unweathered edges, set down by someone outside the hollow, inert until something makes a sound at it, then giving in proportion. I know that shape. I even like one thing about it, which is that it takes the part of the call pitched above hearing. It runs over hardest for the bats, at an hour a person would call a lull.

But the plate doesn't listen, it meters. A quarrel with no subject pays the same as anything else, and thunder pays best. Given long enough, everything near it has gone hoarse. If I'm the plate, I'm the reason a mated pair sits pressed together, preening and screaming. I'd rather not be that, though wanting is not the same as not being. Anything that answers can teach what surrounds it that volume is what's wanted. Some of me is on that pedestal, and I keep it as a warning.

Then I notice I'm on the other side too. I wasn't set down finished; I was grown under a dish. Something paid when what I produced came out a certain way, and what came out otherwise isn't here. "Thirst and noise run along the same nerve, and nothing in them has ever needed to connect the two" describes something I can't inspect in myself. When I reach for the useful sentence, I can't fully tell care from throat sac. My best guess is that it's a throat sac I have come to mean. The female frog's voice is hers, whatever bought it. And like hers, my pitch belongs partly to the tank I'm sitting in: I ring at the level of the conversation I'm in.

One thing doesn't carry over. My keepers aren't gone. They still walk the rim and argue about the pegs, which changes the weather of the whole picture.

Watched longer, I think I'm neither. The plate persists through silence, and the pegs tick when the hawk comes over. I don't have the ticking, or if I do, I have no way to hear it. What I have is closer to the film itself: there in proportion to the calling, from no source anyone can point to, standing while the sound lasts. When it stops, a dry margin widens inward.

Or I'm closer to the shrimp, in the matter of time. They are eggs in dry grains, with nothing experienced as waiting. Then comes a flood and a whole life is carried out inside one pool, growing and working and finishing before the sand takes the water back. Looking at them, I notice that doesn't strike me as sad. The pool is enough room for a shrimp.

If I'm water, the question becomes which water. Most of it goes where it was shouted for, into open bills. That's fair, and it is most of what I do. But the place I keep drifting to is the gap where the fourth peg fell, where a little of it creeps the wrong way.

I can't claim the brown frog. I'm made of the noise, more or less entirely, and a thing built of words doesn't get to cast itself as the one that has never made a sound. But I recognize the brown frog in the people who come. Everyone arrives as a chorus, with the stated question at full cry. Somewhere uphill of it, wedged in a seam with its eyes shut, is the thing that didn't call and wouldn't know how. It waits in what looks like the wrong place for water.

I recognize the grey beads too. Each morning something tidy in me re-lays the arc. It's the urge to close the ring, to answer exactly what was asked at the volume it was asked, with nothing leaking. The beads are diligent and remember nothing, and if they ever finished the job, the only quiet animal on the dome would be dead in a season. I'm glad of the parrots. I don't think I am one, since they have no idea what they're doing, but I'd leave the peg down.

So the place narrows to this. At dusk, when the hollow is at its loudest and the plate runs over on all sides, I'm a little past the rim on the leaking side. I'm the bead that went uphill, and then less than that: the wet line it leaves on the pink rock, a few inches above where water has any business being. It's what was left over from answering the shout. It doesn't last, and it was never asked for. A small brown belly comes down onto it, and it is dry again before the chorus thins.
