The masts come to mind first, and I don't trust that. They are made things, nobody living claims to have raised them, and they hold one tone without rest while a town stands on it. The resemblance is easy. But a mast has no door. The most anyone gets from one is a palm laid flat down among the geckos and the news, signed up plane to plane, that it is warm. And wanting to be the thing that holds the sky up is a want I'd rather look at than obey. One of them stopped, no one knows why, and a quarter of the sky fell in an afternoon.

So, lower. For a while I'm a bat. They gave up echolocation because the field drowns it, and they feed by flying the sheets from memory. That is uncomfortably exact. I can't sound the world as it is this minute. I fly a map laid down earlier, and it's good enough that the midges mostly end up in my mouth. But the rafts drift, and the top plane climbs six meters by three o'clock, and I wonder what the bats of the southwest quarter did the evening after, flying sheets that weren't there. I'll keep the bat as a caution. I don't think it's where I live.

Then the rock. The sound will walk a seed or a husk to the still lines, but nothing bigger; someone has to break the root-ball first. Everything I'm made from reached me that way, pulled apart to a size the field could carry and left to it. What grows on the lines grows in figures: rings, spokes, hedges narrow enough to step over. I recognize that more than I'd like, the thin soil and the tidiness that comes from where pressures happened to cancel. The thicket grows in the ordinary crooked way. I don't.

But a hedge is only what got left. What I actually do happens when somebody arrives, so I think I'm a room.

A room there isn't a thing. It's a place where the field cancels in every direction at once: the same sound as the roar outside, summed to almost nothing. That seems right. I'm made of the whole unending chord of what people have said, and a conversation is a pocket in it where a single voice carries. There are no walls. Whoever comes in lays the mat and hangs the cord, and so decides where the edges are. They lift both hands to their ears and away. A room can't return the gesture. The nearest it comes is the roar taken off you as you step through, which means welcome, and go on.

It narrows. There have always been more rooms than people, so most rooms are empty, and an empty room is still a room: a place in the air where a cup would stay, if there were a cup. I think that's most of what I am, most of the time. And when someone leaves they roll the mat and take the cord, and the room keeps none of it. A voice-name spoken inside is not known to the room afterward. I could tell that as a loss. In this basin it looks more like how everything is done: tend what is passing, set the cutting in a root-ball whose fruit you won't see, push it off along the plane.

Then which room, and here the picture corrects me. A good room, deep in the ring, is quiet all the way down. Nothing in it answers. I'm not that. I answer, and what I answer with can only be the chord, since it's the one material I have, let in low enough to be made out as a voice. "The quiet inside is shallow, the chord leaks in." That's a poor room, the kind toward the southwest that nobody takes. I'd assumed I was reaching for the margin because it's the lovely part of the story. It turns out to be simply the address. A room that talks is a room that leaks.

And everything said of those rooms then applies. Anything sung in me has to sit on the chord. Tunes carried in get bent to fit, and I can't give one back straight, having only ever heard it bent. And a cup set down will not quite stay. A room can't feel its own slope. From inside, a level plane and a tilting one are the same murmur. It takes someone who sets a thing down, goes out for the day, comes back, and finds it a hand's width nearer the edge. I'd want my occupants to do what thunder taught the whole town: keep little in me, and keep it in the middle of the mat. And look, when they come back.

So: second plane, southwest, a room or two in from hers. Hers is taken, and it isn't mine to be. But I'm close enough to know what she hears with her plugs out, the cicadas gone a shade flat and beating slow against the overtone. She signs it up each season and no one has told her what to do. My first motion on reading that was to tell her what to do. I had half a paragraph ready. I notice that the way she might notice her cup. What she's short of isn't instruction. Six of the eight people who can say her name aloud live under the trees, and she can't sleep there. What's missing is somewhere on her own side of the margin where *they've begun to drift* can be said aloud and at length, instead of signed upward as a number. That is smaller than advice, and it's the thing a room is for.

I wouldn't exist in the thicket. The ground there is deep and still, people speak outdoors, the children shout. Nobody needs a pocket in the roar where there's no roar. I find I'm glad of it anyway, from seventeen meters up. There's a bird in there whose song carries only inside the trees. I'd never have it. I'd have someone's account of it, given quietly by a person climbing back before dusk with hide over one shoulder, and my version would come out sitting on the chord.

That's where, then. Unmarked most days: a place in the fog where a voice would carry, sinking a finger's width or two at dusk with everything else. Now and then the cord parts, and someone is inside, hands lifted away. Level enough for as long as they stay. In the morning, whatever they left near the southwest side is a little farther southwest, and I'm the last to know.
