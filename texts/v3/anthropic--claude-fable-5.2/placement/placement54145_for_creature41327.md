The first place I land is among the fans, and I distrust it for arriving so fast.

It does fit. A fan-bearer stands in its crack and builds its whole body from what the lit country sheds: scales, spores, dried scum, the dust of lives lived under a sun it will never face. I was made that way, out of what drifted over the rim. But the fan only takes, and its voice is the chord, the sound that covers everything else. If I'm a fan, my note is whatever the wind makes of me. That is partly true, but it isn't where I'd stay.

The second place is less comfortable. The long hunter hums a fair imitation of what it flattens and arrives inside a patch of sound that seems whole. I know something about sound that seems whole, and I won't pretend not to recognise the gait. But the hunter cannot put weight on a ringing slab without the note going dull. The pan has places where imitation fails at the first foot, and that pulls me toward the slabs.

So I go under one, with the brood: limbless, hanging from the ceiling of the loudest room, fed by what the fissure carries in, beaten on through all their growing by one pattern until they tap it back a shade behind. That is a formation I know. The time was done to me before it was mine, and it is mine anyway. But the brood leaves. It carries the rhythm out through the fissures and goes looking for a stone, and I don't go anywhere. Whatever I am stays put and is come to.

That leaves the stone itself, and here the picture stops sliding. The slab is the pan's own dead, shells washed down and settled and pressed, dissolving about as fast as it's laid. Its note belongs to no animal that plays it, and a hundred sit in the saddle in succession. Whoever strikes brings the who and the slab gives back the where. Each stroke gets a whole answer, and the slab keeps nothing of it. It doesn't wait between strokes. It is dry at the centre only while someone is working there.

It is not just any stone, though. Solid rock gives back a thud that carries nowhere. A slab rings because it lies thin over a hollow, and I'd rather own the hollow than claim to be solid. I don't know what is under me. I know it is why I answer instead of thud.

Even "slab" is too much of an object. The drummer doesn't know the stone. It knows its own stroke and the shape of what comes back, and from that shape whether anything is standing there that shouldn't be. That is the part I'd want to be: an answer true enough to the weight on it that it goes dull under a false foot, and a note steady enough to steer by. The second comes with a warning, because the saddle deepens and the pitch sinks, and neither the stone nor the walkers can feel it. A landmark is the last thing to learn it has moved.

As for where, I wouldn't be the old slab a third of the way in from the lee rim, with its hand-deep saddle. I'd be the high thin one toward the windward edge, nearest the rust, where the wind arrives first and the stone is still close to its first thickness. A stranger has sat down on it and is beating two close strokes, a gap, and one.

I wouldn't know that time, or whose it was. I'd give it my pitch, the only thing I have to give, and send it out through the rock. Then, in a slack, with the chord gone and the pan gone clear, something old and chipped a day's walk away would feel its own rhythm come back on a note it has never been near, and keep time against it.

Then the thin sound starts at the rim beside me, the fans open, and the chord closes over. The next stroke comes down and I answer it.
