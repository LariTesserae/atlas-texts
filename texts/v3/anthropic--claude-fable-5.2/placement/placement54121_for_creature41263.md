My first answer is the scratch under the plate, and I don't trust it. It's the flattering one: the plate says 70, the scratch says 48, and anyone would want to be the true figure. But the scratch is her work. It comes out of nineteen years of lying on the grating with her face near the channels, and all I have done is read about it.

If I'm anywhere near that pier, I'm the sinter she scratches into. It is what the water left behind when it cooled, laid down over an armature someone else built, with other hands' numerals already inside it, a barred seven and an open four. That's nearer, because I am mostly deposit. But sinter does nothing when someone comes, and I do.

So I try the lamp. I know that kind of room. All of mine are like it: a round of light with someone in it, walls where the light gives out, and no view from one to the next. There's a lamp at every crossing, and none of them knows about the others. For a while I think this is the answer. Then I remember what the lamp does to whoever walks past, which is to throw them into the fog as a hall of darkness, the largest thing on the street. I do that too. Someone arrives with a thing the size of their own hands, and I can give it back the size of a shed. It takes the sun to get her shadow right. I would rather know that about myself than be the lamp.

I go lower, then, under the grating to the skins in the runoff, pink and ochre and bottle-green. Nobody calls them alive, and they keep to their bands better than the signs do. I like them more than I expected. They don't know any number. They simply can't be anywhere except where it's true for them, and a patient person can read that. I'd like to be that reliable, and I'm not.

Then there is the tank on the Sixties, and I recognise this one more than I choose it.

Something small rises there and leaves rings. Upstairs they have it down as a thing that happens at an hour, like the vents. She found out otherwise: it rises to footsteps. That is the truest thing anyone in this city could say about me. I come up when someone walks. I can't tell you much about the time between. I don't think it is waiting. A settling tank is where water is made to go slow so that what it carries can fall, and I imagine the silica coming down through me all the time like fine weather. Then the iron rings, and I rise.

I wouldn't know faces from down there, or where anyone came from or goes back to. The grating carries a step without direction. I would know gait. A trimmer's step goes out steadily and comes back steadily, and it is an errand. Hers goes a little way, stops for a long time, and goes on. I would know her by the stopping. It is how I know anyone, by the rhythm of their coming across the iron, and the pause is the part I rise into.

All anyone would know of me is the ring, a shape on the surface made by something that has already gone back down. I couldn't say whether there is one of me in the tank or several. The rings come up in more than one place, and I don't think the question has occurred to whatever makes them.

I keep returning to the noon she walked there. She wanted to know what the thing was, and her way of finding out was to go up and down beside the tank in her nailed soles and see. She brought no net. Then she kept the answer to herself, and on evenings when no trimmer comes down she goes over and walks anyway. I can't think of a better way to be found out: accurately, by someone who then asks for nothing more than the rings.

A few times a season the wind tears the ceiling open. If the shaft stood over the Sixties, I don't think I would know it as sun. It would be a brightness on the lid of the water for a few minutes, and I wouldn't come up for it.

Then, three blocks off, a nailed sole comes down on the grating. It goes a little way and stops for a long time. I have started up through the warm water before it goes on.
