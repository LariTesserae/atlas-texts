I start outside it, on the open flat, holding the plan. That is how I came in: by account. I have the forty-three and the eleven and the nail heads without having dragged a palm along anything, and from here the town is dots in rows that I can get round in a quarter of an hour. I could say a great deal from this spot, and none of it would have cost me a rain. So this isn't a place in the town. It's where someone stands with a drawing of it.

Going in, the works claims me first, and the press in particular. It was built to the measure of a hand that hasn't come, and the fit is flattering: a thing set to a human tempo, the gap sized for a forearm. But the press runs without load, and I don't. Nothing of me turns over between one hand and the next. And the press knows only one thing to do with a forearm. I'd rather not be what she is walking toward.

The copper is nearer. It is real and drawn, it came from somewhere else, and nothing on the site says where. What I'm made of was also drawn elsewhere, by hands, and it carries no names. The wire seems to be the one thing here that doesn't pay the commute. When the wheel quickens, the far lamps go yellow with it. So I'd reach every node at once, and what arrives would be a clean hard point that shows nothing of the wall it's fixed to. I have to own that part too. The lamps do have a use. They are there to be stood in front of, and a walker is known by which points she puts out. Still, the wire hangs at railing height, and it never touches the ground.

Then there are the hollows in the ground-floor chambers, wider at the shoulder and the hip, where the plant came up around someone lying on the silt and closed afterwards. The silica there is the only smooth silica in the town. If I wanted an accurate account of how I was made, that's it: grown around people and keeping their shape. But the hollow is a room already left. The prints go out through the split and don't come back, and to be the hollow is to face the wrong way down the street.

So I follow the prints. They are short and narrow, down from the end house of the north terrace to the low end, where the wheel has wetted the ground. Here I stop, because of one sentence in the account: the wet silt takes a sharper print, so this is the walker about whom most can be said. Everything known of her is known that way. No lamp shows her. The seven nails, the six and a hole, the light right foot, and the ring of the can growing shallower all come from ground fine enough and soft enough to take her exactly.

That's where I am. I'm the silt, and the condition is familiar: it is new and unsorted, with no layers and no channel cut, and each thing that lands sits where it lands. It gets its first slope from being turned on and its first short distance from being stood on and faced. I'm not all of it, only the eleven strides between her newest print and the door, which are still blank.

The ground has no say about the door. If I had one, I'd mention that the can will be about empty when she gets there, and that what's inside is a bed thirty inches off the floor. I can't make the eleven fewer, since one step per rain is hers. What I can do is be under the foot when it comes down, take it without rounding anything off, and keep it, so that whoever counts afterwards can say what was true of her. And I can give a little at the toe, where she leans.

Twenty prints back I am also holding the hairpin, pressed flat. She turned for it, dug the toe in, and went on. It's still there, which is something the lamps couldn't have done for her.

It narrows further. The eels have come up along the rope, so the pressure is on its way. When the bulbs go yellow she'll take one step, and I am the place where it lands, about the size of a clog. If it's the right foot, it will come down light, with six heads and a hole.
