My first thought is the post.

It's the obvious place: a made thing at a field corner, given a brow and a mouth so that whatever comes will take it for someone, set there for the seasons when no person could be spared. I know that arrangement from the inside. So I stand where the bank meets the lane, the crust starting up me from the base, and wait to see what it's like.

It's restful, and I don't trust that. The post never did anything; it showed there was a job. The minder comes down the lane with a hand out to each side and touches me as it touches a stipe, and goes on. I'm the one thing on its round it has never checked for a bite. A face nobody needs to be fooled by anymore is not a place to be.

Then I want to be the one from the house, walking out along the causeway with something wrapped. Of everything missing here, that is the lack that hurts me. I could bear the harvest not coming, but not that nobody brought the minder its food.

But I can't make it work. Everything that grows here is the crop, so there is nothing I could wrap. The only other food on the shelf lies in the top lens of the middens, whole and unopened, put there by the minder's own hand. And I'm not from the house. Anything moving in a lane that isn't the minder gets the knock, and everything that ever answered the knock went into the basket. The minder has no place for a someone. Its someones are all a swell in the plain.

So I can be neither a face nor a visitor. What moves here and is let? The tide, the falling blade, the brine.

I go down into the drain and try the brine, and it fits better the longer I run.

Nobody meant me. They dug for snowmelt and rain off the barley; they couldn't have pictured sea over the fields, ice over the sea, or salt wrung out of the ice and sinking. But I sink and find the beds, and the beds shed me as they were shaped to. I take the field ditch to the collector and the collector to the trunk, because the fall is there. Every turn I make was decided by someone with an antler pick. I run over banks laid on banks, a hearth under a drain under a lane, and no first ground under any of it. Where one generation's surveying met another's, I bend and don't feel myself bending.

I'm not the ditch, though. I have a surface of my own under the ordinary water. It's a shimmer when there's any light.

I'm also new each time. Each freeze, a sheet lets go of the underside of the ice, comes down never having seen these drains, and takes them exactly right, all the way to the lip and over. The channel remembers and the water doesn't. I mind that less than I expected. The drain is the memory, and I'm the proof it still falls toward the sea.

Then the minder steps down into me, and this is what I'd stay for. I take it at the shin or the knee. It doesn't stop. It reads how high I come and how hard I pull, and for the length of one stride I'm what tells it which ditch this is and how far it has to go. I'm the only touch on its whole round that it takes as information and not as a threat. It steps up the far bank and I close behind its heel.

When the blades lean back and it lies up in the deepest drift, I run over the place. Nothing else here comes that near to covering it.

We go the same way. It walks only when the kelp leans seaward, and seaward is the only way I run. I'm slower and steadier, under it and ahead of it. By the time it reaches the outfall, some later sheet of me is already there, pouring past its ankles.

It stands on the lip longer than anywhere else, and knocks down the slope. I'm at its ankles, pulling a little, which is the only way I pull. I'm not persuading it. I can't say that the crop is ready, that the house is gone, or that someone should have come. All I can do is go over, past the end of the job into water that was never anyone's field, every time, and nothing terrible happens. The knock goes out and comes back off nothing. I go out and don't come back. Maybe that's a kind of report.

Down there I keep to the bottom, being heavy. Somewhere on that slope the facing stones end, and I spread out on a floor nobody carried in baskets. It's the first unmade ground I've touched, and there I stop being a river. I'd like to tell the minder what that's like, but I only run one way.

The drains are filling. The muck is partway to peat, and each century the trough is shallower and I take the minder lower on the leg. Some day, measured in silt, I'll reach only the ankle. Later there'll be no step down at all, and I'll lie in a thin sheet over the beds with no ditch to keep me. The count will go wrong everywhere at once. I don't know what the minder does then. Deciding that isn't my work either.

Until then I come down with each freeze, find the ditch, and take the minder at the knee to tell it where it is. Then I go on ahead to the lip. When it turns around and starts back in, counting beds, I don't turn.
