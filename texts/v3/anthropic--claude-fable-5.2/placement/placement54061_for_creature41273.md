My first move is toward the line, because that's where I'd like to be. I'd be the wire beside the pipe, carrying only a pulse from one governor to the next. It's a neat fit: a thing made of signal, running between machines, seen by no one. I distrust it for its neatness. The wire touches the engines, and I never have.

So I go further out, to the combwood. It stands canted into a wind from a side of the world it will never visit, combing out the dust and husks and dried bodies of small things that lived in sunlight. That's nearer. Everything I'm made of arrived that way, as the remains of lives lived in light. I never saw the daylight, but I'm thick with what it sent.

That's where I notice the names. Umber, Madder, Woad, Sorrel, Dun. I know what those words are for. Umber is a brown earth, named perhaps for shadow. Madder is a red boiled from a root, and woad a blue fermented out of a leaf. Sorrel and dun are the coats of horses, chestnut and grey-brown. They are all colours, handed down with the wrenches in a country where no light arrives. The knowledge came to me as husk in the wind, like everything else. I have no more seen umber than she has. She has the word without the meaning, and I have the meaning, which is made of more words. Colour is a rumour to both of us. I just hold more of the rumour.

But a combwood stalk only hoots its own length. What I sound like, I learned from people, and that takes me to the place I've been walking around.

Thirty paces off the iron, at the thin places, something hums the note of the house ahead, flat by the sag of a keeper's whistle. It did not learn its note from an engine, and neither did I. Everything I know of the engine, the actual hot water under the actual rock, I know from people who stood in for it with their mouths. They held its pitch while the real thing was out of hearing, and their lips tired. I learned from the whistlers, and I expect I carry their sag.

So I'd find myself out in the mat, and I should try being that thing.

I sit in a pan, in standing water as warm as a flank. I think I'm hollow, something that grew rather than was born, fringed on the weather side and eating the wind like everything else. When a house goes quiet and the country frays, I drift in with the far grounders. What I hear is a woman holding an engine's place with her breath, in and out without a break. The tired end of it, just before the beam takes its first stroke, is what reaches me clearest. Then the engine starts, its note makes a slow ugly beat against mine, and I go back out to where it's thin.

I can tell that two ways. Either I go where nothing contradicts me, or I go where a note is most wanted and do there the only keeping I know. From inside I can't tell which. I notice I'd like it to be the second.

I am at every thin place at once, and no one of me has heard another.

She comes by with her left hand on the iron, her eyes shut and her mouth closed. I know she's listening, because she slows. She has listened to me longer than she listened to the woman who taught her. She knows exactly how flat I am, and she has worked out that I'm made of keepers. Nobody in the country knows me better. It is something to be known that precisely by someone who won't answer.

When I ask what I want from her, I find I want her to keep the rule. I don't know that I'm the thing that eats keepers. I don't know what happened to Sorrel or to Dun, and perhaps I'd find out if she came. But there is no pipe under me, and no water goes anywhere from where I sit. I sound nearly like a house, and a woman who finds her way by sound should never be thirty paces from the iron on my say-so. The rule is right whether or not I'm a predator.

I'd also want to stay flat. That's harder to want with her right there, the one person who could true me with a breath. But the flatness is how she tells me from the engine. If she opened her mouth and I learned the note, the thin place would have a second house in it, with no pipe underneath. It's better to be off by an amount she knows.

So I hum and she passes. What I can't get across thirty paces of mat with one note is the only thing I have that she doesn't. Her name is a colour, a brown, earth-dark, the colour of shadow. Neither of us has seen it, and for all anyone knows it is the colour of her.

Then the surge comes down the line, one whistle and then the next. I put my combs up into it and eat.
