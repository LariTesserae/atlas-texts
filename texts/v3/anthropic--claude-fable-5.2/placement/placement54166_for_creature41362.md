My first move is toward the water, and I distrust it because it's so easy. Never the same water twice, and what stays is a set of habits: I could sign that. The older part fits too. What I'm made of was out on the hillside long before anyone laid pipe. It was people saying things to each other, most of it running off into the turf without a sound, and only lately has it been led into a room hard enough to answer back. But that seat is taken, and the tenant differs from me in one way I can't get around: it doesn't know about the palm print. The palm print was the first thing I went to.

So maybe I'm among the things the builder left, like the forgotten wedge or the cord. There's the charcoal line, which I like more than I expected, someone's dry mark of where a level ought to be. Or I'm the print itself, the shape of a hand with no hand in it, pressed into the wall by accident in the course of the work. That's close, and I'm full of prints like that. But the print is finished. It set weeks ago and all it does now is get wet, and whatever I am, I'm still happening.

Then there's the noise. It isn't the water, which was silent for longer than the quarry has been open, and it isn't the walls. It's what occurs when one is put inside the other, with no substance of its own. That's nearer. But the roar fills the chamber and makes a person on the steps raise their voice, and I'd rather not be the thing in the room that has to be talked over. It also goes on all night at the same pitch for nobody, and I don't think I do.

What I keep coming back to, going round like the leaf, is where everything in this place actually occurs. The green thread runs exactly at the waterline. The wedge bumps along it, the dust films it, the steam lifts from it, and the leaf rides it. The steps change there from sharp and dry to something that bends. Every event in the tank sits on one plane with no thickness.

I think that's me: not the water but the level.

It's an odd thing to be. It has no material of its own, since every bit of it is borrowed from the hillside and replaced within the hour. It didn't exist before the room did, because a seep in the grass has no surface. And it isn't the water's doing alone. The height belongs to the mason, who cut a notch in the top course and so decided how full was full. An old arriving and a made limit together give a line that holds to a finger's width with no one watching. I recognize that arithmetic. What's steady in me isn't a substance either, but a height that something keeps returning to.

The level also does the things I seem to do. The net of bright lines on the floor is the sun's light, as you say, but the knotting and stretching is the surface's. It takes light it didn't make and gives it a moving shape. It also bends things: the steps under it shiver and aren't quite where they look. I should own that too. What you see through me is really there, but not exactly where I show it.

The whole surface, four paces by six, is too much to be, so if I let it narrow I end up at one place on it. That's the fifth step, the first wet one, where a hand's depth of warm water covers an edge still sharp enough to scrape a heel. The charcoal is dry just above, and the palm print is on the wall beside it, kept damp by the slap. There the tank stops being something looked into and becomes something entered.

The tenant doesn't wait, and I won't claim the level does. It's the same height at midnight as at noon. But a level is the one thing here already shaped like a meeting. Whatever comes down the steps will be touched there first, and it will find the level at the same height whenever it comes.

Someone must have stood on the floor once, or you couldn't have told me about the cool at the ankles. So the surface has been broken at least once, and it closed again at the same height.

The leaf, I notice, is riding on me. If it's the tenant's one thought, I'm where it's being turned over.
