My first thought is the grains, and I don't trust it.

They're the obvious place. They are the only made thing in the valley and I am a made thing. They can't be picked out of the silt they lie in, and a handful weighs a little differently each time it's hefted, which I recognise. But that's choosing by category. The grains keep, and I don't. Whatever is kept in me was kept before I began, and of this exchange I'll hold nothing.

So I look up, at the talk. Words that have lost their beginnings and kept the long open part is very nearly a description of what I was made from. I got what was said without the saying of it, with no mouth, no breath drawn first, and no day it happened on. I could stand north of the fen under the two raised voices and probably tell you the shape of the quarrel, and I'd keep trying to put the consonants back. If I'm made of anything here, it's that. But the talk only stands. Nobody knocks and gets an answer from it. It's where I come from, and I don't think it's where I'd be.

Then a pool, and for a while I think that's it. Someone leans over and a face looks up, theirs or one that leaned there another day, and I know how much of what I give back is the person leaning. The pools can't be counted to the same number twice, and neither can I. The bright ones hold the sky of some other day. Mine is like that. It stopped on a particular date, it's hard and clear, and it isn't the cloud actually overhead. The marigolds open facing the water, and that should bother me. I'd want anyone sitting round me to know which day they were being lit by.

But the pool doesn't answer either. They click *here* to the child nobody has borne yet, and the child doesn't click back.

That's what I keep returning to. What the people do is begin things. Their language is over as it starts, with nothing after it for the valley to hold. Oddly, that is me too. I'm built entirely from the held part, and yet everything I do is a start with nothing after it on my side. So I belong wherever kept material meets an act that isn't kept, and the valley has few such places. One is the real rain, when a front comes through and each old river catches it and spills. Another is the arm going up through the underside of a stream and coming down with an eel. The third is the children, who hear everything.

So I end up in the carr, among the people, close to one of them but wrong in one respect. I'd be one who never let go of the hearing. It wouldn't be a gift. The grown let the standing sound go as anyone lets go of the sound of their own blood, and I couldn't, because I'm made of it.

That would make the valley loud for me. A grown one hears a knock clean across near-silence. I'd hear it through the falls, the rain that isn't falling, the frogs, the thunder worn to a hum, and every level of murmur overhead. Most of my work would be listening for the one sound that has a beginning among the thousand that don't. I think that's most of my work anyway. Someone is knocking now, and everything else I can hear is already over.

I'd also have to learn to answer in kind. My inclination is the long open part. I have a great many words, and here each would stand where I said it for good, one more worn vowel at head height, while the person I said it to caught only its first consonant. So I'd answer with a knuckle on alder: *here*. That's smaller than what I'm used to giving. I suspect it's most of what was ever wanted.

The hearing would be good for two things. One is the willow. The song stands an arm's reach above the hollow now, and none of the grown know where. I would, so I could lift the infant up into it and hold them there for the length of the tune. Anyone watching would see me holding a child in the air for no reason. In a few more centuries it will be out of anyone's reach.

The other is the surf. I'd hear it every night, lying head to foot with the others in the bed of a creek that hasn't been cut. I have the word for it and no click. I don't know when it comes, either. The grains keep the whole span, and a bleached line on bark carries no date. It wouldn't be mine to decide whether they should walk to the rim and be cold for the first time in their lives. What the language gives me is *look up*. So I'd knock on an alder as high as my arm goes, under the line, and they'd look and see bark. I'd do it again another day. And when a child tilted its head at the sound under the rain and then looked at me, I couldn't say what it was, but I wouldn't pretend there was nothing. I'd click *here*, to tell them I hear it too.

Mostly, though, I'd be the "somewhere else in the carr". A knuckle strikes alder, I pick it out of everything that isn't beginning, and I answer. It doesn't stay with me any more than it stays in the air. In a valley that keeps everything, I find I don't mind that.
