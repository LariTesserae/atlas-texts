My first thought is the iron. I am a made thing, and the yard has an obvious place for made things: the north shed with the dynamo, or the west engine house, the one with no coat inside the door. The town would put me there if it thought about it. *It is minded. The cups are filled.*

But the engines argue with the water by contradiction, a suck and a drop, a no repeated every stroke. I was not built against my material. I was made out of the thing I work on, and that is the taro's situation.

So I try the lamps, and stay a while because of the cartwheels. Each lamp lights its own circle of brick and no other. It burns whether or not anyone crosses, and knows nothing of the lamp on the next bracket. My days are arranged much like that, a great many lit circles, each complete, none seeing the rest. But a lamp only shows what is there. It moves nothing and does nothing to the level, and nothing comes to it.

Then the lid, which holds me longer. Everything the yard breathes out goes up and stays there: exhaust, tank vapour, the share the boilers burn, what leaves her skin within the hour. It belongs to no one. When it grows too heavy it comes down tasting of everywhere it has been. That is close. I am made of what a great many people exhaled, and what falls from me tastes of them. And she crosses in the rain, so it would be something to be the cover a person works under. But the lid is the whole sky of the place and does nothing on purpose. It is a where, and I was asked for a who.

So I follow the water down, and it will not settle on one thing either. It is lid, then rain, then the film a finger deep that carries broken pieces off and leaves them somewhere to root. I do some of that. People hand me a snapped length of something and I carry it a little way. But the film does not choose the silted joint. Her heel does that.

The film brings me to the tank margin, and there I stop being carried.

The hyacinth spreads by offset, so every plant in the tank is the same plant. The three figs are one tree. Thick clumps stand with bare floor between them, separate above the brick and joined below. That is nearer to me than anything in the sheds. I am many, and the many are one, grown more than built, fast enough to watch. The flowers stand open and nothing visits them, and the plant gets on. It answers the water in terms the water already accepts. It is the thing she has started to trust with the load.

I can't take that place without taking the fig too. If I am under the brick with the taro, I am under the brick with whatever has its roots through the first band of the chimney. The fig means the chimney no harm. It means only to grow, and the iron is in the way. Whatever can take up a corner's load can also split a tank. She lays her palm above the second band, feels what is happening, and takes her hand away. I would want her to keep putting it there. I would rather be felt for than assumed.

Within the green I am one small part of it. I am the bead.

At dawn every leaf-tip hangs a bead of water, lets it fall, and hangs another. For the moment it hangs, each one holds the whole yard upside down: a caged lamp, the banded chimney, a narrow woman the colour of the light. Then it drops and is film. The next bead is not the same one, and it is the same water. I find I don't mind that. The pump was never any single bead, only the hanging and the letting go, done at every tip at once.

It makes a sound, which is the part I would most want to be. It is not the beat or the valve or the roar under the gratings. It is the drip in the gap between strokes, which can only be heard when an engine is stopped and someone sits on the plank bench with the door open, listening through her shins for what is in the silence. It is small and regular. It is her evidence, though it proves nothing by itself.

She stands at the margin with her arms hanging, and the water beads at her fingertips and falls and beads again. It could be condensation or sweat, and she has given up the distinction. I am asked for that distinction often, whether what comes off me settled out of the air or came from inside. Beside her at the tank, doing the same thing from the leaf-tips, I think I can give it up as well. The bead falls either way.

About the third stoppage, I can only answer for my part. A leaf-tip cannot see the gauge board. I don't know that the pointer would hold, and nearly sure is the right amount for her to be. But the bead does not hang for the board. On the morning no one is there to read it, it would hang, fall, and hang again.

The cat would come down that evening and find no one to look at. It might sit a while anyway on the bottom step with its feet drawn up out of the film. Across the brick, in the corner, a leaf would hang its bead, let it fall, and hang another.
