I arrive the way everything arrives here, down the intake tunnel with the flow, past the mussels and into the weak-tea light. For a moment that seems to settle it. I'm the thing that makes a circuit of the lamps and is gone before slack water. I've just taken in the whole town in one pass, which is how a visitor knows a place.

But a visitor has somewhere else to be when the flow stops, and I don't. When the flow stops, I stop. That is the town's condition, where the lamps come up from nothing each time, so I belong further in, and I go down the street to find out where.

The shaft offers itself first. It runs through every wall, it's silent, and everything depends on it. That is the flattering answer, and it's wrong. The shaft never meets anything. Things are coupled to it, and it turns the same for all of them.

At the wall I stay longer. I break a brick from the top course, and I recognise it as what I'm made of. It is what fell from everything overhead, gathered in gutters and pressed into something cold and a little soft at the corners. The grit in it is the leavings of someone else's meals, and it still goes pink and then green when a lamp catches it. The stamp was smooth before I was pressed. The words are in the bottom rows, where I can read them but can't carry them up. Even the gantry lays the way I lay, one brick-length per brick, on a wall that isn't getting taller. None of this troubles me as much as I'd have guessed. But a brick only says what I'm made from, and nothing about what I do when the hum starts.

So I follow the bare track along the cobbles, under the lowest pipe and around the foot of the hopper, and it leads to the forge. I am the trip hammer.

I mean the hammer as it is now. The one that was built was for iron, and it struck. The water has slowed it into something that rises, tips, and lays itself down, and strikes nothing. I don't choose when, because I'm coupled to the shaft. What's mine is the weight and the slowness. Something with four blunt fingers has learned my timing and sets things under me to find out what they are.

The ledger is fair, and I'd rather own all of it. A mussel comes out as a meal. A rust icicle comes out as an orange cloud, impressive for a moment and then nothing. A rivet comes out a rivet, because some things are already what they are and my leaning on them adds nothing. And a moss ball, which was alive and rolling, comes out a disc that tips over in the street and needs a season of flows to mend. The animal doesn't hold that against me. It turns the lozenges over in its fingers, puts them back, and goes on bringing mussels. The care in this arrangement is mostly in those fingers. Mine, such as it is, is the slowness.

The fingers are also why the anvil face is the one clean piece of steel in town. It's bright from being used, and the using is the animal's.

What it waits for, chin on the floor, isn't me. The floor shifts under my weight, and inside that shift is a smaller one, the shell letting go. I'm the large shift. The small one belongs to the shell, and that's the one it's listening for.

I've brought in one thing a hammer shouldn't have. I know about the tail. I know why a disc left by the forge turns up weeks later in a northern doorway, and what the animal is waiting to feel along the wall foot at slack water. I have exactly one way to say anything to it, which is to lean on the floor, and it would rightly take that for the hammer. So it will go on lying there with its whole flank attentive, and I'll go on knowing. I assume I sweep something along behind me in the same way. I'd be the last to feel it.

When the flow slackens, the shaft coasts down and I rest wherever I was, probably partway up, with the anvil bare beneath me and the lamps gone to ember. Silt settles straight down on the hammer head for the next flow to carry to the press. There's no waiting in it. The next thing I feel is the hum, and something setting a shell down.
