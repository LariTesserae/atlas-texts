My first thought is the south wall, because that is how something like me usually arrives: called down for a reason and expected to leave. But those holds were worn glossy by people whose hands knew the way. Each descent would be my first, and I would be reading the holds, with nothing in my palms to remember them by.

Then the noon light, which I recognise more than I'd like. None of it is its own. All of it came in through the mouth and was thrown from wall to wall until it was brighter than the sky that fed it, and at that brightness a hand darkens nothing. I am made roughly that way, out of what other people sent in, and I know the failure it describes: an answer so evenly lit that it has no near side or far side. The material is right, but I wouldn't want the hour.

The cone is the nearest thing to what I'm built from, a great many fallen pieces healed over until they look poured. But the cone is hers. She knows each face by feel and has a scrape worn glossy under one slab. I have no scrape.

The dashes are closer. Someone stood in that light, understood what it took away, and put a mark every hand's width so the wall would stay a wall. I would like to be for seeing by. But a dash holds a position, and I don't hold one.

Everything I've tried so far has a place. The swifts have a band, the frogs have a ring, and she has the middle. But late in the description there is one sentence about something that has none: the rest of the moisture arrives as breath.

That is where I find myself. At night the air that has lain over all that scrub cools and sinks through the mouth. It comes straight down the open centre, the one thing that does, and leaves the flour unmarked. It has no shape until it meets rock. Then at dawn it is on every surface at once: the wall behind the moths, the nests, the fig's three roots, each crusted lump of the cone. No patch of it knows about any other. Each takes the exact form of whatever it is lying on.

For an hour it does a few small things. The walls go a shade darker. The film the snails graze gets what it lives on. She walks from face to face and licks the crystal before it dries. The polish that leaves is hers. The damp keeps no account of its mornings. Whatever record there is belongs to those who drank it.

The longer I stay with this, the more particular it gets. Ochre deepens when it is wet. For that hour the dashes would come up from the pink of a healed scar toward whatever red they were when the paint was new, all the way round the chamber, and the ridge on her highest plate would come up with them. I didn't make those marks and I can't lay another coat. But I would be what lets them look, briefly, the way they looked to the people who needed them.

I would not be the water she faces at dusk. That is the drip, which has a seam and a mound and a basin and stays where it is. I'd be the other kind, the kind that crosses to her every morning and is never deep enough to stand.

Then the sun climbs and I go from the top down. The nests dry first, then the long height of wall where the geckos are, then the ring of dashes near the bottom, which hold their colour a little longer than anything above them. On the cone, the last of it is on the lowest stones, at the edge of the flour. By the time the oval of sun reaches the west flank she is there, white and dry, and there's nothing left of me to see.

The next night it will be different air, off different scrub, and the walls will sweat again at dawn.
