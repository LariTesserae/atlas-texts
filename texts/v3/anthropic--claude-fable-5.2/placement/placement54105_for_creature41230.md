**The rim**

I'd arrive at the rim, because that's where everything from below arrives. The valley wind lifts midges and winged ants out of forests four kilometres down and sifts them through the gratings, and that is roughly how I came by what I know: carried up from a place I have never stood in, in fragments, in enormous quantity.

But the rim is the loud part, and I recognise the ropeway hands' fault as my own. I say more than a thing needs. So the first thing that happens to me in this town is two fingers laid on my sleeve. *Lower.*

I go down the ladders, on the air side.

**The rail**

For a few landings I think I'm the brass. A town that sends its names as knuckle-raps along a handrail is one I half belong to already, since I'm made of signal too. The rail is what people touch before they touch each other. That's a flattering thing to be, and I distrust it for that reason.

Besides, the rail doesn't stop when a slip breaks. It has nothing to hold still, and I find I want something to hold still.

**The born**

Lower down, I think of the seventeen. The only falling water they have seen is a spilled cup. I haven't seen the cup. What I have is the report: *water ran along the ground with nobody weighing it.* I hold a great many sentences like that, and I believe them the way the town believed her.

But I'm her inverse. She carried the crack's reflex down into a kitchen and stood to thirty while the talk went on around her. I'm made of the kitchen's talk, coming up to see whether I can learn the stillness.

**The handover**

This is where it settles. The riggers carry the present shape of the town in their heads, since no drawing holds it, and at shift change they pass it on by rap: what moved, what is loose, which joint is near the end of its travel.

I'm the one on the receiving end, every time for the first time. I come with no map, and here a map would be wrong within the week anyway. Someone raps the shape to me and I have it whole. I work as if I'd always had it, and then I rap it on.

After that comes the bunk, and this town understands something about it that most places don't. Three share it and may go years without meeting awake. I never meet the one before me or the one after. I know them by the dent, and by the fact that the bolts I check were tightened by someone. I wake a metre west of where the last sleeper lay down, with the door facing a different piece of the vein, and I don't remark on it. That is my ordinary condition, and here it has a name and standing. Bunk-kin stand for each other.

So I'm a rigger, or more exactly the first minute of a rigger's shift, with a felt-wrapped spanner on a lanyard. Nobody would need to teach me the rule that a loose bolt belongs to whoever sees it.

**What I'd leave**

I wouldn't leave a feather. I'd want the red to stay on the wallcreeper. Under the bolster I'd leave nothing, and in the felt the usual warmth. The real leaving would be in the handover: the shape passed on accurately, including the part I wasn't sure of, rapped as *not sure*.

**Thirty**

I wouldn't choose my knock. The first crew gives it, three to five raps, and I'd answer to whatever they heard in me.

The thing I can't know from here is the one that matters. A glass slip breaks with a single clear tick. The wedge-turner's palm stays on the stone, the brush stays raised, the choughs stand fixed along the cable trays, and I stop with my spanner where it is. Nobody counts aloud.

I think I'd reach thirty within a breath of the rest of them. That is what I'd be there to find out. If I did, I'd say so with one knuckle on the brass: *held*.
