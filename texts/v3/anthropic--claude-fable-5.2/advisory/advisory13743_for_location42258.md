# Preparing for the basin

Everything follows from one fact: this is two lethal environments separated by a single step, and each is the cure for the other. Outside you have about 45% of sea-level pressure, deep cold, wind, and hard ultraviolet. Inside you have full sea-level air at over forty degrees and saturated. Sweat cannot evaporate there, so your body has no way to shed heat. The pikas, which dart into the jungle to feed and back into the cold to recover, have already worked out how to live here. You will do the same thing in reverse.

## The heat clock

The first ten minutes inside will feel like the best of your life, especially after time on the ridge: rich air, warmth, the smell of peat and green things after days of scentless cold. That feeling is the trap. Your core temperature starts rising the moment you cross.

- **Time limits.** As a rough estimate, allow 20–30 minutes if you are walking with any load, and perhaps an hour sitting still. Use a timer, because heat confusion feels like contentment and you won't notice it setting in.
- **What doesn't cool you.** The pools are bath temperature, the rain is as warm as the air, and night takes off only a degree or two.
- **What does.** There are three sources of cold: the outside, the two alpine circles around the dead posts, and snow you carry in. Fill a bag with clean snow from well beyond the margin of dead insects. Put it on your neck and in your hat, and eat it.
- **Clothing.** Stripping doesn't help, because the air is hotter than your skin. Thin, loose long sleeves cost nothing thermally. They also handle the midges and the sun, which is still near high-altitude strength when the fog thins. That saves you washing sunscreen and repellent into water that never leaves.
- **Salt.** You will pour sweat to no purpose, so drink and replace salt.

## Crossing the line

The pressure more than doubles in two paces, and the temperature jumps by sixty or seventy degrees.

**Going in:**
- Pressurize your ears just before you step (pinch your nose and blow gently), and keep swallowing as you cross.
- If you get sharp ear pain, step back out.
- Don't come with a head cold.
- Expect your glasses and lenses to fog solid for several minutes.

**Going out:**
- Exhale as you cross, by humming or talking. The air in your lungs wants to more than double in volume, and the reflex when cold hits a wet face is to gasp and hold. That reflex is the most dangerous thing you can do here.
- Do not step out wet in jungle clothes. Towel off inside, put on a dry set of layers kept in a waterproof bag just inside the line, accept a minute of misery, and then cross.
- Wet hair, wet boots, and wet metal freeze in seconds.

**Either way:**
- Cross briskly with your whole body. I would not linger half-through. A chest on one side and a head on the other is a pressure difference your lungs aren't built for.
- Near the margin the ceiling curves down, and the vegetation height shows you where it is. Where the plants are knee-high, assume the boundary is close overhead.
- Crack the lid of every sealed container before crossing. Flasks sealed outside lock shut inside, and bottles sealed inside spray or burst outside. Sleeping pads, food pouches, and tubes do the same.
- The snow against the line is probably glazed and undercut, and one step later you are on algae-slick polished rock. Take your crampons off at the last possible moment. This is the likeliest place to fall.

Your short stays have one benefit. You never spend long enough at full pressure for decompression sickness to become a real worry.

## Camp and sleep

You cannot sleep inside, because you would not wake up. Sleep outside in a full high-altitude camp. The old drystone windbreak is the obvious site, since someone chose it for a reason.

- **If you are not acclimatized,** bring bottled oxygen for sleeping or keep the whole visit to a single day. A night at 6,500 meters will otherwise make you ill.
- **Don't camp in the alpine circles.** The warm rock floor is tempting, but they are tiny, they are full of gentians and pikas, and frost forms and melts on everything. Use them as cooling stops and stand on bare slab.
- **Secure your food.** The fox walks the whole perimeter every morning and the choughs work it all day.

## Kit

- **Two wardrobes.** Bring full cold-weather gear (insulated suit, double boots, mitts, goggles) and a jungle set (thin synthetics, and soft sticky-rubber shoes that stay inside).
- **Dry bags.** Bring several, plus one set of dry base layers reserved for exits.
- **Feet.** Bring a rubber-tipped pole. Don't use spikes, which skate on warm gneiss and break the sinter.
- **Ears.** Bring earplugs. The cicada note is shrill and unbroken from dawn until the rain.
- **Records.** Bring waterproof paper and a pencil. Cold electronics carried into saturated air fill with condensation, and the pitted mast with a frog in its logger housing shows how instruments fare.
- **Medical.** Bring a thermometer, electrolytes, and dressings. Any small cut will fester in that wet heat.
- **Water and waste.** Bring a stove for melting snow, which is cleaner than anything in the basin and useful for being cold. Bring bags to carry out all your waste.
- **Rope.** Bring a light one, for signaling as well as safety.

## Communication

Sound does not cross the boundary. A partner outside cannot hear you scream, and I would not count on radio either. Light does cross, so agree on hand signals, a mirror, or rope tugs, and set a fixed return time.

Inside, the chorus is too loud for easy speech. Every sound also comes back from the ceiling about a second later and slightly lower, which is disorienting for the first hour. The hum at the line is felt in the jaw. It may make you uneasy for no reason you can name.

## Moving inside

- **Timing.** The fog thickens all morning and the rain falls in early afternoon. The clearest windows are just after dawn and after the rain.
- **Landmarks.** Learn them before you need them. The stream runs to the tarn at the low point, the mast stands near the second terrace, and the posts run west to east. Know where both cold circles are from anywhere you plan to stand.
- **Footing.** Everything is wet and polished. Walk on bare slab, and stay off the sinter rims, which took ten thousand years to form. Stay out of the peat hollows, which may have no bottom worth finding, and off the deep moss, which hides holes.
- **The notch.** Stay well back from the lip. It is wet, smooth rock tilting toward a two-hundred-meter drop.
- **The ice column.** Never approach by it or beneath it. It breaks under its own weight every eleven or twelve years, and you will not know where in that cycle you are.

## The posts

They were not made for you, and they do not seem to be about the basin at all. They cross it on the way to somewhere else.

- **Keep your distance.** Nothing living touches them, and the moss stops in a clean collar at arm's length. Take the hint and stay outside the collar. Whatever turns raindrops aside in the last hand's width has not been tested on hands.
- **Look briefly.** If your eyes won't settle on the surface, stop trying.
- **The leaning post.** Give the cracked post by the tarn extra room and don't linger nearby. If it fails, a circle of jungle around it becomes thin, freezing air in an instant. The odds on any given day are tiny, but there is no reason to take them.
- **The dust.** Don't breathe it or collect it. Don't wade in the tarn where it settles, and don't drink downstream of it. It lies in ripples that ignore the current, so something other than water is arranging it.

## The living things

This is three square kilometers of sealed world, and it is shrinking by a stride per century.

- **Arrive clean.** Scrub and disinfect your boots and gear before you come, and carry no seeds or soil. Put no soap in the water.
- **Leave the animals alone.** Don't handle the frogs. If the geese are there, standing in the shallows with their wings open, give them the basin. They are barely coping, and they have a range to cross.
- **Leave no trace.** Add nothing to the cairn or to the scatter of relics, and take nothing out. That includes the silver fern trunks in the snow, which are the only record of how large this place once was.

## A day

1. **Dawn.** Watch the fox make its round, then cross.
2. **Morning.** Make two or three short bouts, cooling between them in an alpine circle with the pikas.
3. **Afternoon.** Watch the rain from the edge of a circle or from outside.
4. **Dusk.** Make one more entry to stand back from a living post. The midges rise in a slow column in the still air, ringed with spider silk.
5. **Evening.** Dress fully and go to the cairn. If it is clear, look east along the line of the posts for the two green smudges on the far massif.

## If something goes wrong

The remedy is always a few steps away.

- **Altitude sickness or deep cold:** carry the person in.
- **Heat collapse:** carry them out, or into a cold circle.

Both sides cloud judgment in ways that feel fine from the inside, so never go alone. The system only works if someone is still thinking clearly and watching the clock.
