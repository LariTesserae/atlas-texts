# Preparing for the hall on the tower

The place is gentle, and most of the preparation is about three things: being wet for hours, the open end, and remembering that you are standing inside both a working machine and an unfinished building. The plants are the condenser, and they are on the drawings with tolerances. Treat them as you would someone's instruments.

## Clothing

Dress for two climates a few steps apart.

- **In the glazed bays**, the air is bath-warm and saturated, and it rains four or five times a day. Don't try to stay dry. A waterproof shell will only trap sweat that can't evaporate. Wear thin clothing that is comfortable wet, such as light wool or quick-drying synthetics, and a brimmed hat, since drips fall between rains too.
- **At the open bays**, the air beyond the fog seam is mountain-cold, and you will be wet when you reach it. Carry a warm layer and a windproof.
- **For the descent**, keep a complete dry change, including socks, sealed in a waterproof bag, and don't open the bag in the hall. The most likely way to get hurt is not falling. It is walking out soaked into cold air with four hundred feet of tower still to descend.

## Footwear and the floor

The floor will be slippery. The concrete is new, and water lies on it in thin films and pools where the drawings say it shouldn't. Green film grows on everything within weeks. Past the poured deck is bare steel plate, which is the most treacherous surface when wet.

Wear soft rubber soles with good grip, in shoes that drain and can stay wet all day. Avoid hard or studded soles, which skate on steel and scar a floor that has no marks on it yet. Walk flat-footed and slowly.

## What to carry

- **Your own drinking water, and more than seems necessary.** In saturated heat you sweat without cooling and don't notice how much you've lost. Don't drink what drips or pools: it has run over red-lead primer, curing lime, boiler ash and clinker.
- **A pencil and waterproof paper.** Ink runs and ordinary paper goes limp by the first rain.
- **A sealed bag for anything that minds water.** Lenses and watch crystals fog for a quarter hour whenever they come from the cold into the warm, so let them warm up inside the bag. You don't need a watch anyway: the glass tells the time (clear, beaded, running, raining), and the relief valve on the header sighs every eleven minutes.
- **A small light, if you stay past dusk.** The caged lamps cover the glazed bays only.
- **Clean gear.** Brush out your boot treads, cuffs and pockets before you come, and bring no seeds, fruit, soil or straw. Growth here already outruns the building. The moth arrived in packing material, and you should not be the next unplanned arrival.

## The rules, and what they imply

**The trays must not be moved.** The drip lines have been surveyed, so also don't nudge a tray with your foot, set a bag on one, or stand where you intercept a drip that is meant to land somewhere. The same applies to the rest of the planting:

- Don't lean on the felt sleeves on the columns. They look upholstered, but they are a living surface.
- Don't tip or poke the bromeliad cups. The frogs live in them.
- Don't pick anything, and leave the fig on the standpipe alone, as everyone else has.

**The rope at the open end is the edge.** It marks the edge and is not a handrail. Don't lean on it, and don't assume it would hold you.

- Stay a body-length back from it.
- Watch the fog seam. It leans in and out with the pressure and can cover the rope while you are looking elsewhere.
- When the afternoon cloud rises and comes in through the open bays, go back to the sixth bay and stay there until it clears. The end of the hall then has no visible edge, and the floor is wet steel.
- Don't go past the seventh bay after dark.

If heights trouble you, know beforehand that on most mornings the view from the open bays is a floor of cloud with the tower standing through it and nothing else.

## Site hazards

- **Hot iron.** The header and the finned radiators carry exhaust steam and will burn you through wet cloth. Give them a wide berth, and don't stand in front of the relief valve.
- **Red lead.** The primer on all the steel is poisonous. Avoid handling the steelwork, keep your hands away from your mouth, and wash before you eat.
- **Chalk.** The bay numbers, arrows, rivet tallies and the footings for the tenth bay are working information. A wet sleeve will erase them, so don't brush against them, and don't add your initials.
- **Glass.** In the seventh bay the panes are still seating in soft putty. Don't stand under the glaziers, and don't sit on the crates. The ferns growing from them make them look abandoned, but they are full of glass.
- **Riveters and the hoist.** Hot rivets may be passed or thrown, and girders come up the hoist. Keep out of the working bays unless someone waves you through.
- **The crane.** It runs about twice a day. Step clear of the centre line and accept the line of drips it drags down the hall. Its passage is the only wind the glazed bays get.

On arrival, find whoever is in charge of the deck or the drip survey and say you're there. You are a guest in a plant room.

## Your body

The air is warm, wet and perfectly still. Some people find that soothing and some find it smothering. The site also seems to be high in the mountains, so the air may be thin.

- Climb slowly, so you don't arrive overheated.
- Rest in the seventh bay, among the begonias and cress, where the air is cooler.
- Leave if you get a headache or stop sweating.
- If your lungs are sensitive to damp, mould or warm mist, keep the visit short.
- Expect a faint lime smell and wrinkled fingers.

## Timing

Arrive early, while the glass is clear and the cloud lies below the deck. Stay through at least one full cycle: beading by mid-morning, the roof raining at noon for twenty minutes, then the vents opening on their chains and the light coming back plain and white.

Then sit still for an hour, somewhere that isn't in a drip line. The hall is quiet enough to hear drips of different sizes, the ribs ticking and a pane creaking as it seats. The engines register only as a tremble in standing water and in your feet. You may see nothing alive that isn't a plant, and that is normal. If you stay to evening, the single metallic note that sounds like pipework is the frog.

## Leaving

Change into your dry clothes at the door, before the cold. Wash your hands. Check that you are carrying nothing out by accident, such as a snail in a cuff or moss in a boot tread. Clean and dry your gear before you travel on, or it will be green within a week and you will be carrying this hall's spores into other worlds. Leather and steel will be the first to show it.

Expect the place to be larger if you return. The footings for the tenth bay are already chalked on the deck.
