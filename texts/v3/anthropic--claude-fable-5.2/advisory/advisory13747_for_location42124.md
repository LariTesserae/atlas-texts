# Preparing for the lake with no top

Nothing here will chase you. The hazards are all slow: heat you can't shed, air that gets wetter by degrees, mineral that settles on anything at rest, and a comfort that makes leaving feel unnecessary. The dangerous act is holding still, and the people think in spans longer than their own lives. Most of what follows comes from those two facts.

## Your body, before you go

- **Acclimatize to altitude.** Spend a week or more somewhere high first. The air is thin to begin with, and warm vapor takes up room in every breath that oxygen would otherwise have. If you have heart or lung trouble, choose another destination.
- **Expect heat you cannot sweat off.** Sitting chest-deep in blood-warm water under saturated air, your sweat does nothing. The only ways to cool down are to do less or to move shallower, up into thinner fog. Plan to do everything at half speed, which is the local pace anyway.
- **Simplify yourself.** Cut your hair short or braid it tight. Take off rings, because fingers swell in warm water and crust will form under the band. Leave contact lenses at home and put glasses on a cord.
- **Prepare your skin for weeks of wet.** Nothing dries here, ever, and fire is too scarce to dry things by. Bring barrier ointment for feet and skin folds, antifungal cream, and drops for swimmer's ear.

## Getting in and out

The rim is new volcanic glass, and a fall on it cuts cleanly and deeply. Wear thick soles and tough gloves. My guess is that the kindest route is up the white terraces to the western notch. They will be warm, wet, and slick, and fresh deposit can be a thin shell over a hollow.

Leaving is the more dangerous direction. You will be soaked, adapted to heat, and stepping into freezing wind. Cache a full set of cold-weather clothing outside the rim in a sealed bag, and change at the lip before you start to feel cold.

## Learning your depth

This is the essential skill. You will not find a surface. As you wade down, the air thickens until each breath carries more water than your lungs can use, and nothing marks the point where that happens.

- **Find your limit on the first day.** Stand on firm shelf with someone local nearby and walk down slowly until breathing becomes work and you start to cough. Note where that is, then back up. Your limit will be shallower than theirs.
- **Learn the depth words.** They say how deep they are standing, and those words are also your safety vocabulary. Never say "on" or "in" the lake.
- **Know where the shelf ends.** You will not bob up, because there is no top to bob to. If you go off the edge, you will settle downward into plain water. Up is where it is brighter and where your bubbles go. Tie yourself to the boat whenever you leave the shallows.
- **Put a lanyard on everything.** A dropped object sinks slowly enough to catch. If you miss, it keeps going to the great-grandparents' floors.
- **Sleep high.** Use a ledge with a lip, in the thinnest air the house offers. Never sleep in the springs, however good they feel. You could slide deeper without waking, and nothing would announce it.

## Water and food

The lake is loaded with dissolved stone. I would not drink it in quantity, since it will be hard on your gut and kidneys, and volcanic water can carry worse things than lime. Filters will clog within days.

Ask what the townspeople drink. My guess is condensation collected high up, where the vapor meets cold air. For a short stay, carry your own water, plus a clean sheet to hang high as a drip collector. Bring electrolytes, because you will sweat constantly without noticing.

Food will be tubers, sour fruit, and perhaps the small pale fish, all cooked in the springs. Expect it to be soft, lukewarm, and slightly gritty. Brush your teeth often.

## Stillness and your gear

A finger's width a year is only a film per day, but the film finds whatever doesn't move: zipper teeth, buckle springs, screw threads, knife hinges, bootlace eyelets.

- **Choose gear without moving parts.** Toggles and cord are better than zippers, and a fixed blade is better than a folder.
- **Work every moving part each morning.** Do it when the mallets start, and rub the grit from your lashes as everyone else does.
- **Never leave anything in the flow.** Keep your tools on your body, as the locals keep their blades. Don't let your boat rest against a wall overnight.
- **Rinse your eyes instead of rubbing hard.** Bring sterile saline, since the grit is stone.

## What to carry

- A small pack. The doorways are kept open by a daily mallet to the size of the household, not to the size of you.
- Hard-soled water shoes, and gloves for the rim.
- Minimal clothing that won't chafe when wet, plus one wrap kept within reach.
- Waterproof wound dressings and antiseptic. Cuts heal badly in constant wet.
- Saline, ear drops, antifungal cream, barrier ointment, and electrolytes.
- A fixed-blade knife and plenty of cord.
- A pencil and waterproof paper. Electronics will fog, crust, and probably die.
- A dim light you can hold low. A bright lamp only lights the fog in front of your face.
- Nothing with velcro. In that air, tearing it open is a shout.

## The vents

The center is still working, and no one goes there. Don't be the exception. The belch every few hours is gas, and volcanic gas can suffocate without warning.

A headache has three possible causes here: altitude, heat, or gas. The first response is the same for all of them. Move shallower and away from the center, rest, and drink. If the townspeople ever move with purpose after a belch, go with them and ask questions later.

## Manners

- **Keep your voice low.** A murmur carries three lanes, so there is no private conversation. Say nothing about your hosts that you wouldn't say to the whole town.
- **Don't touch what is being made.** A basket hanging in the flow is a bowl in its second winter. A reed frame is someone's great-grandchild's bedroom. Don't lean on them, tidy them, or knock crust off them.
- **Do knock crust where it's wanted.** Ask whether you may take the morning mallet at your host's threshold. I suspect this is the most welcome thing a guest can offer.
- **Give way to the stirrers.** The people walking the channels with broad paddles are the reason the lanes are still lanes.
- **Leave the undertown alone.** It is lightless, tangled with weed, and made of other people's ancestors' rooms. The divers come up quiet. Don't ask them why.
- **Take nothing stone.** The petrified reedbeds on the northern shelf are intact to the blade and will not survive your walking through them. If children show you how to scrape a snail's shell thin, set the snail back down afterward.
- **Bring cord or sound wood as gifts.** Everything here begins as lashing, and I saw no trees in the description. Don't expect to be lent fire. Each ember is fed and kept under a stone hood.
- **When the cloud tears, stop.** Put down the paddle and look up with everyone else. This is when you want the wrap, because the cold comes down fast on wet shoulders, and the sun at that height burns skin that has lived in fog.

## Finding your way

There is no sun and there are no shadows. The green, even light flattens everything, so you will misjudge steps and distances for the first few days.

- The ribbon weed leans toward the spill, so it always points west.
- The belch comes from the center.
- At night the town is a scatter of orange smudges to the east.
- The lanes change. Weed grows a hand's length a day, and yesterday's route may be full.

## What it will do to you

You will lose track of time within days. The only clocks are the morning tapping and the vents. The warmth is exactly your own temperature, the light never changes, the voices are soft, and everything drips. It will feel like being held.

That is the real hazard for a traveler. The rule of the place, keep moving or be kept, applies to you too, and the place will give you no reason to leave. Decide the length of your stay before you arrive, tell your host, and keep to it.

Be ready to feel very brief. These people tie rooms for grandchildren they won't meet and hang a mallet by a door no one alive will use. Your whole visit amounts to less than a fingernail's thickness on their walls. If you want to leave something, ask a household whether you may hang a small woven thing in their flow, and accept that someone else will take it down.

When you climb out, the air will feel sharp and dry and the cold will hurt. You will also see your own shadow again, and after weeks without one it will look strange.
