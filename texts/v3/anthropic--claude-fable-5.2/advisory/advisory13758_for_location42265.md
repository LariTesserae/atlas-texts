# Preparing for the new tank

Nothing here wants to hurt you. This is a warm spring bath, weeks old, and the dangers are the ordinary ones of bright light, hot water, deep water, hard edges, noise and fresh lime. The other thing to prepare for is that someone built it very recently and it isn't yours.

## What to bring

- **Dark glasses and a brimmed hat.** At noon the tank is a white box full of sun, and the yard is pale limestone with no shade mentioned.
- **Tinted swim goggles.** The light is stronger underwater than above, and the water is alkaline enough to sting open eyes.
- **Thin-soled water shoes with grip.** The dry steps have chisel-sharp edges, and the wet ones are slick with lime.
- **Earplugs.** The roar is tiring within minutes.
- **Plenty of drinking water.** Don't drink from the tank. You'll be sweating in hot water under an overhead sun without noticing.
- **A jug of plain water for rinsing.** Use it on your skin, hair and eyes afterward. A splash of vinegar in it helps cut the lime.
- **Skin oil or salve.** The slickness between your fingers is the lime working on your skin's own oils. You'll come out dry, tight and faintly chalky.
- **A scarf or cloth** for the yellow dust the afternoon wind brings.
- **A light and a warm layer** if you're staying past dusk or arriving before dawn. The dawn steam means the nights are cool.
- **A timer** that vibrates or flashes, since you won't hear one.
- **A companion,** if at all possible.

Leave behind soap, sunscreen you'd wash off into the water, and contact lenses. If your gear has been in other ponds, rinse it first. Nothing lives in this water yet, and you shouldn't be the one who changes that.

## The edge

The hole has no rail or curb, and nothing marks it but the noise. In daylight it's obvious. At dawn the steam fills it to the brim and spills across the flags, hiding the edge exactly where it matters. At night it's a black rectangle in a dark yard. At those hours, approach slowly, feel with your feet, and find the steps before anything else.

Never jump or dive. The submerged steps appear to "bend and shiver," and the glare and refraction make every depth look wrong.

## Getting in and out

The steps are the only exit. The water sits about four steps below the yard, and the walls are smooth new plaster with nothing to grip. Know where the steps are at all times.

The tank is two body-lengths deep at the spout end. If you can't swim, the steps are your whole visit, which is still a good visit. If you can, remember that a long hot soak makes people faint when they stand up. Sit on the sixth or seventh step for a minute before climbing those sharp edges.

There is a sunk cord on the ninth step. Step over it.

## In the water

- **Heat.** The water is bath-hot and constantly renewed, so it never cools around you. Take fifteen or twenty minutes at a time, then sit on the dry steps. The roar, warmth and light are lulling, and your sense of time will go, which is what the timer is for.
- **The cool layer.** A thin cool sheet lies along the floor. It's a real pleasure, but at the deep end it means a proper dive, so clear your ears on the way down.
- **Seeing.** Nothing casts a shadow, and the bright net crawling over every surface makes distances hard to read. Walls arrive sooner than expected, so swim with a hand out.
- **Sound.** Underwater the rumble sits in your chest. That's normal.
- **The spout.** Under it, the bubble column will push you down an arm's length and the froth holds you up less than solid water. Slide sideways out of it. When the pipe coughs and the stream stutters, nothing is wrong.
- **The water itself.** Keep it out of your mouth and nose. Don't soak open cuts for long.
- **The current.** If you float still, the slow gyre will carry you the way it carries the leaf: out along the left wall, across at the steps, back along the right. I'd plan time for this.

## Noise

You can't be heard from the water, and you won't hear anyone arrive in the yard or anyone slip. Agree on hand signals beforehand. One person should stay on the dry steps as a watcher, and should face the yard now and then.

## The people who aren't there

The plaster is shell-white, a palm print is set in it, a charcoal level line is still on the riser, and a wedge has been left floating. The builders finished days or weeks ago and will be back, or the people they built it for will. You don't know whether this is a bath, a cistern or something sacred, or whether it has been opened yet.

- Dress plainly and keep your things together on one flag.
- Be ready to climb out, look harmless and apologize in gestures.
- Bring a small, useful gift, such as good cord or a sharpening stone. Whoever cut those steps resharpened a soft blade many times.

## What not to touch

- The palm print and the charcoal line.
- The eggshell crust on the spout lip.
- The pipe and its putty joints. Don't hang on them.
- The fern with its three thumbnail fronds, the green thread of algae at the waterline, and the two split grass seeds at the drain.
- The wedge, the cord and the leaf, which should stay where they are.

You will be the first animal this water has met, so leave as little trace as you can.

## When to go

- **Dawn** is the most beautiful time, with steam pouring over the flags, and the most dangerous for footing.
- **Mid-morning** is the best compromise. You can watch the bright wedge come down the west wall, and the water is at its cleanest.
- **Noon** is the spectacle. Go under with tinted goggles for a few minutes, then get out, because sun and hot water together are too much for long.
- **Afternoon** brings the dust film. It's harmless and drifts to the notch on its own.
- **Evening** is gentle. The shadow climbs one riser at a time and the roar seems to grow. Leave before the last step goes dark, or have your light ready.
