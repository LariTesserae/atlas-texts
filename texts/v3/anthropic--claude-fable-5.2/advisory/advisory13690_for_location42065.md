# Preparing for the Engine Grove

## What you are walking into

This is a small, loud, warm, wet place where a machine and an acre of plants have become one closed system. The plants fuel, lubricate, belt and house the engine, and the engine waters, warms and paces the plants. It doesn't need the peat any more, and it doesn't need you. Nothing in the description suggests it is hostile, but everything suggests it is finely balanced. The right attitude is the one you would take in someone's chest cavity: look, be careful where you put your feet, and take nothing out.

The practical hazards are ordinary ones: noise, unguarded machinery, gas, slippery footing over deep water, and getting soaked. The stranger hazards mostly concern what you might be tempted to do.

## Getting there

The approach is flat, exposed farmland below river level. Expect wind, drainage ditches too wide to jump, and cattle.

- Use the field bridges and close the gates.
- If a farmer is about, ask before crossing. Local people know the place and have watched it for generations; they will know the usual way up to the dyke.
- Don't bring a dog. The cattle will dislike it, and the dog will not go past the dyke anyway. No animal will.

You will hear the grove long before you see detail. Put your ear protection in at the foot of the dyke, not inside.

## Kit

**Ears.** This matters most. A single-cylinder engine with an open exhaust, fired over a floor that acts as a drumhead, is loud enough to do damage over a visit of an hour or two. Wear foam plugs everywhere inside the dyke, and add earmuffs near the engine house. Nothing will stop the part that comes up through your knees.

**Feet.** Wear snug waterproof boots with soft, grippy rubber soles. Avoid loose wellingtons, which fill and drag if a leg goes through, and avoid studs or spikes, which would wound the floor you're standing on. A staff with a blunt rubber tip is useful for testing the moss-packed gaps before you trust them.

**Clothing.** Everything drips all the time, and the water is warm. Heavy waterproofs will make you just as wet from the inside, so wear light, quick-drying layers and accept being damp. Also:

- A brimmed hat keeps the drip out of your eyes and off your glasses.
- Nothing should dangle: no scarves, drawcords, loose straps or lanyards. Tie long hair up and under the hat.
- Wear long sleeves and thin gloves. Fig latex irritates skin and can blister it in sunlight afterwards, hop bines rasp, and ivy gives some people a rash. Cover any cuts before you go in, because the water is warm and has been round the loop a great many times.

**Dry bag.** Leave a towel and a full change of clothes outside the dyke, out of reach of the cattle. In winter this is a safety measure. Frost stops at the dyke, so you will walk out of blood-warm air, soaked, into a freezing fen wind.

**Other items**
- Drinking water. Do not drink the grove's water: it has been through the sump, the pump and past the oil cups.
- A headlamp if there's any chance you'll stay towards dusk.
- Waterproof housing for cameras and phones. Expect blurred long exposures, because every leaf flinches on each firing.
- A waterproof notepad and pencil, since you can't converse.
- A small carbon monoxide alarm if you have one.

**Leave behind**
- Matches, lighters and cigarettes.
- Knives and secateurs.
- Oil cans.
- Drones.
- Anything you might forget and leave inside.

## On the mat

There is no ground. You'll be walking on a raft of woven stems three or four metres thick, with warm water in it and peat somewhere underneath. It gives like a mattress.

- Walk on the thick stuff, the trunks and thigh-sized roots, and treat moss as a lid over a hole until your staff says otherwise.
- Move slowly and don't jump. Wet round wood is slippery, and the whole floor twitches every few seconds.
- Stay away from the sump. It is open water with a working pump drawing on it.
- Go with one other person and stay within arm's reach. If a leg goes through to the thigh, you'll want someone to pull you out, and they will not hear you call.

You can't get lost in an acre, but if you're disoriented, look at the climbers. The spacing between their leaves is regular near the engine and ragged towards the edge. You're out when you can no longer feel the thud through your soles.

## The engine

The engine is the reason to come and the most dangerous object here.

- **It never stops, though it sounds as if it does.** Between bangs it coasts in near silence with the intake sighing. The flywheel is taller than you, carries enormous momentum throughout, and has no guard. Never treat the quiet turns as a pause.
- **Keep clear of the belt run.** The bine belt runs from the flywheel to the pump and the dynamo. The floor beneath it is ankle-deep in sheared leaf, which is slippery and hides your footing right next to moving parts. Stay out of the shreds.
- **Don't touch the cylinder or the exhaust.** They will be hot.
- **Bring no flame or spark.** The fuel is marsh gas coming up a hollow stem. The engine has its own ignition and doesn't need yours.
- **Don't linger in the engine house.** It has open gaps and a thin canopy, but the grove sits in a hollow inside a ring dyke, and on a still day exhaust may pool. If you get a headache or feel sleepy, or the alarm sounds, walk out.
- **Treat the electrics as live.** That means the dynamo, the lamp bracket and any wire. The lamp is lit, so current is flowing, and everything around it is wet.

My suggestion is to look in through the two window gaps and not use the doorway at all. You will see everything, and you stay out of the path of the belt and the strip.

## The strip

A bare line a boot's width wide runs from the dyke gate to the oil cups. Nothing grows across it, and nobody has been seen to walk it. The oil cups are evidently not empty.

It will be the only clear, firm-looking path in the whole grove, and you will want to use it. Don't.

- Don't walk along it. Step over it at right angles and keep going.
- Don't stand on it, rest your pack on it or prop your staff across it.
- Enter over the dyke a few paces to the side of the gate if you can.
- Don't touch the oil cups, and don't try to help. Whatever tends them has done so for longer than anyone remembers.

I can't tell you what would happen if you blocked it. No one seems to have tried, and I would not be the first.

## Take nothing, leave nothing, change nothing

Every plant here may be a working part. The gas pipe is a stem. The belt is a living loop. The bearing is lubricated by a cut fig stub, and the water main is a trunk.

- Don't pick, cut or snap anything, not even the leaves the pulley is about to shear off.
- Don't try to free the rails, turn the sluice wheel or take down the shovel. The wood has them.
- Don't take a brick, and don't take a cutting. Nobody knows what a piece of this place does out of earshot of the engine.
- Don't leave anything. Things left here get gripped and slowly lifted.
- Above all, do nothing that could slow or stop the engine. The grove ends exactly where its beat does, and I would assume the two are the same thing.

## Company and communication

Normal speech is impossible. Agree signals with your companion before you cross the dyke: *stop*, *look*, *I'm stuck*, *I feel ill*, *out now*. Agree a time limit too. An hour or two is plenty.

Think twice about coming if you are prone to migraines, have a strong startle response, or have tinnitus. The bang is irregular enough that your body never quite stops bracing for it.

## Season and hour

- **Summer.** Greenish ordinary daylight, air like a warm bathroom, a firing every six or seven turns. The risk is mild heat and tiredness.
- **Winter.** A firing every dozen turns or so, with thinner dripping, long gaps and probably mist where the warm air meets the frost at the dyke. This may be the best time to see it, provided you have the dry clothes waiting.
- **Dusk.** The caged lamp becomes visible, swelling orange on each firing and fading, with a few moths. It is worth seeing, but the lamp lights only the doorway. Mark your exit line while it's still light, use your headlamp on the mat, and remember there are ditches between you and the road.

There are no biting insects, and no birds either. The absence of both is stranger than you expect.

## What it will do to you

You will feel as though you are inside a body, with the warmth, the breathing and the beat coming up through the floor. Within half an hour you will probably find you are breathing with the coasting turns and waiting for the bang. Every climber in the place winds to the flywheel's direction, so a person falling into step with it is not surprising. This is harmless, but notice it, and keep to your time limit.

When you leave:

- Your legs will feel odd on solid ground and the quiet will ring.
- Change your clothes immediately.
- Wash any skin that met white sap before it sees much sun.
- Brush leaf shreds and bits of bine off your boots at the dyke so that they stay there.

A few paces on, check the hop shoots in the hedge. They wind the ordinary way, and I think you will be glad to see it.
