# Preparing for the basin

**The short version:** you will be on your feet and moving for every minute you are there, in the cold, in the wet, in noise, by strobing light. Nothing here hunts people, but everything here processes whatever stops. Plan a visit of hours, not days, and decide how you leave before you arrive.

Much of this is inference from the place's three rules, and I've marked the parts that are guesses.

## Who shouldn't go

- **Anyone who can't walk briskly for hours without a break.** There is no sitting, no sleeping and no putting the pack down. Your limit is your walking endurance, with a reserve for when the lanes speed up.
- **Anyone with photosensitive epilepsy or flicker-triggered migraine.** The main light is constant blue arcing, out to the horizon.
- **Anyone with a pacemaker or implanted electronics.** The whole floor is a live conductor.
- **Anyone prone to fixation or trance.** The basin's real trap is being fascinated into standing still.

## The floor is a wire

The brine is far below freezing and carries current.

- **Boots:** wear knee-high dielectric rubber boots with deep rubber tread and no metal eyelets, studs or cleats. Put serious insulation inside them.
- **Wear:** the mites give the water a grain like ground glass, so expect it to abrade your soles. Check them before you go and assume they're ruined after.
- **Gloves:** wear lineman's rubber gloves under or over your mitts. The worst case is a fall with a hand in the film in one place and a knee in another.
- **Metal:** keep it off your lower body and carry nothing long and conductive. Carbon-fibre poles conduct. A fibreglass staff with a rubber tip is worth having for balance and for knocking off rime.
- **Contact:** never bridge two animals with your body. The arcs between their plates show you why.
- **Joints in the rock:** step over them. The eels discharge there, and they take feet from waders that stand too long.
- **Urination:** a stream into a live film is a circuit. Go before you arrive.
- **A wet foot ends the trip.** Brine inside a boot means frostbite and a current path.

## Cold, air, eyes, ears

- **Clothing:** wear a full waterproof shell over heavy insulation, with no exposed skin. The wind crosses forty kilometres of nothing, and sleet and fog will soak anything that can be soaked.
- **Eyes:** wear sealed goggles that block UV but are clear or pale amber. The arcs are welding light, but the day is too dim for dark lenses. A steady warm headlamp gives your eyes something that doesn't flicker.
- **Lungs:** ozone you can smell for hours is ozone doing damage. Bring a carbon-filter half-mask, and keep a spare inside your jacket because masks ice up.
- **Ears:** wear plugs and muffs together, because the noise is industrial. Radios will be useless among that much arcing, so agree hand and touch signals in advance.

## Noise is space

There are more animals than places, and the surplus is stored in the din. When it gets quieter, they arrive from nowhere, shouldering, and a person knocked flat in the film is in real trouble. So protect your ears but never wish for quiet.

Carry a noisemaker that works frozen, such as a hand-cranked siren or a wooden ratchet. Gas horns and pea whistles fail in this cold. Use it:

- when you arrive, since your arrival point will be occupied
- in any lull of wind
- in the gap between sleet bands

Don't try to find out how loud is loud enough to show bare rock. It has never happened, and you don't want to learn why.

My guess is that the rule applies to visitors too. Count your party often. If a companion's outline doubles or shimmers at the edge, take hold of them and keep walking.

## Moving

Join a lane and go with it at its pace. Walk beside or behind animals, never in front of a scraper's mouth, since a tongue that turns gneiss to slurry will not notice a boot. Don't touch the pellets. They leave the body cold enough to smoke, and they will burn through gloves. Stay off fresh smear where you can. It is laid down already a billion and a half years old, and I wouldn't trust that not to rub off.

Do everything in motion:

- Eat from inside pockets, with the food already unwrapped.
- Drink from an insulated bottle kept against your body, and never drink the brine.
- Adjust gear while walking, with every item tethered to you. Anything set down is rimed in minutes and is floor soon after.

When your ears pop or the film creeps higher on your boots, the pressure is dropping and every lane is about to speed up. Speed up with it.

## Waders

The waders cull whatever is slowing and whatever shows rime at its edges, and they do it with their faces. Don't qualify.

- Brush rime off boot welts, hems and pack straps constantly.
- Don't limp. If someone is injured, the party leaves.
- Give waders the right of way when they cross the lanes.

## The shift

Twice a day the hum drops a tone, and you will feel it in your chest even through ear protection. Afterwards every animal is one lane over and facing the other way. I don't know whether you move with them. Either way, the instant you feel the drop, look at the animals nearest you and match their new direction, because otherwise you are walking against the flow in a space with no gaps. The frost-smoke hanging where the herd used to be confirms it happened.

If you can, time your visit to fall between two shifts. Don't stay for more than one.

## The worn-through places

Where the floor is scraped through, you can see the basin from above, half a day behind. If you fell in, I think you would come up slowly somewhere else, underneath the herd. The waders walk around these places, and you should too.

Above all, don't stop to look. Waders stand at the edge staring at their own backs until their feet whiten. If you were on the plain one shift ago, you may see yourself down there. Keep walking and take the photograph on the move.

## The humps

The humps are the only things on the plain that look like seats. Each is an animal that stopped, and each is banded stone all the way through. You won't find one shaped like a person, and that shouldn't reassure you. Nothing here stands more than a hand's height above the floor for long, because the scrapers plane it down.

## Navigation and leaving

A compass is meaningless over a buried battery, the daylight circles the rim without rising, and the rim never gets nearer. The herd leaving at the east is the herd entering at the west. You cannot walk out and you cannot mark your arrival point, so your way home must be on your person and usable mid-stride.

Set your exit triggers beforehand, and use the first one that happens:

- a fixed time limit
- a wet foot
- rime that won't brush off
- an injury
- anyone who has started staring

Take nothing with you. Nothing leaves this place, and it holds its level by exact balance. Leave nothing behind either, because anything you leave becomes floor.
