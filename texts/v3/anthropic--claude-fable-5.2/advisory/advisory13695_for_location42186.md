# Preparing for the spring-house under the flooded meadow

Most of what can hurt you here is ordinary: cold brown water, total darkness, a sharp-edged hole, and a small space under a cracked slab. The strangeness is slow and quiet, so prepare for the ordinary dangers thoroughly and for the strange ones with good habits.

## What the visit is

My advice is to visit with your arm, not your body. The room was made for one person to crouch in and fill a vessel, and the only entrance now is a forearm-wide gap with cutting edges. You dive three metres, lie on the sound part of the roof, and reach in. Everything worth knowing here is known by hand anyway.

Expect a small, slow place. The whorl ticks once an hour and the eel moves once a day, so one visit will probably give you only textures, pressure, cold, and, if your reach allows, the sill.

## Getting there

- **Don't go alone.** Bring a small boat, a tender who stays in it, and a weighted line with a buoy dropped beside the roof slabs. At three metres you can find the slabs from the boat by probing with a long pole.
- **Diving.** Three metres is shallow enough to tempt carelessness. If you breathe compressed air, never hold your breath while ascending, even from this depth. Breath-hold dives are possible but give you under a minute each in cold, blind water. A small tank or surface-fed hose is better for a calm visit.
- **The meadow itself.** Flooded farmland hides fence wire, gates, ditches, and twine, so carry shears or a line cutter. The water is field soil and runoff, so cover existing cuts, have your tetanus cover current, and wash well afterwards.
- **Cold.** Water that makes the knuckles ache after a minute calls for a thick wetsuit or drysuit, hood, and boots. Plan several short dives with warming breaks, not one long one.
- **Darkness.** Your torch will show you brown fog and, at the gap, weed pressed against the lens. Practise beforehand in a pool with a blacked-out mask: finding your line, your cutter, and your way up by feel.

## The gap

- **Test the slab before you trust it.** It has already cracked once. Rest your weight on the intact slab or the bank, never on the dropped corner, and check that nothing rocks before your arm goes in.
- **Protect the forearm, not the fingertips.** Wear a cut-resistant sleeve from wrist to shoulder, with a thin glove or a bare hand below it, because thick gloves would blind you. You can lay a strip of leather or slit hose over the edge while you work, but take it with you when you leave.
- **Strip the reaching arm.** Take off rings, watch, and bracelets. Cold shrinks fingers, and anything dropped toward the middle is gone.
- **Keep your head outside.** Your bubbles should rise through the brown water, where your tender can see them. Bubbles inside that room never arrive anywhere, and I would not add yours.

## What your hand will find

Memorise the room in your own hand-spans before you go: six, four, six, four, about 1.3 metres by 0.9.

**The fill.** The pressure closes evenly around your hand at once. I expect it to feel wrong in a particular way: pressure without resistance. Nothing bunches ahead of your fingers or drags behind them, because nothing in there moves aside for anything else. Where you feel grit and softness in a single thickness, you have found two plants in one place. Be gentle with the stonewort, which snaps. The fallen chips on the floor are still sharp.

**The wall is safe and the middle is not.** Followed along the wall, the room is tiny and the gap comes round again quickly. Reached across, it has no far side. Keep your hand in contact with stone and treat the middle as a drop of unknown depth. If you lower a knotted cord, tie the free end to yourself first and expect it to run out.

**Down will misbehave.** Silt lies on the underside of the shelf, and your arm may agree with it. Your hand may feel weighted toward whatever surface it is touching, while your body outside still knows where up is. That disagreement can bring vertigo or nausea. Keep your other hand flat on the outside of the roof slab, on stone that is only stone, and navigate by the wall, never by your sense of gravity.

**The made things.** The shelf and the bowl are there to be felt, but leave the bowl where it is. Stonewort runs through the clay, and I would not find out what lifting it means.

**The eel.** It lies in the trough, if your reach gets that far. Rest your hand near it if you like, but don't grasp it. If you feel two lengths facing opposite ways, don't try to settle the question by following either one to its end. The room hasn't settled it, and your fingers won't.

**The sill.** This is the real destination: a thread of colder water, and a hand's breadth of bare stone where the weed leans away. Depending on where the gap sits over the room, your arm may or may not reach it. If it does, rest your hand there for a moment.

**Sounds.** Between breaths you may hear the tick of a whorl breaking, or the knock from the middle, like a full jar set down on wood. It comes from farther off than the room is wide, so don't reach toward it. If you want to hear more, hang a hydrophone at the gap, not in the middle, and let the tender listen for an afternoon.

## If you are thinking of going in bodily

I wouldn't. A slim person without a tank might pass the gap, but inside there is no open water and no bubble trail to follow up. Down attaches to whatever you touch, so the exit may stop feeling like it is overhead. I also can't promise that the room's refusal to displace things stops at your skin.

If you go anyway:

- Breathe from a hose padded where it crosses the edge.
- Run a line to your tender.
- Never lose contact with the wall, which will always bring you back to the gap.
- Set a time limit before you go in, and keep it.

## Take nothing, leave nothing

The room keeps what enters it. Growth is only about a centimetre a week, so a few minutes will not root you there. Anything you leave, though, will be grown through and become part of the room, including part of what is closing in on the sill.

In the other direction, I don't know what happens to two plants sharing one place once they are carried somewhere that insists on one thing at a time. I would not find out with a sample in my hand.

Count your gear before and after. Count your fingers too; it costs nothing.

## Afterwards and timing

Rewarm, wash, and clean any nicks. Write down or dictate what you felt as soon as you are in the boat, because tactile memory fades fast and there will be no pictures. Your arm may remember the even pressure for a day or two.

Go soon. The clear patch around the sill is about a hand's breadth and loses a finger each season, so in a few years there will be no place left in the room where one thing is one thing.

Go the way the place was built to be used: one person, briefly, for one thing.
