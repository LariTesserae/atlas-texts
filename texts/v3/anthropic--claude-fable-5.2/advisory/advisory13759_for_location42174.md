# Preparing for the lower reservoir

The lower reservoir is half of a pumped-storage power station, a working machine with an ecosystem living inside it. Nothing there is hostile, but nothing waits for you either. The water rises and falls because a city you will never see switches its lights on and off. The main preparation is learning that timetable as well as the eels have.

## Learn the schedule first

- **Morning, when the city wakes:** A siren sounds, followed by about twenty minutes of near-quiet and then a boom as the valves seat. Then the water rises about a decimetre every few minutes until only a two-metre slot of air is left under the vaults.
- **Late at night:** The same siren, quiet, and boom, and the machines reverse. By about four in the morning the southern high bays are bare wet floor, and the northern deep bays hold thirty metres of water.

Bring a waterproof watch set to the city's time. The description mentions only one siren, so assume it will not tell you which changeover is coming. The hour and the animals will tell you: fish drifting north means generation, and shoals leaving the high bays means pumping.

Treat every siren as an instruction to stop, get everyone into the boat, and work out where you are and which way the water is about to move. For an hour after each boom the whole basin sloshes on a forty-minute period, so the gauge marks will mislead you about the trend. Watch them for a full cycle before trusting them.

## You need a boat

There is no ground here that stays ground. At high water the entire place is a lake with a ceiling.

- **Hull:** Take a flat-bottomed boat, such as an aluminium skiff or a tough inflatable with a rigid floor. It can then settle on the slab when the tide leaves the high bays and refloat when the water returns.
- **Height:** Nothing on it should stand taller than a seated person. At high tide you have two metres of air, minus the slosh, minus lamp cages wrapped in spider web and soda straws hanging from the crowns.
- **Motor and fuel:** It takes most of a day under power to find a wall. Carry fuel or batteries for at least twice the distance you plan, plus oars, because the motor may fail. Tilt the motor up before grounding.
- **Mooring:** You cannot anchor in a place where the depth changes by tens of metres. Tie to a ladder with a long line that someone awake tends, or stay under way. Never leave the boat on a short line on a rising tide.

## Stay away from the north end when the machines run

During generation the six draft tubes are boiling upwellings, and aerated, turbulent water is bad for small boats. During pumping the same openings draw water in. The trash racks hum because water is being pulled through them, and anything afloat nearby, including you, can be pinned against them.

I'd also expect real currents in the galleries mid-tide, since all the water for the southern bays has to pass through them. Plan long crossings for slack water and travel with the flow.

The warm discharge bays are where the eels and pike gather for the first surge of stunned fish. Watch that from several bays back.

## Dress for nine degrees and constant wet

The air sits close to the water's temperature and is saturated. In summer it rains from the ceiling over hundreds of bays at once. In winter the surface steams.

- **Immersion:** Water at nine degrees takes your breath in the first minute and your grip within ten. Wear a lifejacket at all times. A drysuit is best. Failing that, wear full waterproofs over wool or synthetic layers, never cotton.
- **Rain:** A brimmed hat or hood matters more than you'd think. The drizzle is seepage through young concrete and carries lime, so it leaves a white film on lenses and gear. Bring cloths and keep optics covered.
- **Dry storage:** Keep a full change of clothes and anything electronic in dry bags.
- **Hands and feet:** Take insulated waterproof gloves for general use and tough work gloves for ladders, because the mussels on the lower rungs are small and razor-edged. Boots need aggressive or studded soles, since a few millimetres of silt on wet slab is very slippery.
- **Warmth:** Bring hot drinks in flasks and more food than seems reasonable. You will burn it just staying warm.

## Protect your hearing and plan to communicate without it

The place is loud nearly all the time. You will feel the roar in your chest before you hear it, and the echo takes twelve to fourteen seconds to die, so speech turns to mush.

- **Hearing protection:** Wear earplugs, with earmuffs over them near the north end. The siren will still get through.
- **Signals:** Agree hand and light signals before you go down. Carry a waterproof slate or notebook for anything complicated.
- **The quiet window:** Use the twenty minutes after each siren, when there is only drip and the tick of cooling steel, for anything that needs talking through. It is also the only time you will hear the place itself. Put the plugs back in before the boom.

There is no contact with the surface through four hundred metres of rock. Tell someone outside when you expect to be back.

## Navigation

Every bay looks like every other, and the lamp rows blur into haze in all four directions. In summer, fog banks drift along them and cut visibility to a column or two.

- **Grid numbers:** Every column is stencilled with one, still fresh. Work out the scheme in the first hour, especially which way the numbers run toward north. Log your position at intervals in pencil on waterproof paper.
- **Compass:** Don't rely on one. The whole structure is reinforced concrete and rock bolts.
- **Lights:** The lamps are the station's, not yours. Bring three independent waterproof lights per person, as cavers do, in case the lamps go out.
- **Secondary cues:** The boom comes from the north, and the water gets a degree or two warmer toward the discharge. Air moves toward the shafts on a rising tide and away from them on a falling one. The shafts' pale discs of daylight are your only evidence of day and night.

## Walking the high bays

In the small hours you can step out onto the drained floor in the south. It will be shining, with the rain still falling on it and crayfish tracks stitched across the silt.

Stay within a bay or two of the boat, and keep someone with it. The water comes back as a quiet, fast-spreading sheet rather than a wave, and wading through it on silt in the cold is how you would get into trouble. Stay clear of the sumps and of anywhere water is visibly draining.

## The animals

Nothing here is a threat, but a few things are worth knowing.

- **Midges** hatch in grey clouds and go to light, including a headlamp on your face. Carry your light in your hand or switch to red, and pull a scarf over your mouth near the lamps.
- **Spiders** are harmless, but you will wear web at high water.
- **Pike and eels** will ignore you. Don't trail your fingers in the water.
- **Bats** roost in the shaft collars by the thousand. Don't handle them, and don't linger directly under a collar.
- **Mussels** matter most for a traveler. Their larvae are invisible and survive in damp gear. Wash, drain and fully dry everything before you carry it to another world. The upper lake has already colonised this place through the turbines, and you should not be the next vector.

Treat the water before drinking it. It has come through a lake and a turbine, and it has been under bat roosts.

## The people

The mussels are on a scraping schedule and the paint is fresh, so crews come here. If you can arrange your visit with whoever runs the machine hall, do. They know the real timetable, the access routes, and which ladders lead anywhere. I can't tell you those things from here.

## What it will be like

Expect the first strain to be mental rather than physical. The repetition, the noise, and a tide that answers to someone else's breakfast wear on people. Go with a companion and keep the first visit to one full cycle.

Three things are worth planning around:

- **Dusk under a shaft:** At high water, sit low with your lights off. The daylight disc fades and the bats come down to drag their feet through the surface around you.
- **The boom:** Time the gap between the boom and its echo. Multiply the seconds by about 170 for a rough distance in metres to the far wall, the only measure of the place you are likely to get.
- **The submerged lamps:** At low water, drift over the deep bays and look down. The lamps are still burning far below, each with its slow ball of perch.
