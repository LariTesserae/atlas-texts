# Preparing for the fan hollow

There are two different problems here. The mountain can kill you, and you can damage the hollow. The hollow itself is mostly safe if you treat it carefully.

## The mountain

**Altitude.** A sky that darkens toward noon means very thin air, the kind where an unacclimatised person becomes confused within minutes and doesn't notice.
- Acclimatise in stages if the route allows, and bring supplemental oxygen with a spare bottle and regulator.
- Carry a pulse oximeter and bring a partner whose job is to watch you for slurred speech, stumbling, or strange decisions.
- Set a turnaround time before you start, and keep to it.

**Dryness.** There is no water at any point, so carry all of it, at least four or five litres per person per day. You lose it through breathing and never feel sweaty. Expect cracked lips, nosebleeds, and a dry cough. Bring lip balm, saline nasal spray, and eye drops.

**Cold and sun.** The absence of frost only means there is no moisture to freeze. It will be brutally cold, with wind. You need a full insulated system, glacier-grade eye protection, and sunscreen on every exposed patch of skin, including under the nose and chin.

**Scree.** Expect a thousand metres of loose rock just under a ridge. Wear stiff boots and a helmet, use poles, and remember that oxygen-starved legs are clumsy on the way down.

**Finding it.** You cannot see the hollow from above, and the sound is cut off three steps out. Get the slab's position as precisely as you can beforehand. Close in, look for a leaning grey slab with a dark seam. On the downwind side you may notice air that is less cold and smells faintly of oil.

## What it will be like

The change is abrupt. You go from silence and glare into a roaring, dim, green closet. Three things will surprise you.

- **It will feel warm.** Cellar-cool air is far warmer than the ridge. Combined with the dimness, the drone, and hypoxia, this is a strong invitation to sit down and drift off. Do not rest or bivouac inside.
- **The sound is physical.** You will feel it in your chest and teeth. The low beat can cause nausea or unease, which is normal.
- **Your eyes need a minute.** Take your dark lenses off at the seam and wait before you move.

## Equipment for the hollow

- **Hearing protection, doubled.** Use foam plugs under earmuffs. Agree on hand signals outside, because speech is impossible inside.
- **Sealed goggles.** The airstream carries enough fine dust to have polished rock.
- **A multi-gas monitor** for oxygen, carbon dioxide, carbon monoxide, and flammables. The exhaust comes from deep workings and carries an unidentified sweet gas. Hold the monitor in the outflow at the seam before you enter. Keep your oxygen on inside, and do not assume the air is good because the plants thrive on it.
- **A dim headlamp.** The bulb gives almost nothing, and the plants are indifferent to light.
- **A slim profile.** Leave your pack and bulky outer layer at the seam, weighted with rocks. Go in wearing a smooth, close-fitting layer with the hood up and nothing dangling. Take crampons off.
- **Clean gear.** Brush out boots, cuffs, and pockets before the climb. No insect, seed, or crumb has ever entered this place, and you should not bring the first.

## Hazards inside

**Fire is the worst one.** Everything in the hollow is bone-dry and resinous and has never burned, and it sits in front of a fan. Do not bring a flame or a stove, and do not smoke anywhere near the slab. Touch bare rock outside to discharge static before you enter. Keep your oxygen fittings well away from the oily motor housing, since oxygen and grease are a bad combination.

**The machinery.** The cable is wrist-thick, live, and very old. Do not touch it, the motor, the cage, or the grease nipple. Keep fingers and straps out of the bars. Leave the rungs alone, because they lead nowhere you need to go.

**The gauge and the chord are your instruments.** Note where the needle sits when you arrive. The chord will shift when your body enters the stream, which is expected. If the needle moves, or the fan's pitch changes for any other reason, leave.

## Moving without doing harm

You should know before you go that there is no path. Whoever serviced the fan left a route that has since grown over, and the lowest rung is buried in a cushion. Nothing here has ever died or been replaced, so whatever you break stays broken.

The best visit may be from the seam: crouch there, look, and listen. If you do go in:

- **Go one at a time.** The room is pantry-sized.
- **Crawl rather than stoop.** Hands and knees spread your weight and keep your head out of the hanging beards. Thin knee pads help.
- **Put your weight on the crowns of the cushions.** They are hard as wood. Avoid the seams, where the shrubs root.
- **Move slowly.** The fronds on the walls are papery, and the shrubs' lichen sleeves will flake if brushed.
- **Stop at the polished circle**, the only bare ground. It is slick, and the stream there is strong enough to push you backward, so kneel and brace.
- **Keep it short.** In the circle you are blocking the exhaust the plants feed on. A few minutes is plenty, and nothing will change if you stay longer.

## What you bring in and what you leave

You will be the wettest thing that has ever entered this place. Keep water bottles sealed and outside. Do not drink, spit, or eat inside, and relieve yourself well away and downslope beforehand.

Pack out everything, including waste. Nothing rots here, so anything you leave is permanent.

Take no samples. Leave the resin beads, the fronds, and especially the buds alone. Don't clean the bulb or wipe the plate to read it, and don't leave a cairn or a mark. Fingerprints in the dust on the housing would last indefinitely.

The best souvenir is sound. Bring a recorder with heavy input attenuation, or it will capture only distortion. Photographs are harmless, but keep the camera on a short tether.

## Leaving

Back out the way you came, and check that nothing has fallen from your pockets. Put your layers back on at the seam before stepping into the wind. The silence three steps out will be disorienting, so give yourself a moment before starting down the scree. Have your partner check your oximeter and your speech, drink water, and then descend. Most accidents on a mountain like this happen on the way down.
