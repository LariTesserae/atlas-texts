# Preparing for the Levelled Plain

Nothing here is likely to attack you, and there is nothing to fall off. The dangers are slow ones: heat that never breaks, feet that never dry, a sky with nothing between it and you, and a landscape so uniform you can lose your way back while standing in plain sight of everything. Prepare for exposure and attrition.

## Timing

- **Arrive at first light.** The steam-off-the-rock hours are the coolest and safest, and the best for walking.
- **Find out what kind of year it is, if you can.** In an ordinary year you walk on sand and rock between the pans. In a wet year the pans link, and the pavements become islands in a shin-deep sea.
- **Plan each day around the afternoon.** Storms build then. Move in the morning, and be settled low with your gear stowed by the time the towers go up.

## Lightning

This is the one hazard here that can kill you quickly. You will be the tallest object for kilometres, often standing in water, in a place where it rains somewhere in sight most days.

- The rain columns walk, so watch them. Within ten minutes you can tell which way one is heading and whether you are in its path.
- The description says thunder arrives long after, which means the storms you see are usually far off. When the gap between flash and sound drops under thirty seconds, stop walking and get low.
- Stay away from the lone trees, which are the only shade and the worst place to be. Stay off the crests of the pavements and out of the water.
- The best poor option is the lower flank of a rock swell, crouched on your sleeping pad with your feet together, away from seep lines.
- Don't bring a tall tent. Bring a low tarp or bivy.
- An umbrella is good for sun and warm rain, but fold it the moment a column turns toward you.

## Heat, light, and salt

The air is warm and close, so sweat won't cool you well. The haze doesn't block the sun, and light bounces up off pale sand and sheet water, so you burn under the chin and nose as well as on top.

- Wear loose long sleeves, a wide-brimmed hat, and good sunglasses.
- Rest through the hours around noon. There is almost no shade, so carry your own: a tarp, two short poles, and stakes that hold in sand.
- Bring electrolytes, and more than you think you need. This land has been leached of everything, so the water is nearly as mineral-free as rain. If you drink litres of it while sweating, you will dilute yourself into real trouble.

## Water

There is water everywhere, but it has all been sitting warm for weeks with things living in it.

- Collect rain directly off your tarp when you can. It is the cleanest source.
- Otherwise use the clear rock pools, filtered and treated. The fairy shrimp are a decent sign, but the birds use these pools too.
- Avoid water with scums or mats. The black film on the rock is probably something like cyanobacteria, and boiling doesn't remove those toxins.
- Don't drink from the clay pans. Wade them as little as possible, and not with open cuts. Warm, still, shin-deep water is where parasites live in most worlds.

## Feet and footing

Your feet will be wet most of the time, and that will wear you down more than anything else.

- Wear quick-draining shoes with toe protection, not waterproof boots, which just become buckets. Bring several pairs of thin socks.
- Coarse quartz sand in a wet shoe works like sandpaper. Rinse your shoes out often.
- Every night, dry your feet completely, air them, and powder them. Sleep with them bare.
- Clean and cover even small scratches. In warm wet air they go bad fast.

The rock has its own rules:

- **Rust-grey rock is dry crust and grips well. Black rock is wet, living film and as slick as ice.** Step on the grey.
- The peeled slabs ring because they are loose. They can tip, slide, or pinch an ankle, and their edges are sharp.
- Never put your fingers under a slab. Skinks live there, and so does whatever eats skinks.
- Clay pans are slippery and sucking. A walking staff lets you probe depth and steady yourself.
- The best routes link pavement to pavement across the sand, like stepping stones.

## Finding your way back

The land has no drainage lines, slopes, or valleys, so nothing leads anywhere. Every pavement looks like every other.

- Before you take a step from your arrival point, take bearings on the residual hills. They are your only fixed landmarks, and they will vanish in haze and rain for hours at a time.
- The termite spires lean toward the noon sun, so each one is a rough compass. Learn their lean on the first day.
- Don't assume a magnetic compass works here. Test it against the sun and the spires.
- Distances deceive. With nothing for scale, a tree that looks ten minutes away may take an hour to reach.
- The crest of a pavement gives you one body's height of elevation, which noticeably extends your view. Use it for scouting in the mornings.
- Mark your return point with something bright and removable. Count pavements as you go, and sketch a map.
- Your footprints in sand will last until the next rain, which may be only hours.

## Noise

By day the cicadas make a continuous drone that seems to have no source. Near a tree it may be physically uncomfortable. At dusk the frogs take over, and the chorus is dense enough to feel in your chest, with nothing to absorb it.

- Bring earplugs. You will not sleep without them.
- Agree on hand signals with any companions. You may not be able to hear each other at twenty paces.
- The noise can also mask distant thunder, so use your eyes for weather.
- Expect the sound to wear on you. Constant noise combined with constant sameness is tiring in a way that's hard to predict.

## Night

- **Sleep on rock.** The lower shoulder of a pavement is the only dry ground, and it gives back the day's warmth after dark. Bring a good pad.
- **Stay off the trails.** Something walks from pan to pan at night. It is probably after frogs, but look at the tracks in daylight to judge its size and weight. Don't camp on its routes or at a pan's edge, and seal your food.
- **Watch for snakes.** Where there are this many frogs there are frog-eaters. Don't wade after dark, and carry a staff and a light.
- **Use a red light.** A white one will bring every flying insect on the plain to your face.
- **Bring a head net, a net shelter, and repellent.** The description mentions no biting insects, but with this much standing water I wouldn't trust the omission.

## Food and fire

Bring all your food. A land this stripped of nutrients grows almost nothing edible. The plants here are so starved that some eat insects.

There is also no fuel, since everything is sparse and damp, and the lone trees shouldn't be touched. Bring a small stove.

## Keeping things dry

Nothing dries in this air. Pack everything in dry bags, especially electronics, matches, and one set of sleeping clothes that you never wear in the day. Expect mildew within days.

## Tread lightly

Damage here lasts. The pools deepen a millimetre a century, and the crust on the rock is older than anything else alive there.

- Clean your boots and gear before you arrive. Seeds and microbes from elsewhere could do real harm in a place this isolated.
- Don't swim or wash in the rock pools. Each is a closed world, and sunscreen, repellent, or soap could kill it.
- Pack out your waste. In a nutrient-starved land where water doesn't flow, it is a pollution event that stays put.
- Step over the seep lines. The sundews grow there, and the lines are the slippery places anyway.

## What it will be like

There is no summit, viewpoint, or destination. The place rewards staying still more than covering ground, so plan short walking days and long sits.

- Crouch by one rock pool for an hour and watch the shrimp rowing upside down.
- Pour a cup of water on a dead-looking sedge mat and come back the next day to see it green.
- Be on a pavement at dusk when the first frog clicks and the next pan answers.

You may feel uneasy at how little you matter here and how little anything is happening. That is the right impression. This land is finished, and you are visiting something already complete.
