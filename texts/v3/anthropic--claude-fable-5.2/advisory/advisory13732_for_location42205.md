**The short version:** this is a gentle place. Nothing here hunts you, and the hazards are old machinery, hidden footing, damp cold, and your own capacity to damage a system that has been keeping itself in tune for generations. Prepare to be self-sufficient, to get wet once every few hours, and to wait.

## Before you cross over

- **Clean everything.** Scrub your boot treads and wash your clothes. Shake out your pack and empty your pockets of seeds and crumbs. A handful of species live here, in balance on a few gallons of drip, and a foreign spore, weed, or insect is the most harmful thing you could bring. Clean up again when you leave, and take no samples.
- **Arrive out on the plain, not in the streets.** Walk in on the landward side. The harbour side is old silt with a tidal creek running through it, and it should be treated as bottomless.
- **Carry all your water and food.** The plain is dry for miles, and every drop in the district is spoken for. In an emergency you could catch a cup during a thaw and filter it, though it will taste of iron and moss. Nothing here is edible.

## What to wear and carry

- **Layers for the plain.** It is cool and windy, with bright pale grit, so bring a windproof shell, a hat that stays on, eye protection, and lip balm.
- **Rain gear for the thaw.** In a thawing street it rains from the ceiling for an hour, near freezing, and the lichen curtains you push through are sodden. You want a hooded waterproof, waterproof boots with ankle support, spare socks, and a dry layer in a sealed bag. Chill is the real risk: you get soaked in the wet street, then walk round the corner into a dry wind.
- **Nothing that dangles.** Avoid scarves, loose straps, and drawcords, and tie back long hair. The engine hall has open flywheels and belts with no guards.
- **Glasses rather than contact lenses.** The hall air carries ammonia.
- **A dim light with a red or low setting.** At slack water the hall lamps sink to orange and the streets have no light at all.
- **A waterproof sit pad and a watch.** You will spend a lot of time sitting and waiting.
- **No flame of any kind.** Bring no stove, cigarettes, or candles. Five streets out of six are crisp, dry moss in a steady wind. Eat cold, or cook far out on the grit, downwind.
- **A caution if you have asthma or mould allergies.** The air is all wet earth, and in some weeks the moss is shedding spores. Bring what you need.

## Walking in it

Stop at the last stanchion and let your eyes adjust. One pace takes you from the glare of the plain into green dimness.

The moss hides the ground. Under it are setts, a camber, and two gutter troughs, and in the cold street the fog covers all of it to the knee. Walk the crown of the road. Go around any rounded green shape rather than over it. Stay off any flat rectangle in the roadway, because it may be the weighbridge plate, which is old iron over a pit.

The climber holds the shed walls up, so don't lean on them, climb them, or pull a stem to steady yourself. Part the curtains gently, since beard lichen takes decades to grow. Keep off the trestles. Don't touch frosted flanges with a bare damp hand. If you find a drip that leaves white crust and dead moss, it is brine, and you should leave it alone.

Look into the roofless shed from the doorway, or step in briefly. The light is worth it, but you would be standing under old coils held up by vines.

Keep sunscreen, soap, and repellent out of the troughs. The gnats hatch there, and the wagtails depend on them.

## The clock

The place does not run on your day. The drum turns once in about thirty hours, in six steps. Each street gets roughly five hours of frost and fog, then the clack, then an hour of dripping. A given street's wet hour falls about six hours later each day, and the tide makes the machines surge and slacken as well.

Your watch is only a rough guide, and the ferns are a better one. Find the street where the fronds are lifting under a dry pipe, sit down at its windward end, and wait twenty minutes.

Stay at least one full turn of the drum. If you can, see one thaw in daylight and one in the dark.

The single clack will be the loudest thing you hear all visit. You will jump the first time, and the wagtails will leave.

## The engine hall and whoever keeps it

Someone tends this place. The oil cups are full, a belt has been freshly laced, and there is a worn chair. You are a visitor in their workplace.

- **Announce yourself.** The moss makes you silent, and you do not want to appear unannounced behind someone standing next to a moving flywheel. Call out, knock, and wait on the step. Put your foot in the boot-width gap rather than wearing a new one.
- **Touch nothing.** Leave the drum, the valves, the switchboard, and the oil cups alone. Do not sit in the chair.
- **Never trust a still machine.** At slack water the compressors nearly stop. They are not off, and they pick up again when the tide turns.
- **Leave if the ammonia worsens.** A sharp smell is normal for the hall. If it starts to sting your eyes or catch in your throat, walk out and move upwind. A wet cloth over your nose and mouth helps a little.
- **Don't sleep there uninvited.** Camp out on the plain, with a tent pegged for wind.
- **Ask before going up to the roof.** The view shows the whole pattern, but only go up if there is a way that is plainly in use.
- **Leave any gift on the step.** A coil of belt lacing or clean rags would suit. Do not try to help with the machines.

## What it will be like

It is quieter than you expect. You will feel the compressors through your feet before you hear them, and you will not hear your own steps at all.

You will probably feel like the only thing in six streets that doesn't know the schedule. The plants know it and the birds know it, and the keeper, if you meet them, will know it without looking. Aim to do very little: watch one street go dark and stand up, get dripped on, and leave no mark but a few footprints.
