# Preparing for the room under the ice

You will be a large, warm, clumsy object inside a sealed and tuned instrument that runs on a one-degree temperature difference. Most of your preparation concerns two things: what the note can do to you, and what your heat and weight can do to the room.

## The note

This is the main hazard. A sound that sorts springtails into drifts, pushes falling drops sideways, and makes water cavitate where it is focused is far beyond what ears tolerate. My guess is that it is as loud as standing beside a jet engine, though I can't know the real figure.

- **Protect your ears twice.** Wear deep-fitted foam plugs under heavy earmuffs, and put them on before you arrive. At this low pitch the pair gives roughly 30 decibels of protection, and your skull conducts the rest.
- **Keep the visit short.** Plan your first visit in minutes, not hours. Bring a sound meter rated well above 140 decibels (ordinary ones stop short) and let it set your time.
- **Expect to feel it in your body.** Your chest will hum and your vision may shimmer. You may feel breathless, nauseous, or anxious for no reason. If that builds, leave.
- **Stay away if you are vulnerable.** Do not go if you are pregnant, have an ear infection or a cold, or have existing hearing damage.
- **Stand in the lanes.** By ordinary acoustics, where the air holds still the pressure swings hardest. The pale ridges should therefore be the worst places for your eardrums and the dark lanes the best. The sound lies in sheets across the room, so only your position along its length matters. Move your head slowly along the room's length and find the quiet, then face along the room so both ears sit in the same sheet. The plant already breaks some rules, so test this before you trust it.

## Cold, wet, and air

The room is at freezing, saturated, and dripping, and the floor is knee-deep wet felt.

- **Dress for wet cold.** Wear a waterproof shell or drysuit over wool or synthetic layers, with tall waterproof boots and waterproof gloves. Bring a helmet for the new end.
- **Expect to chill fast.** Air rushing back and forth in the lanes strips heat like wind, though it goes nowhere.
- **Check the air.** The room is sealed and nobody has certified its atmosphere, so carry an oxygen and carbon dioxide monitor and a small escape breathing set. As a rough check, the note's frequency times twice the stripe spacing should be about 330 metres per second. If it is, you are breathing something close to air at freezing.

## Arriving and moving

- **Arrive inside, at the old end.** It is the tall, finished part. Do not cut in through ice or membrane. The room silences itself when a wall opens, and you should not be the cause.
- **Walk in the lanes.** The stripes are a stride apart, so you can step from lane to lane over each ridge. Look first, because the hunters stand straddling the lanes and the grazers are thumbnail-sized.
- **Tread lightly.** Wear soft soles with no spikes, and expect to sink. Bring a closed-cell foam pad to sit or kneel on, which spreads your weight and keeps your heat out of the mat.
- **Tether everything.** Anything small and loose will creep toward the ridges and vanish among the springtails.
- **Don't climb.** The ribs are glossy and wet.

## Your heat

The engine's warm side is one degree above freezing, and you are thirty-seven.

- **Keep to the centreline.** The floor gills sit low between the ribs along the walls.
- **Insulate well.** It protects the room as much as it protects you.
- **Cover your breath.** A heat-exchanger mask keeps your warm, wet breath off the roof.
- **Never touch the clear membrane.** It is one cell thick, and the ice worms threading it probably can't survive the warmth of a hand.
- **Bring no flame.** Leave stoves, flames, and chemical warmers behind.
- **Go alone or in a pair.** More people means more heat.

## Communicating

Voices are useless. Agree hand and touch signals beforehand, especially for "leave now", and carry a waterproof slate. Set a hard time limit before you go in, because the note erodes your sense of time. Use a dim red lamp, and use it sparingly.

## The animals and the pulse

- **Leave the animals alone.** Don't handle the hunters, which are probably harmless but unknown. Don't scoop the drifts, wade in the pool, or drink from it.
- **Fold up when the room throbs.** The throb is the grazers calling. It will feel like a second heartbeat laid over yours, and it may bring vertigo. The ridges blur while it lasts, so you lose your footing guide. Do as the hunters do and wait.

## The front

The best view is from well back, at night, with your lamp off and your eyes given twenty minutes to adjust. You will see the blue glimmer and its double in the pool.

Going closer has real costs:

- You must crawl over sharp stones under a weeping, unfinished roof.
- The sound is concentrated there.
- Your breath will be against bare ice.

Do not put a hand in the film, since cavitation that chips ice is not kind to tissue. Do not press your palm on the leading edge.

## Blisters and the silence

- **Don't lean on a blister.** You cannot put a bare ear to one without removing your protection, so don't try. A gloved fingertip, briefly, will feel its buzz, and a contact microphone will record it.
- **If the note fails, stay still.** You will almost certainly not see a blister open. If one does, the drifts will spread across your path. Keep your plugs in, because a new note is coming. The quiet places will have moved when it arrives, so find them again before you walk.

## What to leave and what to take

- **Come clean.** Clean your gear beforehand and bring no crumbs. Carry all waste out. This is a closed world.
- **Take nothing.** Do not touch the black boss at the old end. Check your boots, cuffs, and pad for fragments before you leave. Whatever started this room arrived as a single grain, and you should not carry the next one somewhere else.
- **Record the pitch.** A tuner reading is worth having. The note falls a semitone every four years or so, so the reading dates your visit.

## Afterwards

Expect ringing ears, dulled hearing, and a strange loud silence. Rest somewhere quiet for a day. If the ringing lasts longer, see someone about it, and shorten the next visit.
