# Preparing for the town under the leaves

This place is gentle, and I see nothing in it that wants to hurt you. The trouble is that it is built for people who grew up breathing from bells in near-freezing water, and you did not. The real dangers are the cold, the pressure, and your own hurry.

## Before you go

- **Train your breath and your ears.** The sleeping bells are "a long swim" apart. You need to swim 25 to 30 metres underwater on one breath, calmly, and to equalise your ears down to forty feet.
- **Get used to cold water.** Meltwater that numbs the teeth will make you gasp, and within minutes it will take the strength from your hands. Weeks of cold swims beforehand blunt this.
- **Acclimatise to the altitude.** The lake lies above the last trees. Spend a few days high before you ask your lungs to do anything unusual.
- **Learn to read knots by touch.** The cords are tied at the height of a passing hand, which suggests they are read with the fingers, in the dark, in passing. Practise feeling the difference between knots with your eyes closed.

## When to go

Go in high summer. The roof is full, the light is good, the water under the leaves is at its warmest, and each bell gets a steady thread of air.

Autumn is tempting, with the roof sinking and the sky showing, but you could be caught there when the ice closes. Do not go in winter. The air in each bell falls to a bead at a time, and my guess is the town has exactly as much breath as it has people. A guest then is one breather too many.

## What to bring and what to leave

**Bring:**
- **A thick wetsuit with no metal on it.** Check the zips, buckles and eyelets. Nothing in the town is metal, and I would not be the one to introduce it.
- **A simple mask.** Your eyes will not focus underwater without one, and seeing "the far side of a lane" is most of how you will stay oriented.
- **Stone weights.** The suit will float you, so use stones on cord with a knot you can slip, as the bells do. Better still, ask to borrow some.
- **Wool to sleep in.** Bell air is lake-cold and saturated, so nothing will ever dry. Wool stays warm when wet.
- **Dense, fatty food that needs no cooking.** There is no fire down there and never has been. You will be offered raw char and fish fat. Take the fat, eat it, and wear it on your skin as well.
- **Black glass as a gift.** They already trade for it from lower down, so good obsidian needs no explanation.

**Leave behind, or cache at the rim:**
- **Metal of any kind**, including knives and jewellery.
- **Soap, sunscreen and repellent.** You will be swimming in their drinking water and the plant's.
- **Anything that makes flame.** A bell holds very little air.
- **Loose cord.** The caddis build from whatever scraps they find, and yours would stay on the lake bed for good.

## Arriving

Do not walk onto the leaves. From the rim they look like ground, and they are not.

Do not enter by the open black water under the fall either. It is the only obvious door, and it is the one place that "belongs to no one". It is also the coldest and most turbulent water in the lake.

I would come up the outflow stream, the way the obsidian must come, and wait at the south notch. Someone will notice. Waiting is the first courtesy here, and it may take a day.

Plan to sleep on shore at first and visit the roof workrooms by day. Stay a night in a mid-water bell only if invited. Sixty bells is a fixed number, so any space you take is someone's.

## Breathing and pressure

Air in a bell is compressed by the water above it. This has three consequences.

1. **Never hold a bell-breath while going up.** A lungful taken on the floor more than doubles in volume by the surface, and the thin mountain air makes it worse. Breathe out steadily the whole way up, every time. This is the one mistake that can kill you in seconds, and local children presumably learn it before they can talk.
2. **Come up in stages after a long stay.** The town's three depths work as a staircase. If you have slept in mid-water or spent hours in the floor quarter, spend a good while in the roof bells before leaving the water. Then leave downhill by the outflow, not up over the rim.
3. **Watch the air itself.** The thread of bubbles is thin and the plant's breath is sometimes stale. If you get a headache or feel short of air, the bell has too many people in it. Say so and move.

If you are lost or out of breath, go up. The roof is never more than forty feet away, and the leaves will lift. Raise an edge and breathe as the frogs do. Do this once on purpose early on, so you know how it feels before you need it.

## Cold and bell life

Limit your first swims to minutes. Constantly wet skin softens and splits, and the minnows that follow every swimmer will pick at any break. They are harmless but persistent, so grease your skin and cover any cuts.

Nights in a bell are completely dark and not quiet. You will hear the fall in your chest, the frogs droning through the roof, snails rasping on the tap outside, and someone knocking somewhere. Earplugs help, but take them out before you enter the water, because a plugged ear cannot equalise.

Ask where waste goes. Water moves from the fall to the outflow, so I would expect everything unclean to go south.

## Finding your way

Underwater you cannot tell where a sound comes from, but you can feel how strong the fall is. The heavier the pressure in your chest, the further north you are. The long lanes run fall to outflow and the short ones cross them.

Have someone teach you three knots before any others: the lane-marker, your host's bell, and the warning for a weak stem. Read them lightly and never pull.

Do not strike a stem idly. Knocking is speech, and it carries through the roots to the whole town. Learn the knock for "help" and whichever one means you, and use no others.

## Manners

- **Tie nothing.** Every knot here says something, so a careless one is a false statement, and under the fall it is forbidden outright.
- **Do not rest your weight on young stems** or touch the tied-aside shoots. Each one is a year of someone's patience.
- **Leave the taps alone.**
- **Leave the snails where they are.** They have been put there to graze.
- **Leave the otters and the wintering newts alone.**
- **Take nothing living.** A caddis case made of twine might be given to you, but do not help yourself.
- **Do not suggest improvements.** Everything here changes at one joint a year, and that is deliberate.

## Leaving

Go out by the south notch, where a few leaves always go over, turning. Within a few steps the stream below is ordinary water, and the quiet will feel strange for a day or two.
