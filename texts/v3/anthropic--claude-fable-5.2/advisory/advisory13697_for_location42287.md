# Preparing for the drowned valley

Nothing in this valley is hunting you, but it is forty meters down, five degrees, black, and in motion for all but about an hour a day. It will treat you as it treats a hazelnut shell or a torn kelp plant: it will move you, bury what you put down, and erase where you were. Almost all of the preparation is about timing, and about learning to perceive with your hands.

## Before you go

**Train first.** For an ordinary person this is a serious dive: deep, cold, tidal, and effectively blind. If you don't dive, spend a season learning in cold water. Then spend more time in a flooded quarry with your mask blacked out, finding a line, reading a gauge pressed against your faceplate, and sharing gas by feel. If you find you can't stay calm when something touches you before you can see it, send an instrument instead.

**Arrive at the surface.** Never arrive on the bed itself. Come out above the valley on a boat with a crew who will stay there. You need a weighted shotline to the bottom, someone tending it, and a boat able to follow you if you drift.

**Look at the valley from above.** The only way to see its shape is with sound. Run a sonar over it and study the picture: the kilometer-long ridges, the whaleback flanks, the winding thread of the old river. Memorize it, because below you will only ever know the square meter under your hands.

**Choose your time.**
- **Season:** Go at midsummer. Winter gales reach all the way down, and that is when the peat ledge calves.
- **Moon:** Go on neap tides, at the quarter moons, when the run is weakest and the still periods longest.
- **Hour:** Pick a slack that falls near noon, when the brown-green dimness will at least tell you which way is up.
- **Tide table:** If this world has none, make your own. Sit over the site for several days and watch the angle of a weighted line. The water near the bed turns before the surface does, so trust the bottom of the line.

## What to bring

**Against the pressure:**
- You will be breathing gas at five times surface density and using it five times as fast.
- Even fifteen minutes on the bottom owes decompression stops on the way up.
- Bring redundant gas, ideally a mix with some helium. Narcosis at forty meters in total darkness tends to arrive as dread.

**Against the cold:**
- Wear a drysuit with thick undergarments, dry gloves, a hood, and a heated vest if you can get one.
- Use regulators rated for cold water, since ordinary ones can freeze open.
- Protect your hands above all. They are your eyes here, and five degrees makes them stupid within twenty minutes.

**Against the dark:**
- Bring a light, but expect little. The water is so loaded that a strong beam just lights a brown wall a hand's width away.
- A small, narrow light held off to one side will show you a few centimeters of whatever you are touching.
- Instruments must be backlit and readable at the faceplate, or audible.
- Wear a compass, though you will mostly navigate by line.

**Against loss:**
- Clip everything to you. Anything set down will be under sand or a hundred meters away by the next tide.
- Carry a reel so you can run a line from the shot and follow it home.
- Carry two cutting tools where either hand can reach. Drifting wrack, kelp, and your own line will all try to wrap you.
- Stay in touch contact with your companion, or on a short buddy line.
- Carry an inflatable marker buoy for the ascent.

## The visit

**Going down.** Descend the shotline on the last of the dying tide. The line will lean and thrum, and you will hang off it like a flag. Light fades from green to brown to nothing within the first fifteen meters. On the way down, listen for the dry ticking of the cobble pavement and the hiss of sand. Both will be fading. When the ticking stops, the valley is ready for you.

**The stillness.** You get ten or fifteen minutes. The streaming sand sinks out and the water goes quiet. Turn your light off for at least one of those minutes and hold still. You will feel nothing, hear only your own breath, and know that fine grey silt is falling straight down onto your shoulders, just as it falls on the stumps and the wolffish. That is the truest experience of the place available to a visitor. If you sweep a hand hard through the water, you may get a few blue-green sparks.

**Reading the bottom.** Your hands will tell you where you are:
- **Cobble pavement:** Tight, egg-smooth stones mean you are on the flanks. This is the firmest footing.
- **Sand:** Ripples under your palms mean you are on the back of a sand wave. Their steep faces point the way the last tide was going.
- **Peat ledge:** Fibrous, crumbling material means you have found the old riverbank. Stay off its lip, never get beneath it, and do not hold the stumps. They are soft as cheese and will come apart.
- **Grey mud:** The channel fill will swallow a fin and whatever visibility you have.

**The animals.**
- **Wolffish:** Do not put your hand into any crevice in the boulder field. The wolffish is not aggressive, but its jaws are built for crushing shells, and it has held that hole longer than you have been diving.
- **Sand eels:** If the sand beside you erupts with small, hard, wriggling bodies, you have disturbed the sand eels. They will be gone in two seconds.
- **Seal:** A large, smooth shape that bumps you and brushes you with whiskers is the seal. It is doing what you are doing. Let it.
- **Boulder crowns:** The pink crusts and moss-like tufts are living at the edge of what is possible. Keep your knees and fins off them.

**The flints.** Among the rounded stones your fingers may find one with an edge. Handle it carefully, because it is still sharp enough to open a dry glove, and a flooded glove ends the dive. Feel the bulb on its face and the scars on the core. Then put it back. The tide has been uncovering and reburying these things for eight thousand years, and they belong to that process. If you need to take something, take a measurement or a photograph at point-blank range.

**Leaving.** Keep one hand resting on a ripple crest. When you feel grains begin to roll back the way they came, the bed is turning, and the water above will follow within minutes. Go at once. Do not wait for the ticking to start, and do not try to swim against the tide, because no one can. If you have lost the shotline, don't search for it. Send up your marker buoy and ascend beneath it. Let the tide carry you through your decompression stops while the boat follows the buoy. Drifting feels like perfect stillness, because you are moving sideways with everything else in the water.

## Afterward

Rinse everything, because the sand will be in every seal, zip, and valve. Warm up slowly. For a day or two you may feel a quiet disorientation. You will have stood on a riverbank where people cracked hazelnuts and dropped blunt tools, and found it neither ruined nor preserved, just continuing. The hollow your knees made will be gone before you are dry.
