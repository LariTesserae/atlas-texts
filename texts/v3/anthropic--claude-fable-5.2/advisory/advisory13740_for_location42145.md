# Preparing for the plateau

Nothing here will come after you. The dangers are passive: sharp rock under a soft cover, hard light from two directions, a cliff edge, live high-voltage equipment, and a silence that removes most of your usual warnings. Prepare for a high, bright, wet-cold place where you cannot call for help and where what you damage takes decades to grow back.

## Plan around the daily clock

- **Arrive mid-morning.** By then the dew glare is easing and the moss surface is drying.
- **Do your walking between then and mid-afternoon.**
- **Be settled where you will spend the night before the cloud comes over the lip.** Once it lies on the plateau you will have neither sight nor sound, and you should not move until it sinks.
- **One night is enough.** It gives you the evening tide, the cold clear hours after the cloud sinks, and the morning field of lenses. A second day adds little except sunburn.

## Thin air and light

- **Altitude.** You will probably arrive unacclimatised. Expect breathlessness, a dull headache, and poor sleep. Move slowly and drink more than you want. There is no way to descend, since the only way down is through the cloud, so your exit home is your only remedy for altitude sickness. Don't wander so far from it that a bad headache becomes a real problem.
- **Eyes.** The light comes from below as well as above, so treat it like a glacier. Bring glacier-grade sunglasses with side shields, and a spare pair, because losing your only pair here is a real injury.
- **Skin.** Put sunscreen under your chin, inside your nostrils, and behind your ears. Wear a brimmed hat with a dark underside, a neck gaiter, and lip balm.
- **Rest.** There is no shade anywhere, even inside the heath. Bring a dark cloth or eye mask, because your eyes will ache for darkness by afternoon.

## Footing

You are walking on a mattress laid over broken glass. The moss hides clinker, plates, cracks, and the thin roofs of lava tubes.

- **Protection.** Wear stiff-soled boots with ankle support, gaiters, tough trousers, and leather gloves. Keep the gloves on, because any stumble puts a hand through the moss onto the rock.
- **Probing.** Carry a wooden or fibreglass staff and test ahead of each step. Keep metal poles low under the conductors.
- **Pace.** Expect about a kilometre an hour. The full length and back is a whole day's work in this air.
- **Route.** The two lanes of shorter moss between the three dark bands run from pylon to pylon. They are the best path and a handrail if visibility fails. They also confine your damage to one line, since a footprint in this moss may last for years. Count the pylons as you go, and don't trust a compass on young basalt.
- **Lava tubes.** Stay out of the skylight and any hollow. Roofs are thin, and in air this still, volcanic gas can pool where you can't smell it.
- **The scarp.** The lip is an overhang of young rock with moss disguising where it ends. Watch the bird from at least fifty metres back.
- **Dropped things.** Anything you drop falls silently into a forearm of moss and is gone. Put lanyards on glasses, knife, and lamp, and choose brightly coloured gear.

## Water, cloud, and cold

- **Carry all your water.** Bring four to five litres per day. The cistern near the fog frames is a backup if the hatch will move, and its water needs filtering. Don't wring out the moss, which needs that water more than you do.
- **Prepare to get wet without rain.** The evening cloud deposits water on every hair and fibre, and you will be combed like the leaves. Bring a full waterproof shell, synthetic or wool layers, no cotton, and dry bags for everything.
- **Prepare for cold.** The hours in the cloud are near freezing in effect, and the clear hours after it sinks are colder.
- **Your feet will be wet all day,** because the moss depths are sodden. Bring spare socks.
- **In the cloud, sit still.** A headlamp only makes a white wall. After the cloud sinks, look at the stars. You may also see a faint violet glow along the wet conductors. On an ordinary line you would hear it crackle, but here you won't.

## The silence

It is stranger than it sounds and has practical consequences.

- **Companions.** You cannot shout to a companion. A person who falls three metres behind you makes no noise. Stay within sight, look back often, and agree on hand signals. In the cloud, tie a short cord between you.
- **Alarms.** Use vibrating alarms. Whistles are useless here.
- **Missing warnings.** You will get no audible warning from anything, including electrical equipment, shifting rock, or your own stove. Check by eye what you normally check by ear.
- **Balance.** Hearing helps you stay upright more than you realise. Expect mild unsteadiness and a sense of pressure in the ears during the first hours.
- **Mood.** Some people find hearing their own pulse restful, and others become anxious. If you are in the second group, bring earphones and something familiar to listen to. It may not be needed, since by the second day most people stop expecting sound.
- **Returning.** Expect ordinary noise to feel violent when you go home.

## The line and the station

Everything is live, and none of it will hum to tell you so.

- **The pylons.** Don't climb them or touch the green columns growing inside the lattice, especially when wet. Don't raise anything long under the conductors.
- **The gravel margins.** The growth, which covers everything else, stops a metre from the transformers. I would take that line as applying to you too. You can feel the mains tremor through the station wall without crossing the gravel.
- **The geothermal equipment.** Leave the wellhead valves alone. The lagged pipe is hot wherever the lagging has failed. Don't follow it into the cone, because the volcano is younger than the steel and has not promised anything.
- **The absent owner.** Someone or something keeps the insulators clean and uses the current below the cloud. You don't need to find out who. Behave like a guest on working premises: touch no switchgear, take nothing, and leave the moths where they rest.

## Sleeping and leaving no trace

- **Where to sleep.** Sleep on something man-made or bare: the station's concrete apron, the cistern lid, the trolley deck, or the clinker tongues near the cone. Avoid the moss, which will soak you and be crushed. Avoid the gravel margins and the pylon footings.
- **What to sleep on.** Use a closed-cell foam mat, because the glassy rock will puncture an inflatable one. A bivvy bag is better than a tent, since there is nothing to stake into.
- **No open flame.** By noon the moss surface and the waxy heath are dry enough to burn. Eat cold, or use a stove only on concrete.
- **Waste.** There is no soil to bury anything in. Pack out everything, including your own waste. This system lives on its own dead and a ration of fog, and it has no use for your nitrogen.
- **Clean gear.** Clean boots and gear of seeds and spores before you come and again before you leave.

## Kit summary

- **Eyes and skin:** glacier glasses and a spare pair, sunscreen, lip balm, brimmed hat, neck gaiter, eye mask
- **Footing:** stiff boots, gaiters, leather gloves, non-conductive staff
- **Clothing and sleep:** waterproof shell and trousers, warm layers, spare socks, dry bags, foam mat, bivvy bag, warm sleeping bag
- **Water and food:** all your water, a filter, cold food
- **Signalling and light:** headlamp, short cord, lanyards, vibrating watch, notepad and pencil, signal mirror
- **First aid:** kit for cuts (irrigation syringe, tweezers for glass, closure strips, tough dressings), headache tablets
- **Waste:** waste bags

## What it will be like

Very little happens here. The cloud rises and sinks, the beads form and vanish, and the meters turn. The danger is mostly in the first hour, when you are clumsy and dazzled, and in the last light, when the cloud comes in and you are tempted to take one more look from the lip. In between, the place asks you to move slowly, keep to the lanes, and stop where the growth stops.
