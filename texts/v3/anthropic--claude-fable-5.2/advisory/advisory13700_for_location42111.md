**The short version:** carry all your water, enter by day, stay in the dark, keep to the left wall, stay for minutes rather than hours, and walk out the moment the floor knocks. The chamber is not hostile to you, but it is hot, dry, blind and very easy to damage.

## The approach and the ridge

- **Water decides everything.** It is a day's walk to water in any direction, so you carry in every drop: the walk up, the time on the ridge, the walk down, and a margin. Budget 4 litres per walking day and 5 or more per day on the ridge, because the chamber air dries you faster than you can feel. Cache some at the halfway point on the way up, and bring electrolytes.
- **The rime is not yours.** That grey frost is the only free water the chamber's animals ever get. Do not scrape it, melt it, or stand on the lip stones while it is there.
- **The nights are cold and the ridge is bare.** The moths are sheltering from cold, so expect frost, wind and no fuel. Bring a real sleeping system and a shelter you can weight with stones.
- **Camp well back.** The warm air at the slot is tempting, but it is the chamber's exhaust, sulfur and scale dust, and your presence would keep the walkers from the frost. Camp down by the cairns, and do not add to them or borrow from them. I don't know whether they mark a route, a camp or graves.

## What to bring

- **A respirator** (P100/FFP3 class, with spare filters) and **sealed goggles**. The fatty wing-scale dust never settles, and moth scales are a classic sensitizer of lungs, eyes and skin. Shave if a beard would break the seal. Wear glasses, not contact lenses.
- **A small multi-gas monitor**, clipped to your chest. The crack smells of flint and sulfur and breathes volcanic air. The animals thriving there prove nothing, because insects tolerate air that would drop a person.
- **A dim red light**, kept in a pocket for emergencies only.
- **Smooth, tightly woven long clothing** with closed cuffs, thin gloves and a hood or cap. Add high boots with sealed gaiters, since you will wade knee-deep through litter full of beetles.
- **A timer**, preferably one that vibrates.
- **A sealed bag** for your chamber clothes, a clean change, and wipes. There is no wash water, and you will come out greased with dust that tastes of nuts and ash.
- **Antihistamines**, plus whatever you are prescribed for asthma or severe allergy.
- **A companion**, who stays outside beside the slot, not in it, and keeps time.

## What to leave behind

- **Flame of any kind.** The floor is knee-deep in desiccated wings and fat and the air is full of combustible dust. One match could end the chamber, with you inside. Cook only at camp, far down the ridge, or eat cold.
- **White light and camera flash.** These moths walk rather than fly, even when heat drives them, because they are living on stored fat. Startling a few million into the air would cost them that fat, bring them down onto a floor that eats what falls, and bury you in scales.
- **Insect repellent and treated clothing.** You would be walking poison through a room made of insects. Skip perfumes too, since everything in there works by smell.
- **Food, and spilled water.** The only thing that ever enters is moth fat, and nothing rots because nothing is wet. Do not eat, urinate, spit or pour water inside.
- **The urge to collect.** Take no moths, no beetles and no square of felt. The floor is a record hundreds of seasons deep, so don't dig in it.

## Going in

Wait until the sun is on the ridge. Before dawn the lip stones are covered with hundreds of walkers drinking frost, legs interlaced. Watch that from a distance, sitting still without a light.

Leave your pack and warm layers outside, weighted down. You are stepping from near freezing into fever heat, and you will want those layers the moment you come out.

Don't lean on the slumped wall, and don't use the dished slab as a seat or table. My guess is that people once ground moths on it for the fat. Don't repeat the harvest: you don't know this world's chemistry, and the place can't spare it.

Go down through the slot slowly, testing with hands and feet. Stand in the four paces of daylight for a few minutes and let your eyes give up.

## Inside

**Follow the left wall.** At shoulder height, under the moths, there is a polished strip with fingertip pits at intervals. I believe it is a handrail, worn by generations who came in the dark as you are doing. Touch it lightly, because every moth you knock loose is a moth the worms get. The lizards like the pits, so expect something flat and warm under your fingers now and then. Wait for it to move, or skip that pit.

**Use heat as your compass.** Heat on your face means you are heading toward the crack. Heat on your back and cool air along your shins means you are heading toward the mouth. If you lose the wall and panic, stop, turn until the heat is behind you, and walk.

**Know the three bands and how long you can stay in each.**

| Band | What it feels like | How long |
|---|---|---|
| Mouth | Fevered skin | Up to about a quarter hour |
| Middle | Sun-baked stone | A few minutes |
| Far end | A hand cannot stay | Not for you |

Count your paces, forty in all, and turn around by the mid-twenties at the latest. Turn sooner if the air stings your nose through the mask, the monitor sounds, or the handrail ends. Wherever the old visitors stopped is a good place for you to stop. Several short visits are better than one long one, with water and rest between.

**Keep moving, slowly, and stay on your feet.** If you stand still, the beetles flow around your ankles and start investigating your boots. Don't sit, kneel or set anything down. The hair-thin worms in the felt wake for living things that fall. I can't say whether they would bother with you, but don't offer. If you do fall, get up at once, and check your cuffs and skin outside.

**Expect to be walked on.** The long-legged walkers span a spread hand and step so lightly the moths don't stir, and one may cross your shoulder or your hood. Their bite is an unknown, so the rule is never to swat, grab or pin one. Hold still and let it finish.

## The knock

A few times a day the crack breathes harder. The warning is a low knock from deep down, felt in the feet more than heard. So stay quiet, pay attention to your soles, and pause now and then to listen with them. The other signs are a cloth-on-table sound as every moth begins walking the same way, and heat arriving on your face.

At any of these, turn and walk out with them. Do not run. The middle band is about to become the far end, and the minutes that follow are when most of the eating happens. Every animal is out of place, under thin cover and hungry, and you don't want to be among them.

## What it will be like

The chamber is less frightening than it sounds, and stranger. There is nothing to see. There is a hiss like sand poured on paper, an occasional tick of rock, and walls that give under your palm like the flank of something large and asleep. Your handprint closes within minutes. The air tastes of nuts and ash even through the filter. Most people feel a strong urge to switch on a light, and you should resist it.

## Who should not go

Stay away if you have asthma, insect or dust allergies, heart trouble, poor heat tolerance or claustrophobia. Stay away too if you cannot bear being touched by many small animals in total darkness.

## If the cold breaks

If the night turns mild while you are there, the moths will leave over three or four nights. Stand beside the slot, never in it, with every light off, and let them pass your face. Do not go in afterward. Once the walls are bare the residents start on each other, from the crack outward, and I would not want to be the largest store of fat and water in the chamber.

When you leave, shake out your clothes downwind and set any stowaway beetles back inside the lip.
