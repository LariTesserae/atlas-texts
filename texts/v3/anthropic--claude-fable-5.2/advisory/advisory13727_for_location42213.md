# Preparing for the saddle

Nothing here will come at you. The description says the loudest event is a stone sliding off its pedestal, so there are no predators, avalanches, or thunder. The dangers are quiet ones: thin air, light, cold, dryness, and two edges. You are not going to cross this place, but to sit on its eastern rib for one turn of its day and watch.

## Altitude

An indigo sky and rock too hostile for lichen suggest roughly 6,000 meters, with about half the oxygen you are used to.

- **If you walk up** from the brown country below, as the builders did, take at least two weeks and sleep only a few hundred meters higher each night.
- **If you arrive directly**, you start a clock. You will feel breathless but fine at first, then get a headache and nausea within hours. Staying overnight unacclimatized risks fluid in the lungs or brain. Either acclimatize at a similar height first, or bring supplemental oxygen and keep the visit to a single day.
- **Carry a medical kit**: a pulse oximeter and altitude medication (acetazolamide, dexamethasone, nifedipine) that a doctor has shown you how to use.
- **Decide your exit rules beforehand and write them down.** Leave if you stumble on flat ground, hear a wet cough, have a headache that painkillers don't touch, or get confused. Hypoxia makes you stupid and confident at once.
- **Bring a companion and assume there is no rescue.** Rehearse your way out until you can do it with numb hands and a slow mind.

## Light

At noon the whole field is a lamp, and the light comes from below as well as above.

- **Eyes**: bring glacier glasses of the darkest category with side shields, plus spare goggles. Without them you will be snow-blind by evening, which feels like sand under the eyelids and lasts days.
- **Skin**: use SPF 50+ and reapply it. Cover the underside of your nose and chin, the insides of your nostrils, and your ears. Use a zinc lip balm. If you pant with your mouth open, the roof of your mouth can burn, so wear a buff over it.

## Cold and wind

Dawn cold "hurts the teeth", so expect −20°C or worse, plus wind. The saddle is a funnel, and the finger-long rime shows the wind blows all night.

- **Clothing**: wear a base layer, fleece, and windproof shell, with a heavy down parka and insulated trousers on top. Bring liner gloves and expedition mittens, double mountaineering boots, and chemical warmers. In thin air you make less heat than you expect.
- **Sleeping**: bring a bag rated to −30°C, two mats, and a bivy sack or low tent. Sleep will be poor. You will wake gasping, which is normal periodic breathing but unpleasant.
- **Shelter**: use the lee of the windbreak. The builders put it there because that spot works, and it sits away from the horns, which shed the slabs that become tables. Rock falls mostly as the sun arrives and as the evening cold tightens.

## Water, food, fire

The air takes rime "without melting", which means it is very dry. You will lose liters just by breathing.

- **Water**: carry four to five liters per person per day, with electrolytes, since meltwater has no minerals. You can dip from a runnel near the rock between noon and the cloud, but only then. Keep bottles inside your jacket or bag, because everything freezes by dusk.
- **Stove**: bring a cold-rated one, with matches or a firesteel, because lighters fail up here. Water boils near 80°C, so bring food that only needs soaking, and things you can eat with no appetite.
- **Fire**: there is no fuel. The one length of silver wood is not fuel, and the ring is not yours to light.

## The ice

You will not walk it. The slots are a boot wide and waist-deep between blades of hard ice. They can wedge a leg or snap an ankle, and the people who lived here needed stone tools and real labor to cut one lane.

- **Seeing it**: bring binoculars and a polarizing filter. From the rib you can see the platform, the dip of the old lane, and the black glass, green pin, and shell bead hanging in their wells.
- **The edges**: bring crampons and a pole for the rock near the lips, where melt refreezes. Keep away from the western edge, where the grey tongue hangs.
- **The tables**: do not lean on, sit on, or shelter under one. One day each stalk gives way.

## The afternoon cloud

It "arrives all at once." With no shadows, the white ground and white air look the same, and there is a drop on either side.

- Be at the windbreak by early afternoon, with warm layers on, before the cloud comes.
- When it comes, sit down and stay there. The graupel is harmless. Listen to it hiss, and to the hush of the wind through the blades.
- Do not go looking for the edge to see where it is.

## Leaving nothing

This place files what it is given. Any small dark thing you drop, such as a lens cap, button, or crumb, will warm, sink, and join the second floor a forearm down, beside the bead. It will stay there for good.

- Tether your gloves, glasses, and caps, and eat over a bag.
- Pack out all waste, including your own. Nothing rots here.
- Do not move stones, even to anchor a tent. Bring your own weights or use your pack. A shifted slab starts a new pedestal, and that changes the saddle's shape for decades.
- Do not touch the cord, the sherds, or the wood, and do not reach into the wells.
- Leave no offering, however much the altar-or-floor seems to ask for one. An offering is a deposit.

## What it will be like

It will be lonelier than you expect. Nothing lives here, and the only visitors are a dead moth and a feather. It is silent enough at dawn that you will hear your pulse.

It will also be more orderly than you expect. The day goes locked, then sweating, ticking, open-eyed, white, gold, and creaking, then locked again, and by evening you will feel you have watched a mechanism rather than weather.

Stay for one full cycle if your body allows, and for the fifteen minutes when the ice pings and the stars come out early. Then go down, and take with you only your notes and photographs.
