Most of the preparation for this place is training, not gear. You will be functionally blind, at altitude, soaked, and inside a roar the whole time you are there, and the residents get around by skills you don't have yet.

## Train before you go

- **Practise being blind.** Spend a few weeks doing ordinary things blindfolded: walking with a long cane, packing and unpacking, eating, dressing, finding a toilet. Pack your bag the same way every time so your hands know where everything is.
- **Train your ear.** Streets are identified by their interval against the Tap's chord, so what you need is relative pitch. Practise naming and singing intervals against a drone. Your route home will be a short tune, so learn it and hum it. If you can't hold a pitch, arrange a resident guide and don't go without one.
- **Acclimatise.** The air is thin enough that newcomers climb stairs in stages, and the city is built in terraces. Spend time at altitude first, climb slowly, and know the signs of altitude sickness. A headache with confusion or a wet cough means you descend.

## Audit everything for light

Any light on an open terrace brings ten thousand animals onto you within seconds, so check every item you own.

- **Leave behind:** torches, matches, lighters, stoves, cigarettes, UV water purifiers.
- **Switch off and bury deep, or leave:** phones and anything else with a screen.
- **Check the small things:** indicator LEDs (tape them, then tape them again) and luminous or tritium watch dials. Bring a tactile watch instead.
- **Don't assume red or infrared light is safe.** Nobody has told you what the animals can sense.
- **Don't count on night vision.** The eyepiece glows on your face, and in saturated fog over uniformly wet walls it would show you very little anyway.
- **Remove steel spikes before the city.** Steel on glassy rock can spark.

## What to wear and carry

- **Clothing:** Wear wool or synthetics. Cotton stays wet and down collapses, and nothing dries here, since your sleeves are damp within a minute. Keep one complete dry set sealed in a dry bag and do not open it until you leave.
- **Head and eyes:** Bring a hood or brimmed hat and clear goggles. The fliers are overhead on every ledge and droppings fall constantly. Tie back long hair, because the air is thick with wings.
- **Hands:** Thin, grippy, washable gloves. You will trail a hand along walls coated in bacterial film and pebbled with grazers.
- **Feet:** The film is as slick as the inside of a cheek. Felt soles, the kind anglers use on slimy rock, are my best guess. Carry removable ice spikes for the approach only.
- **Ears:** You need your hearing to navigate, but the roar never stops. Use flat-response musician's plugs that lower the volume without smearing pitch, and heavier plugs for sleep.
- **Quiet kit:** Pad anything that clinks, especially glass. The fliers' voices sound like struck bottles, and you don't want to be saying something in their language by accident.
- **Records:** Bring a sealed audio recorder with tactile buttons, not a camera. The place is made of sound, and that is what you can bring home.

## The approach

The valley is the most physically dangerous part. The rock is young, glassy and sharp enough to cut, and away from the city's warmth it is all skinned in hard clear ice. You need sturdy boots, tough gloves, spikes, poles and real cold-weather layers.

If you can, come in the brief months when the sun clears the horizon, so you have twilight for the walk. Direction is no problem, because you walk toward the roar. A light is probably safe far out, where everything is frozen and dead. Put it out when the roar starts to resolve into separate voices, or when you first feel fog or warmth, whichever comes first.

## Getting around

- **Use every cue.** The junction tones are the signs. The flier colonies pitch their calls to their street, so each district has an accent. Water always runs toward the low end of the saddle. The Tap's chord in your sternum grows stronger toward the centre.
- **If you're lost,** stop, put a hand on a wall, and work through those cues one at a time. Don't follow water downhill to "see where it goes". It goes over the lip, onto warm slick rock and then onto ice.
- **Keep to the wall side of terraces.** There are rails, but they are also nesting ledges, so don't grab or lean on them without feeling first.
- **Expect the spans to push back before you touch them.** Treat it as a courtesy warning and don't lean into it.
- **Ignore the rock when it cracks like a rifle.** Everyone else does.
- **Discount old directions slightly.** The tones drift flat as the towers grow, so anything from an earlier traveller is a little sharp.

## The animals

- **The barrel-bodied wall-lickers won't yield.** Shoulder through them firmly, as the locals do. They grumble, and that's all.
- **Keep your hands out of the gutters.** They are thick with crustaceans, and the long-fingered rakers work them from the walls above.
- **You will crunch grazers underfoot.** It can't be avoided.
- **The fliers will avoid you.** They navigate by clicks, and you will feel their wingbeats on your face as pressure. Just don't put your hands on ledges full of chicks.
- **Learn what a moving silence means.** A hole in the noise crossing a tower face is the hunter. It takes fliers, not people, as far as anyone has said. My instinct is to stand still and keep your hands in, but ask a resident on your first day what they do, and copy them. Here a silence is the alarm signal.

## Food, water and health

- **Shellfish allergy is a serious problem here.** The water runs thick with crustaceans and the diet almost certainly leans on them. Carry epinephrine, or reconsider the trip.
- **Nothing here is a plant.** Bring vitamin C for any long stay, vitamin D for the dark, and some food of your own. Expect everything to taste faintly of ammonia within a day, including what you brought.
- **Drink what the residents drink.** Carry a pump filter or chemical tablets as a backup.
- **Cover every cut with a waterproof dressing.** The approach rock will cut you, and the city is coated in bacteria and guano runoff. Wash your hands before eating.
- **Look after your skin.** Constant damp means trench foot, chafing and fungus. Dry your feet whenever you sleep, and bring powder and antifungal cream.
- **Watch your lungs.** Ammonia in thin air is hard on them. If you have asthma, bring your medication and a low threshold for leaving.

## People

No one will read your face, so unlearn nodding and pointing. Speak close to the ear, and expect touch to do the work that gestures do elsewhere. Ask what the conventions are and don't guess.

Good gifts are things for the nose, tongue and fingers from a world with plants: spices, tea, dried fruit, sugar, a textured cloth. Pictures are useless. Be careful with anything that makes a sustained tone, because the tones are how people find their way.

## Staying sane and leaving

There is no day, no season and no visual world. Keep your own schedule by tactile watch, and bring melatonin if you use it. After a few days your brain will start supplying lights and shapes in the dark. That is normal, but they aren't real, so don't walk toward them. The low chord in your chest may leave you uneasy or queasy at first. Decide in advance how long you will stay.

When you go, change into the sealed dry set at the city's edge, before the cold. Wet clothes in that valley will freeze on you. If the sun is up beyond the cloud cap, cover your eyes, because after the city even grey light will hurt.
