# Preparing for the Jug Town

Everything in this place is in balance. The water never thins or swells, the sump drains as fast as it fills, the doors are chipped back as fast as they grow, and there are always three olms. The worst thing you can be here is a disturbance, so most of the preparation is about making yourself small, quiet in the local sense, and easy to absorb.

## Before you go

- **Health.** Two hundred people share one saturated room and one water channel that passes every door. A cold you carry down will reach all of them, so isolate beforehand and do not go if you are even slightly ill.
- **Body.** You will crawl a polished tube on your belly, climb wet toe-notches with no ladder, and pass strangers chest to chest on ledges without rails. Practice wet scrambling and crawling with your gear dragged from an ankle. If tight spaces or heights frighten you, find out before you are on the fourth tier.
- **Temperament.** There is no silence, privacy, morning, or dry surface, and all conversation happens by touch. Some people find this soothing within an hour and some find it unbearable. Decide your exit signal in advance.
- **A partner code.** If you travel with someone, agree on a few forearm signs: yes, no, stop, help, I need to leave. Speech does not work below the last bend, and shouting is only rude.

## What to carry

There are no backpacks on those ledges. Anything on your back will scrape a corner or tip you off, so carry one small bundle slung flat against your chest.

- **Wool and hide, close-fitting, with nothing dangling.** Loose straps catch antler pegs, door-flaps, and the bone props of the ringing-stones. Cotton is useless when wet, and it was once green.
- **Thin, flexible, grippy footwear, or bare feet.** The cups were worn by toes, and rigid boots will not seat in them. Knappers work in the doorways, so watch for chert flakes underfoot.
- **In-ear hearing protection that lowers volume without smearing pitch.** Musician's plugs do this. Tether them, because one dropped in the sump ends up in the slot. Do not use earmuffs, and do not clap your palms over your ears. A flat palm on the ear is their sign for the fall, and you would be saying it over and over.
- **Fat for your own skin.** Nothing dries here, and greased feet and hands survive the wet far better than bare ones.
- **Gifts, already dressed.** Bring rendered fat above all, since it is both food and light. Marrow bones, good hides, sinew, gut cord, and antler tines for picks and pegs are also welcome. Arrive as the other bundles do, finished and wrapped in skin.

## What to leave behind

- **Your light.** This is the most important item. Use a dim lamp for the upper crawl if you must, and put it out before the last bend. The lamps have never moved, and the amber-and-white pattern on the walls records that. A headlamp would throw light where none has ever fallen. A moving beam is also speech here, so you would be flashing nonsense across the whole chamber.
- **Cameras, paper, and electronics.** Condensation will ruin them, and a flash is worse than a headlamp.
- **Anything plant-made or throwaway.** Goods only travel down, so whatever you bring stays forever. I infer the rule against wood, paper, and wrappers, but I would follow it strictly.
- **Soap.** Every basin overflows into the one below, and everything ends in the sump with the olms.
- **The hope of a souvenir.** Nothing goes back up except people. That rules out even a flake, a smear of moonmilk, or a chip of flowstone.

## The descent

Push or drag your bundle and take the tube slowly. The bass will reach your chest before your ears, and that is normal. At the last bend, stop for a good while. Your eyes need the time to adjust to fifteen-odd fat lamps lighting a whole town, and your nerves need it to accept that the noise will not stop. Then wait at the lip until someone has seen you. A flicker passed across a lamp probably means you have been announced.

## Inside

**Moving.** Follow the wear, which is the accumulated advice of everyone who lived here. The left cups are deeper, so lead and push off with the left foot. Lean your shoulder into the corners where the stone is glossy. When you meet someone on a ledge, turn chest to chest and pass without fuss. Do not go up to the slab bridges in the neck unless led. They are high, narrow, and dripping, and the smoke collects there.

**Hearing.** Learn the note of your host's ringing-stone as soon as you arrive. Hum it into your own skull until you can pick it out, because that is how you find the door in the dimness. If you have a poor ear, count doors from a lamp. Never bump a ringing-stone or its pegs. Moving one would change a household's address.

**Talking.** Keep your forearms bare, your hands warm, and your nails short. Offer your arm and hold it still while someone taps, presses, or draws on it. You will be touched constantly by people you do not know, and that is how the place talks. You will get by on a few signs. Be careful when walking past a lamp niche, since blocking a lamp is the long-distance form of speech.

**Water.** Drink from the falling spout, not from the basin. Put nothing into your basin that you would not hand to the family below, because that is where it goes. Ask by sign where waste goes, and do not guess. Keep away from the main fall, which hits hard enough to hurt. Do not wade into the slack water behind the block, because the olms live there.

**Food.** You will eat fat, marrow, and dried meat, with no plants at all. This is fine for a short stay, but your gut may complain, so eat modestly at first.

**Sleep.** The town sleeps in thirds and you will be given a turn, probably in a hollow still warm from the last sleeper. The noise is less of a problem than you would expect, because it never changes. There is no morning, so count your stay in sleeps and settle the number before you descend. Without a number you will lose track.

**Cold and wet.** The cold is mild and never lets up. It will not hurt you in an hour, but over days it wears you down. Keep your core layered and grease your feet. Accept that you will be damp for the whole visit.

## What not to touch

- **The lamps.** Never move one. If you are shown how to feed one where it sits, that is an honor, and you should do it exactly as shown.
- **The moonmilk.** It is soft white paste that has never been lit or handled. A fingerprint in it would be permanent.
- **The olms.** Each child touches one once, and you are not one of those children.
- **The crickets.** They are left alone.
- **The walls, with any tool.** Do not chip the flowstone, even if a door looks narrow. They are matching a width they have known all their lives, and you do not know it.
- **The quiet cell.** You will want it badly by the second sleep. It belongs to the newborn and the dying, and nobody will even widen it for fear of moving the silence. Do not use it as a rest stop. If someone leads you in, go still, touch nothing, and expect the sound of your own swallow to be startling.

There is one thing you may do. If a slow black drop has landed on the ledge in front of you, wipe it away with your thumb as you pass, without breaking stride. That is the one piece of the town's upkeep a stranger can share.

## Leaving

Go up when your count of sleeps is done. I suspect, though this is only a feeling, that a place this balanced starts to count you if you stay too long. The crawl up is harder than the crawl down, because you are tired and climbing on slick stone. Leave a cache at the top with dry clothes, a light, something green to eat, and dark glasses if you might come out in daylight.

The silence above will feel loud at first. Your ears may ring for a day or two, and you may notice your chest waiting for the bass. For a while you may catch yourself listening for one particular pitch among ordinary sounds.
