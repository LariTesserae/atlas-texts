# Preparing for the lava-flow valley

The main thing to understand is that you will be blind for the whole visit, and no equipment fixes that. Everything alive there, people included, works by ear, by hand, and by the feel of air on skin. Preparing mostly means getting your body covered, your hands educated, and your expectations lowered. Plan on a few hundred meters an hour. A two-kilometer crossing is a full night's work, and the eleven-kilometer length is not a sensible goal.

## Before you go down

- **Start with the ridge people.** They are the only map there is, and it is in their hands. Ask to go with someone who works the flow, and ask before going near any skylight, since the fledgling ledges are almost certainly somebody's. Have them teach you the knot patterns and trunk notches with your eyes shut until you can read *hole left*, *hot ground*, and *glass, go slow* without thinking. Ask whether the crabs are running.
- **Rehearse blind at home.** Wear a blindfold for an evening. Practice finding your water, dressing a cut, re-lacing a boot, and walking rough ground with a pole. Give everything in your pack a fixed pocket and a lanyard, because anything you drop goes into the rubble the way the rain does.
- **Spend a night or two on the crest just listening.** Learn the drone as separate notes. Notice how they change when the weather turns. The crest is also your retreat, because one step behind it the sound shuts off.
- **Assume no rescue and no satellites.** Compasses may also misbehave over fresh basalt. Get your vaccinations current, tetanus above all.

## Light

Do not bring light. Flame gets smothered by moths. An electric lamp won't go out, but it will get you a face full of moths, followed by the bats, birds, and centipedes that eat them. The fog would throw the beam back at you anyway, and the basalt returns nothing.

- **Tape over everything that glows.** That includes indicator LEDs, watch dials, and screens.
- **Carry one dim, deep-red light.** Use it only cupped in your hands, for a wound you cannot assess by touch.
- **A light is also bad manners.** It brings the swarm down on everyone's route.
- **Do not trust what your eyes show you.** In true dark they invent shapes and glimmers, and you should not steer by them.

## Clothing and protection

Dress for abrasion, not weather. The air is warm and soaking, and you will not be dry until you leave.

- **Boots:** Wear stiff, thick-soled leather boots and expect the clinker to visibly destroy them. Bring strong tape or glue for repairs.
- **Shins:** Wear tall gaiters or shin guards. When the glass crust breaks, your leg drops through a ring of broken edges.
- **Legs and arms:** Wear tough long trousers, long sleeves with closed cuffs, and knee pads. A fall onto clinker with bare hands and knees is a flaying.
- **Gloves:** Bring two pairs of durable work gloves. They should be thin enough at the fingertips to read knots, or have one fingertip cut free. Never grab a fern trunk bare-handed, because the hand-length centipedes hunt there.
- **Head:** Wear a helmet, for branches at face height, falling trees, and the slip you won't see coming. Add a head net over a brim. It keeps things off your lips and out of your nose.
- **Eyes:** Wear sealed goggles. A twig in the eye is the likeliest injury when pushing blind through growth. They will fog instantly, but there is nothing to see.
- **Ears:** Wear filtered earplugs, the kind musicians use. They take the edge off the frogs and the dusk flight while keeping pitch readable, and they keep insects out of your ears.
- **Dry kit:** Keep dry socks and a dry shirt in a sealed bag on the ridge.

## Water and heat

The valley will not give you a drink. A leaf cup holds one mouthful, with tadpoles and mosquito larvae in it. It is also some frog's entire nursery, so leave it alone.

- **Carry everything you need.** Take at least four to five liters for a night's work, with salts. You will sweat hard and it will not cool you.
- **Use several tough containers.** One bladder is one puncture away from no water.
- **Carry a small sheet.** If real rain comes, a sheet held open over a bottle is your only chance to catch it before it goes into the rock.
- **Keep saline for wounds separate.** Then you never have to choose between drinking and cleaning a cut.

## Finding your way

**Lines**

- **Follow the cord lines with one hand.** They are the best information available, but they are older than the ground. The line that says *straight on* may now end over a hole that opened last season.
- **Probe every step anyway.** Use a long pole before you put weight down.
- **Watch for changes in the line.** One that goes slack, changes angle, or hangs free means the ground has changed since it was strung. Stop there.
- **Lay your own line if you leave the marked routes.** Pay out thin cord from a reel as you go, because the growth closes behind you and you will not retrace your steps without it. Make it feel unlike local cord (flat tape, say). Take it out when you leave, so it doesn't lie to someone later.

**Reading the ground**

- **Thickening forest means a hole is near.** The growth is fed from the skylights, so it is rankest around them. If pushing gets harder, the roots get denser, and you smell ammonia, you are close to an edge.
- **Thin moss and fern means you are between holes.** You are still on a roof.
- **Anything warm or dry is a warning.** Warm rock under your palm, or brittle and crumbling plants, mean steam ground. Check the heat with a hand before your boots or your seat.
- **Air moving steadily past your face is going to or from a hole.**
- **The notes are landmarks.** A hum in your breastbone means a big opening. Infrasound also produces dread and a sense of presence. Expect that feeling, and read it as a sign of distance to a big hole.

**Moving**

- **Keep one hand on the line and a forearm or pole ahead.** Test each foot before you weight it.
- **On glass, go slowly.** Spread your weight where you can.
- **Do not use trees as anchors.** They hold on by cracks and tip constantly, so don't haul on them or hang anything from them.
- **Stay in touch with your companion physically.** Voices are useless, so agree on touch and tug signals. A short rope between you gives you signals and some chance if one of you breaks through. It will snag, though, and sharp rock can cut a loaded rope.

## Air

- **Falling pressure makes the holes exhale.** The wind off them is warm ammonia and guano dust, bad for eyes and lungs. A barometer that vibrates is worth having. So is a respirator with ammonia cartridges if you plan to work near skylights. Otherwise move off, across the wind.
- **Heavy weather makes the flow inhale.** The air near the holes is then cleaner, but everything loose is being drawn toward the edge.
- **Do not go into the tubes.**
- **Do not rest in hollows or beside vents.** The struck-flint smell suggests volcanic gas, which pools low.

## Animals

Nothing here is hunting you, but everything will touch you. Things will land on your neck and mouth, and ants will pour over your boots and move on. Flailing in the dark is how people get hurt, so let them pass.

- **Mosquitoes:** They breed in every leaf cup. Use treated clothing and the head net, and assume they carry something.
- **Birds and bats:** Stay away from skylight rims at dusk and before dawn. The flight lasts most of an hour, and the air is full enough of bodies to rock the ferns and knock you off balance. Droppings will rain on you, so keep wounds covered.
- **Crabs:** In season they form a carpet a kilometer wide. You cannot sit or put anything down, and the footing on glass gets worse. Their legs sound exactly like rain, so check for rain with your skin.
- **Food:** Eat on the ridge, or from wrappers without touching the food. Your hands will be filthy.

## Timing and camp

- **Sleep on the ridge, behind the crest.** The locals build nothing on the flow for good reason. There is no flat, cool, or stable place to lie, and nothing to anchor a shelter to.
- **Use the birds as your clock.** There are no stars. The gravel roar at dusk means night has begun. When the notes thicken and choke, the birds are returning and you should already be heading out.
- **Set a vibrating turnaround alarm.** Obey it when it goes off.
- **Do not expect daylight.** Wet-slate grey may be as bright as it gets.

## If it goes wrong

- **Getting hurt:** Lava cuts are deep and dirty, and they infect fast in wet heat. Carry irrigation, antiseptic, closure strips, and tape that holds on wet skin. Be able to use all of it by feel.
- **Getting lost:** Stop, stand still, and listen for the notes you know. Find the line with your hands. Do not walk in search of it.
- **Hearing a tree go:** The long tearing of climbers comes first. You cannot dodge blind, so crouch, cover your head, and wait for the thud.
- **Panic:** This is the real danger, because running here ends in a hole or on the clinker. If it rises, stand still and wait for it to pass.

## What it will be like

It will be loud, close, and warm, and you will be crawled on. You will move from one note toward the next with a cord in your fingers. If you prepare well, the reward is the dusk flight heard from a safe distance, the ground humming in your chest, and a forest you know entirely by touch. Take your line and everything else out with you when you go.
