# Preparing for the Saddle

You are packing for two places that sit one step apart: a cold, high mountain and a warm, wet floor that buries whatever stays on it. Most of the preparation follows from that, and from the fact that the ground is younger than your boots and still arriving.

## Getting there

- **The approach is an alpine climb.** The Saddle is a day's climb from any other warmth, and frost comes every night. You need real cold-weather gear, time to adjust to the altitude, and footwear for loose scree.
- **Come in from above.** The open sides end in an overhanging lip above a drop into cloud, so your way in is down the flank of one of the peaks, across the scree to the frost line.
- **Arrive in the morning.** That is the only clear part of the day. You need those hours to learn the layout before the cloud climbs in at midday, above all where the lip is.

## Crossing the frost line

The floor is body-warm, the air is saturated, and the rain lands warm. You will be soaked for your whole stay and nothing will dry. A pace uphill, it is freezing.

- **Change at the line.** Crossing the frost line in wet clothes after dark is the easiest way to get hypothermia here. Keep a full dry set of clothes in a sealed bag on the cold side and change when you cross.
- **Dress light on the floor.** Wear quick-drying layers and expect to be wet anyway.
- **Look after your feet.** They will spend the day in a finger's depth of warm water. Bring several pairs of socks and dry your feet completely each night.

## Rules of the floor

**1. Do as the frogs do.** The stone skins over whatever rests on it.

- At a fingernail's thickness a day it will not trap you, but anything left for an hour will stick, and anything left overnight is sealed in. A dropped tent peg or lens cap becomes a smooth bump by the end of the week, like the dead frogs.
- Shift your seat pad every so often. Keep your pack on your back or hung up, and tether small items.
- You cannot stake anything. There is not a crack or a pinch of soil on the whole floor.

**2. Clean up before dusk.** The day's deposit hardens all at once at dusk, with the rattle, so that is your deadline.

- The goats' hooves are glazed white, and your boots will go the same way. Glaze fills the tread, and smooth soles on wet stone with algal film will put you on your back.
- Scrub your soles with a stiff brush every evening. Rinse the paste from buckles, zips and pole joints while it is still soft.

**3. Never walk with the water.** The pools move overnight, so they are useless as landmarks, but the sheets all end up going over the lip.

- In cloud, with noon as dim as late evening, walking downstream takes you to the edge.
- Steer by the things that stay put: the hut, the gauge post, the bench back and the nest chimneys.
- The shadows will mislead you because they lean toward the nearest mast, whatever the sun is doing. They are no good for direction, but they do tell you where the nearest mast is when you cannot see it.

**4. Leave broken stone alone.** The edges will open a boot sole, and a hand more easily.

- Wear stout soles and bring gloves.
- Carry a first-aid kit that can handle a deep, clean cut a day from help.
- Don't break slabs yourself. If you must carry a shard, wrap it like a blade.

**Walk slowly.** Shuffle rather than stride, because the floor is covered in frogs that move a body-length at a time. Step around the foam rafts on the pools, which are their eggs.

## The masts

I can't tell you what the masts are, so my advice is to treat them the way the martins do: touch the edge of the dusk and leave again.

- **Assume you may slow too.** Rain and midges slow near a mast, and light slows or is taken. I would not assume your limbs, your pulse or your watch are exempt.
- **The stone grows fastest there.** The collar under each point shows it. You do not want to be slowed in the place where the floor rises quickest.
- **Don't touch a mast,** and put nothing in the gap beneath its point.
- **Know where the stakes lead.** The row of orange caps runs toward the nearest mast, so if you follow it in fog, know where it ends.
- **The surveyors left no explanation.** Whoever kept the gauge and set the stakes stopped, and nothing says why. It may mean nothing, but keep it in mind.

Do stand a few paces off during the late rain, though. Reach into the edge of the dusk and pick a drop out of the air, then step back. If you have a companion, compare watches afterwards.

## The lip

The lip is a shelf of thin daily layers pushed out over nothing, with water sliding across it and no rail. Nobody knows how much weight it holds. The real edge of the mountain is buried somewhere behind it, and you cannot tell where.

- **Stay well back.** Go no nearer in cloud than you would in the clear.
- **Look at it from the side.** The best view is from the old rock on the flank of a peak. From there you can see the layers stacked like pages and the dripstone hanging beneath, without standing on it.

## Where to sleep

**Sleep on the old rock.** Camp a little above the frost line, in the lee of a boulder, with a proper cold-weather bag. Anything pitched on the warm floor will be bonded to it by morning, and a wandering pool may move in with you during the night.

Up there you also get the best of the night. The cloud drops below the lip, the sky clears, and the fog stands in a wall at the boundary just below you, with the frogs calling behind it.

**Don't count on the hut.** The floor has risen to its windowsills, so the door is half buried and the inside is either a pit or a pool. Martins nest under the eaves, and the three ferns in the gutter are the only rooted plants on the Saddle. Leave both alone.

## Water, food and waste

- **Water.** The floor water carries whatever the stone is made of, along with algae, frog foam and midge larvae. The goats lick the fresh layer and thrive, but I would still not drink it. Melt frost from the old rock, or catch rain on a tarp out in the open before it touches the floor.
- **Food.** Carry all of it. Nothing here is forage.
- **Waste.** There is no soil to dig in, and whatever you leave on the floor is kept forever. Pack everything out.
- **Salt.** The goats come for it and will not mind where it comes from. Keep sweaty straps and boots out of their reach, and relieve yourself well away from camp.
- **The stoat.** It eats its kills on the old rock, which is where you will be sleeping. Keep your food sealed.

## Noise, midges and storms

- **Noise.** Thousands of frogs with voices like struck pipes call all night, over martins, hooves and hissing water. Bring earplugs for sleeping and agree hand signals with any companion.
- **Midges.** The afternoon columns are thick enough to lean on. A head net and close-fitting glasses will spare you from breathing them.
- **Thunder.** Storms come most afternoons, and you will be in a high pass, standing in a sheet of mineral water among five tall masts. I don't know how lightning behaves around the masts. When it starts, I would get off the wet floor, away from the masts and the lip, and sit low among the boulders on the old rock until the peaks have finished passing the thunder back and forth.

## A day there

If you can, stay one full day and night so that you see the whole schedule.

- **Morning.** Watch the frost margin steam and the goats stand at it. Walk the perimeter while you can see, and fix the lip in your mind.
- **Midday.** Stay near the fixed landmarks as the cloud fills the saddle and the midges rise.
- **Late afternoon.** Watch the slow rain at the edge of a mast's dusk.
- **Dusk.** Have your boots scrubbed and your gear off the floor. Then stand still and listen as the ticking runs together into a rattle that sweeps from mast to mast. The frogs stop for it and then start again.
- **Night.** Cross the line, change into dry clothes, and watch the fog wall from above.

## Packing list

- Alpine layers and a cold-rated sleeping system
- Light, quick-drying clothes and several pairs of socks
- Dry bags, including one for the change of clothes at the line
- Stout boots with good wet grip, and a stiff brush
- Gloves
- A headlamp for the noon dusk
- A head net and close-fitting glasses
- Earplugs
- A tarp and a stove for collecting and melting water
- Waste bags
- A first-aid kit that can handle deep cuts
- Tethers for small items
- A simple watch

## What to expect

Everything you are told about the place will be out of date, including this. The floor rises by something like a forearm's length a year. By the time you arrive, the orange caps may be gone, the bench back will be lower, the lip will be further out, and the last notch on the gauge post may be under stone. The floor you stand on will be buried within a season of your leaving.

It is nonetheless a welcoming place. The ground is warm after a cold climb, and it is crowded with animals that came for the same reason you did. Like them, you can stay as long as you keep moving.
