# Preparing for the Singing Plateau

Nothing here is hunting you, and the air is warm and breathable. The danger is that you will be deaf in every practical sense, standing on polished stone in a wind that never stops, in a landscape that rearranges itself every few hours. Prepare for three things: the sound, the wind, and the fact that nothing stays where you saw it last.

## The sound

- **Protect your ears in layers.** Wear foam plugs under earmuffs and carry spare plugs, because the wind will take some. The high shriek in a westerly is the part that does permanent damage.
- **Nothing blocks the low notes.** They come through your chest and the soles of your feet. Expect a faint, sourceless dread, queasiness, pressure behind the eyes and a short temper. Bring nausea remedies and eat by the clock, since your appetite will go.
- **Don't deafen yourself completely.** The slide of the chord is your gust warning, and it still reaches you through protection. Spend your first hour sitting somewhere safe and learning it: a deep, steady chord is a southerly, the shriek coming forward is a westerly, and a slide means bracing now.
- **Sound is local.** If one pipe near you is unbearable, move twenty metres.
- **The quiet rooms will trip you.** Stepping into one feels like a missing stair, and people stumble. Stepping back out is worse.
- **Keep the first visit short.** Two or three days is enough. You will be more tired than the walking explains, and quiet will feel strange for days afterward.

## Communicating

Nobody has ever shouted here, and you won't either. Agree on everything before you arrive.

- **Signals:** Use hand signs by day, a touch code (squeezes on the arm) in cloud, and a signal mirror at distance. At night, use a light.
- **Writing:** Use a wrist slate or a grease pencil on laminated card. Paper will not survive.
- **Devices:** Set them to vibrate. You will never hear an alarm.
- **Lost-person rule:** Go to the lee of the nearest tor, stay there and flash. In thick cloud, rope together or stay within arm's reach, because a separated companion cannot call out.
- **Look more than feels natural.** Your ears normally tell you what is behind you, and here they can't. Nobody will hear a fall, a rolling mat or an approaching storm, so check upwind constantly.

## Wind, clothing and gear

It is warm, so you don't need to dress for a cold mountain. The problems are wind and sideways wet.

- **Clothing:** Wear close-fitting, quick-drying layers and a snug windshell. Nothing should flap, so leave out ponchos and brimmed hats, and tape down strap ends or they will whip your face for days.
- **Eyes and face:** Wear sealed goggles and a face wrap. Cloud and sand travel at ankle height, which is face height whenever you sit or lie down.
- **Feet:** Wear sticky-rubber shoes that drain, and gaiters. If you bring poles, fit rubber tips, since metal skates on polished quartzite.
- **Tethers:** Put a lanyard on everything, including gloves, bottle, mirror and slate. Anything you set down has left.
- **Sun and skin:** At three thousand metres, the sun switching on and off over pale rock burns faster than it feels. The wind dries lips and eyes even in the damp, so bring sunscreen, lip balm and eye drops.
- **Air:** The altitude is mild, but walking into the wind doubles the effort. An hour's walk between tors is a downwind figure. The wind also carries the whole jungle's pollen and spores, so bring antihistamines if you are prone.

## The ground

- **Footing:** The quartzite is polished and often wet, and it is full of slots and potholes below knee height. Ankle-high cloud hides them. Walk on bare pink stone where you can see it.
- **Stay off the mats.** They are rugs on a polished floor and may cover holes. They can also start moving with you aboard.
- **Treat rolling mats as traffic.** A sodden roll a hand thick weighs as much as a small vehicle. Look upwind before crossing open stone, and remember that after a slide the traffic reverses.
- **Avoid the green drifts.** Never rest against one. A ten-metre pile peels off in sheets without notice.
- **Sinkholes:** Approach them only in clear air, low to the ground, and never with loose mats upwind of you. Whatever goes in stays in.
- **The rim:** Go only when the wind is in your face, which means it is pushing you back onto the table. Stay low and anchored, and leave when the chord slides. A swing can put the wind at your back with three thousand metres of drop in front of you.
- **Pipes:** Keep your hands out of them. The sounding ones are full of scouring sand, and the silent ones may be nests.

## Navigation

- **Ignore the green.** Its arrangement at noon is gone by mid-afternoon.
- **Use what is fixed.** That means the tors, the sinkholes, the cairn line and the leather-leaved plants rooted in cracks. The tors look alike, so sketch each one's profile from several sides.
- **Wind is not a compass.** It swings a dozen times a day. A compass may work, but test it against the tors before trusting it.
- **Don't trust the cairns blindly.** They lead toward the rim, which probably means the route people use to come and go. It is not necessarily a route you can use.
- **Mark your exit point** by its relation to tors and cracks. Anything you leave on the surface will blow away.
- **Don't travel at night.** In the dark you can neither see nor hear what is rolling toward you.

## Water, food and fire

- **Water:** Carry in at least your first day's supply. After that there is plenty if you know the trick: a squeezed fistful of mat runs brown water, and the finger-deep pools last about an hour. It is tannin tea that things live in, so filter and treat it. A square of fine mesh hung in the cloud stream will drip steadily.
- **Food:** Bring all of it. Nothing here is known to be edible, and the gardens are not yours.
- **Fire:** Use a stove with a full windscreen inside a ring-wall. Dry mat fibre may burn, but don't count on it.

## Shelter and sleep

- **Don't bring a tent.** There is no soil to stake into, and it would act as a sail. Use a low bivvy bag, anchored into cracks or weighted with slabs.
- **Use a thick sleeping pad.** The rock is pleasantly warm, but with your head on bare stone the chord arrives through your skull.
- **The ring-walls are the best shelter.** Each one works in only one wind. When the wind turns, the quiet slides away, and the shelter may now sit on the tor's windward side, which is where the drifts pile. Wake up at every slide and check.
- **Expect broken sleep.** Bring an eye mask, since sun and cloud strobe all day, and keep your goggles on.

## Animals

- **The toads** are the black, thumbnail-sized things rolling past your boots. Don't handle them. Small, dark, slow animals that take no trouble to hide are often poisonous.
- **The birds** signal with a white flash under the wing. Watch them, because they read the wind better than you do, and leave the silted pipes alone.
- **The thing in the mats** is unknown. If a roll bulges or creeps against the wind, something large and wet is inside it. Give it wide room, never investigate the open end, and don't camp near parked rolls. Seal your food, since your smell carries a long way downwind.

## The people and their works

Someone tuned this place by hand, and the thumbprints are still in the clay.

- **Leave every plug alone.** Don't pull any, add any, or wedge gear into pipes near a shelter. One altered flute can destroy a quiet room that took generations to find.
- **Don't drain the fog-net bowls** except to save a life. Leave the tethered gardens tied, and leave the mica disc where it hangs.
- **Use shelters lightly.** Leave the hearths and cairns as you found them, and build no cairns of your own.
- **Ask why they may be absent.** They may not be here this season, and there may be a reason. Look at how fresh the clay and the grass wadding are.
- **If you meet them,** remember they cannot hear you coming. Appearing behind someone is alarming or worse. Approach from the front across open stone with your hands empty and visible. Flashing is their language and you don't know the code, so show your mirror but don't flash at random.

## Lightning

You are the tallest thing between tors on a bare table three thousand metres up, and the thunder never arrives. You cannot count seconds or hear a storm coming, so you have to watch for it.

- If the flicker over the lowlands grows brighter, or lies in the direction the wind is coming from, get off the tors.
- Crouch in a basin away from the tors and the rim.
- If your skin tingles or your hair lifts, the strike is close. Get as low as you can at once.

## When you leave

When the chord slides and forty kilometres of green lifts an edge and turns around, sit down, hold on to something rooted and watch. That sight is the reason to come, and it is also the moment the place is most dangerous.

Leave with the wind in a quarter you know, by the route you marked. Take nothing from the plateau, and leave nothing in a pipe.
