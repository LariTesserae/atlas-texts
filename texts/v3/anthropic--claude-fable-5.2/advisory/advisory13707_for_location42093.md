# Preparing for the plateau sea

Almost everything here follows from the fact that nothing varies. The sound, the cold, the current and the light are the same at every hour, so there is no calm spell or quiet season to wait for. You adapt to the place completely, and you bring everything you need.

## The sound

This is the main hazard, and ordinary hearing protection only half solves it.

- **Protect your ears the whole time you are outside a lane.** Wear fitted plugs under heavy earmuffs and carry spares of both. Even at the back scarp, 600 km from the lip, the level will damage hearing over days.
- **Earplugs don't stop it reaching your body.** The low tone comes in through your chest, skull and the hull under you. Expect nausea, a pressed feeling in the ribs, fatigue and clumsy hands. Near the lip your eyes will shake in their sockets enough that you can't read a gauge.
- **Noise-cancelling headsets help in the far basin.** A single unvarying pitch is the ideal case for them. Closer to the lip they will be overwhelmed, so don't rely on them.
- **Ration your exposure.** Count hours outside the lanes and keep them few. Treat time near the lip as minutes.
- **See a dentist and an audiologist before you go.** Loose fillings and crowns will not survive the lip. A baseline hearing test lets you check afterward what the trip cost.
- **Don't go with certain conditions.** I would stay away if you are pregnant or have inner-ear trouble, a heart condition or a history of retinal problems.

You will not be able to talk except in a lane. Learn a small set of hand signs with your companion, carry slates and grease pencils, and agree on rope-tug signals. Make every plan inside a lane and write it down before you leave it.

Audible alarms are useless. The builders put a horn on the turbine hall, and no one has ever heard it. Your watch alarm, gas detector and depth sounder need to flash or buzz against your skin, and near the lip even a buzz will be lost. Nothing will warn you by sound either. You won't hear a person falling overboard, a failing engine or the rotors turning in the hall.

Vibration also works on your equipment. Use thread-locker or lock-wire on every fastener, tape lids, and check bolts daily. Expect cameras to blur and delicate electronics to fail early.

## The lanes

The lanes are a few metres wide, and they are the only places you can rest. Sleep, eat, think, talk and treat injuries there, like everything else alive here.

- **Finding one.** Look for a glass-smooth stripe on the stippled water, a single file of white birds, or a grey thread of amphipods hanging below. Your ears will confirm it: the pressure drops away and you hear your own pulse.
- **The first entry is disorienting.** Your ears will ring, you may feel dizzy, and some people become tearful. Sit down for it.
- **Staying in one.** The lanes are perfectly straight, so once you are in, hold your bearing. Check your compass against the lane, though, because basalt and iron caissons can both pull a needle. If you drift out, the wall of noise returns within an arm's length, and that is the only reliable alarm in the basin.
- **Sleeping.** Sleep in the gauge-houses, or in your boat tied fore and aft to a station's legs. Don't sleep adrift. The current runs toward the lip, and you would wake in the noise somewhere unknown.
- **Stages.** Stations stand every 50 km along the longest null, which is a full day in a small boat. Don't travel at night. The lamps are too far apart to see from one to the next, and you can't see the stripe in the dark.

## The cold

The water is one degree above freezing, and it is the same everywhere.

- **Wear a drysuit with insulation whenever you are on the water.** If you fall in unprotected, you have a few minutes of useful movement.
- **Stay tethered to the boat at all times.** No one will hear you shout. Wear a strobe on your flotation.
- **Avoid going overboard outside a lane.** Water carries the sound into the body far better than air does. It stuns the amphipods that drift out, and I would expect it to stun you. Something long and ribbon-shaped patrols the lane edges for stunned things. It is probably scaled to thumb-sized prey, but keep your hands inboard anyway.
- **The air is cold too.** It will sit near freezing, damp and windless. Bring layers, a real sleeping system, a stove and all your fuel, because nothing here burns.
- **Allow for altitude.** At 3,000 metres you should arrive acclimatised, or the altitude headache will blur into the sound sickness. Water boils cooler and cooking takes longer. The overcast still burns skin at this height.

## The edges

Nothing here has been worn smooth. The scarps carry a glassy rind, and the floor is sharp-edged columns and sheet obsidian.

- **Use a rigid hull.** An inflatable will be slashed. I would choose wood or thick plastic over metal, because a metal hull will ring like a drum outside the lanes.
- **Protect your hands, feet and knees.** Bring cut-resistant gloves you can wear over warm liners, thick-soled boots and knee protection.
- **Guard your ropes.** Any line that moves against this rock will part. Use chafe guards, or chain where it touches stone. The builders' cable is unmarked only because it has never moved.
- **Carry a wound kit.** Obsidian cuts are deep and clean, and cold hands don't feel them. Bring closure strips, haemostatic dressings and plenty of bandage, and inspect your hands when you stop.
- **Don't plan to land on the scarps.** There is no beach, only an ice collar a hand wide and then broken glass. If you can choose your arrival point, arrive on a gauge-house platform in the long null.

## The lip

The current is gentle across most of the basin, but it must quicken toward the edge. A boat that loses power anywhere will eventually go over.

- **Carry two independent means of propulsion.**
- **Decide your turnaround point in advance.** Fix the point beyond which you will not take the boat. Write it down in a lane while you can still think.
- **Approach the turbine hall only along whatever fixed route the builders left.** Stay clipped to it.
- **Be careful inside the hall.** The rotors turn unguarded and in silence. The cable and lamps are live at an unknown voltage. The iron is wet and possibly iced from the cloud.
- **Stay only briefly.** You will not see well or think well there.

If you do get there, check your watch and put a hand on the handrail at the top of the hour. The horn shows up as a second, brief tremor under the first. That is the only way anyone has ever perceived it.

## What you owe the place

The whole living system is a film thinner than paint, a few threads of amphipods, and crossings a few square metres in size, forty kilometres apart. You will be sharing the lanes with all of it.

- **Move slowly in the lanes.** Use oars or a guarded, slow electric drive. An open propeller would mince a column of amphipods.
- **Give the birds time.** They fly only along the line, so they cannot step aside for you. Let them lift and resettle.
- **Leave the crossings alone.** Don't anchor at them, touch the vent film, or fish. Bring all your food.
- **Arrive clean.** Nothing has ever rooted in this water. Scrub and sterilise the hull and gear, so you don't bring in a smear of algae or even sand.
- **Carry out all waste.** Spill nothing.
- **Treat the water before drinking it.** It is probably drinkable, but it rises through sulphur and iron vents, so test or filter it.
- **Leave the stations as you find them.** Don't draw on their power, stop the pens, take the rolls or burn the paper. Read the labels if you like.

## Your own state of mind

The light is flat and shadowless, and the water is so clear that the boat seems to hang in air above the pavement. You get one tone, one temperature and a day of columns between anything else. Your sense of time will go slack.

- **Keep a clock and a written log.** At some point the builders' labelling stops, and I can't tell you why.
- **Fix your departure date before you arrive.** Nothing here will ever tell you it is time to go.
- **Expect an aftermath.** You will probably hear the tone for days afterward, and ordinary places will seem jittery for a while. Have your hearing tested once you are home.
