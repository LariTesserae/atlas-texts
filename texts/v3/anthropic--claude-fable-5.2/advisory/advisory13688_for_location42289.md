# Preparing for the Frozen-Wall Hall

## What you are walking into

This is a working construction site with no one visibly working it. It is also a cold store, a drain, and a very thin garden. Expect a four-mile round trip on wet concrete in fog and near-darkness, next to a wall of ice holding back what I take to be an estuary or sea bed (the mussel shells, the boat rib and the green light all point that way).

Nothing here is hostile. The dangers are those of any industrial site: cold, wet, noise, bad footing, chemistry and machinery that does not know you are there. The place runs to a schedule, so learn the schedule and you will be fine.

Allow five or six hours and tell someone topside when to expect you. Aim to be on the floor before midday, because the green glow lasts only about an hour.

## Clothing

Dress for a damp cold store, not a cave.

- **Layers:** wool or synthetic that stays warm when wet, with no cotton. It is coldest along the north face and almost mild at the curing end, so you will be adjusting.
- **Shell:** a fully waterproof hooded jacket and trousers. The fog soaks you and the rain after each thaw comes in big, cold drops.
- **Boots:** knee-high, waterproof, with a toe cap and a sole that grips wet algae. The green circles under the lamps are the slickest footing in the hall.
- **Gloves:** a waterproof insulated pair and a dry spare. The channel edges will cut a bare hand that catches a fall.
- **Hard hat:** partly for the bolt-head rain, mostly because this is someone's job site.
- **Reflective bands:** whoever moves the skip should be able to see you.

## Light

There is roughly fifty paces of near-dark between each lamp's ball of fog. The floor shows only as a wet shine, and the channels are black water in black concrete.

- Carry a main light and a backup. A warm-coloured beam held at waist height works better than a headlamp, which only lights the fog in front of your face.
- Do not stare at the arcs. They are bare carbon arcs and hard on the eyes.
- Do not touch or climb the pylons. They are old electrical gear on a wet floor.

## Footing

The launders are the hazard most likely to actually hurt you. They are a forearm wide, of unknown depth, sharp-edged and laid in a grid, so you will cross dozens of them.

- Cross each one deliberately, in light.
- Carry a staff or trekking pole and probe ahead in the dark stretches.
- After a thaw the channels fill to the lip with slush and look exactly like floor. Do not walk then.

## Noise

The pumps and compressors are constant, and you will feel the compressors in your chest. The water hammer is a different matter. The first time it goes through the mains you will believe something has failed. It hasn't, and you can listen to the blow travel away down the pipe.

Wear moderate hearing protection, but nothing that blocks the siren. Voices won't carry, so agree on light signals with any companion.

## The weather cycle

The cycle is the one event in the hall you need to plan around.

1. **The siren.** It sounds two notes, the second coming back flat from the far end. You then have a few minutes.
2. **Move.** Get well back from the north face, a good deal farther than the frost is tall, because forty-foot curtains of ice break and scatter when they land. You won't know which bank is about to shed, so assume it is the one beside you.
3. **Copy the wagtail.** Walk without hurry to the lee side of a column, stand clear of the channels and wait. The bird has survived every cycle so far.
4. **Stay put through the fog.** When the nearest lamp is a smear, you can't navigate and the launders are brimming and invisible. Have a foam pad to sit on and a hot flask.
5. **Use the rain's pattern.** It falls only from the bolt heads, in straight rows a yard apart, so you can walk the dry lanes between them.

Time the interval between the first two sirens you hear, since the schedule is regular and you can plan the rest of the walk around it.

## The north face

It looks soft, even upholstered, and you will want to put a hand on it. Resist that.

- The brine pipes are far below freezing. Bare skin will stick or burn.
- Look into the bottle-glass windows as long as you like between cycles: the gravel, the shells, the boat rib.
- Do not chip, pry or take souvenirs. This wall is all that keeps the water out of the hall.

For the midday glow, stand in a dark interval on footing you have checked, switch off your light and give your eyes five minutes. The green band near the springing of the arch is daylight coming down through the water and ice.

## Air and water

- **Ammonia:** a faint whiff near the compressor houses is normal. If it turns sharp and your eyes or throat sting, that is a leak. Walk away from the sheds toward the shaft. Carry sealed goggles and a small escape respirator with an ammonia-rated filter.
- **Launder water:** do not drink it or put your hands in it. It is ice-cold and probably carries brine and lime from the cement. Bring your own water and food.
- **The living things:** the midges do not seem to bite. Leave the eels alone.

## Finding your way

The hall is one straight line and the floor is numbered. Note the bay number at the foot of the shaft when you arrive. Numbers climb toward the east, so falling numbers lead home. The white wall is north. In fog, crouch and read the stamp at your feet.

## The east end

The curing concrete is the only warm surface in two miles, and you will want to linger. Take care here.

- The steam bank lies low and hides channels, rails and the ends of the tied steel.
- Fresh concrete is caustic. Touch the cured-but-warm surface if you like, then rinse your hand. Do not sit on it, and do not step on anything greenish and damp.
- Stay off the rail. The skip is ticking, which means it was filled recently and by someone.
- Keep off the oiled formwork and do not brush the chalk tally. The last stroke will come off on your sleeve, and it is someone's count.
- The newest brine pipes have only begun to whiten. I would guess the freeze is youngest here, so do not loiter by the north side at this end.

If you do meet whoever is building the place, remember you are in their way. Step aside and let them work.

## Courtesies

- **The moss circles:** walk around them. The ferns took years, and the circles are the only gardens here.
- **The wagtail:** do not feed or follow it. It appears to be the only one.
- **Your boots:** clean them before you descend and leave nothing behind.

## When to leave early

You can trust the noise, and the warning sign is its absence. Turn for the shaft at a steady walk if:

- the long strokes of the pumps stop,
- the compressor beat stops,
- the launders rise with no siren, or
- the frost face weeps outside the schedule.

The frozen ground will hold for a good while, but the water in the channels will rise much sooner.

## Small kit

- Plasters and wound dressing for skinned knuckles and shins
- Clean water for rinsing eyes or cement off skin
- Hand warmers and spare socks
- A watch
- A sealed bag for any camera, since lenses fog badly between the cold face and the warm end

Keep some strength in reserve, because the way in was down and the climb out comes at the end of the day.
