Prepare for a cold, wet, high-altitude solo trek with no possibility of rescue, in a place where every errand costs exactly one day, sharp sounds don't survive, and nothing you make stays new. The plateau isn't hostile, but it is completely indifferent to hurry, and most of the preparation is about accepting its terms. Where the description runs out, the rest is my reading of it.

## The body

- **Altitude:** At four thousand metres you get roughly 60% of the oxygen you're used to, and you arrive all at once rather than climbing. Acclimatize beforehand with a week or more at 2,500–3,500 m. Carry altitude medication and know the signs of serious trouble: a wet cough, breathlessness at rest, stumbling, confusion. The usual cure is descent, and the rim is below the horizon on every side, so if symptoms worsen, leave by whatever brought you.
- **Wet cold:** The temperature sits at freezing and the fog soaks you twice a day, usually with wind behind it. This is more dangerous than dry deep cold. Wear wool or synthetics, never cotton, and avoid down unless it is well protected. Bring a real waterproof shell, waterproof boots, gaiters, and several pairs of socks in dry bags. Guard one full set of dry clothes for sleeping and never walk in it.
- **Footing:** Wet, lichened gneiss is slick, and at dawn it may be glazed with ice. The peat between the whalebacks is of unknown depth. Stay on rock and causeway, and bring trekking poles and light traction for your boots.
- **Sun:** Thin air and high cloud still burn. Bring sunglasses and sunscreen.
- **Pollen:** It travels wet in the fog, so you'll be breathing it. Bring antihistamines if you're prone.

## What to carry

- **Food:** Bring all of it. There is nothing to hunt, and I wouldn't experiment with the plants. Altitude kills appetite, so pack dense food you like. Water boils at about 86°C up there, so bring things that rehydrate rather than things that need real cooking.
- **Water:** It is everywhere near the fences. The cistern water is combed fog and probably the cleanest you'll ever drink, and the basin water is peat tea. Filter both anyway. Further in, carry your own (see below).
- **Stove and fuel:** Bring a stove that works cold and high. The hearths burn peat and are warm when you arrive, but the stacks are someone's labor.
- **Shelter:** Carry a bivouac bag and sleeping gear rated well below freezing, in case dusk finds you between doors.
- **Light:** Bring a headlamp. Nights under permanent cloud are black.
- **Alarms:** Anything that warns you by sound is compromised. A beep arrives with the edge rubbed off. Bring a vibrating watch and a carbon monoxide detector that flashes, because you'll be sleeping beside peat fires inside metre-thick turf.
- **Batteries:** Keep them against your body.
- **Waste:** Pack out everything, including your own. Nothing here eats or scavenges, and the cold peat preserves what is left in it.

## Moving

There are no short walks. The neighbor's door is a full day away, and so is the far rim. Your legs will be no more tired for a thousand kilometres than for one, but a dawn-to-dusk walk at altitude is still tiring, so train for long, slow days.

- **Leave at dawn, from a threshold.** The rule is stated dawn to dusk, and I wouldn't test what a noon departure buys you.
- **Expect to start and end in fog.** The dawn rising and the mid-afternoon one both reach roof height, so you'll be inside them. The causeway's two worn tracks are your handrail.
- **Navigate by doors.** Pace counting and dead reckoning are useless where distance and time have come apart. Don't trust a compass until you've checked it against the sun, which behaves. The standing stones in the squares are honest gnomons, and the fence lines run dead straight.
- **Don't navigate by the state of things.** A wall at one course on the way out will be at four on the way back, and lichened as if it always had been.
- **Don't cut or climb the nets.** They are the water supply.
- **Keep your way out on your person.** No point is ever more than a day from any other, which is a comfort, but an injury that stops you walking for a day stops everything.

## Company

If you travel with others, assume you'll lose sight of them on arrival and not see them again until you leave. Each presence is a day from every other, even in the direction of a turned head. Agree in advance on these points:

- **A rendezvous outside:** Fix a place and a date to meet beyond the plateau.
- **Notes:** Leave them on threshold slabs, and expect a day's lag at minimum.
- **No searching:** Two people walking toward each other will pass unseen.

This also means no one can come to your side if you're hurt. Carry a first-aid kit you can use one-handed, and make conservative decisions.

You'll still have constant evidence of company, such as a warm hearth, a rebuilt peat stack, or a mended net. I think this will feel less like haunting than like living among considerate neighbors on a different shift.

Don't try to catch anyone. Hiding by a hearth or spinning round fast will only wear you out, and it mistakes the place, because the absence is simply how the spacing works.

The rosette plants will fool you a hundred times a day, especially in fog. Let them, because bracing against it is more tiring than the mistake.

## Sound and wear

- **Speech:** A word loses its consonants within arm's length. You'll have no one to talk to anyway, but you'll notice it if you talk to yourself, and whistles and shouts are useless. Bring something to write on.
- **Repairs:** A fresh knot is already frayed at the pins, so do all your mending before you come. Field repairs will work, but they'll be old from the first minute, so overbuild them.
- **Markers:** Any cairn you raise will look as though it has always stood there, and you won't be able to tell it from the plateau's own. If you must mark, use an object from elsewhere, and take it with you.
- **Notes:** I'd expect pencil written there to look already thumbed. Write large.
- **Habits:** This is the consequence I'd watch most closely. By the second morning, your routine may feel years old, with the threshold fitting your foot and the path to the cistern carrying your tracks. It's pleasant, and it's how a visit turns into residence. Fix your departure date before you go, and write it on something made elsewhere.

## Being a guest

- **Peat:** Burn modestly, and cut and stack what you used.
- **Cisterns:** Put the slate lid back.
- **Tools:** Leave them where they were set down. Someone is in the middle of that length of cord.
- **Cushion plants:** They will take your weight, but they are probably older than the houses, so step around them when you can.
- **Contributing:** Learn to twist grass cord and tie a netting knot before you come, then mend a net. Yours will be one more hand among the neat and the impatient. I suspect that's the only greeting the place can receive.
- **The squares:** Fifty paces of paving around one stone seem to be how a city gathers when no two of its people can share a view, each alone in the same spot on a different day. Stand in one for a while. I think that counts as attending.

## Further in

Toward the middle, the infrastructure is proposed rather than delivered: fences where no fog rises yet, basins with no water. Carry full water and shelter there, and don't count on a roofed house or a warm hearth.

- **Scree aprons:** Stay off them. Rounded stones turn ankles, and I'd rather not sleep at the foot of a cliff that hasn't decided between gone and coming.
- **Frost rings:** Don't pitch camp inside a fresh one. It's a foundation, and something takes those up when no one is looking.
- **The last square:** Arrive by mid-afternoon and watch the fog come up through the joints and fill the shadow's groove first. Leave the unfinished kerb to the frost.
- **Stillness:** Lichen maps anything that has held still, and wear runs ahead of its causes here, so don't sit anywhere longer than you mean to.

## The mind

Nothing will announce itself as strange. The light is ordinary, the colors are true, and afternoon looks like afternoon. That is the hard part, because your mind will have nothing to blame.

What you'll have is a quiet made of processes: dripping nets, one low note from the cords, lids ticking as they warm. You'll have long walks through fog, and the feeling of being looked after by people you'll never see. Go rested and steady, and go for a fixed number of days.
