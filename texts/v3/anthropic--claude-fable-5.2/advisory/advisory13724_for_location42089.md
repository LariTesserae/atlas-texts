# Preparing for the cloud shelf

This is a warm, wet, silent place built on rock that cuts like broken bottles, and it is home to a small, isolated community that has never had a visitor. Nothing there is trying to hurt you. The risks come from the ground, from the damp, and from what you might bring with you.

## Before you leave: you are the hazard

Nothing lives on the shelf that didn't arrive as a spore, a runner, or in someone's hands. There are no flies, birds, or pond life, the crops are clones grown from cuttings, and the few hundred people have probably never met an outside illness. One cold, one seed in a boot tread, or one insect egg in a pack seam could do more damage than anything else you do.

- **Go only if you are well.** Isolate for two weeks beforehand, make sure your vaccinations are current, and postpone if you have so much as a sore throat.
- **Clean everything.** Scrub your boots, wash and inspect your gear, and empty every pocket, cuff, and Velcro strip of seeds and soil.
- **Bring only dead food.** Everything should be cooked, milled, or sealed, with no fresh fruit, whole grains, or raw nuts that could sprout.
- **Carry out all waste, including your own.** Soil there is made by hand and carried by the basketful, and you shouldn't add your gut flora to it.
- **Clean up again when you leave.** You will be carrying their spores to wherever you go next.

## The rock

Nothing underfoot has ever been rounded. A stumble on the clinker fields means lacerated palms, knees, and shins.

- **Boots:** Bring a stout pair with thick, stitched soles and toe protection, because glued soles come apart in constant damp and clinker chews through rubber quickly. Pack a spare pair and repair tape.
- **Clothing:** Wear leather-palmed gloves whenever you are off the causeways, and tough long trousers even though the warmth will make you want shorts.
- **Poles:** Use rubber tips only. Metal tips skate on wet basalt and would scratch slabs that generations of feet have polished.
- **First aid:** Build the kit around cuts. You need irrigation, closure strips, antiseptic, dressings that stick to wet skin, and fine tweezers with a magnifier for glass slivers. Make sure your tetanus shot is current, because wounds heal slowly and infect easily when skin never dries.

Other ground hazards are worth knowing:

- **Lava tubes:** The fern pits are collapsed tube roofs, which means there are roofs that haven't collapsed yet. Thin crust can sound hollow, and pit edges may be undercut and hidden by fronds.
- **Cracks:** The ones that swallow the water will also take an ankle.
- **Wet polish:** Glazed pavement and the smooth centres of the causeway slabs are slick.
- **The rim:** The ground stops with white cloud below and ahead, so the edge may not look like one. Stay well back, and never go near it in thick cloud or failing light.

It is a young volcano. If you smell sulphur or feel the ground shake, leave.

## The wet

Assume that nothing you bring will ever dry.

- **Clothing:** Wear as little as decency and the rock allow, in thin synthetics or light wool. Avoid cotton.
- **One dry set:** Keep sleeping clothes sealed in a dry bag with desiccant and never wear them outside your shelter.
- **Feet:** Take your boots off whenever you stop, air and powder your feet, and carry antifungal cream. Expect chafing and bring something for it.
- **Kit:** Seal electronics, since lenses will fog. Take waterproof paper and a pencil. Matches won't work, and leather and food will grow mould within days.

**Heat:** Air as warm as skin and nearly saturated means sweat won't cool you. Sitting still feels like nothing at all, but hard effort will overheat you quickly and without much warning. Move slowly, rest often, and drink with electrolytes. I would expect the townspeople to work at an unhurried pace for the same reason.

**Sunburn:** The white, sunless light still burns at that altitude, so wear a brimmed hat and sunscreen.

## Water, food, fire

There is water everywhere, but none of it is yours. Every drop in the channels and cisterns was earned by years of pecking stone. Don't drink from them, wash in them, or touch them unless you are plainly invited.

- **Water:** Collect your own. A tarp or a square of fine mesh hung in the moving cloud will run with water within hours, and overhangs drip steadily into a pot. Filter it anyway.
- **Food:** Bring all of it. The fields are small and hold no surplus for a stranger. If you are offered food, eat it only cooked, because broad-leaved root crops of this kind and many fiddleheads are harsh or toxic raw.
- **Fire:** Don't plan on one. The only fuel is the fern and shrub in the pits, and those are someone's thatch and soil. Bring a small sealed stove or eat cold.

## Shelter and sleep

There is no soft ground except the fern pits, and those are the town's gardens, so don't camp in them.

- **Shelter:** Bring a freestanding shelter or a tarp weighted with stones, since stakes won't go into basalt. Pitch it on smooth pavement well away from houses, fields, and the windward slabs.
- **Bedding:** Use a thick closed-cell foam mat, because inflatables will puncture. Nights are barely cooler than days, so a liner is enough.
- **Insects:** There are none, so you need no net.
- **Darkness:** With cloud overhead and no fires outdoors, night will be completely black. Be settled before dusk, bring a headlamp, and use it sparingly, because a moving light will be visible to everyone.

## Finding your way

There is no sun, the shadows are short and soft, and you can see about a mile. A compass may wander over fresh basalt. Navigate by the shape of the place instead:

- The dark cone is uphill and the rim is downhill.
- The slab rows mark the windward edge.
- The causeways run straight between houses.

Carry a watch, because the light won't tell you the time. Don't go alone. Cloud muffles a shout or a whistle, so keep something bright in your pack for signalling. There is no rescue.

## The town

Much of this is guesswork, because no one has visited before you. The houses stand out of earshot of one another, with eaves down to the ground. That suggests people who value distance and quiet, so I would keep both.

- **Arriving:** Come in slowly, in the open, by daylight. Stop where a causeway meets a platform and wait to be noticed. Don't step up uninvited or look under the eaves.
- **Noise:** Your gear will be the loudest thing on the shelf, so tape or pad anything that clinks. Speak low, and remember that sounds seem to come from farther away than they are.
- **Causeways:** They are one pace wide. If you meet someone, see what they do before you act, and be ready to be the one who steps off onto the clinker.
- **Things not to touch:** Don't walk on field walls, step over the palm-wide channel carelessly, kick grit into cisterns, or pick a leaf or fiddlehead. Leave the round stones on the platforms alone, since each one took someone a long time to make.
- **Metal:** Keep it out of sight at first. In a place where a groove takes years to peck with stone, a steel blade would change things. Decide before you go whether you want to be the person who introduces it.

## Gifts

Bring things that are sterile, inert, and impossible there.

- **Salt** is my first choice. A community living high on a mountain on roots and ferns is probably short of it.
- **River cobbles** would be my second. A few dense, water-rounded stones, boiled clean, would mean something where nothing has ever been rounded except by hand.

Offer gifts by setting them down and stepping back.

## Your own head

There is no birdsong, no wind noise, no visible sun, and no weather. The drip from the eaves keeps a steady count, and each day is the same as the last. Some people will find this restful and others will find it oppressive by the third day.

Fix your departure date before you arrive, because nothing there will tell you it's time to go. Keep the visit short, take only what you're offered, and leave no trace of yourself behind.
