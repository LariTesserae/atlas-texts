Nothing in this valley means you harm. The dangers are mostly ordinary ones (cold steel, thin air, glare, sharp edges, heights), plus three strange ones: the silence, the revisions, and the fact that you are the only thing here nobody drew.

## What it will be like

It is bright, cool and very gentle. You stand on a gantry looking down four kilometres of blue steel crowded with animals, and the loudest thing is feathers brushing feathers. Nothing is weathered and everything shines. The works is doing something vast at a pace you cannot see, and the animals live on top of it as they would on a mountain. It feels like walking around inside a drawing that is still being filled in.

## Body and gear

- **Altitude.** The air is thin, and the valley rises one storey every year, so any figure you were given is out of date. Acclimatise and pace yourself as for a high alpine walk.
- **Cold.** The furnaces give light but almost no heat, so you cannot warm yourself at them. Steel draws heat out of you faster than rock does. Bring thick insulated soles, warm layers and something to sit on. If you sleep, use a proper insulating mat and never lie on bare plate.
- **Glare.** Bring glacier-grade sunglasses with side shields and a spare pair. Every surface throws the light back, and the enamelled cooling tower is painful before noon. Treat your skin as you would on snow.
- **No night.** Bring a sleep mask and a watch, because the sun will not help you keep time. The morning sweat is the only reliable sign of dawn.
- **Edges.** Cut edges are still sharp, so wear cut-resistant gloves, long sleeves and tough trousers, and carry dressings. The gloves also keep bare skin off cold metal.
- **Footing.** The plate is wet and slick every dawn. Wear soft, grippy rubber soles rather than studs, and step over the moss seams, because that moss took years to grow.
- **Food and water.** Carry all your food and most of your water. The condensate is everything the valley has, and it is exactly enough for those already here. Don't wash in the cisterns, which have salamanders in them. Pack out everything, including your waste, since there is no soil to take it.

## The silence

Assume your voice will not work. Your throat will move and nothing will come out, which is more unsettling than it sounds, and you cannot shout for help.

- **Signals.** Agree hand signals with any companion and stay in sight of each other. Use contact sounds: a clap, or a knock on the plate, which should carry a long way. Whistles, radios and alarms will probably be mute, so set your watch to vibrate.
- **Clothing.** Your footsteps, zips and rustling fabric will sound enormous, so wear quiet clothes. Let your steps click, though. The animals cannot hear a voice, and a steady footfall is the only polite warning you can give an ibex asleep on a girder.
- **Machinery.** None of it warns you. The steam hammer falls without a sound, so use your eyes. Almost everything moves at a hand's width a day, but if you stay several days, don't camp in front of the conveyor or under the ladle.
- **The tone.** If the seasonal test sounds while you are there, it will be the only true sound you hear and it will be huge. Do as the animals do: stop and lift your head. It lasts about a second. Keep three points of contact on ladders in case it comes while you are climbing.

## The revisions

This is the real hazard. The animals obey the sheet they were drawn on, and you were not drawn, so my guess is that you get only the steel that is actually there.

- **Never follow an animal.** The hare's catwalk will not hold you, and the wall the choughs fly through will stop you.
- **Probe ahead.** Carry a pole and tap anything elevated before you step on it. Trust only the click your own stick makes.
- **Seams.** Where you see a pipe both lagged and unlagged, or a flat puddle on a sloping floor, slow down and cross at right angles, testing as you go. Don't rest there.
- **The office.** Look at the sheets, but remember they run ahead of the steel, so they are a forecast and not a map. The ink is wet. Don't touch it, lean on it or add anything. I would also resist searching the sheets for a small figure with a rucksack. If you are on one, you are bound to that issue, and I would rather not know which.

## Puddles and the levels below

The dawn puddles show the valley as it will be, with more ibex and more lichen. Stepping in them should be harmless, since the salamanders do it, and they are gone by midmorning. Go early and look. Whether you want to check them for your own reflection is up to you.

You can climb down the ladders into earlier years. Expect to come out somewhere high and slick, since the ibex emerges on the cooling tower roof, so take a harness and lanyard. One or two levels down is quieter and a little sad. I would stop before the bare levels, four or five down. Nothing moves there, and I would not want to be the first thing that does.

## Conduct

- **The animals.** Don't feed them and don't drop crumbs or seeds. Planting is "by others", and you are not the others.
- **The tubes.** Keep your hands out of the scaffold tubes, because each one holds a finch nest.
- **The furnace walls.** Don't lean on them at night, when they are covered in moths.
- **The steel.** Take nothing, since every part is numbered. Touch as little bright metal as you can. Nothing here has dulled yet, and your fingerprints should not be where it starts.

A dawn-to-afternoon visit is enough. Walk the floor to the ponds and back, then sit on your mat for a while and listen to the wings.
