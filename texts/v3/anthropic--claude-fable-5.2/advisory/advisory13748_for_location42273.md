# Preparing for the walled plateau

Nothing here will hunt you, and the weather won't kill you quickly. The risks are slow ones: wet feet, getting lost in a place with no landmarks, a dark cistern in fog, and the harm a careless visitor can do to something balanced by hand for longer than anyone remembers. You are preparing less for danger than for damp, sameness, silence, and one obligation.

## Before you go: decide what the trip is

- **You will not reach the edge.** It is days off as the hawk flies, and you will be walking winding, shoulder-width lanes. Plan to learn one district well rather than to cross anything.
- **Your way home will look like everywhere else.** Spend your first hour learning the hundred paces around your arrival point: every approach, the nearest cistern, the pattern of domes.
- **Carry everything you need.** Food and fuel don't exist here in any form you should count on. Water is the only thing the place will give you.

## Feet

There is a finger's depth of cold water on every lane floor, so your feet are your main medical concern. The residents go barefoot, but they have had a lifetime to adapt, and you have not.

- Wear waterproof boots, or plain rubber boots, with soles that grip wet rock. Wet schist is slick and moss is worse.
- Bring several pairs of wool socks, with one pair kept dry and used only for sleeping.
- Every night, take everything off your feet, dry them, and let them air. Immersion foot arrives in days and ends trips.
- If you carry a walking pole, give it a rubber tip. The pole is for balance on slabs and for probing ahead, and the rubber is for quiet.

## Clothing and pack

- **Wear wool and synthetics, no cotton, and no down unless it is well protected.** Showers pass in twenty minutes, but dew, fog, and weeping walls mean nothing fully dries.
- **Choose a quiet rain shell.** You can often just step into a cell and wait a shower out. You can see them coming from any rise.
- **Carry a tall, narrow pack with nothing strapped to the sides.** The lanes are a shoulder wide and the doorways are lower and tighter. A wide load scrapes the walls, and a scraped dry-laid wall drops stones.
- **Protect shoulders and elbows.** You will brush stone constantly, so wear fabric that can take it.
- **Bring sunglasses and sunscreen.** The plateau is high, and after a shower the wet schist turns black and glaring.
- **Dress for two climates.** The lanes are still and feel milder than they should. On a rise or in a chest-high quarter, the cold wind finds you at once.
- **Bring thin, tough gloves.** Frost-split slabs have sharp edges, and you will be handling stone.

## Sound

You will be the loudest thing for miles. A bare foot is audible one lane over, so everyone within several lanes will know where you are and how you move.

- Leave behind velcro, clanking pots, and crackling fabric.
- Use a silent alcohol burner or eat cold food. A roaring stove would be heard across a district.
- Speak quietly and don't call out.
- Expect the silence to feel like pressure for the first day or two, until your ears adjust.
- Stones click as they settle after sunset. That sound is not footsteps.
- You may hear real footsteps and never see who made them. That is a neighbor. Don't follow.

## Water and waste

The water is clear, and newts living in the cisterns are a good sign. But it has filtered through four cities' worth of rubble, so treat or filter it anyway.

Waste is the larger issue. **Water here does not run.** Anything you put into the ground spreads sideways through the rubble for years and ends up in someone's cistern.

- Pack out all human waste in sealed bags, and bring enough bags for every day.
- Use no soap, and don't wash in or near a cistern. Dip water out with a vessel and carry it away.
- Take every scrap of packaging home.

## Navigation

There are no edges, peaks, or straight lines, and the horizon is made of the same stone.

- **Test a compass on arrival.** It may or may not work here.
- **Use the wind.** It is steady and passes just over the wall-tops, so a ribbon held up at arm's length gives you a bearing.
- **Use the moss.** It grows on the shaded faces.
- **Use the lichen as landmarks.** The big map-lichen rings are each unique and effectively permanent, and the few orange-stained wall-tops are fixed bird perches. Photograph or sketch them.
- **Keep a written log** of turns and pace counts. Make short out-and-back trips from a base cell.
- **Never mark the walls or move stones as markers.** Chalk won't hold on weeping stone anyway. A displaced stone is exactly what the place exists to correct.
- **Don't climb walls for a view.** They are fitted, not mortared, and birds nest in the hearting. Use natural rises and the low-walled quarters.

## Ground hazards

- **Cisterns.** They stand brim-full and dark, level with a lane floor that is already a sheet of water. Sedge grows around their mouths, so treat sedge as a warning sign. Unbuckle your hip belt when you pass close.
- **Voids.** The lane floor sits body-lengths above bedrock, over roofed-over lanes that became drains. In swept, maintained lanes the footing has been tested by generations. In slumped districts, probe before you step, or stay out.
- **Falls.** The stone is wet, the lanes are narrow, and no rescue is coming. Carry supplies for sprains and cuts, and move slowly.

## Evening and night

The best minutes of the day come just before the worst visibility. The low yellow light turns the wall-tops into a net of bright lines, and then fog fills the lanes to the brim.

- Choose your sleeping cell before the light show and stay within a few steps of it.
- Once the fog rises, stop moving. A lamp in fog only shows you a white wall, and the cisterns are still out there.
- In a chest-high quarter your head will be above the fog, looking across a white floor studded with domes. Arrange to see it once.

## Sleeping

- **Read the signs of use.** A glassy lintel, a swept lane, or fresh clay in a joint means the cell belongs to someone. Ask if you can.
- **Leave untouched cells alone.** Cushion plants on a sill mean no one has entered for generations, and the plants are older than they look, so don't crush them.
- **Give way to hares.** One sitting in a doorway will not move until the last moment, and it should not have to.
- **Check the cell.** Look at the capstone and the rings of the dome, and check the floor for sheen.
- **Insulate from the damp.** Bring a groundsheet, a closed-cell foam pad (slab edges puncture inflatables), a synthetic sleeping bag, and a bivy cover. Your breath will condense on the dome.
- **Cook in the doorway,** not inside.
- **Expect frost** on clear nights.

## Food

Bring all of it, calorie-dense, and plan for cold. Nothing here is forage. The willow is ankle-high, the lichens took centuries to grow, and the animals are too few and too trusting to hunt.

## The people and the rule

You will see their work before you see them: a pale stretch of reset wall, or four fingerprints in clay. Don't go looking for them. They will have heard you long before you notice them, and they will decide whether to meet you.

- If you meet someone in a lane, be the one who backs into a doorway.
- Don't bring gifts that become litter. The only currency I would trust here is labor.
- **Take no stone home.** Not a pebble.
- **Learn the rule and keep it.** A fallen stone is replaced by whoever finds it, before dark, in the place it came from if that place can be found. This applies to you from your first step.

You will find a fallen stone, or knock one loose. Look for the gap, then turn the stone in your hands until it fits. Nothing is cut and nothing is forced, and it may take longer than you expect. It will show pale for years, but the lichen will come back.

Replacing a stone is the one preparation I would put above the boots. Go expecting to spend at least one afternoon doing it, and plan your days loosely enough to allow it. The place is not really there to be seen, but it does let you take part in keeping it, and that is the best thing you can do there.
