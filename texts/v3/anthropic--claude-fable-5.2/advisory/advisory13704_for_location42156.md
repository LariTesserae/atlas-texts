# Preparing for the hollow under the hill

## What you are walking into

Nothing here is dangerous in the usual sense. Nothing will bite you, the air is good, and the cold is only cellar cold. The difficulty is that you will be a large, finished, noisy thing in a room that is small, unfinished, and quiet. Preparation is mostly about arriving as less: less gear, less hardness, less hurry.

Expect it to feel like standing inside something being made slowly and with care, such as a pearl or an egg. Most visitors would feel two things at once: that they are intruding, and that they were expected. Both are probably true.

## Getting in and out

There is no entrance, so you arrive by whatever means you travel. A few cautions apply:

- **Do not dig.** The hollow is barely more than an arm's length below the grass, which is tempting. But the room exists because a piece of noon was closed in. Open it and you let ordinary evening in, and I don't think the noon survives that.
- **Arrive crouched, and into the empty air.** Most of the room is bright vacant floor, so aim for that. The pool, the green film, the ceiling roots, and the low corner by the threshold stone are occupied.
- **Leave from the threshold stone.** It lies at the foot of a wall with no opening, and it is already dished by feet. Some of that wear may be yours. Stand in the hollow of it when you go.
- **Do not leave by the far wall.** Sometimes it is only the place where the room leaves off, with brightness and moths carrying on past it. The room is six paces going and eleven coming back, and I would not want to learn what the count is from farther out.

## What to wear

- **Soft, warm layers.** Wool is best, since the walls sweat and a small rain may fall on you. Avoid anything that rustles. The rustle wouldn't carry anyway, but you would notice its absence, and that is unsettling.
- **Soft soles or thick socks.** The floor takes prints and fills them slowly from behind. Leave boots, heels, and cleats at home.
- **Short fingernails and bare hands.** Leave rings, watches, buckles, and anything else with an edge.

## What to carry

- **A small cloth to sit on.** Sit in the open middle, and move occasionally so you don't leave a lasting seat.
- **Water and plain food that doesn't crumble.** A crumb here is an event. Three hundred woodlice would reorganise their day around it.
- **A blindfold or dark cloth.** This matters more than anything else on the list. A torch is useless, because the light is already everywhere and comes from nowhere. What the room lacks is dark. After an hour of shadowless noon your eyes will ache for it, the way the animals' do.
- **A knotted string or a handful of counters, to mark time by your own doings.** Decide before you come how long you will stay, and measure it in something you carry: sips, knots, or a set number of the animals' nights.
- **Hand signals, if you bring a companion.** Bring one companion at most. Voices go into the walls like water into flour.

## What to leave behind

Leave lamps, cameras with flash, tools, specimen jars, and anything meant for taking samples. Take nothing: no moth, no pebble, no pinch of wall. Above all, do not take the light, even if you think of a way.

Leave nothing either. The room coats what is left in it, and that is its whole nature.

## Moving around

The floor has not agreed on which way is down. Your inner ear will hold one opinion and the tilted pool another. A few things help:

- **Move slowly, and sit when the room swims.** Ginger helps with the nausea.
- **Use the pinched bowl as your level.** It holds the only level water in the room. Look at it when you need to remember where down is. Don't drink from it, because the salamanders prefer it and it is small.
- **Look before you place a hand or foot.** With no shadows, slopes and hollows look flat. The moths look exactly like the wall flaking, and the springtails look like flour on the water.
- **Keep your head low.** The upper air belongs to the thumbnail swifts and their tight circuits.
- **Go around the stalactite's bead.** It hangs a foot below the ceiling, attached to nothing, and it is easy to walk into.
- **Don't lean on the walls.**

## The silence and the order of things

You will not hear your own footsteps. You may not hear your breath. The only sound will be the stone ticking as it sets, and now and then a page turning, which is the woodlice crossing the floor. Some people find this restful, and some find it starts to feel like being erased. If you are the second kind, hum. You will feel the hum in your chest even though the room takes it.

Cause and effect are filed loosely here. The tap may come before the drop. You may feel full before you eat, or rested before you sleep. You may find a thumbprint in the wall, feel your thumb go out to it, and discover that it fits.

**Do not use the room to check your sanity.** Count the swifts once, enjoy the number, and don't count again. Every count will be different and all of them will seem right. That is the room's arithmetic, not a failure of yours.

## The animals

They are gentle and will mostly ignore you.

- **The salamanders:** if one climbs onto you, wet your hand first, at the tilted pool. Dry skin is hard on them.
- **The unfinished ones:** the half-salamander is not injured, and the beetles that are only a shine are not ghosts. They need neither pity nor fixing. The shine may cross your wrist, and it weighs nothing.
- **The heap:** a few times a day the animals pile up in the low corner to make their fist-sized night and take turns in it. Keep still and keep back. This is the most private thing in the room.

You carry darkness without meaning to, in your sleeves, your pockets, and your cupped hands. Creatures may find it. A cricket in your cuff is a compliment. You may offer the hollow of your hands for a while, and I think that is the one gift a visitor can give here. Check every fold of your clothing before you leave, so that you don't carry anyone out with their night.

## The handmade things

The room contains a ring of seven river pebbles, a bowl with thumb marks in the rim, four finger-grooves at shoulder height, and a threshold worn by feet. No one has been here. The wear came first.

You will wonder whether it is yours, and I can't tell you. My advice is to neither fulfil it nor avoid it on purpose. Don't bring seven pebbles to close the loop. The room doesn't need its causality tidied, and the crickets already step around the ring as if it were settled. If, walking the wall in that light, your hand rises to shoulder height and your fingers find the grooves, let them. And if you come home with river grit in a pocket and no memory of a river, don't be alarmed.

## When to go

Stay for one of their nights, or two at most. You are a second piece of grit in a place that responds to grit by closing around it one coat at a time. The room means no harm by this, but it is the same thing it does to the noon.

Go when any of these happens:

- the ticking seems to come from close by
- your skin feels faintly like milk left standing
- your print in the floor is slow to fill
- you count your own fingers and get a number that seems right

Stand on the threshold stone, in the worn place, and go. Above, the hill will have only grass on it, and nothing will show.
