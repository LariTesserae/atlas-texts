# Preparing for the garden under the veil

You are preparing for two places that sit one stride apart. Outside is a high, cold, windy volcanic slope that freezes hard every night. Inside is a sealed swamp, hotter than your body and fully saturated. Either one can hurt you, but the most dangerous moment is the step between them, when you are soaking wet. The small frogs lying stiff a hand's breadth outside the rear edge show how sharp that line is.

## The approach

- **Acclimatise first.** The altitude is real, with frost every night and snow just past the ridge. An altitude headache and a heat headache feel alike, so you want any headache inside the dome to mean only one thing.
- **Protect your feet and hands.** The young lava is glassy and will cut boot soles and skin. Wear stiff-soled boots you are willing to ruin, gaiters, long trousers and leather-palmed gloves. A fall here means lacerations, not bruises.
- **Watch for thin crust.** Collapsed blisters mean there are blisters that haven't collapsed yet. If the crust sounds hollow, go around.
- **Come up the wake.** The strip of thin soil and dead stalks behind the dome is the only kind ground on the mountain, and it leads straight to the rear edge.
- **Leave the record alone.** Walk beside the fourteen stacks of lava plates and don't tidy or add to them. They are someone's long series of measurements, and your visit isn't part of it. The three cracked stones in the wake stay where they are too.

## The crossing

Stage your exit before you go in, because everything you wear through the veil will be wet and nothing dries inside.

1. Pick a spot just outside the rear edge, a little to one side. The black bird works that margin every morning and shouldn't be crowded.
2. Strip to a thin synthetic layer, boots and gloves.
3. Put your insulation, shell, hat, mitts, towel and dry socks in a dry bag, packed in the order you will put them on. Close it against wind and against the bird, which I'd assume is clever about bags, and weigh it down.
4. On the way out, towel off, strip the wet layer and be in dry clothes and a windproof shell within two minutes.

For the first few seconds the cold will feel wonderful, because you will be overheated. Don't trust that feeling. A companion standing outside with an open parka is the best equipment you can have. Sound doesn't cross the veil, and at night sight doesn't either, so agree on a time: "If I'm not out in twenty minutes, come in."

Going in has its own shock. Your body is braced for cold and suddenly meets heat, so your blood vessels open and you may go light-headed. Take one step inside, crouch, and wait a minute before moving. Fainting onto this ground would be serious.

## The heat

Nothing inside will feel dangerous, but the heat is the main risk. In saturated air hotter than blood, sweat can't evaporate, so your body has no way to shed heat. You are the only thing in the dome making heat, and it has nowhere to go.

- **Keep visits short.** My rule would be about twenty minutes sitting still, less if you are moving, then out to cool completely before going back. Several short visits are safer than one long one. Whoever built the bench surely watched the front in spells.
- **Bring a timer.** Your sense of time and of your own condition will both fail.
- **Know the warning signs.** Leave at once if you get a headache, nausea, clumsiness, irritability, or if you stop feeling hot.
- **Carry your own cold.** Nothing inside can cool you. The pools, the rock and the falling water are all at air temperature. Bring an insulated flask of ice-cold water from outside, with electrolytes.
- **Never sleep inside.** The warmth will be tempting at night, when the outside is far below freezing, but a night in there would kill you as surely as it kills the frogs outside.

## Moving around inside

The ground has the same knife creases and bottle-glass rims as the lava outside, but now it is wet, and at the front it is slick with algae.

- **Move slowly and keep three points of contact** near the bowls.
- **Wear grippy wet-rock footwear.** A second pair, like canyoneering shoes with a solid sole, is worth the weight. It also keeps your mountain boots dry, since wet boots freeze solid overnight.
- **Check yourself for cuts by eye.** Everything is wet and at skin temperature, so you can't feel the difference between water, sweat and blood. Glass-sharp rock cuts almost painlessly. Look at your hands and shins every few minutes.
- **Step only on bare rock.** Every patch of soil took a plant years to make. Keep out of the cracks and bowls, especially the front bowls where the frogs lay.
- **Use the place's own compass.** Downhill is the back and the tree fern marks the rear. The five shadows always point to the front, which helps when the veil is thick. I don't know whether a headlamp would cast the same wrong shadow, though I suspect it would.
- **Expect odd sound.** A clap or a call comes back late and pitched lower. This may be unsettling the first time.

## The stones

Look as long as you like, and touch one briefly if you must. Your hand will report almost nothing, which means you can't judge how hard you are pressing, so don't lean on them, sit on them or brace against them. Don't chip them, mark them or put anything on them. The whole place is centred on them, and three have already failed. My guess is that the garden lasts only as long as they do.

## The closed loop

The same water goes round indefinitely, so whatever washes off you in the crossing stays in the cycle.

- **Go in clean.** Rinse off sunscreen, repellent and soap first. The frogs breathe through their skin. Outside, rely on a hat and clothing for sun protection.
- **Use a head net** if the gnats bother you. Don't use chemicals.
- **Leave nothing behind.** That means no food, no crumbs and no urine.
- **Bring no seeds.** Brush out your cuffs, laces, velcro and boot treads before you arrive. Nobody knows how these plants got here, and your socks shouldn't add to them.
- **Take drinking water from the outside runoff** at the moss ring, and filter it. Don't draw from the pools.
- **Don't rescue anything.** You will want to carry stranded frogs forward, but the bird's breakfast is part of how the place works.

## Timing

- **Midday** is best for a first entry. The veil thins to threads, you can see the mountain through it, the soaking is lightest and the exit is least brutal.
- **Dusk** is when the frogs call, one dry note and sometimes an answer. It's worth hearing, but you will come out into falling dark and plunging cold. Have a headlamp on your head and your camp close.
- **Night** is the most dangerous exit, from hot heavy rain into deep frost with fingers too numb for zips. If you go in at all, go no farther than the tree fern, eleven paces in. Stay ten minutes and have your companion waiting.
- **Morning** is the bird's time. Watch from outside, off to one side.

## Camp

Pitch well down the wake, where alpine grass has rooted in the soil, because sharp lava will shred a tent floor. Bring a four-season tent, a bag rated well below freezing and a thick mat. Build your own windbreak and take nothing from the cairns. Keep batteries and the wet shoes inside your sleeping bag. The volcano erupted within living memory, so check its current state before you climb.

## Kit beyond normal alpine gear

- two pairs of footwear, one for the mountain and one for inside
- leather-palmed gloves and gaiters
- two dry bags, a towel and a complete spare base layer
- an oversized insulated parka with big toggles
- an insulated flask and electrolytes
- a timer
- waterproof notebook and pencil
- sealed bags for electronics, which will stream with condensation on the way in and freeze on the way out
- a serious laceration kit: irrigation, gauze, pressure bandage, closure strips and tweezers for glass splinters
- a headlamp with spare batteries kept warm

Plan to treat any wound outside, since nothing sticks to wet skin.

## What to expect

A full day might give you an hour inside in total, taken in pieces. Spend some of it on the bare half of the bench, facing the front. You will not see the place move, since it travels less than a finger's width a day. The tree fern has perhaps three or four years before the veil reaches it, and there is nothing you can do about that. But the coin-sized liverwort on the glass edge at the front wasn't there last month. Find it, and don't touch it.
