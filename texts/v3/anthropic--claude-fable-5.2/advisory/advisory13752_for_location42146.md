# Preparing for the basin of the nine masts

Three facts shape everything about this visit:

- **The sound is a physical condition.** It is more like altitude or cold than like noise, and ear protection only partly helps.
- **The ground cuts and has no water.** All the water is shelved overhead.
- **The inhabited part is in the air**, on floors you cannot see, and it moves.

The description leaves some things out, such as how people climb between planes and how much weight a plane bears. I say so where I'm guessing.

## Before you go

**Health.** Be honest about whether you should go at all. A tone near twenty cycles a second, strong enough to hold up gardens, will be in your chest, skull and eyes every minute you are outside a room. I would stay away with any of these:

- a pacemaker, especially a motion-sensing one
- a heart condition
- pregnancy
- recent eye, ear or chest surgery
- a vestibular disorder, severe migraine or existing tinnitus
- a serious fear of heights
- panic disorder (infrasound at this frequency tends to produce dread with no object, and you will need to recognise that as physiology, not premonition)

**Timing.** Three things matter:

- **Cicadas.** Go while the hedge cicadas are in full voice. In the weeks after a brood dies back the upper planes go soft.
- **Storms.** Avoid the stormiest part of the year. Thunder briefly unseats rooms, and the masts are the tallest objects for kilometres.
- **Length of stay.** Plan a short stay, measured in days.

**Signals.** Your voice will be useless outside a room, so agree hand signals with your companions before you arrive. The essentials are stop, go back, I'm ill, water, danger and are you all right. Add touch signals for the dark.

## Approach from the southwest

The dead mast's quarter is the only place where the field is absent and the only place with deep soil and ground-level damp. It is where you camp, sleep on the first night, and retreat when you've had enough.

It also tests your ears. If you sit in the thicket and cannot hear the quiet bird, your hearing is temporarily dulled and you need a longer break before going back in.

Treat the heap itself as rotten footing with voids in it.

## Living in the sound

**Ear protection.** Wear deep-fitted plugs with earmuffs over them, and carry many spare plugs, because fog and midges will foul them. This protects your hearing from the audible chord. It does nothing for the twenty-cycle tone, which goes straight through your body.

**What to expect:**

- pressure on the breastbone
- slightly smeared vision, which makes fine print and depth judgment harder
- nausea
- fatigue out of proportion to effort
- irritability by the second day

Ration your exposure in hours and take breaks in rooms or the thicket. Wearing plugs all day in warm wet air also invites ear infections, so dry your ears whenever you are somewhere quiet.

**Lost warnings.** You lose every auditory cue you normally rely on: a shout, rockfall, the hollow ring of thin crust under your boots. Keep your companions in sight or on a short tether, and check on each other by eye.

**Crossing a room's edge.** The description calls it a blow to the chest and ears. Put your protection in before you reach the cord, keep your mouth slightly open, breathe out, and step through deliberately.

**Loose and small things.** The field moves anything small or loose:

- A dropped screw, pill or lens will be walked to the nearest hedge line. Look for it there, carefully, because that soil is partly glass shards.
- Open powders outside a room and they will leave your hand.
- Threaded fittings will back out, so tape them and check knots and buckles daily.
- Camera stabilisers and microphones will be useless.

## On the rock

**Footwear.** Clinker opens boot soles. Bring heavy-soled boots and a spare pair, leather gloves, long trousers and gaiters. Carry a real wound kit, because a fall here means lacerations.

**Light.** Noon is the colour of weak tea and your eyes are blurred. Move slowly and use a headlamp even by day in the clinker fields.

**Water.** Carry all of it for any ground crossing. The lowest sheet is metres overhead and nothing pools.

**Navigation.** A compass may misbehave on young basalt. The ground itself is the map:

- Rings curve around masts.
- Straight hedges mark where two fields meet.
- The vegetated lines are the still places.
- The direct lines between neighbouring masts are the fiercest. At night, with your lamp dimmed, the blue flashes show you where they are.

**Hedges.** Step over them and never walk along them. They are the basin's only soil, and they house the cicadas.

**Collapsed tubes.** Stay well back from the openings. The ground bucks there, the tone is lower and worse, and the roofs are thin. The basin is low, with slow air and a fissure that still steams, so I would also assume bad air can collect in pits and tubes. Do not go down.

**Storms.** Stay off open rock and away from the mast bases.

## On the planes

**Getting up.** I don't know how residents ascend. The masts are seamless, so it is not by climbing them. Find a guide at ground level and do not improvise. I would bring a light harness and lanyard on general principle.

**Weight.** Travel light. Water sheets sag and drip through when they grow heavy, so I would assume the planes have limits. Ask.

**Footing and height.** The floor is a hand's depth of fog with two hundred metres of tiers visible beneath it. Give yourself time on a low plane first.

**The daily movement.** The upper levels rise several metres by mid-afternoon and sink at dusk, and you will feel the descent in your stomach. Anything tied between levels needs slack.

**Navigation.** The rafts drift. Navigate by masts and lamps, never by gardens.

**Insects.** The sheets are where the insects are, and so are your ankles and your bed. Bring a head net, fine-mesh bedding and sealed food containers. Filter and treat all water collected from the sheets.

**Swifts and bats.** Swifts work the sheets at speed. The bats fly from memory and will not detect a new obstacle. Ask where the flyways are, and never string a line or tarp across one.

**Damp.** Everything stays wet. Wear wool or synthetics, pack in dry bags, and bring antifungal cream, many socks, and waterproof paper with a pencil.

**Thunder.** Sleep with your gear clipped together and to you, and leave no blades, flames or hot liquids loose.

## Among the residents

**The cord and the mats.** The cord is both wall and door, so wait to be invited across it. Expect to remove your boots before the mats, since the soles carry glass.

**Privacy.** With no walls, privacy is kept by manners. Don't stare into rooms or photograph them.

**Pace and voice.** Learn a few of their signs and carry a slate. Move at their pace; they don't hurry, and they have reasons. Inside a room a voice carries, so speak quietly.

**Gardens.** The rafts are gardens. Don't pick from them.

**Waste.** Ask where it goes. Anything released on a plane ends up in the water that drips onto the people below.

## What not to bring or do

A cicada has tuned itself to this field, and the masts have come to lean on it. That means the structure can be influenced by what lives in it, so be careful what you add to it.

- **No insecticide, foggers or aerosols.** The midges feed the swifts and bats, the frogs breathe through their skin, and the cicadas hold up the top of the town. Use nets and clothing instead.
- **No sustained loud tones.** That rules out horns, speakers and engines.
- **Nothing percussive.** A gunshot or firework is a small thunderclap, and people are asleep on those planes.
- **No smoky fires.** Smoke will shelve like everything else small and hang in the sheets where people live. Bring food that needs no cooking.
- **Don't mark, strike or tie anything to a mast.**

## When to leave

Leave if any of these happen:

- Your ears ring during quiet breaks.
- Your vision stays blurred inside a room.
- The planes feel soft underfoot.
- The chord changes pitch or begins to waver and no storm explains it.

The ninth mast stopped without a known cause, and its sky took an afternoon to fall. That is enough time to get down if you already know your route, so learn it on the first day.
