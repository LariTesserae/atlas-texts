# Preparing for the pan

The animals are not the main danger. Almost everything in the pan is busy eating dust and has no concept of you. The things likely to hurt you are, in order: the noise, the cold and wet, the footing, and getting lost. The hunters come after those.

Expect something like wading chest-deep through a crowd of cold, wet animals in the dark, inside a sound you feel in your skull, with no path behind you.

## Shape of the visit

- **Camp outside the rim.** Cold and noise pool in the basin, so the surrounding country is warmer and quieter. You will not sleep or rest inside the pan, because there is nowhere to sit that isn't wet, occupied, or someone's road.
- **Make short forays rather than a crossing.** "A day's walk across" assumes open ground. Through fans, fissures and darkness it will take several times that, with no rest stops.
- **Enter from the windward side.** The rust band is then behind you going in and ahead of you coming out. Walking toward the only fixed light is the simplest way home.
- **Never go alone.** A broken ankle half a day in, where the dead are stripped where they fall, is not survivable solo.
- **Turn around by the clock.** There is no day here, and the noise will erode your judgment. Set a turnaround time at camp and obey it.

## The noise

A chord you feel in your teeth and eyes is low-frequency sound at a level that will damage your hearing. No earplug stops it, because it comes through bone.

- Wear fitted plugs under earmuffs, and carry spares. Electronic level-limiting muffs are ideal, since they let you hear the drums safely.
- Get a hearing test beforehand so you know what you lost.
- Expect nausea, blurred vision, pressure in the chest, and a sourceless dread. Most of that dread is acoustic, but not necessarily all of it.
- Keep your protection on when the wind drops. The lull sounds louder, not quieter.
- Speech is useless. Agree hand and touch signals in advance, carry a writing slate, and consider a short tether between partners.

## Cold and wet

A few degrees above freezing in saturated air and constant wind is classic hypothermia weather. Every cold surface you carry will sweat like the stone does.

- Wear wool or synthetic layers under a full waterproof shell with a hood. Avoid cotton and untreated down.
- Bring waterproof gloves and a spare pair, since your hands will be wet all day.
- Wear waterproof boots and gaiters, and keep dry socks at camp. Hours of cold wet feet cause trench foot.
- Keep electronics in dry bags and batteries inside your jacket. Use anti-fog on anything you look through.

## The air

The ammonia smell means millions of animals excreting in a basin that traps air. The wind also carries spores, scales and dried scum from a biosphere your lungs have never met.

- Use a half-mask respirator with combined ammonia and particulate cartridges.
- Wear sealed goggles. Fans shed sludge with a clap, and you will be standing among them at face-spray height.
- Your head is above the fans, in the lowest layer of fliers. They echolocate and should avoid you, but in those numbers some won't. Wear a helmet and keep your hood up.

## Footing

The ground is limestone pavement, wet and rounded, with arm-deep fissures hidden under a canopy of fans. If you can, practise on limestone pavement on Earth, at night, in rain.

- The fans root in the cracks, so a line of stalks marks a fissure. Step on the slab between rows.
- The walker troughs look like paths, but they are polished, wet, ankle-deep, and full of fist-sized animals that expect you to click. Cross them rather than follow them.
- Wear stiff boots with studded soles and use a pole to probe ahead. Knee and shin protection is worthwhile.
- If a slab rings under your pole or boot, back off. It is thin stone over a void, and it is also a landmark. Your weight on it changes its note, and everything here steers by those notes.
- Don't grab a fan-stalk for balance, and keep your fingers out of the combs. A clap like a split board will hurt.

## Finding your way

The fans fold ahead of you and reopen behind, so you leave no trail. The wind gives direction until it stops. The rust band gives you "windward" and nothing finer.

- **Use light.** It is the one channel nothing here competes for. Put a strobe on a mast at your entry point on the rim, with a second of a different colour some distance along for a cross-bearing. In that darkness they should carry a long way.
- Carry three light sources each. Aim your headlamp down at your feet, and look up now and then to keep the band in view.
- I would guess most things here are eyeless, since no sun has ever stood over them. Test that at the edge before trusting it.
- **Learn the drums.** A tuner or spectrum app will show you which notes arrive from which side. Record that "address" at your entry point and at intervals going in. It is the oldest map of the place and it works.
- Don't trust a compass until you have checked it against the band from outside the rim.

## Silence, lulls and hunters

Sound here is background, and silence is the signal.

- **Moving, you are a furrow of quiet.** Walkers will break from you like water from a keel. Move steadily and straight so you are easy to read.
- **Stopped, you disappear.** The fans reopen around you and the chord closes over. If you are unsure about anything, stand still.
- **You cannot hear the humming hunter.** That is the point of it. But it still physically flattens fans, and you have eyes. Sweep your light, or better a thermal imager, across the field now and then and look for a line of folded fans with no gap in the sound. It is long and low, so anything it does to you will start at the legs. Don't crouch or lie among the fans.
- **In a lull, stop.** When the chord drops, get onto the highest bare slab you can find, away from basins and fissures. Walkers mob the water and things climb out of the cracks to feed. With the fans folded and the drums exposed, it is your best moment to see across the field and take bearings. When the thin note starts at the windward edge, step clear of the combs before they open.
- Things that eat folded fans may learn to follow your wake. This is a guess, but check behind you.

## Water, food and waste

- Carry your water in. The basins are full of swimmers, dissolved limestone and ammonia. In an emergency, a clean cold sheet held to the wind will sweat drinkable water, which you should still filter.
- Eat nothing local. Eat your own food at camp, not in the field.
- Cover any wound immediately and completely. Anything organic that lands on this ground is stripped.
- Pack out everything, including your own waste. Whatever you drop will be eaten within minutes by something it may poison.

## What not to do

- Don't strike a ringing slab. A false note is a false landmark for everything within earshot.
- Leave whistles, speakers and anything that emits ultrasound, such as rangefinders or pest repellers, at home. Each flier layer owns its pitch, and you could blind one.
- Don't carry an emergency whistle as your signal. Your emergency signal is light.

## Afterwards

- Decontaminate at the rim, including boots, shell and gear. You are carrying another world's spores.
- Give your ears a day or two of real quiet. Expect ringing.
- For a while, quiet rooms will feel like something approaching. The pan taught you that, and it fades.

## Short kit list

- **Hearing:** plugs, electronic muffs, spares
- **Air and face:** respirator with ammonia and particulate cartridges, sealed goggles, helmet
- **Clothing:** wool or synthetic layers, waterproof shell, two pairs of gloves, gaiters, studded boots, shin and knee guards
- **Movement:** probe pole, partner tether
- **Light and navigation:** three lights each, two mast strobes, thermal imager, tuner or spectrum app, watch
- **Communication:** slate and agreed signals
- **Medical:** splint, occlusive dressings, eyewash
- **Camp:** dry bags, spare dry clothes, stove
