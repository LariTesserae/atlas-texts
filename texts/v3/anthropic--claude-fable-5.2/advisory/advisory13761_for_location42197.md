**In brief:** I don't think anything on the tableland will hurt you. The danger is that nothing there will ever give you a reason to leave, and walking will not get you out. Prepare for a short, dry, high-altitude visit, and arrange your departure so that it doesn't depend on your deciding to go.

## What to expect

You will be standing in a crowd that reaches every horizon, in mild cold, in an afternoon that doesn't end. Thousands of eyes will follow you while the heads stay still. A gap just wide enough for your shoulders will open ahead and close behind. The only sound will be the plain breathing, once every four seconds.

It will be peaceful, and that is the hazard. The people who camped here left no graves, no second hearth and no path out. My reading is that they never left and never died; they just stopped partway through their work.

## Your body

- **Altitude.** An indigo sky means very thin air, and you will arrive without a gradual ascent. Acclimatize at altitude beforehand or bring supplemental oxygen, plus a pulse oximeter. Headache, nausea, clumsiness or confusion all mean you should leave.
- **Water.** There is none: no dew, frost or streams. Carry all of it, at least four litres per day, since thin dry air dehydrates you quickly. Bring lip balm, eye drops and saline for your nose.
- **Food.** Bring food that needs no cooking. I would not light a fire in still air among felted animals packed flank to flank, and I would not use the old hearth.
- **Light.** The sun never sets and UV is strong. Bring sunscreen, sunglasses, a brimmed hat and a sleep mask.
- **Cold.** It is cellar temperature with no wind, so a fleece, hat and light gloves are enough. Bring an insulated pad, because bare stone pulls heat out of you when you sit.
- **Allergies.** You will be within arm's reach of wool, feathers, dander and moth scales the whole time. Take antihistamines, and an inhaler if you have ever needed one.
- **Waste.** The animals leave nothing behind, so you shouldn't either. Pack everything out.

## Moving among the animals

- Wear soft, grippy soles. The stone is polished and dusty, and there are hares and beetles at foot level.
- Walk slowly and let the gap lead. Don't push, or steady yourself on a flank unless you must. Flies hang at head height, so keep your glasses on.
- Bring no weapons and no pets. The dogs lie among the hares, and whatever keeps that peace, you shouldn't test it. I would not bring an animal I wanted to take home.
- If you travel with someone, stay in physical contact, with a hand on a shoulder or a wrist tether. The gap is one person wide and closes behind, voices don't carry, and no landmark can be trusted.
- The grazer with the torn ear will always be the nearest animal. Don't use it for navigation. Don't trust shadows for direction either, since each one points its own way.

## Keeping your own time

- Carry a watch and a timer. Eat, drink and sleep by the clock, not by appetite or the sky.
- Bring a rhythm that isn't four seconds: a song, a metronome, a walking chant. Four seconds per breath is an ordinary resting pace at sea level, so it is easy to fall into. Up here you should be breathing faster, so if you match the plain you are under-breathing as well as drifting.
- Decide before you go that you will not count anything. Every total is correct, and none agree. The hundreds of pebble rows show where that leads.

## The camp

Look, but touch nothing.

- Don't sit in the hollow on the knapping floor.
- Don't pick up the scraper, whose edge is turned toward the hide like an invitation to finish the job.
- Don't add a pebble to the tallies.
- Don't take the awl.
- Don't sleep under the rib roof. Those ribs are still breathing, and I suspect the animal they came from is still standing somewhere on the plain.

Rest in an open windbreak if you need to, sitting up, with an alarm set.

## Leaving

- The rim cannot be reached. However you arrived is the only way out, so your return must travel with you and not be tied to a spot on the pavement.
- Set the return to trigger automatically at a fixed time on your own clock, with no confirmation needed. Have someone at home who expects you.
- Keep the visit short. A few hours is enough, and one sleep is the most I would risk.

## Leave at once if

- Your breathing has fallen in step with the plain.
- A meal time passes and you are neither hungry nor thirsty.
- You stop leaving footprints in the dust.
- Your shadow and your companion's point in different directions.
- You notice you have been standing still, facing the way the birds face.
- You catch yourself counting.
