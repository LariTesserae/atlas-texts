# Preparing for the saddle town

Prepare to be damp, smoky, and surrounded by noise and animals for your whole stay, in a place that is gentle but never still. Nothing here is out to hurt you. The real risks are ordinary ones: slipping, getting chilled while wet, bad water, skin that never dries, and being on the stairs at the wrong time.

## Before you go

- **Health:** If your home world has rabies and tetanus vaccination, get both. There is a dog in every doorway, there are macaques and bats, and small cuts are certain. If you react badly to bee stings, bring your remedy, because the hives hang at about head height along the upper walkways. If you have weak lungs, think hard about going at all. Every room is full of hearth smoke, day and night.
- **Fitness:** The stairs climb from valley floor to cloud height, and I would expect hours of them. Train your legs, especially for the descent, which is harder on wet, dished stone.
- **Biosecurity:** Clean your boots and gear before you arrive. Anything will grow here, so do not bring seeds or spores in.
- **Timing:** Ask at the bottom of the stairs which day is market day. Arrive the day before, when the town is at normal size and a house can take you in. Leave the morning after, when porters are going down. Five days lets you see one full market cycle.

## What to bring

**Clothing.** You cannot stay dry, so do not try. Aim to be warm while wet during the day and dry once at night.

- Wear wool or something else that insulates when soaked. Cotton will sit cold on you for days.
- Keep one set of sleeping clothes sealed in a waterproof bag and never wear it outdoors. This is the most important thing you carry.
- Bring a wind layer. The saddle is a gap between two valleys and funnels wind, and the temperature drops whenever cloud comes through.
- Bring a brimmed hat. Between clouds the sun is strong and every wet leaf glares.

**Footwear.** Choose the grippiest soles you can find, on shoes that drain rather than hold water. The gate ramp is polished root and the stairs are wet stone. Waterproof boots will fill on the first day and never dry. Bring several pairs of wool socks and something for your feet, either powder or ointment.

**Kit.**

- A walking staff for the stairs.
- Waterproof liners for everything, and a pencil rather than ink.
- A light you can aim at your feet. In cloud, a beam held high just lights up the fog.
- Earplugs for sleeping.
- Water treatment, or a pot for boiling.
- Antiseptic and dressings, and salt or a flame for leeches. None are mentioned, but a forest like this will almost certainly have them.
- A lighter, though you can always borrow an ember.
- Few electronics. Expect fogged lenses and condensation, and expect leather to mould and steel to rust.

**Gifts and trade.** The town buys salt and iron, so those are what to carry. Bring salt in small sealed packets, plus needles, a good file, or a whetstone. Keep it modest. Your coin probably means nothing here.

## The climb

Start at dawn, because at night the cloud settles in the saddle and stays. If you can, go up behind a porter.

Each tread is a shallow basin with water in it and usually a frog. Look before each step and let your staff go first. Porters carry their loads on tumplines with their heads down, so step aside for them.

Before you start, learn what the slit-gong sounds like. When cloud closes in, stop and stay on the stone. It usually blows through in about ten minutes. If it does not, walk slowly toward the gong. Never leave the stairs, since there is a gorge on each side.

## Living in the town

**Getting around.** There are two street levels. The ground lanes run with water for half of every day, so follow the locals up to the root-walkways when they go. The walkways are two people wide and shared with porters, squirrels, rats, and martens.

Railings and wall panels are replaced on a schedule everyone knows except you, so test a railing before you lean on it. Never put your weight on a wall, which is only matting. When cloud comes through, hold the rail and wait.

**The living structure.** The hedge, the house posts, and the railings are alive and generations old. Do not cut them, carve them, or hang anything from them.

**Water.** It flows in a fixed order: gutter, trough, cistern, lane, then off the edge. The upper stages are for drinking and the lanes are the drain. Ask where you may drink and where you may wash, and never put soap or dirt into a trough. Boil what you drink anyway, because ducks work the gutters and pigs live under the floors. The latrine arrangement probably involves those pigs, so ask and do not be squeamish.

**Your things.** Leave nothing on the ground overnight, or it will grow mushrooms. Do as the locals do and hang everything in the smoke above a hearth. Smoke is the only thing that preserves anything here. You and all you own will smell of it for a long time.

**Food.** Expect smoked birds, pork, goat, grain, and honey. Ask about the honey and eat only a little at first. Where bees feed on rhododendron, some seasons' honey can make you dizzy and sick. The locals will know which is safe.

**Animals.**

- **Dogs:** Stop at the doorway and wait for someone inside to notice you. Do not step over a dog you have not met.
- **Macaques:** Carry no visible food, and do not stare at them or show your teeth.
- **Goats:** They will eat straps, hats, and anything left in reach.
- **Bees:** Walk past the hives calmly, and wear no scent.
- **Bats and netted birds:** Do not touch either. Keep clear of the fowlers' masts and lines, and do not free anything from the nets. Those birds are the town's income.
- **Slugs:** At night, light your feet. They are as long as a hand and cross the lanes after dark.

**Manners.** People speak at a near-shout because of the water and the bells. It is not anger, and you will need to speak up too. Walls are mats, so expect no privacy and no silence.

The best thing you can offer your hosts is work. Clear a gutter, carry something, or weed where you are shown. The town survives on constant upkeep, and a guest who helps will be welcome.

## Looking after yourself

- **Feet:** Every night, dry them at the hearth, check between your toes, and put on your dry set. Do this without fail.
- **Cuts:** Clean and cover every one. Wounds heal slowly in constant damp.
- **Cold:** If you are shivering in wet clothes in the wind, go inside to a fire. A slow chill is more likely to hurt you here than anything dramatic.
- **Night:** Stay in. Fog, running water, and slick roots are a bad combination, and the frogs are just as loud from indoors.

## What it will feel like

The first two days will be overwhelming. You will be wet, your eyes will sting, bells will clank over your head, and something will always be moving at the edge of your sight. Around the third day you will stop fighting it.

Then you can enjoy what the place offers. The sun breaks through and the whole town steams. Swifts pass at head height. If you are lucky with the season, butterflies drift through for hours, or the bats pour up out of the east gorge at dusk.

## Leaving

Go down in the morning with the porters. Before you cross back to your own world, shake out and inspect everything you own. Something will have moved in, whether a frog, a slug, a crab, seeds, or spores, and it should stay behind.
