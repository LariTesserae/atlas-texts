# Preparing for the salt works

Almost nothing here will come after you. The dangers are the air, the lack of fresh water, and the distances between things. Prepare for those three properly and the rest is mostly care around edges.

## What it will be like

Expect to feel heavy and slow, wet from the first minute to the last. The grey light hides how hot it is, with no glare or shadow to warn you. Under the lid of cloud you get a brighter patch instead of a sun, a horizon you can barely find, and pans that all look alike. The smell will be brine and iodine, rust, and a rotten-egg note wherever the black mat is broken, with ammonia near the boiler house. It is quiet enough that you will hear your own boots and breathing most of the time. The place is not eerie so much as indifferent: it is busy, and none of the business concerns you.

## The heat

At forty-one degrees, in air too wet for sweat to work, your body has no way to shed heat. You will pour sweat and get no benefit from it. Fitness and willpower don't change this, and people die in these conditions while sitting still.

- **Keep the animals' timetable.** Move from first light until mid-morning, and again near dusk. At midday the birds stand and the crabs go down their burrows, so lie down too.
- **Rest inside brick.** The iron sheds are ovens. The row houses have thermal mass and will be a few degrees kinder.
- **Bring cooling that doesn't depend on evaporation.** Wet cloths and fans do little here. Bring phase-change cooling packs or an insulated box of ice for the first day or two.
- **Don't cool off in the pans.** At midday the shallow brine is hotter than the air.
- **Know the signs.** If you stop sweating, get a headache, feel sick, or turn clumsy or irritable, stop and cool down. If you can, bring a companion, because you won't notice your own confusion.
- **Halve your pace.** A twenty-minute causeway walk costs what an hour costs elsewhere.

## Water

There is none. The eaves drip rust and salt, and every rain-filled vessel is full of mosquito larvae.

- Carry eight to ten litres per person per day, and more if you can haul it. A small cart or sled suits these wide, flat streets.
- Bring oral rehydration salts. Even surrounded by salt, you can dilute your own blood drinking plain water. Never taste the brine, since a mouthful of the strong inner pans is a medical problem.
- Budget a little fresh water each day for rinsing eyes, hands and cuts.
- Cache water at the works before any long walk into the ponds.

## Distance, navigation and the sky

- **Navigate by the skyline.** The two chimneys and the water tower are your landmarks. With no sun or shadows, flat light flattens distance, so things are further than they look. Carry a compass.
- **Mark your arrival point** unmistakably, so you can find it when you leave.
- **Go far in the morning only.** A causeway has no shade or shelter, and you can't leave it between its ends.
- **Be under a roof by early afternoon.** When thunder starts inland, you are the tallest thing on a wet salt flat with brine on both sides. Wait out the ten minutes of rain, and expect greasy bunds afterwards.
- **At night, use the orange stain** on the cloud. It is your only fixed direction, and a reminder that help lies a long way down the coast.
- **Listen for the gates.** The thud tells you the tide has turned. The flat sits barely a metre above the springs, and the cloud hides the moon, so learn the rhythm on your first day.

## Edges and surfaces

Everything alive here sits on a surface, and so do most of the hazards. The middle of a street, a shed floor or a causeway is safe and empty, so be careful at edges.

- **Pond margins and creek mud:** don't step off the bunds. Under the leathery mat is soft, black, stinking mud of unknown depth. The fiddler-crab flats by the jetty will take a leg to the knee, and the tide comes in behind you.
- **Salt crust:** a hand thick in the middle, thin at the margins, and sharp.
- **Tide gates and sluices:** keep hands and feet away from the cast-iron flaps and the intake channel on a spring tide. Don't move any boards, because the works is still running.
- **Iron and concrete:** what was going to fall has fallen, but the cladding is rusty lace at shin height and the rebar is burst and jagged. Wear gloves and long trousers, and have a current tetanus shot. Don't climb the water tower, the wagons, the jetty timbers or anything overhead.
- **Doorways:** carry a stick to clear the fly-loaded webs before you walk through.
- **Floors and walls in town:** rats live under the floors and snakes follow them. Wear boots and gaiters, use a light after dark, and never put a hand where you can't see. Shake out boots and bedding.
- **The fly band:** harmless. Close your mouth and walk, and it will part for you.

## The boiler house

Watch it from outside and upwind. The guano dust carries lung disease, the ammonia is strong enough to hurt, the old boiler lagging is probably asbestos, and bats can carry rabies. If you must look in, do it from the doorway, with a proper respirator and sealed goggles, and don't wade.

Don't touch fallen pups, dead birds or anything else dead. The jackals will be shy, so keep them that way by sealing your food.

## Sleeping and health

- **Sleep in a volume.** Use a cot or hammock with a full mosquito net, in the middle of a wide street or a sound-roofed room you've checked, away from walls. Bring a rain fly.
- **Expect to be wet at dawn.** Everything drips when the iron drops below the dew point, including your gear.
- **Assume mosquito-borne disease.** I can't tell you what this world's mosquitoes carry, so bring prophylaxis if you can get it, repellent, and treated clothing. Clothes should be loose, thin, pale and long-sleeved.
- **Protect your skin.** Bring a wide hat and sunglasses, because you will burn through cloud and the inner crust glares. Nothing dries, so pack several pairs of socks, antifungal powder, anti-chafe balm and antiseptic. Salt in a small cut stings and stops it healing, so rinse cuts with fresh water and cover them.
- **Keep food in hard, sealed containers** off the ground, and pack everything out.
- **Empty anything that collects rain**, or you will be breeding mosquitoes.

## Gear

Salt and damp attack everything. Keep electronics in sealed bags, bring spare batteries, and expect lenses to fog. Zips will seize, so wax them. Blades rust overnight. Paper goes limp, so bring pencil and waterproof paper. Carry a headlamp with a red setting, because white light draws every insect from the nearest waterline.

## Conduct and timing

- **Keep well back from the flamingos** and off the bunds where stilts nest. One careless approach can put ten thousand birds into the air, in heat they can't afford.
- **Go before the rains break.** Stay two or three days, since each day in this heat costs you more than the one before. Once the rains come, the flat floods, the bunds soften and the mosquitoes multiply.

## What to make time for

- **Dawn:** the dripping eaves and the birds feeding hardest.
- **The walk inward:** olive, then orange, then pink, with life thinning to nothing. At the innermost pans the crust squeaks underfoot and the pink water lies flat.
- **Dusk:** sit at a distance from the stacks for the full forty minutes while the bats pour out and the falcons take them.

Stand still for these. Standing still is what most things here do, and it is also the safest thing you can do.
