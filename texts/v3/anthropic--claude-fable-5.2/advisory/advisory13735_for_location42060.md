# Preparing for the plateau

Nothing here is trying to kill you. There are no predators larger than a fox and no violent geology. What the plateau does instead is wear on you: it is wet, alkaline, bright, loud, and raining droppings, all of it continuously. Prepare for attrition rather than danger.

## Your body

- **Altitude.** Two thousand metres is mild, but combined with warm, saturated air it will tire you more than you expect. Sweat won't evaporate well, so take the first two days slowly and drink more than feels necessary.
- **Sun.** The haze does not protect you. Thin air, white roofs, white streets, and kilometres of shining pan mean light comes from below as well as above. Bring dark glasses with side shields and sunscreen for the underside of your chin and nose.
- **Skin.** Soda water feels silky because it is turning your skin oils to soap. After a few days your hands crack and your hair turns to straw. Bring a heavy barrier cream or plain oil, and keep any cut under a waterproof dressing, since the runoff carries guano.
- **Eyes.** Wear glasses rather than contact lenses, and pack saline eyewash. A splash of pan brine in the eye is a real injury.
- **Feet.** They will be warm and wet all day, which invites fungus and skin breakdown. Pack many socks, antifungal powder, and something to wear indoors that lets them dry. Dry them every night without fail.
- **Lungs.** Bird and bat droppings carry respiratory diseases where they dry to dust. Wear a proper filter mask near the nitrate sheds, the abandoned terraces, and the kiln vaults.
- **Allergies.** If you react to shellfish or eggs, think hard before going. My guess is that shrimp and eggs are in nearly everything.

## Water

Do not drink the seep water. It is strongly alkaline and probably carries minerals you don't want. Rain runs off roofs white with guano, so that is no better. An ordinary filter removes neither problem.

I suspect the locals drink condensate from the boiler-houses, which is effectively distilled. Ask on arrival and drink what they drink. It will taste flat and contain no minerals, so bring electrolyte salts. Carry a cup with a lid; you will see why within the hour.

## The ground

- **Read the colours.** Orange and yellow mats mean hot water, while purple means cooler. The mats are the nearest thing to a thermometer you will have.
- **Expect to slip.** Wet travertine under a bacterial film is like greased glass. Wear rubber soles with deep tread and walk where the locals do.
- **Distrust fresh crust.** Pale new carbonate near hot channels may be a thin skin over water. Keep to worn paths.
- **Give the plant room.** Live mains carry ground heat and pressure valves vent at street level. The dead pipes stacked along the streets are safe to touch.
- **Avoid low, closed spaces.** Water that deposits carbonate is releasing carbon dioxide, and geothermal ground often adds hydrogen sulphide. Both gases pool in cellars and cuttings. Don't sleep below street level unless locals do, and leave at once if you feel dizzy.

## Clothing and gear

- **Hat.** Get a broad one, in oiled canvas if you can buy one there. This is the single most important item.
- **Fabrics.** Alkali destroys wool, silk, and leather, so wear cotton or synthetics and rubber boots. Choose pale colours, because dark cloth shows every hit.
- **Metals.** Soda pits aluminium, and iron rusts in days. Prefer stainless steel, brass, and plastic.
- **Descaling.** Bring citric acid or vinegar. A short visit won't crust your gear, since the walls gain well under a millimetre a year. But zips, threads, and lenses will pick up a gritty bloom that a weak acid wipe removes.
- **Dry storage.** Use sealed bags with desiccant for electronics and documents. Nothing dries on its own here.
- **Pack shape.** Bring a narrow one. Old-quarter doorways have grown inward, and a wide load will not fit through.
- **Dusk kit.** Bring a head net for the midges and carry your lamp at hip height. A headlamp draws the cloud straight into your face.
- **Ear protection.** You will want earplugs for sleeping and near the works.

## Talking

Outdoors, speech is useless. People sign at the pan edges and save their voices for inside. This suggests some preparation:

1. Learn a handful of signs on your first day: yes, no, where, how much, thanks, hot.
2. Keep your hands free and visible, which means carrying a backpack and nothing in your hands.
3. Don't gesture idly. In a place where hands are speech, fidgeting may read as babbling.
4. Carry a waterproof notebook and a wax pencil as a fallback.

Being invited indoors to talk is probably a real courtesy. Treat it as one, and don't shout questions in the street.

## Food

Nothing green grows, so anything from a plant has been hauled up the scarp. I'd expect the local diet to be eggs, birds, shrimp paste, and possibly fly larvae, with imported grain. Bring vitamin C and whatever dried fruit you can carry. Fresh fruit or herbs would likely make a welcome gift. Eat indoors or under cover, and hang your food at night, because the rats are good climbers.

## Lodging

Your room will be narrow, warm, and damp. A sheet is all the bedding you need. Keep everything off the floor and away from the walls, since both are wet.

There may be a channel of water running across the floor from the threshold. Leave it alone and don't block it with your bag. The geckos on the wall are eating flies on your behalf.

## Customs

- **Never harm a fox.** Don't chase one or throw things at one, even if it is investigating your pack.
- **Don't remark on the droppings.** Nobody else does. Flinching, joking, or complaining marks you as a nuisance.
- **Don't flush the birds.** Guano is a measured harvest, and the ledges belong to someone. Startling a colony for fun is probably close to trampling a crop. The same goes for taking eggs.
- **Leave the blind fish alone.** If children must put them back, so must you.
- **Be careful on the eastern scarp.** The smooth white shapes in the hot seeps are the dead. Don't sit on them, touch them, or photograph them without asking.
- **Don't expect haste.** This city measures change in decades. Impatience will get you nowhere and will be noticed.

## The daily rhythm

- **Early morning** is the clearest. Take the scarp road then for the long view: the white rings of old terraces, the steaming works, and the pink water beyond.
- **Mid-morning** brings the haze, and distant landmarks dissolve. Street names are worn nearly flat, so navigate by tram lines, by the stacks, and by the pan edge.
- **Shift whistles** send a hundred thousand birds up at once. Learn the times, and be under a roof or at least under your hat.
- **Early afternoon** brings rain off the pans. Afterwards the streets are slick with re-wetted guano.
- **Dusk** is the bat column, which takes forty minutes to pass and is best watched from across a pan. The midges rise at the same time, so put on your net before they do.

Use the trams for distance. The city is wider than a week of walking.

## What it will feel like

The smell arrives first: ammonia, brine, and a little sulphur. You stop noticing after a day. The fly-line looks appalling, but walk into it calmly with your mouth shut and it parts for you.

The harder adjustment is that nothing is ever still, quiet, dry, or green. Some travellers find this oppressive by the third day, so a small green object from home may be worth carrying. Others find the place oddly restful, because nothing in it is in a hurry.

## Leaving

Scrub your boots and gear thoroughly before you step onward. Brine shrimp eggs survive years of drying, and bacterial mats travel well. You will smell of ammonia for about a week.
