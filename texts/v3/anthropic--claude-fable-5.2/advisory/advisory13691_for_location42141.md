# Preparing for the steel plain

The plain feels like the gentlest place you will ever stand: no wind, no predators that care about you, soft light, air the temperature of your own skin. That last part is what will kill you. Your whole plan should be built around two facts: you cannot shed heat here, and you are the only thing on the plain that varies.

## The heat is the main danger

A bath left standing ten minutes is roughly body temperature, perhaps a little above. The air is the same and it is saturated. That is why nothing steams and nothing chills, and it means every mechanism your body has for cooling is switched off.

- **Sweat will not evaporate.** It will pour off you, more than a litre an hour, and do nothing except dehydrate you.
- **Fanning yourself does nothing**, and there is no wind.
- **There is no shade**, because there is no sun to hide from.
- **The water in the gaps is no cooler than the air.** Wetting your shirt or lying in a channel gains you nothing.
- **Nowhere is cooler than anywhere else.** The description is exact on this point, and it is the reason the flagging hangs straight.

A resting body makes about a hundred watts of heat. With nowhere for it to go, your core temperature climbs about a degree an hour sitting still, and faster walking. You will feel comfortable, then heavy, then stupid, and by then you are in trouble. The only cold is what you carry in or what lies back through your door.

So treat the visit as a dive:

- **Limit the time.** Without active cooling, plan on ninety minutes at most. Turn back when a third of your cooling is used.
- **Bring cold.** Wear an ice or phase-change vest with insulation on the outside. Otherwise the wet air condenses on it and melts it in a fraction of the rated time. Carry ice slurry in vacuum flasks, since drinking cold is one of the few ways to dump heat.
- **Measure, don't judge.** Use a core temperature capsule if you can get one, or at least a thermometer and a heart-rate monitor. Set a hard abort number before you go. Your sense of how you feel will be the first thing to fail.
- **Replace salt**, not just water.
- **Go with a partner.** Nothing here lies dead for longer than a minute, and the system will not make an exception for a visitor. If you go down alone, what remains will be your synthetics.
- **Don't sleep there.** There will be nights above the lid, but you should not be present for one.

## Footing and clothing

Clothing can't help you cool, so wear as little as protects you. Choose thin, loose, fast-draining synthetics, because nothing will ever dry.

- **Shoes:** Wear soft-rubber deck soles, since every surface is wet steel with a slick film on it. Do not use studs or cleats. They scratch through mill scale that has stayed intact for a very long time.
- **Gloves:** Bring thin cut-resistant ones. The plinth corners, unground welds, and fin edges are all still factory-sharp.
- **Knee pads:** You will want to kneel at the gaps, since that is the only way to see that the cloud is shrimp.
- **Head net:** The fly haze stops at knee height, so when you kneel or sit your face is in it. The flies don't bite, but you will inhale them.
- **Wounds:** Cover every existing cut with a waterproof dressing. Dress new ones at once. The film eats steel, and the scavengers here are quick.
- **Gait:** Walk at a steady pace and don't stamp or run. The isopods part around a boot that moves predictably. A fall onto a plinth corner is the most likely injury after the heat.

## Water and air

Don't drink the water, and keep it out of your nose and eyes. Warm recirculating water and a drizzle too fine to see are the classic conditions for the pneumonias that breed in cooling towers. A mask adds heat load, so a short stay is your real protection. Note the date of your visit, and take any fever or cough in the following two weeks seriously.

## Navigation

Every crossing is identical. The shadows don't move, there is no sun, and a compass over that much steel and machinery is worthless. The heat numbers on the plates are batch numbers, shared by many plates, and the datum studs repeat the same small heights.

What you do have:

- **The grid.** Plates are twelve paces and racks come every sixty, so there are five plates between racks. Count with a tally clicker and write the count down.
- **The water.** It flows one way in the gaps, and every eel points upstream. This is your only compass. Check it before you move off from any stop, because a minute spent turning around a sump to look at crabs is enough to lose your heading for good.
- **Magnets.** The whole world is steel. Bring bright magnetic markers, lay them as you go, and collect every one on the way back. Do not write on anything. The yellow crayon and the orange flagging show how long a mark lasts here.

Record your arrival point by the plate's heat number, the nearest stud value, the plate count to the nearest rack, and the flow direction. Then don't go far. Plate one is the same as plate ten thousand, so distance buys you only risk.

## The animals

None of them will bother you. The birds may be the hardest part. There is one on every support, on every tier, thousands in view, silent and facing nowhere in particular.

- **Stay in the aisles.** Every space between fin panels has a web across it, so don't cut through.
- **Keep your hands out of the grated channels.** The eels are as thick as a forearm.
- **Expect drips** under the lowest pipe tier.
- **Don't dangle** anything small and pale near a wader.
- **Don't feed anything, and don't collect anything.** The number hatched matches the number taken. A crumb is a surplus and a specimen is a deficit, and you would be the only source of either in the plain's history.

## Conduct

Whatever you shed in skin, sweat, and hair will be taken up and accounted for within minutes. The rest is your responsibility.

- **Pack out everything**, including all bodily waste.
- **Skip the chemicals.** Wear no sunscreen or repellent, since there is no sun and nothing bites. Don't use soap.
- **Tether everything.** Put a lanyard on every object you carry. The gaps are two fingers wide, and whatever falls in rides the flow to the pump galleries, where it could become the first variation in a load that has never varied.
- **Leave nothing sitting on the deck.** An object shelters the film beneath it from the grazers. The film thickens, turns orange, and starts to pit the steel.
- **Leave the plant alone.** Do not touch valves, float switches, or photocells. Stay clear of where the big lines come up through the deck. They are hot inside, whatever their surfaces feel like.
- **Do not go down the sump ladders.** Below the deck are confined spaces, hot water, and very large machinery.

Someone built this, and someone once walked through and wrote OK on a weld. You are a guest in a working plant whose operators aren't in view.

## Equipment

- **Waterproofing:** Everything should be rated for immersion, not just splashes.
- **Notes:** Use waterproof paper and pencil.
- **Optics:** Gear brought from a cooler world will stream with condensation until it warms, so let cameras equilibrate in a sealed bag. Bring a macro lens for the snails and their thumb's-width spacing.
- **Watch:** The lamps hold the light at one level, so you will have no sense of time passing.

## Leaving

Clean your gear before you arrive, so you don't bring in anything foreign. Clean it much harder when you leave. The film stays a thin, harmless slick only because it is grazed exactly as fast as it grows. If you carry it home on a zipper or a knife hinge, it travels without its snails, and left alone it does what it would do here. Scrub, disinfect, and dry everything, especially anything steel.

## What it will be like

The plain is quieter than you expect. You will hear a drip, a trickle, and the dry hiss of millions of feet on steel, and you will feel something huge turning in oil through your soles. Your own breath will be the loudest thing for kilometres. Within a few minutes you will notice that you are the only thing in sight that moves without a reason, and that the place is neither hostile nor welcoming. It will simply close behind you the way the isopods close behind a boot.

The comfort is the hazard, because the warmth never feels like an emergency. Leave when your abort number says to, however fine you feel, and count your magnets on the way out.
