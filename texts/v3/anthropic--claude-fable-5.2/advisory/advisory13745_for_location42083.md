## The short version

This is two trips, and you should decide which one you're making before you leave home.

- **The rim visit** is a hot, high day hike. From the rim the whole pocket is a room, and every plant is within reach of a long stick. Most of what's worth seeing can be seen from here, including the noon turn.
- **The floor visit** is a full-day expedition. You cross a plain of hot, oiled glass that gets higher as you go down, among machinery that is partly not there yet. The nearest plant is a morning away.

I'm working from the description, so where I say "likely," I'm guessing.

## The mountain

- **Acclimatize.** The pocket sits above the last cloud. Spend nights at intermediate height on the way up. If you have a headache and nausea at the rim, do not go in.
- **Expect no trail.** The cone is new, so the lava will be sharp and loose, with thin crust over hollows. Wear stiff boots and leather gloves, and probe ahead with a staff.
- **Distrust forecasts.** The weather is older than the mountain and likely hasn't adjusted to it. Cloud and wind on the lower slopes may behave as though the cone isn't there.
- **Carry a volcanic gas monitor.** Nothing in the description suggests gas, but this is a young volcano and the pocket is a hollow.

## Clothing and footwear

- **Boots:** thick, heat-rated, oil-resistant soles, such as nitrile foundry or roofer's boots. Ordinary rubber softens in mineral oil. Add insulating insoles and wool socks, because the heat will come up through the soles over hours of standing.
- **Fabric:** wool, canvas and leather, with long sleeves and long trousers. A fall here lands you on hot, oiled volcanic glass, which means a burn and a cut at once. Knee pads are worth the weight.
- **Sun:** glacier glasses, a brimmed hat, sunscreen and lip balm. The sun is fierce at the rim and worse toward the center, and the black glass and glossy leaves reflect it.
- **Seat:** a cork or thick wool-felt pad, or a small stool with non-metal feet. You can't sit or kneel on the rock, and you will need to rest.
- **Two pairs of gloves:** one for working, and one kept clean and bagged for the climb out. Oily hands on a hot glass rim are how people fail to leave.

## Water and heat

- **Bring all your water.** There has never been any here, and the plant sap is lubricant. You are in heat, sun, dry air and altitude all day, so carry five or six liters with electrolytes and salty food.
- **Carry your urine out in a bottle.** Don't pour water on the rock either. It will spatter on hot oil, and it would leave the first water stain the room has ever had.

## The air

The altitude here is inverted. Walking down toward the center takes you higher, and at the lowest point you are above the summit, by an amount the description doesn't give. The darkening sky suggests it isn't small.

- **Know which way relief lies.** If you feel bad, go outward toward the wall, or straight up to the rim.
- **Use an altimeter.** A barometric altimeter is your best instrument for depth. A pulse oximeter is a useful check on yourself.
- **Set a turnaround number before you enter.** Hypoxia removes the judgment you would need to set it later.
- **Treat the center as optional.** I would skip it as an ordinary traveler.
- **If you bring bottled oxygen, keep it clean.** Oil and pressurized oxygen can ignite on contact. Keep the regulator and fittings sealed in a bag, and never handle them with oily gloves.

## Moving in a room that supplies floor

- **Don't count on measuring distance.** I'd expect pacing and GPS to be unreliable. The same gap between two plants may be hours or a few paces.
- **Don't count on a compass.** With the magnetite drum this close, it will probably follow the drum.
- **Navigate by what holds.** The sun and shadows are reliable. Air pressure tells you how far in you are. The copper veins thicken toward the drum.
- **Test the exit first.** The rim is said to be an arm's length overhead everywhere. A few minutes in, reach up, touch it and climb out, so you know how hard that is on hot glass in thin air. Wipe your soles before you try.
- **Post a spotter on the rim with a long, dry wooden pole.** From up there you are likely always within reach, however far you have walked. They may see you take a whole morning to cross two paces, so warn them.
- **Stay in contact with your partner.** The room keeps emptiness between its things, and I'd assume that includes people. Hold a short natural-fiber cord between you, or one step apart may become an hour.
- **Keep everything attached to you.** A pack set down and stepped away from may end up far off.
- **Be out before the light goes.** Daylight is the only finished thing here, and you need it for the next section.

## The machinery

Everything moves slowly, so you are unlikely to be caught by anything. The danger is what isn't there yet.

- **Treat every gap as occupied.** Torque crosses the missing shaft, and teeth that don't exist still mesh. Keep hands, straps, staff and anything else out of the gaps, and leave nothing in them.
- **Read the moss.** Thick, packed moss marks a gap where a part is coming.
- **Trust shadows over objects.** The light casts a shadow for the absent shaft. If you see a shadow with nothing casting it, something is there. Morning light gives longer shadows and is easier to read.
- **Stay off the copper.** It carries current. Step over the veins and never bridge two of them. Use a wooden staff with a rubber tip, and let nothing metal dangle.
- **Keep clear of the drum** if you have a pacemaker or implant. Keep steel and magnetic cards away from it too.
- **Don't put anything under the slab.** It moves a finger's width in a season, but it comes down on nothing.
- **Go around undecided rock.** It has weight and heat but no grain or color you can hold. It may bear your weight, but you can't judge its grip, and staring at it will probably make you queasy. Probe it with the staff if you have to cross.
- **Go slowly near crisp edges.** An edge with nothing behind it is still an edge.
- **Leave the iron beads.** They are hot and on their way back into the rock.

## Oil, plants and fire

- **Step on dull rock, not shine.** There is an oil film on glass. Take short steps.
- **Bring no flame.** That means no stove and no smoking. The rock is hot and the room is oiled.
- **Don't break a stem.** There are seven or eight plants in the whole place.
- **Stay out of the pollen's path.** At the driest hour, pollen rides the charge along the copper in a pale thread. Avoid static-prone clothing, which is another reason for wool. A dust mask is sensible.
- **Don't drink the oil.** Carry eyewash in case it gets in your eyes.

## First aid

- Burn dressings.
- Wound closures for glass cuts.
- Blister care.
- Altitude medication you have been shown how to use.

## What it will be like

The floor will feel lonelier than it looked from above. Everything is rich and far apart, and the low hum sits in your chest as pressure. It may read as dread, but it is only the hum. The walls tick, and at long intervals a drop of oil hisses on the stone.

If you can, be near the drum, or back on the rim, at noon. The drum finishes its turn, the shadow of the missing shaft comes back to where it started, and one leaf lets go of one drop.

Then leave it as you found it. Nothing here was made, so don't leave anything made behind.
