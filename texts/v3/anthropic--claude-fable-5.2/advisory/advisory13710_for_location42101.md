# Preparing for the plateau

Almost nothing here will attack you. The dangers are slow ones: wet feet, lost bearings, a soft edge, and a warm ground that makes sitting down feel like the best idea you have ever had. Prepare for a place that absorbs things rather than one that fights them.

## Arriving, and the rim

- **Arrive on top, well inland, if you have any say.** The edge is not rock. It is an overhanging lip of wet fibre forty metres thick, with nothing beneath the last stretch but air and falling mist. It will feel exactly like the ground everywhere else until it doesn't.
- **Stay hundreds of metres back from the rim.** The one exception is wherever the pebble line begins. Someone has been fetching stones from the rim for a very long time, so a safe approach presumably exists there.
- **Expect altitude.** A day or two of headache and short breath is likely. Plan easy first days.

## Walking

Every step sinks and the surface gives nothing back, so walking is like crossing a wet mattress the size of a continent.

- **Halve your usual distances, then trim them again.** Carry less than you think you need of everything except socks and food.
- **Copy the grazers and spread your weight.** Bring bog shoes or small snowshoes, and poles with wide baskets.
- **Use grazer tracks.** A line of heavy prints is proof the mat bears weight there.
- **Look twice at hummocks before stepping on them.** If one breathes, you are inside a resting herd. Back out slowly the way you came.
- **Step between the cushion domes where you can.** Thousands of small amphibians live in them. Don't handle them: your skin chemistry is bad for them and theirs may be bad for you.

## Wet

Nothing you bring will be dry again until you leave, except what you protect on purpose.

- **Feet are your main medical risk.** They will spend all day in warm, tea-coloured water. Take quick-draining footwear or tall rubber boots, and several pairs of socks. Keep one pair sealed for sleeping only. Dry, air and powder your feet every night.
- **Wear synthetics or wool, never cotton.** Breathable shells stop working in saturated air. A poncho or even an umbrella will serve better in windless drizzle.
- **Dress for cool damp air.** The cold starts above the knee, and the warmth comes from below.
- **Cover every cut.** Clean and re-dress wounds daily, and carry antifungal cream. The whole place is a warm, wet culture medium.
- **Protect electronics and paper.** Use dry bags and desiccant, and bring waterproof paper and a pencil. There is no sun to charge anything, so carry all the power you need. Lenses will fog and then grow things.

## Sleeping

Wherever you lie, your weight presses a hollow, and the hollow fills.

- **Use a tent with a deep waterproof floor, or a bivy, on an inflatable mat.** The mat is there to spread your weight. The ground supplies the heat.
- **Choose a freestanding shelter.** Push pegs in by hand and never hammer them (see below).
- **Don't hang anything from the trees.** Every branch is someone's habitat, and something long and low uses the crowns as a road. I have no reason to think it is dangerous, and no evidence that it isn't. I would sleep in the open between trees rather than under one.
- **Ventilate.** You are lying on a ferment, and carbon dioxide can pool in still hollows. If you wake with a headache or feel oddly heavy and content, stand up and move.

## Fire, water, food, waste

- **No fire.** There is no dead wood, since even broken twigs are green inside. There is nothing dry, and burning anything on forty metres of peat-in-waiting would be wrong. Bring a small stove on a base plate, or eat cold. A hot drink is worth its weight here for morale.
- **Catch rain on a tarp for drinking.** Water is everywhere. The ground water is probably tolerable if filtered and treated, but it is acidic, tannic and years old.
- **Bring all your food, sealed.** Anything opened moulds within days. Don't eat the local life.
- **Pack your waste out.** This is one body of water going round and round, taking years to cross the mat. Whatever you put in stays, and people drink from it. Use no soap, repellent or chemicals on the ground.

## Finding your way

There is no sun, shadow, star, stream or landmark, and the fog erases the horizon. This is how you actually die here: you lose your exit and walk in circles.

- **Test a compass on arrival and trust it only if it behaves.** I can't promise this world has a usable magnetic field.
- **Keep a written dead-reckoning log.** Count paces, and wear a watch, since "noon" is only a brightening.
- **Carry flagged wands.** Press them in by hand at intervals and collect every one on the way back. Leave nothing: a cache left for a season becomes a hummock.
- **Use your own trail at night.** Your prints glow blue-green for about an hour. Look back, and if the line curves, you are drifting. Keep your lamp dim or red so you can still see the glow, and so you don't blind animals made for twilight.
- **Use the stone line and the knotted arches as a handrail.** Walk beside the stones, never on them.

## The ground is the air here

Sound dies within a few metres, so shouting to a companion is useless. Stay within sight of each other.

Everything that matters travels through the mat, and that includes you. Your footfalls move outward at about the speed of a brisk walk, which is faster than you can manage on this surface. Everything in your path knows about you well before you arrive.

- **Walk evenly.** Don't stamp, run, jump or hammer. A stamp is a word here, and you don't know which one.
- **Listen with your feet.** Several times a day, stand still, barefoot if you can bear it, for a few minutes. A slow rhythmic pressure in the soles is a herd, most of a day out. A faint purr is the amphibians answering something. With practice you will get a sense of direction.
- **Give herds the right of way.** They are slow, and you will have hours of notice.
- **Know what the groan is.** A few times a year a district of the mat settles with a sound below hearing. You would feel it as sudden causeless dread and pressure in the chest. Sit down and let it pass. This is one more reason to stay away from the rim.

## The people

Someone lives here, has done for an almost unthinkable time, and was at the dome very recently. One knot is still pale. The knots continue below the surface as far as an arm can reach, and the mat rises only a finger-width in a lifetime. You are looking at one unbroken habit older than most civilisations, carried on with bare hands. Nothing is cut or burnt, and there are no tools.

They felt you coming. If the dome is empty, that may be why.

- **Leave their works alone.** Don't untie a knot or tie one, and don't lift or add a stone. I don't know whether these are roads, records or graves, and neither do you. A stranger's knot might be a courtesy or a forgery.
- **Keep any blade in your pack.** Nothing here needs cutting, and a blade is not a thing to introduce casually to people who have chosen, or only known, hands.
- **Use the dome only in real need.** It is the one dry floor for days around, trodden dry by generations of feet. If you must shelter there, keep to one side. Use no stove inside, leave nothing, and go if your ankles tell you someone is coming.
- **If you meet them, sit down and keep your hands open and visible.** Stay quiet and let them set the distance. Sitting is the least threatening thing you can do in a place where footsteps are speech.

## Body and mind

Expect weeks of shadowless grey-green, with dripping, silence, and no far and no near. Some people find it the most restful place they have ever been, and some come apart by the fourth day. Bring one brightly coloured object and keep a routine.

The warmth from below, the dim light and the tiredness will all argue for staying put. The local rule is that stillness becomes plant, and some of those hummocks are grazers that stopped walking. So make a rule of your own: move every day, even a little, and shift your gear when you do. Things will start growing on your pack straps within a week or two.

## Leaving

Clean everything before you come and again before you go. You will be carrying spores in your seams, your boot treads and your hair. Check your skin and nails for anything green.

Count your wands. Then look back once at night and watch your own prints dim at the far end. Within an hour the plateau will show no trace of you, and that is the right outcome.
