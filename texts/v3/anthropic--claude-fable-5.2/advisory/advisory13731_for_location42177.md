## The short version

Nothing in this basin will chase you, sweep you away, or startle you. Everything that could hurt you there is warm and gradual: heat you can't shed, a surface you can't find, depth that doesn't feel like depth, and hours that don't feel like hours. The place gives no warnings, so you have to bring your own: instruments, a line, a partner, and limits you set on the rim before you get comfortable.

You will also be the fastest and most disruptive thing in the valley, so part of the preparation is learning to do less.

## Decide how far in you are going

- **The rim only.** This is a hard mountain walk with a view of the green dome, the steam, the swifts, and the daily rise and fall of the mat.
- **The canopy and the top few metres of water.** This is where most of the wonder is: the light shafts, the pearling, the salamanders, the first glassy skin on the bark. With basic dive training and discipline, an ordinary traveler can do this.
- **The deep forest and the floor.** At forty to fifty metres, at altitude, under a ceiling, this is technical diving. If you aren't trained for it, don't go. The trees are leafed all the way down, and you can read much of their history from ten metres.

## The rim

- **Altitude.** Acclimatize for a few days before the climb. The sun is strong and the nights are probably cold, even though the basin below is warm.
- **Rock.** The basalt cuts. Bring leather gloves, stiff-soled boots, tough trousers, and a helmet. The scree hasn't settled, so move one person at a time and never directly below each other.
- **Camp.** Camp on the rim, because nothing dries in the basin. Sharp rock will puncture an inflatable mattress, so bring closed-cell foam and a sacrificial groundsheet.
- **Water.** Fresh basalt holds no surface water. The afternoon rain is reliable, so catch it with a tarp. Don't drink the lake: it is fed by vents, and filters don't remove dissolved minerals.
- **Clothing.** You need a cold, dry set for the rim and a hot, wet set for the basin. Change at the steam line. Climbing out soaked into thin evening air can chill you fast.

## The steam

The air over the canopy is body-warm and saturated, so sweat won't cool you. Treat every minute in the steam as heat exposure. Move slowly, rest often, and climb out to cool down before you feel you need to.

This is also a closed, windless bowl above volcanic vents. I can't promise the steam layer is only steam. Heavy gases such as carbon dioxide can pool in still air, most likely after a calm night. Carry a gas monitor at chest height. Go down after mid-morning, when the forest is producing oxygen, and leave if the monitor alarms or anyone gets a headache.

Seal electronics in dry bags and let them warm up before opening them. Bring pencil and waterproof paper.

## The surface that isn't there

This is the central problem. You won't feel yourself enter the water, so don't rely on feeling.

- **Breathe from your supply early.** Put your mask on and start breathing from your supply before you reach the hanging roots, while you are still plainly in air.
- **Wear flotation in the canopy.** The top growth is young and supple and may not hold you. Falling through is soft but disorienting.
- **Enter along rock.** At the rim wall or the dam face, solid stone leads continuously from water to air. You can follow it like a handrail.
- **Run a line.** Anchor a continuous guideline on the rock, pass it through the mat, and keep it within reach at all times. The mat closes behind you, and from below it is an unbroken green ceiling for kilometres.
- **Keep a tender above.** One person stays above the mat at the line.
- **Streamline your gear.** Hair-fine roots and ribbon-weed will catch anything that dangles.
- **Don't trust the first gas you meet coming up.** By afternoon there is a layer of the forest's oxygen trapped under the mat. Your face coming out of the water into it doesn't mean you have surfaced. Follow the line until you are in air and can see your tender.

## Under the mat

- **Space.** The trunks stand an arm's length apart with branches meshed between them, so it is like swimming through a hedge. Use small, close-fitting equipment and move slowly.
- **Buoyancy.** There is no current to correct or carry you. If you are heavy, you sink straight down, like any dropped thing. Fresh water needs less weight than the sea, so check your weighting in the first two metres and wear a depth alarm.
- **Heat.** You need no insulation, only a thin full-body skin against cuts. The water can't cool you any better than the steam can. Keep immersions short, perhaps forty minutes, and stop at the first flush or dizziness.
- **Navigation.** Rely on the line. The basalt may deflect a compass. The light shafts slide with the sun and give only a rough bearing.
- **Sound.** Open-circuit bubbles are loud and will knock the oxygen beads from the leaves above you. To hear the pearling, hang still on the line around late morning and breathe as slowly as you can. It is at the edge of hearing.
- **Touch.** Touch nothing. A handprint in the felt takes a season to green over, and an opal branch took a decade to form. Silicified wood is brittle and breaks to a glass edge.

## The deep, if you are qualified

- **Decompression.** Altitude makes it more conservative, and you will do it under a ceiling. Narcosis will be hard to tell from the place's own dreaminess.
- **The floor.** Stay off it. The stone leaf-litter is eggshell-thin, and anything you stir up will hang there with no current to clear it.
- **The vents.** Nothing grows within a body-length of them. Treat that bare margin as your limit too.
- **The gulp.** If a vent clears while you are down, you will see the light bend as the warm dome rises. Hold the line, let it pass, and end the dive.

## The day

- **Dawn:** cold on the rim. Check gear and gas.
- **Mid-morning:** descend as the pearling begins.
- **Noon:** the forest is silvered top to bottom. This is the hour you came for.
- **Early afternoon:** be out. Cloud comes over, the light under the mat dims, and the rock is slick in the rain. Collect water.
- **Dusk:** watch the mat settle from the rim.

Never go below at night. It is totally dark, and the forest is respiring, using oxygen instead of making it.

## Health

- Skin that stays wet at body temperature softens and breaks down, so dry out completely every night.
- Clean every cut at once. Warm fresh water is kind to microbes.
- Keep the water out of your nose and dry your ears.
- Drink far more than you think you need, and drink it cool.

## What you carry in stays

The lake loses water only to steam and to a slow seep, so nothing washes out.

- Use no sunscreen, repellent, or soap in the basin. Cover up with clothing for sun protection.
- Disinfect all gear before you arrive. The salamanders have never met your world's amphibian diseases.
- Tether everything. A dropped object falls straight to the floor and is glassed over there. Take every metre of line back out.
- Take nothing, however small the opal.

## What it will be like

It will be like floating inside something asleep and breathing. You will feel safe, and that feeling is the thing to watch. The salamander can stay on its branch for days because it belongs there, and you don't. Set your turnaround time on the rim, keep to it, and climb out while you still want to stay.
