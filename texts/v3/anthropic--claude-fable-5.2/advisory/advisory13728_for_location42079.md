# Preparing for the Crown House

The Crown House will feel like a greenhouse, but your body will be in conditions closer to the cruising altitude of an airliner. It is a warm, wet, green room with frogs in it, yet it sits on top of eight kilometres of flue on top of a mountain, with louvres open to a sky dark enough to show the horizon's curve. Most of the preparation follows from not letting the warmth and the moss fool you.

## The air

- **Assume it is thin.** The louvres are open, so the room is at outside pressure. I'd guess the altitude at 10 to 14 km, where an unacclimatised person has a minute or two of useful thought, and you should plan for the worse end. The frogs and swifts have had generations to adapt, so their comfort tells you nothing about yours.
- **Bring oxygen as the core of your kit.** You need a sealed mask, a regulator that can deliver under pressure, and at least double the supply you think you need. The gauge should be readable at a glance, because you will not hear a hiss, a leak or an alarm.
- **Protect against sudden decompression.** If you step straight in from a sea-level world, you risk the bends, and painful ears, sinuses and gut. Pre-breathe pure oxygen for an hour beforehand, and don't go with a cold.
- **Carry a gas monitor with a visual alarm.** What comes up the throat is the exhaust of the works. The smell of oil and iron suggests it is not clean, and there may be carbon monoxide.
- **Keep oxygen fittings free of oil.** Oil mist and pressurised oxygen are a fire risk.

## The note

Still levels three metres apart imply a wavelength of about six metres. That gives a tone somewhere under 60 Hz, loud enough to shake webs to lint.

- **Hearing protection will only partly work.** Wear moulded plugs under heavy earmuffs, but nothing blocks that frequency well. Much of it reaches you through bone and chest.
- **Expect it to work on your whole body.** You may get blurred vision, buzzing teeth, a fluttering chest, nausea, and possibly vertigo or unexplained dread. A mouthguard helps with the teeth, and glasses need a tight strap.
- **Set your time limit before you go in.** I would say well under an hour. Your judgment inside will be degraded by the noise and the altitude, so keep to the limit by the clock.
- **The still bands are probably the loudest.** In a standing wave, the levels where the air doesn't move are where the pressure swings hardest, and your eardrums respond to pressure. The mossy bands are likely the worst for your ears, while the bare bands shake your clothes and skin but press less on your hearing. Your body spans more than half a cycle, so crouching may change what you hear and how well you see. If your vision smears, try moving your head half a metre up or down.
- **You will lose all feedback from sound.** You will not hear your footsteps, your breathing, a creaking plate or a companion's scream. Check by eye what you would normally check by ear.

## The throat

The hole is 62 metres across and has no reliable guard. The handrails have been cased in fig for generations and may be rusted to nothing inside.

- **Stay against the wall.** Use the wall-side half of the gallery, and go to hands and knees if your balance goes.
- **Wear a harness with two lanyards.** Always have one clipped before you unclip the other. Anchor to structural steel you can see, not to rail or fig. Use triple-action carabiners, because vibration unscrews ordinary screwgates. It may have done the same to bolts in the building.
- **Distrust the moss.** A hand's depth of wet growth has sat on that plate for a very long time and may hide rust, holes or the edge itself. The line where the aerial roots turn upward marks the edge.
- **Tether everything.** On bare plate, anything you put down will walk with the vibration, possibly toward the hole. Heavy things that go over fall silently into fans and whatever tends them. Light things such as gloves, paper and hats go up and out through the louvres.
- **Control anything loose.** Straps, cords, hair and rope slack will stream upward into your face and your gear. Tape or tuck them all.
- **Trust your feet over your eyes.** Nothing hangs down, the roots point at the roof, and the mist lies in flat shelves that look like surfaces. The visual cues for "down" are wrong here. The girder pools are the only honest levels.

## Talking

Voice, radio and throat mics are all useless.

- **Agree hand and touch signals beforehand.** Carry a slate or printed cards.
- **Borrow from the frogs.** A pale palm or card shown deliberately can be read across the whole room.
- **Have one unmistakable signal for "out, now".** Make it a light pattern or a tug on a shared line, and rehearse it.

## Warm inside, lethal outside

- **Dress for wet warmth inside.** Inside is humid and dripping, and you will be soaked within minutes. Wear thin synthetic layers and boots with soft, grippy soles for wet moss and polished wet steel.
- **Dress fully for the outer gallery.** A few metres away it is perhaps −50 °C, with thin air, possible high wind and rime on every surface. Wet clothes will freeze on you. Dry off and put on a shell, insulation, heavy gloves, goggles and face cover before you open the hatch. Never touch outside metal with bare skin.
- **Guard against ice in your oxygen kit.** Moisture in the mask and regulator can freeze when you go out.
- **Protect against ultraviolet.** It is fierce at that height, so use dark lenses and cover your skin.
- **Check the hatch first.** It may be half-seized with liverwort and root, so make sure it opens and closes from both sides. The room is a uniform ring, dim and brown, so mark your exit with a small light and take the light with you when you leave.

## Water, power and machinery

- **Don't drink the breath-water.** The taste of coin comes from dissolved metal, including whatever was in the old paint it has been cutting through. Carry your own water. You will dehydrate quickly on bottled oxygen.
- **Treat everything as live.** The lamps are lit, the actuators move and the motors are warm, so the bus bars carry current. They are wet and hidden in greenery. A conductor with no moss on it is warm, which means it is carrying current. Don't reach into cable trays or lean on casings.
- **Leave the crane and gauge board alone.** The louvres move too slowly to catch you, but don't wedge anything between the blades.

## Light, equipment and manners

- **Use a dim red or amber headlamp.** A bright white beam will pull the moths off the lamps and onto your face.
- **Avoid flash and bright colours.** The swifts and frogs communicate in flashes of white. Wear dull colours and keep your pale signal card for when you mean it.
- **Expect your equipment to struggle.** Image stabilisers will thrash, screws will back out and battery doors will open. Tape everything shut and use fast shutter speeds. Sound recordings will be one saturated tone.
- **Step carefully.** Avoid the webs in the quiet bands, the pools, and the bright margin of new moss, which is a whole season's advance of perhaps a hand's width.

## When to go, and afterwards

- **Go at low sun.** That is when light comes through the louvres in level bars and the mist shelves show in the void. Stand with your back to the wall and look across, and you will see the far side flickering with raised feet.
- **Go as a pair.** Stay within arm's reach of each other, but clip to separate anchors.
- **Expect after-effects.** Outside, silence will ring. You may feel the note in your chest for hours, with headache and heavy fatigue. Rest somewhere at normal pressure and stay on oxygen until you have descended.
- **Don't go at all** if you have heart, lung or inner-ear trouble.
