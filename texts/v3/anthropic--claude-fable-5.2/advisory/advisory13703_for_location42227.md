# Preparing for the kept valley

Nothing in this valley is hostile. The dangers are those of a cold bog, plus the fact that your ears, eyes, legs and sense of number will each give you false readings. Most preparation is about staying warm while permanently wet and about deciding in advance which senses to trust.

## Start at the rim

- **Base yourself on the rim.** Camp on the bare rock where water has scoured it. It is the only place you will ever get dry, so treat the valley as a series of day trips.
- **Spend a day just looking.** Animals turn back here because they cannot parse it, and you will feel the same refusal as mild nausea or vertigo. Let it pass before you go down.
- **Draw your map from above.** The hanging streams read as contour lines in air, they do not move, and they all run to the south-west gap. Mark the pools of hard blue too, without counting them.
- **Check the western sky before each descent.** Once inside the mist you won't see a front coming.

## Cold and wet

The temperature is the average of every season, which I'd guess is around eight to ten degrees, and it never changes. That is not dangerous for an hour. Over days in saturated air, where nothing ever dries, it is.

- **Wear synthetics or wool, not down or cotton.** Assume everything you wear will be wet for the whole visit.
- **Keep sleeping clothes and socks in dry bags.** Open them only inside a shelter.
- **Look after your feet.** Immersion foot is the most likely injury. Wear tall boots or neoprene socks, use barrier cream, and get your feet dry and warm against your own body every rest period.
- **Protect small things.** Cover cuts, since wet skin doesn't heal. Bring a brimmed hat, because glasses bead constantly. Use pencil and waterproof paper, and seal your electronics.
- **Bring antihistamines if you react to pollen.** The alders carry catkins permanently.
- **Carry a stove and a flask.** Wood here will not burn, and I doubt the place lets warmth build up anyway.

## Ground and hanging water

- **Carry a probing pole taller than you.** The peat's depth can't be judged by eye. The silt weighs differently each time it is lifted, so probe twice and believe the worse reading.
- **Choose your footing.** Tussocks, myrtle hummocks and alder roots hold. Smooth bright sphagnum lawns are skin over water, so spread your party out and carry a throw line.
- **Keep the pole ahead of you in open meadow.** A clear stream at chest height, in shadowless dusk, is nearly invisible. Watch for cress mats, the ripple pattern on its underside, or a pale eel passing where no eel should be.
- **Duck under streams where you can.** Where you must go through, treat it as a ford and unclip your hip belt. The current is real, and it hits your chest rather than your shins, which topples you more easily.
- **Don't boat the channels.** Every one ends at the gap, where the water loses its bed and drops.

## Rain

The valley floods from the top down. Each level swells and spills to the next, and the fen rises last.

- **You cannot hear rain arrive.** The sound of rain is always present, so watch for rings on a pool or the highest thread of water thickening.
- **Go out when a front comes.** Move from beneath the levels and head for the rim.
- **Leave by the rim, not the gap.** In flood, the gap is forty metres of stacked rivers falling.

## Sound

- **Bring hearing protection.** You will want it for the hours of thunder-drone, and you will need it at the standing column of waterfall on the north side. That column has an edge, and you step into the full roar all at once.
- **Use the kept sounds as landmarks.** The roar and the frogless frog meadows never move.
- **Agree signals with sharp starts.** Use whistle patterns, hand signs or a tug on a rope, because a real sound is known only by its beginning.
- **Ignore calls that have no beginning.** A cry like that is not asking you for anything, however it sounds.
- **Mind what you say.** I'd assume your own noise will be kept too, thinner each decade, so don't shout anything you wouldn't want standing in the air for a century.

## Light and time

Noon and midnight look alike, so carry a watch and keep a written log.

- **Sleep by the clock.** Post a watch while others sleep in earplugs.
- **Don't read weather or direction from the pools.** Some show the skies of other days.
- **Check your compass at the rim.** I can't promise the grains leave it alone.
- **Pack sunglasses for leaving.** After days of brown dusk, ordinary daylight will hurt.

## Distance, effort and number

- **Set turnarounds by time, not distance.** The middle holds more ground than fits, so crossings run long. Skirt the fen unless it is your purpose.
- **Carry extra food.** You will climb hills that are no longer there, and your legs will pay for them on flat peat.
- **Don't navigate by counting.** "Left at the third pool" will fail because the number won't settle. I'd extend that caution to your own party and kit, so call the roll by name and check gear against a named list.
- **Use the bleached line on the alders.** It is one true level across the whole valley. Its height on the trunk tells you how low you are standing, and so roughly how near the centre.

## Leaving things as they are

- **Carry all waste out.** Water lies a hand's depth down everywhere, so nothing can be buried.
- **Filter the water.** It is peaty but plentiful.
- **Take no silt.** Rinse boots and pole on the rim rock, because you don't want something at home that keeps.
- **Stay out of the dry notch in the fen.** It would make a tempting shelter, but it is space held for a channel that hasn't run yet. The rain and the reeds avoid it, and I'd follow their lead.

## The mood of the place

The bleached line and the faint surf are the sea, which has not come yet. For much of your visit you will be walking below a future waterline, and it helps to know that before you see the line. Then stand still for a while. A heron hunting upward through the underside of a stream is worth the wet feet.
