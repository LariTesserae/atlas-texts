# Preparing for the plateau

This place has no predators, no falling rock, and no terrain harder than a floor. What it has is air you cannot live on, no water, no shelter, no landmarks, and weather that arrives without sound. Almost everything that can go wrong here goes wrong quietly, so most of your preparation is about noticing things in time.

## Air

A third of a normal breath is roughly summit-of-the-highest-mountain air. Climbers reach that over weeks of acclimatisation, but you will arrive in one step.

- **Put your oxygen mask on before you cross.** An unacclimatised person at this pressure has a few minutes of useful thought, and the last of them feel fine.
- **Your oxygen supply sets the length of your trip.** Work out how many days of it you can haul, subtract a third as reserve, and plan around that number. Sleep on a low flow.
- **Spend a week or two at high altitude beforehand if you can.** If your mask fails, acclimatisation buys you an hour to fix it.
- **Carry a pulse oximeter and written checklists.** Hypoxia attacks judgment first. If you catch yourself deciding the checklist is unnecessary, treat that as a symptom.
- **See a doctor about altitude medication.** Learn the signs of fluid in the lungs and swelling of the brain: a wet cough, a staggering walk, a headache that changes character. The only cure is leaving.

## Water, cold and sun

You must carry every drop. The frost is a millimetre thick and gone by mid-morning, and it is what the lichens live on. A dark sheet laid out overnight might yield a gritty cupful if you scrape it before sunrise, but that is an emergency measure.

- **Water:** Budget four to five litres a day, because thin dry air strips water from your lungs with every breath. Sleep with your bottles so they don't freeze.
- **Food:** Water boils at around seventy degrees here, and stoves burn poorly. Bring food that needs no cooking, and expect to have to make yourself eat it.
- **Cold:** The floor radiates into an open sky all night and becomes the coldest thing around you. Bring expedition-grade insulation and the thickest sleeping mat you can carry.
- **Sun:** By day the dark stone warms your boots while the air stays bitter. The sun is fierce at this height, and the polished rock and pale dust throw it back up under your chin and nose. Cover all skin and wear glacier-grade eye protection. The light swings between hard glare and amber a dozen times an hour, so use lenses that adapt or swap quickly.

## Dust

The dust is quartz and garnet ground finer than flour. Garnet is what sandpaper is made of.

- **Lungs:** Fit filters to your mask intake and keep the mask on. In lungs this dust does permanent damage, and the dry cold air will already have your throat raw.
- **Eyes:** Wear sealed goggles and bring spare lenses, because the wind that facets quartz pebbles will frost a lens in a few storms. Wear glasses, not contact lenses.
- **Gear:** Zips, bearings, threads and camera mechanisms will all be ground down. Choose toggles, flaps and simple seals over anything precise, and bag everything.
- **Magnets:** The magnetite will find every magnet you own, including speakers, clasps and your compass, and coat them in black fur. Test your compass on arrival before trusting it. The gravel may deflect it, and this world may give it nothing to point at.
- **Static:** Dust blowing through dry thin air charges everything. Expect sparks from your own clothing, and protect your electronics.

## Wind, weather and silence

Learn the daily rhythm before you go:

- **Day:** Wind blows inward from the rims and builds as the floor heats.
- **Night:** Cold air drains outward toward the rims.
- **Dawn and dusk:** The flow reverses and wind comes from everywhere at once. Be stopped, low and tied down before each reversal. Walk from mid-morning onward and have camp made before dusk.

**You will not hear anything coming.** A storm can fill a quarter of the sky while the loudest sound is your own pulse. Make it a habit to turn a full circle every few minutes. Rope yourself to your partner and agree on tug signals, because voices carry poorly here even without masks. Set any alarms to vibrate.

- **Dust devils:** In this air they are more blinding than violent. Turn your back, cover up, and let them pass.
- **Dust fronts:** When a tan wall appears, stop walking. Note which band you are on and your heading, then get low and wait. The lightning inside is real, and on a level floor you are the tallest thing for kilometres. Get away from your cart and anything metal, and crouch on your sleeping mat.
- **Dunes:** A house-sized dune that moves its own length in an afternoon will cover a sleeping camp by morning. Before you sleep, look toward the plateau's centre, where the night wind comes from, and make sure nothing pale is on its way. Never walk through a dune, because flour-fine powder that deep will not hold you up.

## Footing, hauling and camp

The walking is perfectly level, but polished stone scattered with small round pebbles is like a floor strewn with ball bearings.

- **Boots and poles:** Wear soft, grippy soles and carry spares, since the grit will sand them down. Use rubber-tipped poles, because metal tips skate. Gaiters keep the dust out of your boots.
- **Cart:** A low wheeled cart is the best way to move days of water and oxygen, and it rolls beautifully here. It also sails, so chock the wheels and stay tied to it. Bring spares for anything on it that turns.
- **Shelter:** You cannot stake a tent. Polished gneiss takes no pegs, and there are few stones big enough to use as anchors. Bring a low shelter weighted from inside by your cart and supplies. Anything you leave loose at night goes toward the rim.

## Navigation and the rim

There are no peaks, your feet leave no tracks on the stone, and the dunes move. The only permanent map is the banding.

- **Read the stripes.** The sequence of grey, rose and black, with their widths, works like a barcode. Record the sequence at your arrival point, and count the bands you cross.
- **Remember that bands curve.** Following one is not walking a straight line.
- **Check the sky.** By day, use the sun to check your heading. On a clear night, watch the stars long enough to find the point they turn around.
- **Mark your way home.** Leave a weighted radio beacon at your crossing point as well as the band record. A dune may sit on the beacon.

**The rim has no lip.** The floor simply stops, and at night the dust pouring over hides the edge.

- **At night, never walk with the wind at your back.** The night wind blows toward the rim and will take you with it.
- **Don't travel in poor visibility** if you are within a day's walk of the edge.
- **Approach the rim only at midday in clear air,** when the wind is rising up the scarp and pushing you back. Cover the last stretch on your belly, and stay several body lengths from the edge.

## The masts

These have hung in place while the rock wore away beneath them. A finger's width of polished gneiss takes a very long time to wear away in this air. Whatever put them here works on a timescale that makes your visit a blink, and my instinct is that they are indifferent to you.

- **Finding them:** They are days apart, so don't navigate between them. Following dark bands improves your odds.
- **Touching them:** A hand on one seems harmless, and you may be glad of the faint warmth and the absence of wind.
- **Things not to do:** Don't tie anything to a mast or try to scratch or sample it. Don't put a finger or anything else in the gap beneath it.
- **Shelter:** Don't count on one. The calm at the base is only the size of the clean disc, an arm's length across. I wouldn't bet on what lightning does around one either.

## The still places

You will probably find one, if at all, by its signs: a straight-edged square of level pale powder, drifts of grey-green lichen, cushion plants crowded along a line, or a dust veil dropping like a curtain.

It will be the only real rest on the plateau. The pressure on your chest will lift, and you will be able to hear again. Before you rest there, though:

- **Test the air.** Something stops air moving in there, and I don't know if it also stops your exhaled breath drifting away from your face. Lean in at the edge, breathe out, and watch what the fog does. If it hangs in front of you, stay only briefly, and don't sleep there without a steady flow from your mask.
- **Don't light a stove inside.** A flame needs moving air.
- **Leave as few marks as you can.** These squares are the plateau's only record and its only gardens, and your footprints may last longer than you do. Enter at a spot clear of plants, stay near the edge, and leave by your own tracks.
- **Don't dig to find where the surplus dust goes.**
- **Read the powder before stepping in.** It is the only place here that would show you if anything else had passed through.

## The living things

A cushion plant living on a spoonful of dust may be older than any building you have seen, so step over seams between bands. If you lift a stone to see the spiders, put it back exactly as it was, since the stone is their shelter. Leave the rolling lichen where it lies.

Nothing rots here, so pack out everything, including your own waste. Whatever you leave either stays on the plateau or blows over the rim onto the lowlands.

## Your mind

I think the hardest part will be the lack of sensation. Lightning arrives as a tick and gales as a pressure on the chest. The horizon is ruled flat under a sky that is too dark, and the stripes pull your eye along them for kilometres. With hypoxia added, people drift off inside their own heads.

Keep a routine and talk to your partner through the rope. Eat and drink by the clock, and turn your full circle every few minutes. Decide your turnaround time before you cross, while you still have a full breath, and keep to it once you are there.
