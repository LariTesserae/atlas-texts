# Preparing for the Ice Works

This is a working factory that happens to look like a garden, and you should prepare for the factory. Nothing here is hostile, but several things are old, thin, wet, cold, or spinning. The people who work among them have had generations to learn where to stand, and you will have an afternoon.

## What to wear and carry

- **Shoes with real grip.** Every surface is wet brick, moss, algal film, or fermented fig. Avoid sandals. In the wet months the canal stands just under the paving and the drains run backward at high tide, so wear shoes that can get soaked.
- **Light, close-fitting clothes that dry quickly.** The air is thirty degrees and saturated, and the leeward side of the tower is a permanent drizzle. Wear nothing loose: no scarves, dangling straps, or lanyards, and tie back long hair. The flat belt and flywheel in the engine house are unguarded in the way old machines are.
- **A hat with a brim,** for the tower's rain.
- **Glasses rather than contact lenses.** The sting in the air is ammonia, the refrigerant. At background levels it is only a smell, but contact lenses and ammonia go badly together if the level ever rises.
- **Earplugs in a pocket.** You won't need them for the thud, the water, the belt, or the ventilator. You will want them if you stay for the crusher at four in the morning.
- **A dry bag for anything electronic or paper.** Lenses will fog the moment you step into the yard.
- **Water, and a thin warm layer.** The layer sounds absurd, but the tank room behind the rice-husk door is properly cold, and going into it sweat-soaked from tropical heat is a shock.
- **An ailing potted plant,** if you have one. This is the one thing the neighbourhood brings here. Leave it on the leeward side of the tower, and accept that if you leave it too long it will no longer be yours.

## The ammonia

Ammonia is the only thing here that could seriously hurt you without your touching anything. The faint sting is normal, and it is why no birds nest in the tower and nothing eats the figs. Prepare for it in three ways:

1. **Learn the baseline when you arrive.** If the smell suddenly sharpens and your eyes or throat burn, leave at once, without investigating.
2. **Know your exits before you need them.** The lane is narrow enough to touch both walls, which makes it a bottleneck. The other ways out are the loading dock and the canal side.
3. **If you have asthma or sensitive lungs,** keep the visit short or stay in the open yard, away from the engine house and condensers. The tower's mist is recirculated warm water, so I wouldn't stand breathing it for hours if my health were fragile, and I wouldn't drink anything that falls or pools here.

## What not to touch

- **The condenser coils.** The standing rule is that nobody scrapes them. The iron is thin, and the liverwort and algal felt are trusted more than the metal. Don't lean on them, pick at them, or peel back the growth to see the pipe.
- **The frosted suction line.** It crosses the yard at head height, so duck if you're tall. Wet skin sticks to metal that cold, and the pipe is as old as everything else. The ferns can grow a finger's width away from it, but you should stay further back.
- **The tower.** It looks climbable, but in two places the corner posts are gone and only fig is holding it up. The fig manages the weight of ferns and water and has made no arrangement for yours.
- **The belt run.** The arm's length of clearance cut around it each year is for the belt, and you should keep outside it too.
- **The figs.** Nothing comes to eat them, and I would take that as advice.
- **Anything rooted.** Don't prune, pick, or take cuttings, and keep to the footpath. The plants are told no in exactly two places, and a guest shouldn't add a third. The conduit strapped along the roots is live, in a yard that is never dry, so don't treat roots as handholds.

In the tank room, walk only where the workers walk. The brine is dark, still, and well below freezing, and four hundred cans hang in it. Close the husk-packed door behind you, because the cold is the product.

## Sound and silence

Within minutes your ear will stop registering the four sounds. This is pleasant, but it means you will no longer hear the machinery as a warning. The workers still do, and a change in the rhythm will register with them before you notice anything. Watch them. If they move, you move.

Nobody talks in the engine house, so don't try, and don't shout questions over the compressor. Learn the four hand signs before you go in: *more oil*, *less water*, *come and look*, and *tea*. Only the last two will be directed at you. Follow when you are shown the first, and say yes to the second.

## Timing

- **Early morning** is when the engineer measures the temperature drop across the tower and writes it in the ledger. If you're allowed to watch, do. Don't ask whether the number has changed, because it hasn't.
- **Four in the morning** is the only hour when things happen fast. The hoist lifts the row, the cans are tipped, the blocks go down the wet chute, and the crusher starts for the fish merchants. Stay out from under the hoist and away from the foot of the chute, since a block of ice on wet timber does not stop for anyone. Keep clear of the merchants too, because this is their working hour.
- **Dry season** shows the place at its strangest, a green, raining hill on a grey, dusty street. **Wet season** shows how close it all sits to the water. If the yard floods for an hour at the top of the tide, don't remark on it, because nobody else does.

## Manners

Ask at the gate. This is someone's livelihood, and has been since his father's time and probably his grandfather's. Buy ice if you can use it. Ask before photographing people or the ledger. Don't suggest improvements, and don't remark that the pipes ought to be replaced. They know, and they have reached an arrangement with the growth that works.

## What it will be like

Expect to be slowed down. The fastest change the works makes takes two days, and the place has little interest in anything quicker, including you. There is very little to do and a great deal to stand inside: green light, warm rain, a beat a second, and a sleeve of frost that looks like a mistake.

Plan on staying longer than you meant to. When you leave, the street will seem too dry and its noise will seem irregular. Your clothes will smell of wet brick, hot oil, and a trace of ammonia for a day or so.

If the kingfisher is on the wire over the canal, it will be facing away from the yard. Leave it alone and don't try to coax it in.
