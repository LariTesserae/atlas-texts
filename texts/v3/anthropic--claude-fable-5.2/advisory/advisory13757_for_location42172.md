# Preparing for the drum

This is a new, working piece of someone's water supply, about a hundred days into operation, at altitude, in permanent cloud. The place is not hostile. The main risks are the thin air, the wet metal underfoot, and the harm you could do to it.

## Your body

- **Altitude.** The air is thin and you reach the drum by climbing the stack. Go up slowly and rest on the intermediate terraces. If a headache turns into nausea or clumsiness, go down, which is the one easy direction here.
- **Warm and wet.** Sweat won't evaporate and nothing you bring will dry. Wear light synthetics, with long sleeves because the concrete corners are still sharp. Drink more than you think you need. Bring one warm layer and a shell for the lower terraces at night, away from the drum's heat.
- **Feet.** Bring spare socks in a dry bag and something for chafing. Your feet will be damp the whole time.

## Footing

There is no ground, only decking, cable trays, and sheet-metal roofs.

- Wear soft, grippy, clean soles, and gloves for wet ladder rungs.
- Stay on the decking. The roofs are collecting surfaces and the cable trays are not walkways.
- Tether anything small. Whatever you drop goes through the grating into cloud and lands on a level you can't see.
- Cloud moves through the railings most hours, so you may not see the next stair. After dark, carry a headlamp. The drum itself is a green lantern you can steer by from anywhere below.

## What to pack

- All your food, as there is nothing to eat here.
- A water filter. Gutter water is condensed cloud off metal and is probably fine filtered. Never drink from the drum's own lines, which are nutrient-dosed.
- A sleeping pad thick enough for grating, and an eye mask, because the lamps never go off.
- A watch with a seconds hand, a waterproof notebook, and a pencil.
- A small close-focus monocular for reading stem tags and the cut ends behind the bar.
- A lens cloth. The gallery glass is warm and stays clear, but your lenses will fog when you come up from the cooler terraces.
- Sealable bags and bottles for all your waste.

## The catchment rule

Every surface below the drum is a roof that feeds it, and everything in the drum goes down to wherever the water is wanted. Whatever you leave ends up in the supply.

- Do not urinate, spit, wash, or rinse anything on the terraces. Pack out all waste.
- Eat over a bag. No insect or bird will clear your crumbs, so they go into the gutters.
- Before you cross over, scrub your boot treads and gear. Bring no soil, seeds, fruit, or pond-damp equipment.

Nothing has ever grazed in the drum, and nothing has yet died. One snail egg or foreign spore could end that. The faint, even haze on the inside of the glass shows what happens when something arrives with nothing to eat it.

## Hazards of the works

- **Electricity.** The lamps, a transformer you'll feel through your soles, and the conduits all sit in permanent wet. Touch nothing with a stenciled arrow on it.
- **The glass.** The gallery windows hold back nine meters of water in a structure three months old. A flat palm on the glass is fine, but don't tap it, lean gear on it, or set anything hard against it.
- **The vent.** The gas is almost certainly oxygen, released at the top of the center column among the lamps. Carry no flame and don't climb the roof frame.
- **The door.** You have no reason to open anything. The arm, the sickle bar, and the intake slots are on the far side of the glass and should stay there.

## Time and sleep

Inside it is always about ten in the morning, and your body will believe it, so sleep by your watch. The outer wall is warm, which makes it the obvious place to bed down, with the mask on and your feet toward the hum.

The gas cycle runs about nine hours plus the twenty-minute vent. It is not tied to the day, so it drifts. On arrival, look up through the windows. The width of the mirror tells you how long until the valve opens.

## What to see

Give it at least one full gas cycle, and three days if you can.

1. **Start at the door.** Read the plate that lists the plants and find the two yellow ticks. The arm started here and is now a little past halfway through its third turn, so it is roughly across the drum from you. The ribbons at this bearing were cut about twenty days ago and stand around a meter and a half over the line.
2. **Walk the gallery toward the arm.** The growth steps down as you go, then you reach the meter of clear water behind the bar, with every ribbon cut flat at one height. This slot is your only long sightline into the drum. Just ahead of the bar is the cliff, where the tallest ribbons bend along the roof glass.
3. **Feel the drive.** Put a palm on the window and wait for the click, then a minute and a half of nothing. Each click moves the tip a couple of millimeters, which adds up to a hand's width an hour and about two and a half meters a day. Note where the tip is when you arrive and check again after you sleep, since that is the only way to see it move.
4. **Watch the end of a gas cycle from the slot.** Look up and across. The disk is a mirror at its edges and a window overhead. In the last hour or two before the float trips, the longest ribbons ahead of the bar reach through it and lie in the gas. This has only just begun happening. Then the valve opens with a thin hiss, the loudest sound here, and the water comes back over them.
5. **Check the service ladder.** The climbing plant is on the second rung and hasn't found the third. If you stay a few days, you might be there when it does.
6. **Go down a few terraces after dark and look back.** Above the lit drum is a hole of clear air where its warmth has burned off the cloud. It may be the only actual sky you see.

Hold still at the glass. The bubble threads rise perfectly straight because nothing disturbs the water.

## The quiet

There is no wind, no birds, and no insects at the lamps. You will hear the gutters, a hum in your feet, and a tooth seating every ninety seconds. You will be the loudest and messiest thing on the platform, and the only thing there that wasn't built from a drawing. Some people find that restful and some find it eerie. Go expecting very little to happen.

## Conduct and leaving

Someone comes up here, at least every forty days, with a yellow paint marker. The next tick is due in a bit under twenty days. If you meet them, you are a guest in their workplace.

- Don't add a mark of your own.
- Don't break the seal on the binder box or unwrap the spare baskets. Nothing here has needed them yet.
- Take no tags and leave nothing behind.
- Leave by going down.
