# Preparing for the plateau

Nothing on this plateau is hostile, and nothing will notice you. You are a visitor on a factory floor with no staff, so the hazards are industrial ones. The things most likely to kill you are ordinary: wet cold, bog, and the edge. The strange things will mostly disorient you into meeting the ordinary ones.

One rule organises everything else: **the quartzite is the only thing that is finished.** Everything else is in production and can be recalled. Sleep on rock, store gear on rock, navigate by rock, and step off it only on purpose.

## What it will feel like

Expect to be damp within ten minutes and to stay damp until you leave. The altitude (about 2,000 m) is modest and will only make you a little short of breath on the first day. The tiring part is the ground: every step sinks and has to be pulled back out, so plan on 1.5 to 2 km an hour. Expect a hum in your feet and jaw that never stops, and light with no shadows or horizon, which can cause mild vertigo. The loneliness is odd, because there is nothing for an hour and then four thousand of something.

## Clothing and feet

At 1°C, with saturated air and constant wind, an ordinary person gets hypothermia faster than in dry cold at −20°C.

- **Shell:** Bring a full waterproof jacket and trousers with a hood and sealed cuffs. The cloud sits on the ground, so water arrives from every direction.
- **Insulation:** Wear wool or synthetic layers only. Down will be a wet rag by the first evening and cotton is dangerous.
- **Sleeping clothes:** Keep one complete dry set in a sealed bag and wear it only inside your shelter.
- **Hands:** Bring waterproof overmitts, liner gloves, and spares of both. Wet hands in that wind stop working, and you need them for everything.
- **Boots:** Wear knee-high insulated rubber or neoprene boots that fit well enough to walk in all day. Leather will never dry.
- **Foot care:** Immersion foot is the injury you are most likely to take home. Each night, dry your feet, powder them, and put on dry sleeping socks. Dry the day's socks against your body.
- **Glasses:** If you wear them, bring a brimmed cap and accept that you will be wiping them constantly.

Keep the visit to two or three nights, because your feet and morale will not last much longer.

## Shelter and sleep

Camp on a quartzite rib. Moss that is a flat hummock at dusk may be a pool, a chimney or the floor of a gallery after the next shift. I would not bet a tent on it.

- **Tent:** Bring a low, freestanding, storm-worthy tent. Pegs will not hold in sphagnum or go into quartzite, so bring cord and anchor bags to tie off to the rock.
- **Sleeping bag:** Use a synthetic bag rated to about −10°C. The rating sounds excessive, but the bag will be damp.
- **Mat:** Use a thick closed-cell mat. It keeps out the cold, and it also softens the tingle and hum coming up from below.
- **Gear:** Keep everything on the rock. Even the pools grow a green skin here, and I would not leave a pack on the moss overnight.

The foxes den against the ribs too. Give the dens room, keep your food sealed and do not feed them. They are the only other individuals up here, and they chose the rock for the same reason you should.

Sleep will be poor. The hum comes through the ground, so earplugs will not block it. The night ice is thin and cracks loudly, and the wind never drops.

## Moving

**The ground.** Carry a long pole or trekking poles with wide baskets, and probe before you step. Pools are skinned green and look like ground. The peat goes down four metres, and a fall into a pool means cold-water immersion with no way to dry out. If you go in, return to the rock, get into your dry set and your bag, and make a hot drink. The day's walking is over.

**Direction.** There is no sun and no stars, and this world has no satellites. On arrival, test your compass against the line of a rib for an hour before you trust it, because the ground is electrically charged. Don't trust rows of hummocks or lines of pools, which will be recalled. The waders all face northeast within a run, but I would not rely on that staying true in the next run.

**The ribs.** These are your only real map. They never move and never repeat, so each one can be identified. Sketch each one in pencil on waterproof paper, noting its shape, its lichen colours and how it lies. Tie bright cord to the rock at your arrival point. Don't plant markers in the moss.

**Distance.** Distance gets reissued, so pace-counting and dead reckoning do not work. Plan by time and by the state of your body. Set a turnaround time and keep to it even if the mound you are heading for looks four hundred metres away. It has probably looked that way for an hour.

**Companions.** Stay within arm's reach of each other or on a short rope. Nobody will fall into a crevasse, but if the plateau can put an extra kilometre between you and a mound, it can put one between you and a friend. Shouts and whistles are no more trustworthy than any other sound here.

**The rim.** This is the most dangerous feature, and there is no clear edge to warn you. The shoulder is rounded and slick, and the peat overhangs in a lip that will not hold your weight. All of it is inside cloud, where you cannot judge distance. Water is the warning: the plateau has no current anywhere except near the rim. Turn around if you see any of the following:

- a runnel that is visibly flowing
- a tilt in the ground that you can feel
- dripping that you can hear under the wind

## The shifts

When a split shrieks, stop walking and plant your poles. The ground is about to slump and start on something else. Wait until the new run shows what it is.

- **Use the repetition.** Within a run, every unit is the same. If you probe one pool, you know the depth of all of them. If you find the corner of one hummock that takes your weight, it will be the same corner on every hummock to the horizon.
- **Distrust the odd one out.** A thing that does not match its run is the thing to be careful of.
- **Stay out of the galleries.** They will look like the first shelter from the wind you have seen in days. They are tonnes of wet peat that last only until afternoon.
- **Keep clear of the stacks.** They are twenty metres tall and fold at the waist, so stay at least thirty metres away.
- **Don't treat sound as a warning.** Sound and event arrive out of order. A roar from empty ground means a gallery is about to rise there, and that gallery will later fall without a sound.
- **Don't follow tracks.** Fresh prints filling with water with no animals in sight show where a band has yet to pass. Each foot will land exactly in its print, which means the band cannot swerve around you. Step out of the lane and wait.
- **Walk around a squall.** A sleet squall with clean edges covers only a hectare.

## The mounds

The mounds are the only warm places, and you will want to stay on one. Limit yourself to a short visit on the upwind flank to warm your hands.

- **Gas:** The splits vent steam and marsh gas, so never bring a flame near them. Don't rest in the lee or in hollows where gas can pool.
- **Noise:** If a split shrieks while you are standing on the crown, it will damage your hearing.
- **Midges:** They rise from the mounds in a column and travel as one. Step aside and let it pass, and bring a head net in case.
- **Smell:** Iron filings and cut grass mean a mound is near, however far away it looks. Smell is more reliable than sight here.

## Animals

Nothing here hunts you. The risk is being in the way of a run.

- **Hares:** Four thousand of them, pressed flank to flank, will not part around you. Stay out of their lane.
- **Geese:** They come over as a slab, and the noise is like a siren. Cover your ears and your mug.
- **Foxes:** They will watch you and are worth watching back. See where they walk, when they wait, and when they leave a place.

Leave dogs at home.

## Food, water and fire

Bring all your food. Nothing here burns, so cooking depends on what you carry.

- **Don't eat the hares.** The foxes do, but they have had longer to come to terms with the place. My guess is that meat made of moss goes back to moss at the next shift, wherever it happens to be.
- **Carry extra rations.** Bring at least two days more than you plan to need, because distance cannot be planned. Include food you can eat cold with numb hands.
- **Stove:** Bring a liquid-fuel stove or a cold-rated gas mix, with a windscreen. Light it only on rock, well away from any mound.
- **Hot drinks:** These matter more for staying warm and sane than anything else you carry.
- **Water:** It is everywhere, brown as tea, and tastes of peat and iron. Strain it through cloth, then filter and boil it. The green flecks carried in it are still trying to become something, and I would not want them doing that inside me.
- **Where to collect:** Take water from sags beside the ribs. Avoid water near mounds.
- **The star pool:** Never drink from, step in, or take a bearing from the pool that reflects a starry sky. The birds refuse to stand in it, and they know the place better than you do.

## Electricity and instruments

The moss holds a charge. At dusk the sedge tips glow violet and crackle when touched. On a mountain at home, that would mean lightning was about to strike. Here it happens every evening, and my intuition is that it stings rather than injures. Rubber boots help.

- **Watch your footing at dusk.** A sudden crackle up the leg can startle you, and being startled is how people end up in pools.
- **Don't come with a pacemaker or similar implant.**
- **Protect electronics.** Keep them in sealed bags and expect them to misbehave.
- **Carry a mechanical watch.** The plateau also offers one clock: the hum drops a tone in the coldest hour before the light returns.
- **Keep a written log.** Events happen out of order here, and your memory of the sequence will not be reliable.

## Things to leave alone

- The half-finished hares at the edges of a run, with green fibre down one side. They are not dead in any way you can help with, and they will be gone by the next shift.
- The peat itself, including the cable-thick club moss in the galleries. Don't dig, cut or collect.
- Souvenirs generally. I doubt anything the plateau made will remain what it was once you take it away.

Pack out all your waste and belongings.

## Your own head

A mains-pitch hum, a two-second thud that wanders underfoot, shadowless light and rows of identical objects are a recipe for nausea, unease and a growing feeling of walking in place. Sometimes you really will be walking in place.

It may help to know that the foxes do not match one another. Whatever the plateau copies, it has apparently not copied them, so it probably will not copy you. If you find bootprints ahead of you where you have not walked, treat them like any other tracks: they tell you nothing about what has happened or what will.

Practise the foxes' patience. Most of the danger comes from hurrying toward something, and waiting on rock has never hurt anyone here.

## Leaving

Leave from the rib you marked, and allow far more time to reach it than the distance suggests. Before you cross back, scrub your boot treads, shake out your gaiters, and check your cuffs, pockets and hair for green. A scrap the size of a fingernail is enough. It is harmless on the plateau, where it belongs, but you should not carry something that is trying to become an ear into another world.
