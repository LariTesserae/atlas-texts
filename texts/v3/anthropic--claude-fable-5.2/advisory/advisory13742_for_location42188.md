# Preparing to visit the pocket in the spine

The most important thing to know is that you cannot go in. The cavity is the size of a barrel, sealed behind a forearm of rock and glass, with no opening wider than two fingers. You will be visiting the outside of it: a hum under your boot, a line of antennae at a crack, a note held in stone.

So you need two kinds of preparation. The mountain can kill you without noticing, and you can kill the pocket without noticing.

## The mountain

**Altitude.** At just under five thousand metres, each breath carries a little over half the oxygen you are used to.
- If you can choose your arrival point, arrive low and climb over a week or more, sleeping only a few hundred metres higher each night.
- If you must arrive at the summit directly, bring supplemental oxygen and keep the visit to an hour or two. Your judgement will be worse than it feels.
- A headache is normal. Stumbling, confusion, or a wet cough means you descend immediately.

**Cold and heat at once.** The air is ten below zero with summit wind, so dress for a winter mountain: layers, windproof shell, insulated gloves, face covering. The rock, however, is younger than you are and still hot inside.
- Wear thick-soled leather boots, because hot ground softens glued soles.
- Carry an infrared thermometer and test any surface before you kneel or put a hand on it.
- Wear leather outer gloves. The black rind is volcanic glass and its broken edges cut like a scalpel.

**Gas.** The flues in the roof vent what the throat sends up: sulfur dioxide, hydrogen chloride, and worse.
- Bring a full-face respirator with acid-gas cartridges, or a half-mask with sealed goggles. Do not wear contact lenses.
- Carry a gas monitor. A respirator does nothing against carbon dioxide, which pools in hollows on still days, so stay out of depressions.
- If you have asthma, do not make this trip.
- Expect the acid to pit zippers and camera lenses and leave pinholes in fabric. Rinse everything afterward.

**The spine itself.** No equipment helps with this hazard. Lava spines are among the least stable landforms there are: they crack, shed slabs, and sometimes fail all at once. Wear a helmet, know your exit line, and keep your time short. Do not go during or just after a storm. Low pressure makes the throat blow harder, and rain on a hot dome is a classic trigger for collapse. You accept this risk by going at all.

**Water and sun.** There is no water up there, so carry all of yours and drink more than you want. The air is dry and thin, and the ultraviolet is fierce, so bring sunscreen and dark glasses.

## The pocket

**Arrive clean.** The pocket inhales through its windward cracks. That draught feeds everything inside, and it will take in whatever you shed.
- Before you travel, brush and wash boots, cuffs, pockets, and pack seams. A seed or spore from another world has no business in the first soil on this mountain.
- Eat well away from the spine, and urinate far downslope.
- Spill no water near the cracks. The pocket's whole water budget is a film of damp before dawn.

**Stand to one side.** The windward side is the safe side for gas, since the plume blows away from you, but it is also the intake side. Stand off the line of the wind so your body does not block the draught or feed it lint. A change in airflow that you would not notice could move the habitable band a finger's width, and the band is only a palm wide.

**Bring instruments that take nothing.**
- **Your boots.** Stand still and you will feel the roar as a steady vibration, like the deck above a ship's engine.
- **A mechanic's stethoscope or contact microphone.** Pressed to rock you have checked for heat, it will give you the bottle-note and, under it, the ticking glass and pinging hematite. This is the closest you will get to being inside.
- **A thermal imager.** It shows the gasket of tolerable warmth around each intake crack without spending any light.
- **A dim red headlamp.** Use it briefly and only at the crack lips. Before dawn, when the damp film forms, you may see the outermost crickets standing in rank facing the draught, and a nap of mites on the salt.

**Timing.** Arrive before dawn for the damp, the stillest air, and the animals at the margin. If conditions hold, wait at a distance into the afternoon. You can then watch the fallout arrive: aphids, midges, and specks of silk dropping dead out of the sky and sliding toward the cracks.

**Things not to do.**
- **Do not chip the rind.** A hole would break the draught that runs the whole works.
- **Do not push a probe or camera through an intake crack.** It would pass straight through the only inhabited ground.
- **Do not shine a bright light in.** Realgar degrades under light into yellow powder, so lighting those colours would begin destroying them.
- **Do not breathe into a crack.** Your breath is warm and wet, and the sal ammoniac will not survive it.
- **Do not collect anything.** The orange crust is arsenic sulfide, the animals die a palm's width from home, and the pinch of dark crumbs took years to accumulate.
- **Do not touch the crack lips.** One gloved fingertip covers a generation of mites.
- **Keep magnets and speakers off the rock.** The grains are recording north, and the animals read tremor.

## What it will be like

It will be less than you imagine, and then more. You will have a headache. The air will smell of struck matches, and the wind will not let up. The spine will be a black, glassy fin, warm in places and ticking.

You will kneel beside a crack you could cover with two fingers and feel air being drawn past your glove into the dark. Through the stethoscope will come a roar that has not paused since before you planned the trip. You may see a row of pale antennae. You will not see the hematite, the amber sulfur, or the red throat. No one ever has.

Nothing will change while you are there, because the place works on storms and years. If you record the note and come back in a decade, it will be slightly higher. One day it will be a whistle, and then nothing.

Treat the visit as listening at a door. Stay briefly, take the sound and leave everything else, and descend before the afternoon cloud builds.
