# Preparing for the chamber under the salt flat

Nothing in this place will attack you. The danger is the climate, which is gentle, constant, and more than a human body can live in for long. Treat the visit as a long dive: fixed duration, carried supplies, and a turnaround time set before you enter.

## Heat

The air is above body temperature, damp, and still. The floor is warm, the roof is bright, and nothing casts a shadow, so there is no shade, no breeze, no cool ground, and no pool to cool off in. Sweat will run off you without cooling you much, so you will gain heat the whole time, even at rest.

- **Measure first.** Bring a thermometer and hygrometer and read them on arrival. If the air is near saturation, you have a few hours, not days.
- **Bring cold with you.** A phase-change cooling vest and an insulated bag of frozen water bottles work as cooling first and drinking water later. Nothing can be re-chilled inside.
- **Acclimatize beforehand.** Spend ten to fourteen days on daily heat exposure, in a sauna or exercising in hot conditions.
- **Don't go alone.** Agree on the signs of heat illness: headache, nausea, cramps, clumsiness, irritability, and stopping sweating. Confusion means leave immediately, and your partner makes that call, not you.
- **Move slowly.** Hard work generates heat you cannot shed.

## Water and food

There is no stream or pool. The cupful in each crown and the occasional drop from the roof are the entire supply, and the geckos depend on them. The drops have also run across salt and gypsum, so they may be brackish. Treat that water as a last resort.

- **Water:** Carry about a litre per hour of your stay, with electrolytes. Plain water alone at these sweat rates will make you ill.
- **Food:** Bring salty food that survives heat. Nothing here is in fruit or seed, and you should not eat the fiddleheads or horsetail shoots. Many ferns and horsetails are toxic raw, and a picked crozier is unlikely to be replaced.
- **Cooking:** There is no dead wood to burn and no need for a stove.

## Light and time

It is as bright as an open salt flat, from every direction, and it never dims.

- **Eyes:** Bring dark wraparound sunglasses, and a sleep mask if you plan to rest.
- **Time:** There is no dusk or change in the weather to tell you time is passing. Wear a watch with alarms for drinking, for checking each other, and for turning back, and obey it over your own sense of time.
- **Depth:** Shadowless light flattens relief, as in a snow whiteout. Dips, edges, and the height of a step down off a rib are hard to judge, so carry a pole and probe.
- **Fogging:** Glasses and lenses will fog on arrival. Let equipment warm up in a sealed bag.
- **Torch:** You won't need one here. Keep a small one for wherever you go next.

## Clothing and feet

- **Clothing:** Wear loose, thin, tightly woven long sleeves and trousers despite the heat. Crossing means pushing between croziers, and the rust-coloured scales will rub off on you. Fern scales can irritate skin, eyes, and lungs, so add a light dust mask and a neck covering in the dense growth.
- **Gloves and boots:** The basalt is glassy, sharp, and hot, so bring leather gloves and thick-soled boots. On the mat, step softly and deliberately.
- **Feet:** They will be wet the whole time. Bring spare socks and anti-chafe balm. The best place to dry anything is the crisp margin where the mat stops short of the hot rock.
- **Sitting on rock:** Bring an insulating pad for the ribs. The stone ring on the largest rib was built for sitting in.
- **First aid:** Basalt cuts are clean and deep, so carry closure strips and pressure dressings.

## Getting around

- **Use the ribs as waypoints.** From the top of a rib you can see the whole valley. Among the croziers you see only fur and yellow-green light. Sketch the dozen ribs and the walls from your first high point, take a bearing before each descent, and travel rib to rib.
- **Distrust the compass near basalt.** The rock can deflect the needle, so check your bearing again a little way out.
- **Listen for the vents.** The hiss carries in the still air and will guide you to a rib.
- **Think hard before entering the centre.** The floor dips there, the coils stand twenty metres tall, and you will be squeezing between trunks. It is the slowest and hottest travel in the chamber. Volcanic vents and still air also mean heavy gases could collect in the low ground. The thriving geckos suggest the steam is mostly water, but carry a CO₂ and H₂S monitor and retreat uphill if you get a headache or feel breathless.
- **Take care on the lava.** Stay off the vents, since the steam will scald. Probe ropy lava before trusting it, because the crust can be a thin shell over a hollow.
- **Keep your partner in sight.** The mat swallows footsteps, so you will not hear each other moving. Carry a whistle.
- **Know your exit.** The handprints lead to the eastern wall, which may mean a way out. Settle your own exit before you explore.

## Conduct

Nothing here rots, and nothing seems to grow back. The knot in the arch is still green, and the moss has not closed the finger's width around the path stones. Assume that whatever you break stays broken and whatever you leave stays forever.

- **Waste:** Pack out everything, including human waste.
- **The path:** Walk on the fourteen stones where they serve your route.
- **The made things:** Don't untie the arch, restack the ring, or touch the handprints.
- **The living things:** Don't handle the geckos, whose skin is very thin. Move gently near the walls, where the roof comes down and the hanging stems with their snails may be within reach. Never pull on the stems, because they are attached to the only roof there is.
- **Leaving your own mark:** Earlier visitors left small things made by hand. If you do the same, expect it to stay exactly as you leave it.

## The stillness

This last part is intuition. The chamber feels like something about to happen, and the feeling never resolves. It is quiet, bright, and motionless, with a drop falling now and then. Combined with the heat, that makes it easy to sit down in the warm moss and decide to leave a little later.

Everything here has begun and nothing has gone further, so I would not count on things you start inside to finish on their usual schedule. That could include a rest, a wound closing, or a plan to cross. Decide before you enter what you came to do, how long you will stay, and what counts as done, and write it down. Whoever left the handprints kept moving toward the wall while the ground firmed under them, and you should leave the same way, while you still can.
