# Preparing for the sounding city

This place probably won't kill you suddenly. Its failures are slow and well signposted: a span takes hours to sag, and the locals walk away from danger without hurry. What it will do is wear you down through noise, wet heat, crowding, and the absence of anything that stays put. Prepare for attrition more than for emergencies.

## Before you go

- **Hearing:** Get a hearing test so you have a baseline. The locals have had generations to adapt (the dogs gave up hearing entirely), and you have not.
- **Teeth:** The roar is felt in the back teeth, so have loose fillings or crowns fixed first.
- **Health limits:** Think hard before going if you have tinnitus, migraines, vestibular problems, a heart condition, severe claustrophobia, or photosensitive epilepsy. The markets communicate with mirrors and snapping flags, and at night every conversation flickers.
- **Heat:** The climate is a permanent steam bath. Spend a couple of weeks beforehand getting used to exertion in humid heat.
- **Silence:** Practise going a full day without speaking, using only gesture and written cards with a companion. It is harder than it sounds, and it is how you will live outdoors.
- **Your pulse:** Learn to take it at the wrist. Newcomers mistake the city's bass for their own heartbeat, and it is unnerving to feel your "heart" doing something it isn't. Checking with your fingers will stop a lot of 3 a.m. panic.

## What to pack

**For your ears**
- Bring both deep-fitting earplugs and earmuffs, with many spares. Foam plugs will not dry out between uses and will get foul.
- Nothing blocks the bass, which arrives through your skeleton. Expect fatigue, queasiness, and a vague dread for the first few days.
- Bring a visual sound-dose meter if you can get one, and treat the trip as having a noise budget.

**For the wet**
- Nothing dries here, so pack clothes that are comfortable wet: thin, loose synthetics or light wool. Cotton will rot on you.
- Wear draining shoes or sandals with the grippiest soles you can find. The pavements are permanently slick with moisture, orange film, and grey felt. Moderately thin soles are better than thick ones, for reasons given below.
- Feet and skin folds are your main medical problem. Bring antifungal cream (powder cakes), barrier ointment, waterproof dressings, and antiseptic. Small cuts will not scab in this air.
- Bring electrolytes. Sweat does not evaporate, so you will overheat while feeling merely damp.

**Metal and gear**
- The gutter films eat iron. Avoid steel zips, eyelets, buckles, blades, and boot nails. Use plastic, brass, or titanium, and do not set iron down in a gutter.
- Constant vibration unscrews things. Check bottle caps, spectacle screws, and lens mounts daily. Rigid hollow containers will buzz against you, so soft flasks are better.
- Electronics should be sealed and need no sound: microphones, speakers, and vibrating alerts are all useless. Set alarms to flash, dimly.

**For talking**
- Carry a small waterproof slate and marker, plus a set of pictogram and colour cards for food, water, sleep, how much, where, thank you, and help.
- Wear a plain strip of warm, steady light on one sleeve if you can, something you can cover and uncover deliberately. A blinking gadget on your arm is gibberish at best.

**Clothing colour and luggage**
- Dress in dull river-stone colours. Colour, mirrors, and flashes are how this city shouts, so a bright jacket is the equivalent of yelling all day.
- Avoid white that can flap, and never snap open a white poncho. The evacuation signal is a white flash travelling up the street from the gulls' underwings, and you do not want to imitate it.
- Carry a small bag on your front or hip. A backpack is a nuisance in a shoulder-to-shoulder crowd, and it covers the spot between the shoulder blades where people speak privately.

**Food and water**
- I cannot tell you what people eat. Nothing has leaves, so expect fish, crab, and things from the sea or from elsewhere. Bring several days of your own food until you have worked it out.
- Do not drink the milky canal feed. Be wary of the seep water, which is warm and has come up through a thousand years of drowned streets. The daily rain is probably the cleanest source, so a wide collector and a purifier are worth carrying. Watch what the locals drink.

**Leave behind**
- Tent pegs, stakes, and digging tools. Nobody digs, and there is no open ground to camp on.
- Anything that makes a strong, sustained tone. Sound is the load-bearing element here, and where driven sound meets slurry it sets.
- Seeds, plants, and soil. This world has no leaves, and you should not be the one who changes that.

## Arriving

If you have any choice, enter from the inland edge, where the buildings are squat and made by weight and the tone is thin. Walk in over a day or two. This lets your body meet the roar on a gradient, and it lets you learn the chord before your life depends on reading it. Do not arrive by sea: the water is milky and hums for two hundred kilometres out.

## The one rule

Learn the difference between two kinds of quiet:

- **A still point** is quiet but held. The air feels pressed from all sides and goes nowhere. Swifts nest in these places, and their nests mark the safest rest you will find.
- **A dead spot** is quiet because the tone has failed. The wall sweats harder, its surface goes glossy, and its edges start to round.

Your ear protection deafens you to the obvious warning, so do what the deaf dogs do and use your eyes and your body:

- **Feel the block:** Put a palm on a wall when you arrive somewhere and learn what it feels like. Thin soles help here. The chord of a block shifts in your sternum when neighbouring tones lean in to cover a gap.
- **Watch the animals:** The gulls lift first, in a white flash. Then the dogs go, then the people.
- **Watch the fish:** Evenly spaced rows mean all is normal. Scattered fish and thick white water mean a frontage has just slumped nearby.

When the people go, go with them at their pace. Do not run, since panic in a crowd that dense is more dangerous than the wall. Do not stay to watch or to rescue luggage. Usually the neighbouring tones hold and everyone drifts back.

## Getting around

- **Maps:** Do not buy a street map, which is out of date within the month. Get or sketch a picture of the whole city, with its main canals, the paths the remaking fronts travel, and the coast. That one is good for five hundred years.
- **Addresses:** An address is a bearing and a date, so carry a compass and a calendar and write addresses that way.
- **Meeting points:** Agree on a canal junction with your companions. Junctions get lifted, but the market rides up with them, so the place persists even when its height does not. You cannot call out to each other, so agree on a small distinctive signal and a fallback time.
- **Lodging:** Expect it to be tiny, wet, and possibly a storey higher when you come back in the evening. Ask where the remaking front is before you take a room, because a wall going up next door is forty minutes of a saw through glass. Ask for a still point, and find the way back by bearing.
- **Canals:** Stay out of them and away from their edges on frontages that look glossy.
- **Fresh walls:** Do not lean on them, and look up before standing under one. The white crabs grazing there are as heavy as stones.

## Manners

- **Do not speak aloud outdoors:** It is futile, and I suspect it reads as crude.
- **The palm on the back:** If someone presses a palm between your shoulder blades and hums, it is a confidence. Stand still and receive it. You may not understand the words, but you will catch the tone. Do not touch anyone there yourself unless invited.
- **Hands:** Keep your gestures small and deliberate, because your hands are your voice now.
- **Light:** Nothing glows here that shouldn't. Use a warm, dim lamp at night, never a white strobe or a swinging torch beam.
- **Dogs:** They cannot hear you. Let them see you or feel your step before you come close.

## The day's rhythm

- **Before dawn:** In the eastern districts the roar swells an hour before light, so sleep will be short. Earplugs help, but the bass comes up through the bed.
- **Morning:** This is the best time to move. The haze is white and the shadows soft, but you can still burn, and the only shade is the shade of a wall. Wear a close-brimmed hat, since a wide brim won't fit in the crowd.
- **Early afternoon:** The sky closes and it rains warm and hard for an hour. An umbrella is pointless when you are already wet, so use the hour to rest, rinse, and collect water. Everything drops a quarter tone during the rain, and you will feel the city change key.
- **After the rain:** The city steams. This is the worst hour for heat, and a good one to sit still.
- **Evening:** Heat vents are probably the only place anything dries, and each has a brown cloud of moths over it. Cover your nose and mouth, dry your feet and socks anyway, and go to bed with dry feet.

## What it does to your head

There is no silence, solitude, horizon, or greenery, and the street you memorised yesterday is gone. It helps to attach yourself to routines, which hold, because locations don't. The residents live by the same trick: their quarter comes round again in eleven years or so, arcades roughly where they were. Somewhere around the third or fourth day the bass stops being an assault and becomes a floor, and the sleeve-flicker at night starts to look like a city murmuring.

## Leaving

- **Go out the way you came in:** Leave through the thinning inland edge, slowly.
- **Expect your ears to ring:** People who leave say the ringing lasts a week, from the lack of sound. You may sleep badly in quiet and want a low hum running at home for a while.
- **Get your hearing retested:** Compare it against your baseline.
- **Decontaminate everything:** Scrub, dry, and inspect your boots, bag seams, and any metal before you carry them into another world. The grey felt is modest, but the orange film eats iron, and it should not come home with you.
