# Preparing for the saddle

My reading is that the place is survivable and even hospitable in patches, but it is dangerous in three ordinary ways (altitude, industry, terrain) and one unusual way: the mountain is partly made of rock that hasn't arrived yet, and you can't use it.

## The main constraint: there is no way down

The standard cure for altitude trouble is descent, and here it doesn't exist. The cloud deck is three thousand metres below, the sky is near black at noon, and nobody in the terraces has ever been to the bottom station. Do not plan to leave by cable bucket. The buckets go down empty into cloud, and no one can tell you what receives them. Arrive with your own exit arranged.

- **Acclimatise beforehand** if you can, and keep the visit short. I'd count it in shifts, not weeks.
- **Carry altitude medication** and, if possible, a small supply of oxygen.
- **Move slowly.** The quarter-hour walks between buildings are quarter-hours for residents. Double them.
- **Watch the air near the works.** Coke-oven fumes and thin air compound each other. If you get a headache near the battery or gasholders, move upwind before deciding it's just the altitude. Carry no open flame near the gasholders.

## Light from below

The day rises off the cloud tops, so every shadow lies upward. This matters more than it sounds, because your brain reads terrain by assuming light comes from above.

- **You will misread the ground.** Hollows look like bumps and ledges look like overhangs. A headlamp helps even when you can technically see, because it puts the light back where your eyes expect it.
- **You will misread faces.** Everyone is lit from the chin, by cloud-light or by furnace. People will look stern or strange for the first day. They aren't.
- **Your hat brim does nothing.** The light comes in under it.
- **Lamps attract moths.** They'll find yours within minutes, and the bats will follow. Both are harmless, but keep your mouth closed.

## The rock

The stone is all fresh fracture, glassy and sharp, with no scree, soil or weathering.

- **Dress for a fall.** Falling here is like falling on broken bottle. Wear stiff, thick-soled boots, leather gloves and tough trousers, and carry real wound dressings.
- **Don't trust the edges.** A slab tilts upward and simply stops, with no crumbling margin or change of texture to warn you. In permanent dusk you can walk off one.
- **Check for warmth.** Warm rock is the newest rock, and probably the likeliest to part with one of those rifle cracks. Don't bed down on a warm slab, however tempting in the cold.
- **Use the pipe-runs as handrails.** Go from lamp to lamp, and tell someone at each end. The dark between lamps is unsurveyed mountain. Don't take shortcuts.

## Water

The only water is the evening cloud condensing on the iron, and the only rivers are the gutters and catch-sheets on the housing. Those belong to the people who rigged them.

- **Bring all your own water**, or arrange a share before you need it. Never help yourself.
- **Filter any condensate you drink**, and don't drink drips from near the coke battery at all, because of the tar and ammonia.
- **Don't judge slope by water.** It stands in domed sheets on forty-degree slabs and sometimes creeps uphill. When those sheets freeze, you get lenses of ice on steep glass, so bring microspikes.
- **Expect to get soaked in the evening.** The cloud condenses on you as on everything else, and then the cold comes. Wear a waterproof shell over real insulation and have dry layers in a sealed bag. When the cloud is up, stay inside a lit area until it drops.

## Sound

The grind of the rising is constant and low enough to feel in your teeth. Over it come the roll stands, the steam hammer and the cable sheaves.

- **Bring hearing protection** for the mill and earplugs for sleep. The plugs won't stop the grind, which arrives through your jaw and the bed frame. Some people will find it lulling, and some will get headaches and clench their teeth.
- **Don't flinch at the cracks.** A slab parting sounds like a rifle shot. The residents don't react, and you'll want not to jump when you're near an edge.

## The works

This is heavy industry with no guardrails.

- **Stay out from under the cableway.** Full buckets of coal and brick pass overhead.
- **Treat ferns as a warning.** They grow where steam traps vent, so a clump of ferns marks a place where scalding steam comes out.
- **Don't sit on the flanges.** They're hot, and the hay drying on them is a pika's winter.
- **Cover your head at shift change.** Several thousand choughs leave the mill roof at once.

## The green and the animals

The moss strips under the pipes are the whole ecosystem, each one exactly as wide as a drip line. Walk beside them, never on them. They're also slick.

The same logic applies to you. Green follows leaks, and you are a leak. On rock too young for lichen, anything you spill or drop is the first thing ever to be there, so pack everything out and ask at the terraces where waste goes.

The ibex have right of way on the trestle tops. They're after salt, so I'd expect them to take an interest in sweaty pack straps and gloves left outside. Don't walk beneath a file of them.

## What isn't there yet

Out past the end of the eastern slab, ibex lie bedded in open air at the height the spur will have. Pikas run along ledges that are due. Do not follow them.

My evidence that this doesn't work for people is the city itself. The newest terrace hangs in a steel frame eleven metres up, waiting for the ground. If people could stand on the future, they wouldn't need the steel. The animals belong to the mountain as it will be. The residents and you belong to it only as it is.

- **Mind the doors in the hanging terrace.** If you're lodged or invited there, the doorsteps open on a drop. Learn which door is which before you're tired.
- **Leave the brass survey marks alone.** The whole city is built to them.
- **Watch the choughs.** They bank wide around cliffs that are still cold air. I doubt you'd feel anything walking through those places, since the rock rises only a doorstep a year. But the birds know where the faces will be, and I'd give them the same berth out of courtesy.

## The echo

When the shift horn sounds, the echo comes back late, deeper and longer, off summit faces a kilometre above anything that exists. This will make you look up, at black sky with unblinking stars, and for a moment your body will insist that something enormous is there.

Be standing somewhere flat and enclosed the first time, not near an edge. Then watch the choughs rise to the echo, lit from underneath, circling where the peak will be. I think that's the thing worth coming for.

## Short packing list

- Altitude medication and oxygen if obtainable
- Stiff boots, microspikes, leather gloves, tough trousers
- Insulation, waterproof shell, sealed dry layers
- Headlamp with spare batteries
- Ear defenders and earplugs
- All your water, a filter, and all your food (I can't tell what comes up besides coal and brick, so assume nothing)
- Wound dressings
- Bags to carry out everything you bring
- Your own way home

Last, a matter of manners. Don't ask people about the bottom station as if it were a puzzle they've failed to solve. They live at the level the ground is expected to reach, and the ground does come up to meet them.
