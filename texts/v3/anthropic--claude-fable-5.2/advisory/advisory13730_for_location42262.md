# Preparing for the singing pit

This place will probably not kill you quickly. Its dangers are slow ones: thirst, glare, hearing damage, heat, a fall, and judgment worn down by standing for hours inside a sound. What follows is inference from the description, not experience.

## Before you go

Skip this trip if you have a heart condition, are pregnant, or suffer from migraines, balance disorders, or tinnitus. The note you feel in your chest is infrasound. Earplugs do not stop it, and it can cause nausea, blurred vision, breathlessness, and a sourceless dread. Knowing in advance that the dread is physical helps when it arrives.

Go with one other person and agree on hand signals beforehand, because speech will be useless below the rim. Set a turnaround time before you descend and treat it as fixed. Your judgment will be worse inside the sound.

## What to bring

- **Water:** all of it, at least five or six litres per person per day, plus electrolytes. There is no water here.
- **Hearing protection:** foam plugs under earmuffs, with spare plugs. Both go in at the first tremor underfoot.
- **Eye protection:** glacier-style glasses with side shields, plus sealed goggles for the salt grit.
- **Sun protection:** sunscreen under the chin, nose, and brim, since the light comes up as hard as it comes down. Wear pale, close-woven long sleeves, a hat with a chin strap, and lip balm.
- **Footwear and gloves:** stiff-soled boots for the potsherds in the fill. Thin gloves protect you from hot clay and resin, and a little cooking oil takes resin off skin.
- **Food:** cold food only. Bring no stove, flame, or cigarette. The pit is lined with wax, oil, and turpentine vapour and fed by a constant draft.
- **Night gear:** warm layers, because the pan cools fast, and a headlamp.
- **Navigation:** a compass and marker flags. The pan is featureless, and the pit is invisible until you are at its edge.
- **Notes:** a slate or a notebook and pencil. Recordings will clip, and no microphone will capture the note you feel.

Tether everything and cap every bottle. Anything hollow you carry will try to sing, and anything that falls into a jar stays there and changes its note for good.

## The approach

Flag your arrival point and your line of travel. When you feel the tremor in your soles, slow down. When the pressure in your chest begins to swell and fade, stop walking normally. The wind is flowing toward the drop, the glare hides the edge, and a sinkhole lip may be undercut. Go low, probe ahead, and circle until you find the head of the spiral ramp.

Camp on the pan, far enough back that your chest is quiet. Do not camp in the pit, because the quiet spots do not stay put.

## Timing

Descend at dawn, when the jars are cool and the chord is low and almost sweet. Be climbing out by mid-morning. At noon the sun is straight down the shaft, the clay burns, and the only shade is inside jars you cannot enter. The fumes are thickest then, and the pods are splitting. If you want a second visit, go in late afternoon and stay near the top.

## Moving through the city

- **Use the ramp.** Do not trust the ladders: the lashings are ancient, and you would be climbing with shaken balance. Keep away from terrace edges and slumps, where the fill is sharp shards over old hollows.
- **Rest in the green bands and cross the bare ones briskly.** The bare bands are four paces of pressure on the sternum. Keep your jaw loose and keep breathing. If your vision blurs, step back into green and wait.
- **Follow the lizards.** They lie exactly on the centrelines of the quiet, and they are a more current map than the plants, which lag behind the sound. Step around them and do not displace them. I would also avoid wearing or waving anything orange, since that is their language.
- **Keep your time at the bottom short.** The basin is where the pit's own note presses hardest and where heavy vapour is likeliest to pool. Headache or dizziness means you should go up at once.
- **Check each other often.** Do it by eye and by touch.

## Conduct

The city is an instrument that has kept itself in tune for longer than anyone knows, through a balance of growth and wear. Every small thing you do to it moves something else, so:

- Do not pull a rosette, snap a resin thread, or lean on a jar.
- Do not set your pack down in a channel.
- Do not take a shard or touch the scrapers by the kilns. Someone rebuilt this place many times, and nothing says they are finished.
- Do not put your head into a jar mouth to look for the thumbprints. Use the daylight and look from outside.
- Do not expect to solve the mystery. The jars were never houses. You are visiting something made on purpose for a reason nobody left behind.

## Dusk

Be seated near the rim, on a quiet line, before the wind falters. When the voices drop out, high ones first, and the last drones stop, take your ear protection off. The few minutes of silence, with the resin ticking as it sets, are the reason to come.

The lizards will all run at once. Let them. They are moving because the map is about to change, and the place you are sitting may not be quiet in the new one. When the small flat jar near the rim starts up, put your plugs back in and follow your flags to camp.

## Afterward

Your ears may ring, and you may carry a phantom chord for days. Ordinary silence may feel thin for a while.

Before you step back between worlds, brush out your cuffs, seams, and pack. Seeds settle wherever the air is still, and you spent the day standing in exactly those places.
