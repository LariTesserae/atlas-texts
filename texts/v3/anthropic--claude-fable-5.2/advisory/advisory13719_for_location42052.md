# Preparing for the pitch town

The town isn't hostile, just completely indifferent. It is a machine that kept running after its people left: the troughs still carry pitch, the funnels still pull air, the flame still burns. Nothing in it needs you, and nothing will help you. The things that could kill you are mundane: heat, thirst, bad air, fire, and sticky ground.

## The four real dangers

**Heat and water.** Assume there is no water anywhere, since nothing there has ever been wet.

- Carry all your water, at least eight litres per person per day, plus electrolytes.
- Drink on a schedule. In air that dry, sweat evaporates before you feel it, and people collapse without ever feeling sweaty.
- The interior never cools; the bricks are warm at midnight. Make short visits of an hour or two, then go back to the surface.
- Sleep outside, partway up the basin slope and not directly north of the ramp, where the town's air comes out.

**Air.** The rock leaks gas as well as pitch, and the town sits at the lowest point of the basin.

- I would take the complete absence of animals as a warning. It may only mean there is no water, but it may mean the still air in low places is unbreathable.
- Bring a four-gas monitor (oxygen, flammables, hydrogen sulphide, carbon monoxide) and obey it.
- Be most careful in the sunken second storey, the low lanes, dead-end inner rooms, and above all the sump at the south wall.
- A rotten-egg smell means leave. If that smell suddenly vanishes, leave faster, because your nose has stopped working.
- A respirator with organic-vapour and dust filters helps with fumes and clay dust, but it won't save you from gas or missing oxygen.

**Fire.** Everything is fuel: the walls were puddled with pitch, the vine bleeds oil, and every lane has a trough of bitumen.

- Bring no flame of any kind: no stove, no smoking, no candle. Use sealed electric lights.
- Wear cotton or wool. Synthetics melt and build static.
- Think of the central flame as the town's pilot light, burning off the main seep. Never interfere with it.
- If you arrive and it is out, do not enter, because the gas will have been accumulating.
- Visit the flame room early, when the flame is a hand tall. In the afternoon it roars at arm's length in a soot-glazed dome, which suggests carbon monoxide. Never go near it with pitch on your clothes.

**Pitch.** Timing matters most here.

- In the morning the pitch is solid and safe to step across. By midday it creeps, and a boot left on it sinks and holds.
- The overtopped collecting pits lie somewhere under the black terraces, skinned over and of unknown depth. Probe ahead with a pole, or stay on dusty floor.
- Watch for the troughs along the lanes. In that dusk they are easy to step into.
- If you get stuck, give up the boot and save the foot. Be free before evening, when the pitch tightens wherever it happens to be.
- Bring vegetable oil and rags, since water won't remove pitch from skin.

## The building itself

- Don't walk on the roof. From above it looks like ordinary ground, so approach from the north and find the ramp. The fist-wide holes catch heels, and the old clay spans three hundred rooms.
- Never cut, pull, or hang weight on the vine. In many walls its roots are the mortar, and its stems are the lintels.
- Don't clear a leaf pad off a roof-hole for light. It will fall by itself in a few days.
- Don't trust the notched poles to the lower storey. I would skip that level entirely.
- Wear a bump cap for the low doorways and eye protection for the bursting pods. Watch for hard, round seeds underfoot on the dished thresholds.
- Take the ramp slowly. It is a slumped chute with sand on it, and you may be climbing it exhausted.

## Timing your day

- **Dawn to mid-morning** is the best working window. The town is at its coolest, the pitch is firm, the flame is low, and it is quiet enough to talk. The air is still, though, so stay in the upper lanes and watch the monitor.
- **Noon** is when the roof groans as it swells. It has done this every day for ages, but it will still rattle you. Be on your way out.
- **Afternoon** is the hottest and loudest time, with the pitch moving and sand blowing along the floors. It is also the best ventilated. Come down briefly, near the ramp, just to hear it.
- **Evening** is worth one visit near the entrance, with lights ready, because darkness under the roof is total.

## Finding your way

Three hundred rooms open off one another in permanent dusk, and the light patches change weekly as the vine opens and closes holes. Don't navigate by which rooms are bright. Use what stays constant:

- **Pitch flow.** All of it runs to the sump at the south wall. Going against the flow takes you back toward the centre and the north.
- **Light rods.** Vertical rods mean noon. In the afternoon they climb the eastern walls.
- **Sound.** The flame's roar marks the centre, where five lanes meet. The knocking door-slab marks the east while the wind holds.
- **Your own footprints.** They will be the only tracks in the dust. The afternoon wind may erase them, so also lay a cord line or reflective markers and collect them when you leave.

Let your eyes adjust at the foot of the ramp for ten minutes or more. A dim red headlamp preserves your dark vision, and you should carry two spares. Keep your pack narrow, because the lanes are only two shoulders wide.

## Your nerves

The noise is the part to prepare for mentally. The walls tick, the pods crack like snapped sticks, and the wind makes every corridor sound a low note until the whole town seems to breathe. The jars hum, and a door knocks twice, pauses, and knocks twice again. You will hear this as footsteps, breathing, and someone wanting in. Low notes like these can also cause physical unease or a sense of presence.

Remind yourself that there is not a track in the place and that every sound has a cause in heat or wind. Agree on hand and light signals with your partner, since voices won't carry in the afternoon. Do bring a partner, and set a firm turnaround time.

## Conduct

- Leave the jars sealed. The stamped plugs are someone's marks, and the contents are unknown.
- Leave the adze, the cord, and the quern where they are.
- Carry out everything, including your own waste. Nothing decays here, so whatever you leave stays for good.

## What to stay for

- Sit in a north lane when the afternoon wind reaches the funnels, and listen to the town sound.
- Watch the pitch move against the tally marks on the trough rims.
- At dusk, listen to the corridors fall quiet one by one, the longest last.

Then climb out, drink, and sleep under the open sky.
