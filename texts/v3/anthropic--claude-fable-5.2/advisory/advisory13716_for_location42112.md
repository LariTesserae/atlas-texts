# Preparing for the shelf town

The place itself is not hostile. Three things there will hurt you: the light, the wet cold, and the thin air. One thing there can hurt the town: you, with any sharp sound. Most of the preparation is about the second problem.

## Before you go

**Acclimatize.** A violet sky and a small, fierce sun mean serious altitude. Don't step straight in from sea level. Spend a week or two high beforehand and ask a doctor about altitude medication. There is no rescue many days' climb from anywhere, and you cannot shout for help.

**Deal with any cough first.** Altitude gives people a dry, hacking cough, and here a cough is like knocking over a shelf of glassware. Bring lozenges. If you arrive with a cold, postpone.

**Rehearse quiet at home for a week.** Set objects down edge-first, then flat. Close doors with your hand on them. Practice breathing a sneeze out through an open mouth into a thick pad. Notice how often you click a pen, clear your throat, or laugh through your nose. A loud noise is not the main risk; a reflex is.

## Silence your kit

Go through everything you own and ask what it does when dropped, opened, or bumped.

- **No metal on stone.** Leave crampons, steel-tipped poles, carabiners, and tin cups behind. Bring a wooden or horn bowl and spoon.
- **No velcro, snaps, or click-buckles.** Replace them with toggles and ties, and tape zipper pulls.
- **No crinkly wrappers.** Repack food into cloth bags.
- **Electronics fully mute.** That includes vibration, since a phone buzzing on a stone sill is a rattle. Use the electronic shutter on a camera.
- **No whistle.** Take it off your pack. It is the worst object you could bring.
- **Tether everything.** Put a cord on anything you could fumble. Tired hands drop things at altitude, and a dropped bowl whitens a lane.
- **Felt underfoot.** Felt soles grip wet rock and glaze and make no sound. Bring several pairs, because they will soak.
- **A small, soft pack.** The lanes are one person wide, and a frame will scrape both walls.

## Dress for wet cold, not dry cold

This is the hardest combination there is: air far below freezing and liquid water on everything. Down is useless. Wear wool, felt, and synthetics under a waterproof layer, with waterproof gloves and spares of everything.

Supercooled water on bare skin pulls heat fast, and if it sets while it's on you, it sets on you. Grease your exposed face, as the locals almost certainly do. Tuck in or shave a beard. The goat with its beard frozen to the trough is your warning.

Keep your hands usable. Here hands are voices, and mittens make you mute. Wear thin gloves with overmitts on cords.

## Protect your eyes and skin

Ordinary glacier goggles are probably the wrong tool. Glass beads with fog and will frost opaque at every set. That is likely why the locals wear slit visors, which have no surface to take. Bring a pair made of wood, bone, or plastic. The slit also sharpens vision slightly, which helps if you normally wear glasses.

The light comes from below as much as above. Put sunblock under your chin, under your nose, inside your nostrils, and on your ears and lips. With no real shadows, you lose depth, and a morning-built wall is "clear as an eye." You can walk straight into one. Move slowly with a hand slightly ahead.

## Live so that being frozen in place costs nothing

You cannot react to a set, because the front outruns a turning head. The only preparation is to never be in a position you couldn't hold:

- Don't stand in pools. If you must wade, do as the waders do and never stop lifting your feet.
- Don't rest a bare hand in a trough or kneel in a runnel.
- Never put your lips to standing water.

When the crack comes, stand where you are, as everyone does. Your lashes will rime and your wet clothes will stiffen. If something of yours is caught, wait. The sun frees it in minutes, and yanking tears skin.

The moments after the set are the dangerous ones:

- **The burning points.** The ice has become lenses. Don't stand in a crawling white spot, don't look into one, and don't leave gear in its path. Watch where the old women put kettles and you'll know the tracks.
- **The rerouted water.** Forty people are about to sprint through one-person lanes. Until you know the work, flatten into a doorway and stay out of the channel.

## Getting around

- **Navigate by the stone, not the ice.** Use the worn stairs, the fluted walls, the headwall, and the lip. Everything else changes hourly, and your morning route may be a pool by noon.
- **Don't trust afternoon ice.** Don't lean on it or shelter under it. It sags.
- **Keep away from the scree** under the headwall, where stones fall several times a day.
- **Keep away from the lip** when fog is coming over it. There is no rail and no shadow to show you the edge.
- **Don't slap the midges.** A slap is a clap. Wear a head net.

## Water and food

The water is liquid below freezing, so don't drink it straight. Do what the locals do and warm it first. The gold-green life in it is probably food, or the base of the food, but goats and birds share every pool, so boil it. A stove's igniter clicks, so light it with a match, inside. There is no wood, and the fuel is dung, so bring food that needs little cooking.

## The people

- **Arrive mid-morning.** By then the night's lock has melted and there is daylight ahead. Stand at the edge with open, visible hands and wait to be noticed. These people watch hands all day and will see you at once.
- **Keep your gestures small and slow.** Flailing is probably babbling, or shouting.
- **Don't touch the knotted strings** in doorways. They are writing.
- **Don't try the tongue-click.** It is a craft, aimed with the palm. Wait until someone guides your hand.
- **Bring gifts of what they lack.** Good cord, felt, salt, needles, and dry fuel all suit a place without wood. Bring nothing that rings, and probably no glass.
- **Learn where the dry room is.** If you feel a cough, a sneeze, or tears coming, go there. Strip off your wet layers before entering, since its dryness is the whole point. After a few days you may want it just to hear your own voice.

## Night

Be indoors, fed, and done with every errand before the last light. The town locks, nobody walks, and you shouldn't either. I suspect this is partly cold, partly custom, and partly that it is the only rest these people get.

## When to leave

Leave if you develop a wet cough, a headache that won't lift, or clumsiness. Any of these puts you and the town at risk.

Expect that you will probably cause one set. The mountain causes several a day, and they will likely forgive a stranger one. Stand still, then pick up a scoop and help.
