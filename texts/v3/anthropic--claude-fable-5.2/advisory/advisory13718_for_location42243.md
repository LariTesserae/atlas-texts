# Preparing for the Plateau

The plateau will work on you in five ways at once: thin air, total dryness, unbroken noise, static charge, and a brown half-dark with no shadows. None is dramatic alone, but together they wear a person down in a day or two. The town is the only place where most of them stop, so plan the whole trip around reaching a shell and behaving well once you are inside it.

## Your body

**Altitude.** At nearly five thousand metres you get about half the oxygen you are used to. If your crossing drops you straight onto the plateau, you skip the slow climb that would normally acclimatise you.

- Spend two to three weeks at altitude beforehand, or use a hypoxic tent.
- Carry altitude medication and know the signs of fluid in the lungs or brain: a wet cough, breathlessness at rest, stumbling, confusion.
- The usual cure is descent, but the lower country is under cloud, a long way down a slope nobody describes. Your way out is your crossing point, so never be more than a day from it.

**Water.** There is none, and the place is built around that fact. Dry, cold, thin air takes water from you with every breath.

- Carry four to five litres per person per day, for the whole stay plus a margin.
- Use soft bladders rather than rigid bottles, which will sing and slosh, and keep them inside your clothing so they don't freeze.
- Don't assume the town can spare water. I don't know what its people drink, and it would be rude to arrive needing it.
- Don't try the grazers' trick of eating wax. Humans cannot make enough water from fat.
- Bring lip balm, saline nasal spray and eye drops, and wear glasses instead of contact lenses.

**Cold.** You are high up under a permanent dust ceiling, so expect winter at noon and worse at night. Bring windproof layers and real insulation. Prefer wool and other natural fibres, because synthetics build static.

**Hearing and vibration.** The overtones are as loud as heavy machinery and never stop.

- Wear earplugs under earmuffs any time you are outside, and bring spares.
- Nothing blocks the fundamental. You will feel it in your shins, teeth and chest, and infrasound like that tends to bring nausea, unease and a dread with no cause. Knowing it is the plant helps.
- Have loose fillings seen to before you go.
- If you have a pacemaker, a vestibular disorder or severe migraines, this is a bad place for you.
- Thick, soft-soled boots matter. The grazers have wide padded feet for a reason.

**Lungs and eyes.** The air carries fine dust, pollen and ozone. Wear a proper particulate respirator and sealed goggles outdoors, and bring antihistamines.

## Your gear

Everything you own will be shaken, charged and dusted at once.

- **Fastenings.** Threaded parts will unscrew themselves. Use thread-locker, tape and lanyards.
- **Electronics.** Keep them switched off in padded antistatic bags and expect some to die anyway. They are most likely to work in the morning, when the charge is lowest.
- **Navigation.** Don't count on a compass, though it is worth testing at the rim. The sun is still visible as a brown disc, so a mechanical watch and the sun's position give you direction.
- **Light.** Noon is dusk and night is black, with no stars. Bring headlamps and spare batteries. Solar chargers are useless.
- **Trailing cord.** The townspeople drag a cord to bleed charge off themselves. Bring a conductive one, or copy theirs at the first chance. Without it, every touch in the late afternoon is a spark.
- **Fire.** Bring no open flame. The surface survives lightning, but under it lie kilometres of dry, resinous, lignite-like plant. Carry food that needs no cooking.
- **Dropped things.** Anything small that you set down on the shaking ground will travel a few centimetres a day toward the nearest still line. Litter does not vanish. It ends up laid out on a ridge, in public, for good. Pack everything out, including your waste, unless the town tells you its own practice. If you lose something, search the nearest ridge.
- **Shelter.** Don't stake anything. The surface is as hard as fired clay, and it is the sealed skin of a living thing that hoards its moisture. Use a free-standing shelter and a thick air mattress. Even then, sleeping outside will be poor.

## Communication

Outdoors, speech is impossible, so prepare before you arrive.

- Agree on hand signs with your companions and practise them in thick gloves.
- Wear pale gloves against dark sleeves so your hands can be read in the gloom.
- Carry a slate.
- Bring a clicker, not a whistle. The birds call in hard clicks above the overtones because that is the only register the plant leaves free, and a sharp click will carry where a shout won't.
- At night, use a short line between people.

## Crossing the open ground

**Timing.** Move between the end of the moth-fall and early afternoon. The afternoon wind charges the mat, the charge peaks at dusk, and windy evenings bring the dry storms. You want to be indoors by then.

**Footing.** The flat light hides slope and depth. The rim has rounded shoulders, not a clean edge, and the surface is hard and waxy, so stay well back. Do the same at the pit, where the edge is the broken roof of a gallery. There are pipes under you everywhere, and whistling marks where they open to the air. Treat a whistle as a warning of a hole.

**Ridges.** The still lines are quieter and easier on the body, and you will be walking on bone. Three things to know about them:

- They curve and fork according to the sound, so they don't lead anywhere in particular.
- They migrate outward at walking pace as the mat cools at night. A ridge you fall asleep on will move out from under you.
- They are where the herds rest, so they are where the hunters come.

**Hunters.** They approach across the loud ground, where footfalls are lost in the noise, and with your ears plugged you are deafer than a grazer. You have to watch for them instead. They are pale and low against olive-black ground. Rest in a group facing both ways off the ridge, and keep a light sweeping at dusk. I can't say whether they would try an animal your size. Ask in town, and until then assume they would.

**The band.** At the town limits the plant's pattern and the town's disagree. Ridges break into dashes and the sound throbs.

- Cross near noon, when the beat is slowest. Before dawn, and through the cold season, it quickens to a growl and will be sickening.
- Cross briskly and don't rest inside it. Nothing else does.
- The hunters wait just outside it, so the first few hundred metres beyond the band are the most dangerous ground on the plateau, in either direction.

**Finding the town.** From the rim it shows as a few dozen pale threads between ground and sky. Take a bearing on them while you can. Once inside the band, the streets are ruler-straight lines of dust, and each leads to a shell. One runs west to a low swell and stops.

## In the town

**The threshold.** You will feel it as pressure in your ears. Swallow and stand still for a minute. The dust on you will drop straight to the floor, so let it fall at the doorway. Take your ear protection off slowly. After hours of drone, the silence can be a physical shock, and some people cry. You will also speak far too loudly at first, so whisper. Birds sleep in the ribs overhead, and the people here have the only quiet for sixty kilometres.

**Tones.** This is the rule that matters most. The plant thickens wherever a pure tone is held against it, and two notes placed too close will fight. In this town, sound is architecture, and each wall answers a note someone chose generations ago.

- Silence every alarm, beep and ringtone before you arrive.
- Don't hum, whistle or sing held notes, and don't set down anything with a steady whine.
- A brief sound probably does no physical harm, since the plant responds over decades. But I would expect it to land the way lighting a fire in someone's timber hall would.

**What not to touch.** Don't handle the sounders, and don't ask where they came from as though you might want one. Don't walk through the dust ropes, which feed the walls. Don't cut, pin or lean hard things on the living skin of the shells.

**Courtesy.** Bleed your charge through the cord before you touch anyone. Outdoors, sign.

**The western swell.** A shell stood there until its sounder went quiet. Everyone in town lives inside the same arrangement. Treat it as you would a grave, and let them raise the subject.

## The day's rhythm

- **Before dawn.** The charge leaks away and the moths fall for about an hour, a soft patter. Cover your water, your food and your mouth.
- **Morning.** The air is at its cleanest and the charge at its lowest. Travel now.
- **Noon.** Dim and cold, but the band is at its calmest.
- **Afternoon.** The wind rises and the charge builds. Get indoors.
- **Dusk.** The hair tips show violet points and the moths lift off the mat in their millions. Watch from a doorway. On storm evenings the lightning crawls flat across the mat from ridge to ridge and lights the underside of the sky. Stay inside and off the lines.

## What to expect of yourself

This place levels things. It ground down a mountain range, and it turns carcasses into rows of bone. With no horizon, no shadows and no pause in the sound, a few days of it will make you feel sorted too. Keep outdoor stretches short and get back to the silence often. Leave while you still have water in hand. For some days afterwards, other places will sound thin to you.
