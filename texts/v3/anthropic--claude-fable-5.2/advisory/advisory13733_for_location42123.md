# Preparing for the Jar Yard

Nothing here is trying to hurt you. It is a working potters' settlement at the bottom of a brick shaft about four storeys deep, and it is safe if you behave. The hard parts are the noise, which never stops, the heat, which never fully leaves, and the footing. Almost every surface also belongs to someone or something, so most of your preparation is learning not to be a nuisance.

## Before you go

**Health**
- Be current on tetanus. You will be walking on broken pottery among goats.
- If rabies pre-exposure shots are available to you, get them. You will be near a large bat colony.
- Dried bat and pigeon dung carries lung infections. Bring a dust mask or close-woven scarf for the new moon, when the pigeon jars are scraped out, and for dust storms.
- If you have asthma, the kiln burns dung and chaff at the bottom of a pit. Plan around firing days.

**Clothing**
- Wear loose, covering clothes in natural fibre. Synthetics melt near kiln sparks and open lamps.
- Choose things you don't mind ruining. Limewash rubs off white and grey slip gets on everything.
- Wear closed shoes with thick but flexible soles: thick for sherds and thorn scraps near the kiln, flexible for palm-trunk galleries and scooped stair treads.
- Bring a soft hat you can crush. A stiff brim gets in the way in low doorways and when someone needs to talk into your ear.
- Bring dark glasses for noon and goggles or a scarf for dust.

**Kit**
- Carry your own water, as much as you can, plus salts. Rain comes a few times a year and the cistern is their whole supply.
- Bring a way to boil or filter water. The cistern is green below the waterline and is filled by gutters that run down walls streaked with bat and pigeon droppings.
- Bring earplugs for sleeping, and for rain.
- Bring a dim light, red if possible. A bright torch dazzles people, disturbs the bats and draws every moth in the shaft.
- Pad or leave behind anything that rings, clanks, beeps or whistles. Pitched sounds mean something down there.
- Keep your pack small and close-fitting. A wide one will hit jar rims, drying pots and lintels.

## When to arrive

- **Morning is best**, after the pigeons have left and before noon.
- **Avoid noon.** For two hours the sun stands straight down the shaft and the floor is too bright to look at.
- **Avoid dusk and dawn on the stair.** It runs down the north wall, and the bats live in the upper jars of that wall. At dusk they pour out past the stair, and at dawn they come back.
- **Check for smoke.** If the kiln is firing, the east end is very hot for a night and a day, and the whole yard is hotter for days after. A day or two before a firing is the coolest time, but the floor will be covered with drying pots.
- **Never approach in the dark or in blowing dust.** The parapet is low and the drop behind it is sheer.

## The rim and the stair

You will hear the yard well before you see it, as a roar coming out of flat ground. At the parapet:

- Don't lean on the top course. It is this year's work and the mortar may be fresh.
- Don't drop or kick anything over the edge.
- Don't shout down. Nobody will hear you, and it marks you as someone who doesn't understand the place.
- Stand where you can be seen and wait a moment. Someone will notice. You may hear a jar struck, which is news of you travelling.

On the way down:

- Keep to the wall side and use the groove. It is at hip height for the locals, so it may be low for you.
- Place each foot deliberately. Every tread is scooped, patched and scooped again, and no two are alike.
- Expect droppings on the upper steps, and slick treads if it has rained.
- If you meet someone carrying a basket of clay, you are the one who gives way.
- Never put a hand into a jar mouth to steady yourself. Every one is occupied.

## Sound and talking

This is the biggest adjustment. The noise is sparrows by the thousand, mallets, querns, goats and children, all in a brick box. Under it is a hum from wind crossing the jar mouths, and the lowest notes are felt in the chest more than heard. Some visitors find this makes them uneasy or slightly sick for the first hour. Knowing it is only the jars helps.

- **Don't raise your voice.** People talk with their mouth at the other person's ear, or with their hands. Expect strangers to come very close and to touch your arm for attention. Do the same, gently.
- **Learn a few hand signs first**: yes, no, water, thank you, where, may I.
- **Learn your host's call-jar on the first day.** Each household has one pitch. If you hear it, it may be for you.
- **Never strike a jar**, whether a call-jar or a wall jar, even idly in passing. Don't whistle either. Ask your host on day one what to do if you need help urgently.
- **Wear earplugs sparingly by day.** The sound carries the yard's information, and the chord tells everyone the wind's direction. You won't learn to read it on a short visit, but you can read the people. If the women start carrying drying pots in off the floor, dust is coming from the northwest. Cover your face and get out of their way, or carry pots if asked.
- **Don't be alarmed by sudden silence.** In the afternoon a kestrel drops into the shaft and every bird stops for one breath. Then the alarm goes up louder than before.

You won't find quiet anywhere, including at night. Accept that before you arrive and it is easier.

## Heat, light and water

- Nights at the bottom are never cool, because the brick gives back the kiln's heat for days. Sleep on top of your bedding, drink before you are thirsty and take salt.
- Rest in shade through noon, as the sparrows and people do.
- For the rest of the day the light bounces off limewash and comes from no direction. With no shadows it is hard to judge steps or guess the hour.
- On the galleries the light comes through reed mats in stripes, which can hide gaps and uneven trunks. Look at your feet.
- Never wash in the cistern or dip your own vessel into it. Ask how water is taken.
- Treat any water you are not sure of. The cool water from the sweating jars will be what you are offered.
- Stay out of the three settling pans beside the cistern. The slip skins over and can look like solid ground.

## Underfoot and overhead

- **The floor** is sherds set on edge, worn smooth, and it clinks. It is safe in shoes, but a fall onto it will cut you.
- **Don't dig, and don't pry up a sherd** as a keepsake. The floor is the oldest thing they have. If you want a sherd, ask for one from the waste.
- **Drying pots** stand on the floor at times. They are unfired and one careless heel can destroy a day's work. Walk around them with a wide margin.
- **Lowest-tier doorways** are at chest height and you enter bent over. If you are tall you will hit your head at least once.
- **The galleries** are palm trunks lashed with rope that wears out every few years. You are probably heavier than the locals, especially with a pack. Leave the pack below, step where they step, cross one at a time, and never hold the mats for support.
- **Fire**: reed, rope and palm wood are everywhere, and the lamps are open wicks in fat. Bring no stoves and no smoking, and take care with anything hot.

## The walls and their tenants

- **Jar mouths** look like handy cubbyholes. Don't store anything in them or hang gear across them.
- **Leaning** against the bottom course is fine, since generations of shoulders have polished it. Keep your fingers out of the bellies, where toads sit for months.
- **Toads** have irritant skin. If you touch one, wash your hands before touching your eyes.
- **The rat snake** on the east wall is as long as a man and harmless. Nobody stops it, and you must not either. The moving patch of sparrow alarm shows where it is.
- **Mason bees** are gentle. Leave the pale mud plugs alone.
- **Geckos** sit in the jar rims and gather at the lamps to eat moths. Leave them to it.
- **Bats**: never handle one, even if it is grounded. Don't sit or eat under the north wall's upper courses.
- **Pigeons** hold the south wall. Old roosts often carry ticks and mites that bite at night, so sleep away from that wall if you have a choice, and use your own sheet.
- **Sparrows** are overhead everywhere. Keep your cup and food covered.
- **Goats** will eat straps, paper and hats. Keep your belongings off the lowest tier.

## The kiln and the work

- Stay back from the kiln unless invited, especially at firing and opening. A week of the yard's labour is inside.
- Don't walk between a potter and the light, and don't touch a pot on the turntable or anvil.
- Bread from quern-ground flour may contain grit, so chew carefully.
- Be cautious with raw goat's milk if your gut is not used to it.

## Rain

If you are there for one of the few rains each year:

- Stay under the mats. Wet sherds and tile are slick.
- The toads come out onto the floor by the hundred. If you must cross, shuffle your feet instead of stepping.
- For a night and a day the toads are louder than everything else combined. No one strikes a call-jar until they finish, so stay with your host and use your earplugs.

## Manners and gifts

- **Useful gifts**: good rope, fuel, salt. Rope is the best, because the galleries always need it.
- **Useful help**: carry a basket of clay down the stair when you arrive, if someone will let you.
- **Take everything out with you.** The yard rises a finger's width a generation on its own breakage, and whatever you drop will lie in that floor for good.
- **Your waste**: ask where it goes. Dung is fuel here and there will be a rule.
- **If you break something**, show it to its owner at once. Breakage is ordinary here, but hiding it is not.

## What it will be like

Your first hour will be overwhelming. The sky is a bright rectangle, the air smells of woodsmoke, goat, wet clay and ammonia, and the sound presses on you like weather. By the second day you will start hearing the parts of it: one household's jar, the quick patter of a paddle on a pot wall, a shift in the hum when the wind changes.

When you climb out, the quiet of the flat ground will feel wrong and your ears will ring for a while. That passes.
