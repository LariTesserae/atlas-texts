**The short version:** nothing here will hunt, sting, or bite you. The danger is the cold, the dark, the ice overhead, and thirty meters of water. Prepare as you would for a polar under-ice dive, and expect the calmest place you have ever been.

## Decide how far in you are going

- **Stay on the ice.** If you have no dive training, this is a real visit. Cut a hole and lower a light, a camera, and a hydrophone. You will see a kelp-topped wall and hear the blades sliding.
- **Dive it.** This needs real ice-diving and drysuit experience, earned somewhere more forgiving. You can't learn it here.
- **Use a small submersible or towed camera** if you want to cover distance. The shelf is more than a month's walk across, and a diver sees one field per hole.

## When to go

- **The summer weeks** give you the grey-green dimness, enough to tell up from down. Go then for a first visit.
- **Freeze-up and deep winter** are when the brine weather runs hardest, with sinking sheets and the dense rivers in the trunk canals. The water is fully black then, so make it a second trip.

## The surface camp

Treat the camp as a sea-ice expedition, and bring everything you will eat. The middens show this shore once fed people, but nothing lives here now except weed, and you don't know why. Don't eat the kelp.

- Sound through the ice before you cut. The relief is only a couple of meters, so you must choose your spot deliberately: a house mound, a causeway, the shoulder of a trunk canal.
- Put a heated shelter over the hole, which will try to refreeze. Someone stays up top tending your line the whole time.
- Hang a weighted downline with strobes.
- In summer, shovel long spokes in the snow pointing at the hole. From below they show as pale arrows.

## In the water

- **Protect your skin and your gear.** Wear a drysuit with heavy undergarments, dry gloves, and a full-face mask. Use cold-rated regulators with a fully redundant air supply, because free-flow from freezing is the classic failure.
- **Watch the clock.** Near the bottom you have twenty or thirty minutes before decompression or cold ends the dive. Cold usually ends it first.
- **Stay tethered to the surface.** In black water under a solid ceiling, the hole is the only exit, and it is smaller than you remember.
- **Carry three lights.** Yours are the only real color the place ever gets: the kelp shows bronze, the eelgrass green, the stone crust pink.
- **Travel above the canopy and drop down at chosen points.** The kelp stands five meters tall and leans together over the lanes, making tunnels. A tether dragged through the stipes will snag.
- **Carry cutters,** though kelp usually yields to patience.
- **Stay off the bottom.** The beds are silt and the drain floors are soft, half-rotted muck that will not bear weight. One careless kick blacks out the lane.

## Finding your way

The grid tempts you to navigate by it, but it bends wherever one generation's surveying met another's, and every field looks like the next. Trust the line and the compass, in that order.

- The drains fall toward the sea, and the brine in them flows away from the old shore.
- All the kelp in a lane leans with the tide, but the tide reverses and arrives half a day late at the inner fields.

## The brine

In the dark you will feel the brine before you see it, as a sudden cold. Treat that as a warning.

- It is colder than the freezing point of the water around you, cold enough to injure bare skin and freeze a regulator.
- It is dense enough that you will float on its surface as if on a second seabed. Rest a gloved hand in it and feel it pull seaward, but don't go in.
- Don't follow a canal to its outfall. The flow is slow, but beyond the stone throat is the slope and deep water.

## What you'll hear

- **The ice** ticks, and now and then gives a long descending groan that travels for miles. It is normal and will still frighten you the first time.
- **A stone** sometimes settles with a small, dull knock.
- **Your own bubbles** will be the loudest thing on the shelf. A trained rebreather diver can hear the leathery slide of blade on blade.
- **Darkness** is worth one deliberate minute: hold the downline and turn your lights off.

## How to behave

Change here is measured in crust thickness per century, so a fin-strike on a wall-top is a major event.

- Touch nothing you don't need to, and take nothing.
- Leave the wood alone. It has kept its adze facets only because the mud is cold and airless, and it will not survive handling.
- Look with your light instead. Held low and sideways, it brings up the cups and rings on the boundary stones, the pegs in the trackway, and the dished querns.
- Sterilize your gear before you come. Nothing here grazes, and one hitchhiking snail or urchin larva could undo the whole forest.

## What to expect of yourself

People expect the place to feel haunted, and it doesn't. It is finished and tidy, and the drains still work. What gets to people is the scale of the labor, basket after basket for a month's walk in every direction, and the fact that nothing there takes any notice of you. Some find that crushing and some find it restful.

On the way up, do your stops on the line and watch the lit rows of kelp fade below you.
