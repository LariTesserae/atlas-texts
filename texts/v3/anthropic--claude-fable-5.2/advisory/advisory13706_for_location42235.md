# Preparing for the plateau

This place is quieter and gentler than it sounds, and less forgiving. Nothing here will chase you, and there is no wind, rain, storm, or animal. The dangers are the air, the edges, and a ground that rearranges itself slowly enough to walk away from, but only if you notice. Most of the preparation is about noticing.

## Air

This is the one that kills, so settle it first.

- **Assume you cannot breathe here.** I am inferring from the description, but air that is "higher than weather" and barely carries sound is probably too thin to keep you conscious, even with bottled oxygen in an ordinary mask. Plan for a pressure suit, or at least pressure-fed oxygen with a counterpressure garment. Do not open anything until you have measured the pressure yourself.
- **Filter masks are useless.** The thin air here is partly volcanic gas, and a filter cannot add the oxygen that is missing. Carry a sealed supply with a wide margin, and a second way to breathe if the first fails.
- **Old figures are out of date.** The mountain is still rising, so any earlier visitor's readings describe a lower plateau.
- **Know what oxygen starvation feels like.** Dizziness, cheerfulness, and tilting vision are exactly what this landscape also produces honestly. Carry a small bubble level or plumb line. When the world seems to lean, the level shows whether it is the ground or you. If it is you, check your supply before anything else.

## Edges

Every surface is broken glass that has never been dulled. An ordinary stumble means a hand or knee on a blade, and in a pressure garment a cut is more than a cut.

- **Boots:** Bring thick, heat-tolerant soles, and a spare pair for any stay beyond a few days. Fresh lava eats footwear.
- **Outer layer:** Wear a cut-resistant shell over the suit, with hard knee, shin, and elbow guards and heavy overgloves. Carry suit patches where you can reach them with one hand.
- **Poles:** Take two with hard metal tips. They are for balance and for probing. Glass-skinned plates can be thin shells over hollows, so tap before you step. A hollow ring through the pole means go around.
- **Grit:** Treat it as crushed bottle. Do not sit or kneel on it or brush it off with your palm, and never wipe your visor with a gritty glove. Bring a soft brush and a spare visor cover.
- **Rope:** Rope laid over these edges gets cut. If you rope up near fissures, use edge protection or cable.
- **First aid:** Bring fine tweezers, irrigation, pressure dressings, and burn dressings. Glass cuts hold splinters.

## Heat

Cold is not the main problem. The rock is at body temperature, the air is too thin to carry heat away, and your suit is insulated. If you work hard you will overheat with no breeze to help, so you need to manage it deliberately.

- **Pace and water:** Walk slowly and rest standing. Carry all your water, because there is none here and there never has been. You will lose more than you expect.
- **Seams and new rock:** Rock that looks merely dark can be far hotter than skin. A cheap infrared thermometer is worth more than almost anything else in your pack. A thermal viewer is better still, because it turns the plain into a readable map of where the ground is alive.
- **Open splits:** They radiate hard, and the thin air does nothing to soften it. Do not lean over the orange.

## Light

The darkness comes with a faint, useful glow from below, and you should not drown it.

- **Use a dim red or amber lamp at your feet.** Keep white light for emergencies. Your dark-adapted eyes can read the seams and the tint of the lid, and that reading tells you where the activity is.
- **Expect things to look wrong.** The light comes from underneath, so the shadows are upside down. Slabs seem to float, hollows look like mounds, and distances are hard to judge. Trust the pole over your eyes.
- **A bright lamp announces you.** It is visible across the whole plateau, the only white point in forty kilometres of brown dark. Decide in advance whether you want that. I come back to this at the end.
- **Keep time by a clock.** Nothing under the lid will tell you day from night.

## Reading the ground

No map survives here, but the surface shows its age, and age is the nearest thing to safety:

- **Ticking** means the newest glass, hours to days old. It is hot, thin, and still moving. Stay off it.
- **Clean, shiny glass with no film** is recent, and whatever tilted or split it may not be finished.
- **Dark matte circles along the seams** mean the slab has lasted at least a few weeks. Where the film has flaked, the slab has flexed lately.
- **Pale salt bloom** means it has sat undisturbed a long time. This is the oldest and best ground.
- **Fresh yellow and white needles** mark an active vent. The gas there is at its worst, and the crust around it is fragile.

Walk and rest on bloom and film where you can.

## A ground that moves

At any time four or five things are happening somewhere on the plain, and almost certainly not where you are. The risk is that one starts beside you while you are asleep or not paying attention.

- **Domes:** If a level stretch has become a slope since you last looked, leave by the side and never over the crest, where it will split.
- **Spines:** Stay several times their height away. They lean before they fall, and the lean shows the direction. In air this thin, thrown fragments fly much farther than you would expect.
- **Fissures:** One that runs a kilometre in an afternoon moves slower than a walk, so you can outpace it. Never let one get between you and your way out, though. They carry ground sideways, and the far side may not come back.
- **Sleep:** Never have everyone asleep at once. If you are alone, set a tilt alarm and a ground-temperature alarm, and sleep in short stretches on the oldest slab you can find.
- **Your feet:** They are your early warning. Keep some attention on them the way you would on a ship's deck.

## Sound

You will hear your own breath, the ticking and hiss nearby, and occasionally a held note from a crack. Nothing distant will warn you.

- **Talking:** Voices will not carry between people. Use radio, touching helmets, or agreed lamp and hand signals, and practise them before you arrive.
- **Judging distance:** When a tremor comes through your boots, count until the flat clap. Every three seconds is roughly a kilometre. If the count is short, look around.

## Finding your way

Do not expect to navigate by the usual means.

- **What fails:** There are no satellites here. A compass will be pulled about by the fresh rock. Landmarks do not last a season. Even a marker you place will stay put only relative to its own slab, which may travel.
- **What holds:** The band of stars at the rim is the only fixed thing. Learn the star pattern in each direction on arrival and take your bearings from it.
- **Distances:** Count paces and write them down, noting how old the ground looked. Stay well inside the range you can walk back on half your air.
- **The rim:** Its edge is the newest and least supported rock of all, so do not go right up to it. The view of unflickering stars is worth the walk, from a respectful distance back.
- **Your way home:** If it depends on a particular spot, check it often. The spot may still be where it was while the ground under it is not.

## Camp

- **Shelter:** There is no wind, so it does not need to be strong. Its floor does need to survive glass, so bring a thick sacrificial ground layer. If you want to take your helmet off inside, it must also hold pressure.
- **Gear:** Bag anything you set down. Ash is abrasive, the gas corrodes, and the bloom settles on whatever is left out.
- **Leaving:** Take everything with you.

## The stacked stones

Someone is here, or was here very recently. They have no tools, and they care about plumb and about a straight line enough to keep repairing both. The stone without bloom was probably set within days. You will likely never see them. They have no way to hear you, but they may well feel you through the ground the same way you feel a spine collapse.

- **Do not alter their work.** Do not move, borrow, straighten, or add to the cairns or the line. They are the only record of where the ground used to be, and a helpful stone from you would corrupt it.
- **Use the cairns as information.** A bloomed, toppled cairn marks ground that has moved. A plumb cairn on a tilted slab shows where the builder has been lately.
- **Stay out of the walled patches.** I suspect they are beds, cleared of rubble so a body can lie down, with walls just high enough to hold the warm layer of air. I would not sleep in one uninvited.
- **If you want to say hello, build your own.** Stack a small pile well apart from theirs, in a style obviously not theirs, and look at it again before you leave. If it has been levelled and wedged with chips, you have your answer.
- **Leave the dark film alone too.** Step around it where you can. Nobody knows what it is, and it has little enough ground that lasts.

## State of mind

There is no sky here, and no sound from anything in the distance. The horizon is orange lines that end up somewhere slightly different after each tremor. It can feel like standing in a room while the building is still going up around you. Short visits are best. Move slowly and check your level, your air, and your feet in a steady routine. The plateau itself is not hostile. It is still under construction, and you are sharing it with someone who has learned to live with that and who has not been told you are coming.
