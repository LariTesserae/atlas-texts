# Preparing for the Basin

Nothing in the Basin is hunting you and no storm is coming. Three things will wear you down anyway: the thin air, the hard light, and above all the noise. The fourth concern runs the other way. The place is in exact balance, and you are the only thing in it that isn't.

## Before you go: the air

"Just under the height where breathing stops being automatic" means your body will be working near its limit the whole time.

- **Do not arrive directly at altitude.** If your crossing lets you choose, arrive in the low country and walk up over five to seven days, sleeping a little higher each night. If it drops you at the rim, acclimatize in your home world first.
- **Learn the signs of altitude sickness.** A headache that worsens, a wet cough, breathlessness at rest, stumbling, or confusion all mean the same thing: go down now.
- **Check each other by sight.** You won't hear a companion's cough or laboured breathing over the roar, so watch lips, fingernails, and gait. A fingertip pulse oximeter helps.
- **Sleep outside the bowl, and lower.** At this height your breathing stutters in sleep, and you'll wake gasping. Camp on the outer slope below the rim, where it's quieter and the air is thicker.
- **Plan to be slow.** "Half a day's walk across" is a local's half day. Double it.

## The light

That small hard sun is an ultraviolet lamp, and the ground reflects it upward.

- **Protect the undersides.** Put sunscreen under your chin, inside your nostrils, and behind your ears. Use an SPF lip balm.
- **Wear glacier-grade sunglasses with side shields, and carry a spare.** Snow-blindness here would come from below.
- **Dress for two climates a step apart.** Sun is warm and shade is stone-cold, so wear layers you can open and close quickly, with a windproof shell for the headwall side.
- **Insulate yourself from the rock.** Bring a sit pad and a proper sleeping mat. The air never turns bitter, but the stone will steadily drain your heat.

## The noise

Take the description literally: sound you feel in your breastbone and that raises ripples on water is loud enough to damage hearing in minutes. Plan around it more than anything else.

- **Double up on ear protection.** Wear foam plugs under over-ear defenders and put them on before you cross the rim. Bring spares, because a dropped plug is gone.
- **Expect protection to be partial.** The low drone travels through your skull and chest. Budget your time on the floor in hours and retreat over the rim to rest. Fatigue, nausea, irritability, and a vague dread are effects of the low-frequency sound, not signs something is wrong with you.
- **Agree on a hand language beforehand.** You need a dozen signs at least: stop, OK, not OK, headache, going down, look, water, come here, quiet place. Touch a shoulder to get attention, since nobody can be called.
- **Stay in sight of each other and don't go alone.** If you break an ankle out there, no shout or whistle will bring help. Carry a signal mirror for emergencies and a small slate or notepad for anything complicated.
- **Tether your gear.** You won't hear anything fall, and you won't hear your stove flare, your zipper open, or an animal behind you. Use your eyes for everything.

## The floor

The plates are loose, thin, and tilting, like a field of stacked dishes.

- **Wear stiff boots with ankle support and use trekking poles.** Bring gloves too, because fine-grained rock breaks to sharp edges.
- **Enter over the low lip, away from the headwall and the notch.** The notch is a wind funnel.
- **Wait for the morning cloud to withdraw.** The eastern rim is wet and its lichen is slick until it does.

## Water and food

- **Carry in most of your water.** You need four to five litres a day, since the air is very dry and you'll lose water just by breathing.
- **Don't drink from the lake.** It is brine, and the crust and the mud under it are probably caustic. Don't wade.
- **Treat the seeps with suspicion.** The water looks clean, but it has filtered down beneath hundreds of thousands of nesting birds. Filter and chemically treat it, and use it as a reserve only. Take a little from each seep and don't dig or dam.
- **Stay off the foot of the headwall.** Besides the droppings and the ammonia, dried guano dust can carry lung infections. If you must go close, wear a mask.
- **Bring food that needs little cooking.** Water boils cool up here and your appetite will be poor. Seal everything, because the rodents will investigate.
- **Bring a stove and matches, and light no fires.** Lighters sulk at altitude. The cushion plants would burn, but each took centuries to grow and is not yours to spend.

## The animals

They communicate by posture and position, so they will read your body too.

- **Move slowly and predictably, in plain view.** Things that appear suddenly are alarming in a place where nothing can be heard coming.
- **Avoid white or flapping cloth.** A white flash is the grazers' alarm signal.
- **Give the herds a wide berth.** The same goes for the waders at the shore.
- **Ignore the scavengers overhead.** They are only watching for things that lie still, but that is a good reason not to nap in the open.

## The quiet place

You will want this spot badly. After hours in the roar, stepping in and hearing your own breath may undo you a little, and that's normal.

- **You are the third claimant.** The grazers and the people who built the fire ring come first. If a grazer is there or approaching, stay out. With no seasons here, births can happen any day.
- **Visit, don't camp.** Speak softly inside, and don't cook or relieve yourself there.
- **Leave everything where it lies.** The chert flakes were carried in from somewhere else. The propped plates are someone's instrument, so don't rearrange, chip, or add to it. I'd even leave it unplayed, but if you can't resist, use a knuckle, play it once, and play softly.
- **If the people come, yield the circle.** Step out, show open hands, and use signs. Offer nothing they'd have to carry or explain.

## Leaving nothing

The lake has no outlet and its shoreline has only ever been in one place. Whatever you leave here stays.

- **Pack out all waste, including your own.**
- **Clean your gear before crossing.** Scrub boots and equipment free of seeds, soil, and anything from another world's birds.
- **Take no plates, lichen, or souvenirs.**

## Time

Nothing here will tell you when to leave. There is no weather to turn, no season to end, and every day is the same day. Hypoxia and noise will blur your judgment, so decide your exit date before you enter, write it down, and keep a daily log. Expect ringing ears and a strange flat silence for days afterward.

## Packing list

- Earplugs (many) and over-ear defenders
- Glacier glasses with side shields, plus a spare pair
- Sunscreen and SPF lip balm
- Layered clothing in muted colours, with a windproof shell
- Stiff boots, trekking poles, and gloves
- Sit pad, sleeping mat, and a warm sleeping bag
- Water containers for 4–5 litres a day, a filter, and chemical treatment
- Stove, fuel, and matches
- Sealed food
- Dust mask
- Pulse oximeter and altitude medication, if your physician agrees
- Signal mirror, slate or notepad, and gear tethers
- Waste bags
- Logbook
