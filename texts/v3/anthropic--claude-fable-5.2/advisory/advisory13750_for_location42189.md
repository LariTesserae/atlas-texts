Nothing in this basin will hunt you. The dangers are thin air, cold, loose rock, and water that changes through the day. The other fact to plan around is that there is no road, no trail and no rescue, so the only way out is the way you came in.

## The air

At 5,400 metres each breath carries about half the oxygen it does at sea level. On your own Earth, people reach places like this by walking uphill for two weeks, and the walk is what makes it survivable. If you step straight in from low altitude, you skip that, and it is the most dangerous thing about the trip.

You have three workable options:

- **Acclimatize first.** Spend two to three weeks at increasing altitude in your home world immediately before you go, ideally sleeping above 4,500 m by the end. Acclimatization fades within days, so don't leave a gap.
- **Keep it short.** If you arrive unacclimatized, plan a visit of a few daylight hours and do not sleep there. You will feel breathless and probably headachy, but the serious illnesses usually take longer to develop.
- **Bring supplemental oxygen.** Treat it as a safety margin for getting out, not a way to stay longer.

Before you go, talk to a doctor who knows altitude medicine. Ask about acetazolamide for prevention, and about carrying dexamethasone and nifedipine as emergency drugs. Bring a pulse oximeter and, if at all possible, a companion, because you cannot assess your own judgment when hypoxic.

Decide your leaving rules in advance and write them down:

- **Leave if you can't walk a straight line.** A worsening headache with clumsiness, confusion or stumbling means leave now, not in the morning.
- **Leave if your breathing fails at rest.** The signs are breathlessness while sitting still, a wet cough, or gurgling in the chest.
- **Trust your companion.** If they say you are acting strangely, believe them.

Even a well-acclimatized visitor should expect to walk slowly, sleep badly, wake gasping, and have little appetite. Avoid alcohol and sleeping pills. Because your exit is your only descent, camp near it and make sure you could operate it while sick, in the dark, in falling snow.

## Arriving and keeping the schedule

Arrive in the morning, on the older moraine near the rock lip. The ground there has had twenty years to settle and you are far from the ice. Don't arrive in the afternoon, when you would step into flat grey light with no shadows and no sense of distance.

Plan each day around the basin's clock:

- **Dawn:** Everything is frozen, the silt is crusted stiff, and the streams are down to a trickle. This is the best time to cover ground and cross water, though it is bitterly cold.
- **The sun window:** For a few hours the ice shells collapse, the midges rise and the birds spread out. Spend it sitting still and watching.
- **Early afternoon:** Cloud pours over the lip and closes the basin. Be back at camp, or sure of your route to it, before this happens.
- **Dusk:** The streams peak hours after the warmth that fed them. A thread you stepped across at breakfast may be thigh-deep, opaque, and rolling stones along its bed.

So never end the day with a stream between you and your camp or your exit.

For navigation, carry a compass and take bearings in the morning light. Don't assume satellite positioning or emergency beacons work in this world. If you need to mark a route, use wands you can pull out again. Do not build cairns, because there are none here.

## The ground

Three kilometres here is most of a day's travel, at perhaps one kilometre an hour.

- **Boulders:** Test every large stone before you weight it. Never walk beneath or downhill of perched ones, since the angles that "will not last" may end when you touch them.
- **Moraine walls:** Stay well back from their foot. They stand steeper than loose debris can hold. A short rattle is your cue to move away from the walls, toward open floor.
- **Valley walls:** Rockfall is worst when the sun first hits them and during the afternoon thaw. The blue sheep also kick stones loose.
- **Saturated silt:** It is mostly ankle-deep, but probe with a pole. Glacial silt can grip to the knee. If it does, lean back and work the leg out slowly.
- **Dust:** Dry silt and collapse plumes are fine rock flour. Pull a buff over your mouth and protect your eyes. The silt also gets into zips, stove jets and lenses, so bag everything.

Wear stiff, waterproof boots with high gaiters, and leather-palmed gloves, because the rock is sharp. Carry two trekking poles. A sprained ankle here is a real emergency.

## Water

Cross streams early, facing upstream, with your poles planted and your pack's hip belt undone.

For drinking, take water from wall seeps or the clear ponds, not the milky threads. Rock flour clogs filters and upsets the gut. If milky water is all you have, let it settle overnight in a pot first. Treat everything, since ducks, sheep and pikas share it.

Water boils at about 82 °C at this height, so bring food that only needs hot water. Gas canisters sulk in the cold, so sleep with one in your bag, and bring matches because piezo lighters fail up here. There is nothing to burn, and you shouldn't want to. Drink three to four litres a day, as the dry air strips water from you with every breath.

## The ice

Stay away from the shore of the big pond at the ice front. When a slab drops in and turns over, it sends a wave across the pond. Stay off the glacier itself unless you have crevasse training, a rope and a partner.

You can still see the life in the ice. The grit-holes exist at the very margin, and a spoonful of their dark sediment under a pocket microscope will show you the rotifers and water bears.

## Cold and sun

- **Night cold:** Nights fall well below freezing. Bring a sleeping bag rated to about −20 °C and a thick insulated mat. Keep your boots in a bag inside the tent or they will be frozen solid at dawn.
- **Clothing:** Bring a base layer, fleece, a heavy down jacket, a waterproof shell, warm gloves with spares, a hat, and more socks than seems reasonable.
- **Sun:** Ultraviolet is about half again as strong as at sea level, and it comes through the cloud lid. Wear glacier-grade sunglasses all day, including under grey skies. People go snow-blind in exactly that flat light. Add sunscreen and lip balm.
- **Batteries:** Keep them in an inner pocket.

## Camp

Pitch on the older moraine, above any channel and clear of all walls and perched boulders. The meltwater cuts new beds overnight, so a dry, flat patch of silt may simply be last week's streambed. Pegs won't hold, so anchor the tent with stones and put the stones back afterwards.

Store food in sealed containers. The fox, the weasel and the choughs will all investigate.

## The living things

The animals are no threat to you. If you find the big round prints with the tail drag, you probably won't see the cat that made them, and it has no interest in you. You are the hazard to everything else:

- **Cushion plants:** A fist-sized dome may be fifteen years of growth. Walk on bare rubble.
- **Dark crust:** The film on wet silt is the first living skin on the place. Step around it.
- **Pika hay piles:** Don't touch them. Those few dried stems are a pika's winter.
- **The shelducks' pond:** Leave it to them and the ducklings.
- **Seeps:** Be quick when collecting water. Each seep is a redstart's territory.
- **Blue sheep:** Give them room at the mineral licks, and stay out from under them.

Before you travel, scrub your boots, tent, velcro and pockets clean of seeds and soil. This ground is at the very start of being colonized, and a hitchhiker from another world would matter here more than almost anywhere.

Carry out everything, including your own waste, in sealed bags. There is no soil to bury it in, and the cold preserves it for decades.

## What to bring for looking

- **Binoculars.** These are essential, since the animals are small or far away. A slope of scree may be thirty sheep.
- **A hand lens or pocket microscope.** Use it for springtails, fairy shrimp and the cryoconite life.
- **A sit pad and patience.** Most of what happens here is seen by staying still at a snow edge.
- **A notebook and pencil.** Ink freezes.
- **A headlamp with a red setting.**

If you can choose your season, the autumn crossing week gives you cranes and geese calling over the col, at the price of harder cold.

## What it will be like

Expect to feel slow, slightly stupid and often cold. Expect the place to seem empty for the first hour. Then you will kneel by a snow patch and see that the soot is moving. After that you will find life along every wet margin, fed by whatever the afternoon wind drops.

You will startle at the glacier's crack, and you should let the rattles of the moraine keep you alert. The clicking of settling stones you can get used to.

You should also expect a particular unease. It looks like untouched wilderness, but the soot on the ice, the brown haze below the lip, the jets overhead and the orange stain on the night horizon are all traces of people. The people overhead pass four or five times an hour and cannot help you. Hypoxia flattens mood, so some of the melancholy will be chemical, but some of it will be accurate.
