# Preparing for the town under the root plate

## Settle the question of size first

At your ordinary size you cannot visit this place. The whole room is as wide as a bed and a forearm high at its tallest. A knee on the moss, a hand lifting the snow lip, or a torch shone in would do more damage than the shrew ever has. If your way of travelling doesn't bring you in at the scale of the residents, about a spruce needle tall, don't go.

Everything below assumes you arrive small. At that scale:

- **Falls** rarely hurt, but they land you on the floor, where the springtails go off around you like dry rain. That is the last sound you want to cause.
- **Water** is dangerous. A drip is the size of your head, and the pool's surface can hold you.
- **Resin** will hold you too.
- **Cold** gets into a small body fast.

## What it will be like

You will visit a town and never see it. You will come home with no image of it, only a map held in your arms and fingers: so many pulls to the double knot, the bark splice under your palm, the cord dipping toward the water. It is cold, wet, resin-smelling, and nearly silent, with one steady drip. Things with hard backs will walk over your hands and you will let them.

## Training before you go

- **Darkness.** Spend several days doing everything blindfolded: dressing, eating, packing, tying and untying. You should be able to find anything in your kit by touch.
- **Hanging.** You travel hand over hand along cords, across gaps many times your body length. Train your grip and forearms, and practise stopping mid-movement and hanging perfectly still for minutes without warning.
- **Silence.** Learn to stifle a cough and a sneeze completely. If you have a cold, postpone.
- **The knots.** Learn them with your eyes shut:
  - Double knot: a junction.
  - Bark splice: a house. The frozen frog is also marked with one, so ask before assuming a splice means a door.
  - Frayed tassel: a line down to the pool weir.
  - Thorn tied crosswise: a spider's sheet nearby. Feel for it lightly, because it is a thorn.
- **Small animals.** If you can't stay calm with animals crawling on your skin, this is not your destination.

## What to wear and carry

- **Wool or felt, in layers.** The air is just under freezing and saturated, and the walls weep. Down and cotton fail when wet.
- **Quiet fabric.** Avoid anything that swishes, crackles or clicks. That rules out shell fabrics, velcro, zips and metal buckles.
- **A soft sit-sling with two short tethers,** closed with wooden toggles. Two tethers let you pass a knot without ever being unattached. The sling lets you hang still when your arms are spent and takes the jolt when the cords snap tight.
- **Thin gloves with bare fingertips,** so you can read the knots.
- **A padded hood.** Voles run through the roof moss and knock down frost and litter, and the frost beards on the cold roots fall under their own weight.
- **A lanyard on every object.** Anything dropped to the floor is gone within the hour.
- **Cold food wrapped in cloth,** more than you think you need. Nothing gets cooked here.
- **A small gift for the granary,** such as dried fruit or seed.

## What to leave behind

- **Anything that burns or sparks.** That means lighters, matches, stoves, spirits and solvents. Peat burns, pitch burns, and the snow lid would rot and fall.
- **Heat sources.** Warmth is the frog's business as well as the fire law's.
- **Steel tools.** The town works with chert, and steel on chert throws sparks. Bring bone or wood.
- **Lights, cameras and luminous watch dials.** No one in the town has seen the town, and it is not your place to change that. Don't bring a light even for emergencies. You'd be tempted, and your hosts could not know you had used it.
- **A watch of any kind.** It would be wrong here anyway.

## The quiet and the shrew

The quiet is the town's defence. The shrew comes through every few hours, hunting by ear and whisker, and it is five or six times your length. You get three warnings:

1. A pressure in the air.
2. Fast, wet breathing.
3. The springtails going off beneath it, like dry rain.

At the first of these, stop where you are and hang still. Don't finish your reach or adjust your grip, and don't whisper a question. It sometimes takes a house, so being indoors doesn't excuse you from stillness.

Other sounds are harmless, and you should learn to tell them apart:

- A rasp felt through the wood of the big root is something soft chewing.
- A creak through every root at once is wind in the crown.
- A patter from above is a vole passing.

## Moving on the cords

- **Mites.** Box mites walk the cords and will not move aside. Lift each hand over them. Don't brush them off or grip down on one. They are hard as seeds and will roll under your palm.
- **Wind.** When the trunk works against its neighbour, the cords go slack and then tight. At the first creak, lock on and wait.
- **Other people.** Move smoothly. Everyone else on a cord feels what you do to it.
- **Tassel lines.** They lead down to the water. Don't take one alone.
- **The floor.** If you end up on it, the peat itself is soft and moving. Get back up a wall or a line as quietly as you can.

## The other residents

Sizes are rough, relative to you.

| Animal | Size to you | What to do |
|---|---|---|
| Springtails | Cat-sized, in a continuous layer | They are the floor, the larder and the alarm. Leave them alone. |
| Pot-worms | Your own length, in the peat | Harmless, and one more reason to stay off the floor. |
| Money spiders | Dog-sized, active all winter | The sheet is the danger. Respect the thorn knots. |
| Snow flies | Half your height, on the ceiling | Harmless. Expect long legs near house roots. |
| Rove beetles | Up to your size, at the midden | They are hunters. Do your business on the midden line and leave. |
| Stone centipede | Two or three times your length | It has right of way on the east wall. Ask about its circuit on your first day. |
| Voles | Enormous, overhead | Keep your hood up. |
| Wood frog | A hall, frozen in the hinge wall | See below. |

## The frog

Its heart is stopped, but it is not dead. Follow the cords around it, don't linger, and don't lay a warm hand on it. If the ice on it has gone dull and wet, the day is mild. Take the long way round, as everyone else does.

## Time

The clock is a drip onto a stretched leaf, and a day is a fixed count of drips. In hard cold the days run long. In a thaw they pass in half the time.

- **Food and sleep.** Plan these by your own body, not by the local count. "Three days" can mean very different things.
- **Thaw.** The pool rises, the lowest cords are rehung, and the springtails climb the walls. If you can choose, come in deep, steady cold.
- **The darkness.** After a day or two your eyes will invent lights and your sense of time will drift. That is normal. The drip helps.

## Finding your way without sight

- **The drip** marks the centre of town.
- **The slope of a cord** tells you where the pool is.
- **Frost-bearded roots,** colder than the rest, run toward the living trunk.
- **A faint green,** caught only by looking beside it, is the honey fungus in the largest root. Give your eyes most of an hour before you expect to see it.
- **At midday,** one patch of black turns slightly less black. That is the snow lip.

## Manners

- **Voices.** Speak low or not at all.
- **Touch.** Expect to be known by touch and voice. Hands may go to your face and sleeves.
- **Sight.** Don't describe what things would look like as if your hosts lacked something.
- **Loads.** A root bears one house and no more. Ask before bringing your weight and baggage into one.
- **The clock.** Don't touch the clock leaf.

## Leaving

Take everything out with you. Every material in the room is plant, living or dead, and anything else you leave behind would be the only thing of its kind there. The room is new and may not last past spring.
