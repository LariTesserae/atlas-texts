**The short version:** prepare for this as a high-altitude trek on sharp rock, then prepare a second time for a place where nothing accumulates. Everything here is in balance, and you will be the only thing on the shelf with a before and an after. Most of the preparation is about not changing the sum, and not being added to it.

## Body

- **Altitude.** The cloud leans on the ridge above you and breath shows in the shade, so you are high. Acclimatize first if you can, then walk slowly and drink more than you want. The even, clear light will burn you without feeling warm, so bring a hat, sunglasses, and sunscreen.
- **Rock.** Fresh basalt is the main physical hazard. Clinker shreds soft soles, and the glassy edges cut cleanly and deeply. Wear stiff-soled boots, long trousers, and leather gloves for the stairs at the shelf's lip. Carry a kit for lacerations: closure strips, gauze, and tweezers for slivers.
- **Cold and wind.** It is cool, not brutal, but the wind never stops and never changes direction. Bring layers and a windproof shell.
- **Health.** Do not go if you are even slightly ill. This is a small population counted to the last person, and you would be carrying pathogens it has never met.

## What to carry, and why it's all of it

Bring every litre of water and every meal. The cisterns stand a finger below the rim because inflow exactly equals what is dipped out, and your thirst is not in that equation. The granaries are counted too. If someone hands you a cup, I would accept it once, out of courtesy, and not make a habit of it. This place is made of habits.

Pack out everything, including your own waste. Every handful of soil here was carried up in a basket, so there is nowhere to dig a hole.

- **Fuel.** Bring a stove or cold food. Do not burn tussock or the red bark, however freely it peels.
- **Seeds and spores.** Brush them out of your boots, cuffs, and pack before you climb. The moss stops at its crack and nothing tries to cross the bare rock. Don't be the reason something does.
- **Animals.** Bring none. Nothing here hunts or bolts, and a dog would be a catastrophe.
- **Metal.** Keep it out of sight. Their copper is a few chisels wrapped in hide. A steel knife left behind, or even admired too long, is the kind of change this place has no knot for.
- **Gifts.** Bring none that last. Everything here is already full.

Make an inventory before you go up and check it when you come down. Leave with exactly what you brought: no obsidian flake, no sheet of bark, no feather.

## How to move

Stay on the causeways. They are wider than you need and do not wear. The wool-bearers will be lying on them, so walk around them. Nothing will move for you, and nothing needs to.

Don't lean on walls (the rock-hares have the tops) and don't kneel in the moss. Above all, do not touch the block lying beside the eleven-course wall, or the hammerstone on top of it. You will want to. It looks like a job one person could finish in an afternoon. It has looked like that for a very long time.

## How to be near people

The spacing is the etiquette. Hearths sit about as far apart as an unraised voice carries, so that is your distance and your volume. Stand off from a doorway and wait to be noticed. Don't shout or cluster, and if you travel with companions, spread out. You won't share a language, and I suspect not much needs saying. Go slowly, keep your hands empty, and be patient.

If you can, sleep below the lip, off the shelf, and come up by day. The city was built for exactly its number, and there is no spare room in it.

If you see the keeper make a count, watch from the plaza's edge and don't touch the cords. If the new cord gets the same knots as all the others, don't be hurt. That is the best outcome. If the keeper looks at you and hesitates over the cord, I would go down the stairs that day.

## Time

I think this is the real risk. Nothing here will tell you that time has passed. No pile of rubble grows, no wall rises, and the thatch never ages. I can't promise you a night either, so bring a watch and sleep by it, and bring a light in case there is one.

Set your exit before you arrive and tie it to something of your own. Your food is the best clock: when half is gone, leave. Tell someone outside when to expect you.

Keep a daily note and include yourself in it: hunger, blisters, fingernails, the cut on your palm. I don't know whether the place extends its stillness to visitors. But if your notes start to match from day to day, with the cut neither healing nor worsening and the food bag no lighter, stop writing and walk out.

## What it will be like

It will be quiet, clear, and very easy. You will sit on a kerb in the sun with a hare dozing an arm's length away and the channels making their thin sound, and you will feel no reason to be anywhere else. That feeling is the thing to prepare for. The place isn't hostile. It is complete, and it will not push you out or tell you when to go.

So decide when you are leaving before you arrive, and when your own clock says so, go down the stairs. Behind you the two birds will still be turning at the lip.
