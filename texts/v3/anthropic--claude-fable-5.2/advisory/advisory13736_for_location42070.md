# Preparing for the dark tableland

Three things will shape your whole visit: you will be blind, you will never be dry, and the only map is made of sound. The country itself is soft (worn rock, a springy mat, warm water), so what can kill you is getting lost, overheating, slow rot, machinery, and one quiet animal.

## Before you go

**Train blind.**
- Spend several full days blindfolded at home. Pack and unpack, cook, treat a pretend wound, go to the toilet and walk on uneven ground.
- Expect your balance to be worse than you think. On a knee-deep mat you will fall often, though the falls are soft.
- After a day or two in total darkness your brain will invent lights, shapes and movement. Learn in advance that this is normal and means nothing.

**Train your ears.**
- *Pitch.* Learn to hold a note and to hear when two notes are slightly out of tune. "Slightly flat" is a survival distinction here.
- *Tempo.* Practise counting a beat against a watch. Each engine has its own speed as well as its own pitch.
- *Gaps.* Practise noticing when a sound stops. People are bad at hearing absences, and the predator is an absence.
- *Whistling.* Learn to whistle a steady pitch. It is the one useful thing you can offer a keeper.

**Prepare your body.**
- Acclimatise to heat and humidity for two weeks. In saturated air sweat doesn't cool you, so exertion will overheat you quickly.
- Arrive with healthy feet, skin and ears and no open cuts. Warm wet air full of spores will find every break in the skin.

## What to carry

Everything must work by touch, survive permanent wetness, and be tied to you. Anything dropped into the mat in the dark is gone.

- **Clothing:** Wear thin, close-fitting, fast-draining clothes with long sleeves and legs, and nothing waterproof, because you would cook. Boots should drain, since they can't stay dry. Tie down straps and hair.
- **Head net:** A fine mesh over face and ears keeps out tickers and the worst of the drift. Add goggles or a mesh eye-guard. Your eyes are useless here, but you want them working when you get home.
- **Ear filters:** Use passive musician's plugs that lower volume without smearing pitch. A surge is as loud as a mill and can damage your hearing, but your ears are also your eyes, so put the plugs in for surges and take them out between.
- **Staff:** Use it to probe the mat and pans ahead and to find the pipe again if you lose it.
- **Magnet on a wrist cord:** The pipe is iron. When you need both hands or want to sleep, clamp the magnet on so you stay attached.
- **Pitch pipe or tuning forks:** At each draw-house, find its note and remember it. The reference stops your memory drifting flat.
- **Tactile watch and tally cord:** Use them for timing engine beats and surge intervals. Knot the cord to count houses passed.
- **Pack organisation:** Give each pouch a different toggle shape, such as a ball, a bar and a ring. The engine builders solved this problem the same way.
- **Food:** Bring all of it, sealed in tins. Nothing here is known to be edible, and nothing will burn, but you can warm a tin against the pipe.
- **Water treatment:** Water is everywhere, but it carries drift, sour runoff and ground minerals. Filter and treat it, and take electrolytes.
- **Medical kit:** Pack antifungals, antiseptic, drying ear drops, foot powder and a sealed bag with dry socks and a cloth. Dry your feet and fingertips at every rest.
- **Tallow:** Bring it as a gift for keepers and tallow-eaters. Seal your own greasy things (soap, balm, waxed leather) or expect small tongues on them.
- **A sealed dim light:** Treat it as a last resort for a wound or a broken strap. It will show you a few feet of rain and pull your attention off your ears. Never point it at a keeper. You don't know what light means to someone who has never met it, or what it does to sun-born spores.

Leave behind anything that beeps, whines or hums. Sound is the fabric of this country, and a steady wrong note is what the thing in the mat makes.

## Arrival

If you can choose, arrive between surges at a draw-house with your hand already on the pipe.

If you arrive off the line, don't walk toward the first engine you hear. Stay still through several surges and listen for these things:

- **Whistles:** Draw-house whistles sound one after another as the rain front crosses them. The sequence gives you the direction of the line.
- **Beats:** A real engine has a stroke as well as a pitch, and there should be two of them, one on each side of you.
- **Wind and combs:** The wind blows from the lit rim. Every comb is canted into it, so any stalk works as a compass you can read by hand.
- **The falls:** The bass in your chest also comes from the rim, but it is too diffuse to judge distance by.

None of this is reliable enough to count on, so do everything you can to arrive at the iron.

## Walking the line

- **Never leave the iron for a sound.** That is the keepers' rule. It covers an engine's note, a cry, a voice, and a silence.
- Keep one hand on the pipe. If you fall or get knocked off, stop moving your feet and sweep with the staff. The pipe is within reach if you haven't stepped.
- If you ever notice you are walking toward an engine with no iron under your palm, stop.
- Test joints and fittings with the back of your hand first. Some leak scalding water or steam.
- Leave the signal wire alone. It carries the governors' pulse.
- Houses are about thirty miles apart, which is two or three days through the mat. With one keeper to ten houses, you will probably meet nobody, so plan to be alone.
- Go slowly and rest often. You can't see the colour of your urine, so track how often you pass it.
- Start with a short visit: one house and a few hundred paces of pipe.

**Surges.** You will hear each one coming as the whistles sound down the line. Stop, put in your plugs, drop your veil, clamp the magnet and put your back to the pipe. During a surge you can't hear the chord or anything else, so don't travel.

**Daily body check.** Go over yourself by touch. Blood feels like more warm water, so check for tackiness and smell. Look after your guiding hand above all. Macerated fingertips can't read anything.

**Sleep.** Sleep in a draw-house, or astride the pipe at a saddle with the magnet on. The pipe is warm and above the water.

## The animals

- **Grounders:** They read rock through their feet and may not register you. When you hear a herd booming closer, get against or onto the pipe.
- **Combers:** They are harmless unless one falls on you, or a stalk does.
- **Tickers:** They are a nuisance, and the head net deals with them.
- **Bladders:** They aren't animals, but they burst with a wet cough. Rinse off the sour splash and don't mistake the sound for something alive.
- **The hush:** I can't offer a proven defence, only what the description implies.
  - It comes from downwind, as a patch of silence a few yards wide. Combers stop mid-rasp and the tickers part.
  - It feeds best when the engine stops and animals drift and lose their place. So don't be a stray. Stay on the line, inside the engine's sound.
  - If the silence comes, get into a draw-house or put your back to the iron. Don't run into the mat.
  - Don't blast a horn. I doubt it would stop the hush, and it could stampede grounders onto you.

## Draw-houses and keepers

- A beam engine in total darkness is a room of heavy moving iron. Enter along the wall, keep a hand on the stone, and reach toward nothing.
- Touch no valves and bend no gauge pins.
- Don't harm the tallow-eaters. They keep the flywheels free.
- A keeper will hear you long before you notice her. Speak or whistle early, and don't touch her unannounced.
- Offer tallow. If her engine is down for repacking, stay out of her way and whistle its note with her.

## Things to stay away from

- **The rim.** The water sheets go over the edge with no warning, and the roar is everywhere, so loudness won't tell you how close you are.
- **Still hollows.** Ground gases may pool there, and the water may be deeper than it feels. Probe first, and unclip your pack belt near standing water.
- **The growth itself.** Don't cut or break stalks or step through drum-leaves. The forest is exactly as thick as the wind can feed, and you are a mouth it hasn't budgeted for.

## What it will be like

The first hours will be the worst. Your eyes will strain, the bass will sit in your chest, warm rain will run down your neck, and the air will smell sour and greasy. The noise will seem like a single wall.

After about a day it will separate into layers: the falls underneath, two engines in a chord, the stalks hooting, the drum-leaves, and the animals pitched to the nearest house. Once you can hear those layers you will know where you are.

Thunder will come with no flash, and nothing here has a visible cause. When you leave, decontaminate thoroughly so that nothing from the tableland travels home with you.
