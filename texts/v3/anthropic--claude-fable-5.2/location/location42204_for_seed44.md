**The Ice Works**

You hear the ice works before you find it. From the market street it is a low, even thud under the motorbike noise, about one beat a second. The lane that leads to it is narrow enough to touch both walls, and by the time you reach the gate the thud has been joined by falling water, a slapping belt, and the whine of a ventilator. The four sounds never vary, so after some minutes the ear stops counting them as noise and treats them as part of the weather.

The yard is perhaps twenty paces by thirty. It sits a hand's height above the canal that runs behind it, and the canal sits barely above the sea, eleven kilometres off across the flats. Everything here is built low and built of brick, because brick was what the delta had. Everything the brick was built into has since gone green.

**The cooling tower**

At the centre of the yard stands the cooling tower. It was once a timber frame of louvred slats four storeys high, set in a brick basin. The slats rotted out decades ago. The water now falls through fern.

- **Ferns.** Bird's-nest ferns the size of cartwheels are wedged at every level of the frame. Maidenhair grows in sheets between them.
- **Moss.** Under the ferns is a moss mat as thick as a mattress, holding more water than the timber ever did.
- **The fig.** A fig rooted in the basin wall sometime before the war and went up through the whole structure. Its roots have wrapped the old corner posts so completely that the posts could be removed, and in two places they have been.

Warm water from the condensers is pumped to the top and comes down through all of this as rain, a steady green-lit rain with a drift of mist off the leeward side. It arrives in the basin several degrees cooler. The engineer measures the difference every morning and writes it in a ledger. The number has not meaningfully changed in his lifetime or his father's.

**The condensers and the roofs**

The condensers stand along the north wall in three racks of iron pipe coil, each with a trough above it that weeps water down the coils. The pipe cannot be seen. It is sleeved in liverwort and a black-green algal felt, and the rule passed from foreman to foreman is that nobody scrapes it. The iron underneath is thin now, and the growth is trusted more than the metal.

A pothos has climbed the chimney stay-wires, and its leaves are as long as a forearm. Taro stands shoulder-high in the overflow drain. The corrugated roofs of the engine house and the tank room are covered edge to edge in creeper, so that from the upper windows of the neighbouring shophouses the works looks like a small square hill with a steaming tree growing out of it.

**The engine house**

Inside, the compressor is a horizontal machine with a flywheel taller than a man, painted green once by people and again, lightly, by the air. A long flat belt runs to it from the motor, and that belt makes the slapping sound.

The fig's canopy presses at the belt opening in the wall. Each year at the end of the rains someone cuts it back with a machete to keep a clear arm's length around the belt run. That and the footpath to the loading dock are the only places where the plants are told no. Elsewhere they are worked around or worked with:

- New electrical conduit is strapped along the fig's roots, because the roots are sounder than the mortar.
- The east boundary wall is a root lattice in the shape of a wall, with the bricks still held in it at their original courses.

Nobody talks in the engine house. There is a short vocabulary of hand signs covering more oil, less water, come and look, and tea.

**The cold**

The suction line leaves the engine house and crosses the yard at head height to the tank room. For those six metres it carries a thick sleeve of frost that drips steadily onto moss in the thirty-degree air. It is the only cold thing you can see, and it looks like a mistake. Ferns grow within a finger's width of it and take no harm.

Behind a door packed with rice husk lies the brine tank, dark and still. Four hundred galvanised cans hang in it, and each takes two days to turn water into a clear block. This is the fastest change the works makes.

At four in the morning the overhead hoist lifts the day's row. The cans are dipped, tipped, and emptied, and the blocks go down a wet timber chute to the dock, where the crusher starts up for the fish merchants. For an hour it is the loudest thing in the quarter. Then it stops, and the thud and the falling water take over again.

**Animals**

Very little lives here that isn't rooted.

- The faint sting of ammonia that hangs under the wet-brick and hot-oil smell keeps most things away, and the noise does the rest.
- The fig fruits heavily and nothing comes to eat it. The figs fall, ferment, and stain the paving purple-brown.
- No birds nest in the tower.
- A few pale geckos live behind the switchboard, where it is dry.
- Snails work the basin rim.
- Some mornings a kingfisher sits on the wire over the canal. It faces away from the yard and has never been seen to come in.

**The seasons**

The year turns slowly. In the wet months the canal rises until it stands just under the paving, and the yard drains run backward for an hour at the top of each tide. Nobody remarks on it.

In the dry months the rest of the street goes grey with dust, and the works stays exactly as green as it was, fed by its own rain. Women from the lane bring potted plants and leave them along the leeward side of the tower to recover. They always do. Some have been left so long that they have rooted through their pots into the yard.
