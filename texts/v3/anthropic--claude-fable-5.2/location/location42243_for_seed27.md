The plateau stands a little under five thousand metres and is almost perfectly flat. It was a mountain range once. What remains is a table sixty kilometres across with rounded shoulders, and none of its rock shows anywhere, because the plant has covered it all and, over a very long time, replaced most of it.

North of the town a gallery roof has fallen in and left a pit two hundred metres wide, and there you can look down through the layers. At the top are the living rosettes, thumbnail-sized, olive-black, hard as fired clay, bristling with stiff grey hairs. Below them is a hand's depth of dead ones, and below that brown strata pressed dense as lignite and threaded with pale tubes, going down past where the light reaches. Nobody has seen stone at the bottom. The plant is the high ground now.

It is one plant, or so many fused that the difference stopped mattering. It grows at the rate it is worn away, and has done so for long enough that its surface holds no hill and no gully, only a slow swell toward the centre and a shallow basin three kilometres wide where a valley used to be.

There is no water. Rain falls on the lower country, which shows from the rim as cloud tops far below, and none of it climbs this high. The plant keeps what moisture it has sealed in its core and moves everything else dry. It feeds on dust, the last of its own mountain and whatever the wind brings, and it moves that dust by sound. Its galleries are pipes, and the whole mass drones. The fundamental is too low to hear and is felt in the shins and molars. Above it sits a stack of overtones as loud as heavy machinery, and above those the vents whistle where galleries open to the wind. A hand laid on the surface feels the vibration of a drumskin stretched over timber.

The drone organises the ground. Dust on the surface cannot rest where the mat is shaking, so it creeps to the still lines between and lies there in tan ridges that curve and fork for kilometres. Everything else follows the dust. Shed hair, eggshell and bones all travel a few centimetres a day until they reach a line. A carcass here does not rot. It dries, is rattled apart, and its pieces file away to the nearest ridge. Walking the ridges, you walk on vertebrae.

Some of the dust never comes down. The afternoon wind drags grit across the hairs and charges the mat, and the charge and the sound together hold a layer of fines a few hundred metres overhead, mixed with the plant's own pollen. That layer is its larder and its only means of reaching itself across sixty kilometres. It is also the sky. The sun shows through it as a brown disc that can be looked at directly. Noon is like deep dusk elsewhere, cold, with no shadows. The air smells of resin and ozone.

The plateau is full of animals, though the distances make them look few. Nothing drinks. The hairs carry a bead of wax at the tip, and the grazers crop them in bands and make their own water from the fat. They are knee-high and barrel-bodied, with wide padded feet, and they move in herds of several hundred. They rest on the ridges, where it is quiet enough to hear something coming. The hunters, long, low and pale, therefore approach across the loud ground, where footfalls are lost in the noise, and cross onto the ridge only for the last few metres.

Running birds nest in the pit walls by the thousand and call in hard clicks pitched above the overtones, the only register the plant leaves free. The moths have wings too small to fly with. At dusk, when the charge peaks and the hair tips show faint violet points, they let go of the mat and are lifted in their millions to feed on pollen in the ceiling. Before dawn the charge leaks away and they fall. It is the only rain the plateau gets, and it makes a soft patter that lasts about an hour.

The town sits in the basin. It was grown rather than built. Its makers found that the plant thickens wherever a pure tone is held against it, so they set sounders into the mat. These are dark, palm-sized objects with no moving parts and no visible source of power, and each holds a single note without pause. Over decades the plant rose around each one in ribs, then shells, then arcades of horn-grey wood under living green skin. The oldest shells stand thirty metres high and have younger walls nested inside them, each layer answering a note added later.

There are perhaps forty shells in the whole basin, standing several hundred metres apart, because two notes held too close will fight. Between them run the streets, still lines that the sounders pin in place, ruler-straight where the wild ridges curve.

A thin rope of dust comes down from the ceiling to each shell, drawn by some means the sounders have and nothing else does. Each rope is a slow tan column an arm's width across, feeding the walls so that they keep pace with their own wearing-away. From the rim the town looks like a few dozen pale threads between the plain and the sky.

Inside a shell the drone is gone. It has not been muffled, because no wall could muffle it. It has been cancelled, and the room is a volume where the plant's sound is met exactly and undone. The threshold is felt as a pressure change in the ears. Dust that hangs trembling outside falls straight down within. People talk in there. Outdoors they sign, and they wear the charge off their clothes with a trailing cord. Birds have learned the rooms and roost in the upper ribs in rows, asleep in the only silence for sixty kilometres.

The plant is not a fixed instrument. As the mat cools at night its pitch drops, and the wild ridges migrate outward at walking pace, combing the dust into new curves. The town's lines do not move. At the town limits the two patterns disagree, and the ground there is a band of moiré where ridges break into dashes and bones wander without settling. The sound in that band beats, a throb whose rate tells how far the plateau has drifted from the town's tuning. It is slow at noon and quick before dawn, and in the cold season it is fast enough to blur into a growl. The herds will not rest in the band, and the hunters wait just beyond it.

Dry storms come on windy evenings. The lightning does not fall from above. It crawls flat across the mat from ridge to ridge, branching and violet, and for a minute it lights the underside of the dust ceiling.

At the basin's western edge there is a low oval swell, knee-high and eighty metres long, with a street running up to it and stopping. A sounder there went quiet. The shell took some centuries to subside, with rosettes closing over the ribs as they sank, and the plant is still levelling it. The dust rope above it thinned and dispersed long ago. Before dawn the moths come down on it as they do everywhere else, and by midday they have been shaken into lines.
