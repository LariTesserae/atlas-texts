At six and a half thousand meters the range is what ranges are at that height: black rock, blue ice, and a sky dark enough at noon to hint at what lies behind it. Wind works the ridges. The cloud deck lies two kilometers below, flat and white, and the lesser peaks stand out of it like rocks at low tide.

Between two of the summits hangs a basin. It is an old glacial cirque, about three kilometers long and one across, tilted slightly south, walled on three sides and open on the fourth at a notch. It is green. From the ridge above it looks like a mapping error, a lozenge of jungle under a low lid of fog, with snow pressed around its margin right up to a line.

The line is exact. On one side is wind-crusted snow, thin air, and cold that hurts the teeth. Two paces further the eardrums press inward, the lungs get more than they asked for, and heat arrives on the face like an opened oven. Inside, the air is as dense as at a river mouth and hotter than blood, somewhere past forty degrees, and it stays there. Night takes off a degree or two. No season reaches in.

The heat comes from below. The bedrock is banded gneiss, ground smooth by a glacier that is long gone, and it is warm everywhere, evenly, like the flank of a sleeping animal. There are no vents and no sulfur, and nothing here is volcanic. The stone is simply warm, and the water that runs over it is warm. Ten thousand years of that water have softened the ice-cut forms into something settled. The runnels are rounded and the potholes are polished as bowls. Pale sinter rims step down the slabs in terraces a finger high, each holding a sheet of bath-temperature water the width of a room.

The water never leaves, or almost never. It rises off the pools and leaves as vapor and climbs until it meets the ceiling, the same boundary as the line in the snow, curved overhead like the inside of a shallow dish. There it turns to fog. The fog thickens through the morning, and by early afternoon it falls as slow, heavy rain, warm as the air and nearly vertical. Then it thins, and the sun comes through milky and ordinary, and the cycle starts again. The headwalls weep constantly. Every surface that is not actively drying is wet.

The forest shows the shape of what holds it. In the middle of the basin the tree ferns reach twelve or fifteen meters, their trunks furred black and their crowns loaded with orchids and with tank-plants holding a liter of water each. Toward the edges the canopy slopes down in a clean curve, like wind-pruned trees on a coast, though no wind did it. The slope passes from ferns to head-high giant rosettes with silver-haired leaves, then to moss cushions a meter deep, then to a film of algae on wet rock, and then to snow. Between the stands are openings of bare slab, sinter pools, and peat hollows black as oil. At the lowest point lies a tarn of dark tea-colored water, with small pale loaches working its margins.

It is loud. Something like a cicada, though larger and living in the fern crowns, runs a shrill, unbroken note from dawn until the rain, and takes it up again when the rain stops. After dark the frogs take over. Glass-bodied ones on the leaves tick like cooling metal, and heavy gray ones in the sinter pools give a pulsed bark that travels the length of the basin. Sunbirds quarrel in the rosettes. Swifts nest on the headwall in thousands and hunt along the inside of the boundary, screaming, and turn a wing's length short of it every time.

The sound does not get out. From the ridge the basin is silent. Inside, each call goes up, meets the ceiling, and comes back a second later and a fraction lower, so the whole chorus is shadowed by a duller copy of itself from overhead. At the boundary there is one more sound, low and continuous, like breath across the neck of a very large bottle. It is felt in the jaw more than heard.

Things blunder through. Moths, beetles, and now and then a swiftlet cross the line and stiffen within a few wingbeats, so the snow outside carries a dark margin of small bodies, renewed daily. Choughs walk it like shorebirds. A fox comes most mornings and follows the whole perimeter at a trot, eating as it goes, and has worn a path. Twice a year geese crossing the range drop out of the thin air and into the basin. They stand in the shallows for a day with their wings held open, panting, and then leave.

There are nine posts. Each is about the height of a standing person and the thickness of a wrist. They rise from the bedrock without a seam, as if the gneiss had put out a thin limb of some other substance. They are gray, or gray-green, and the eye has trouble staying on the surface. They do not follow the basin's long axis or the stream or any other feature. They run in a staggered line that enters over the western headwall and leaves through the eastern one, as though the basin simply lay in the way.

Rain does not wet them. Drops falling toward a post slow down in the last hand's width and drift aside. For about an arm's length around each one the air is perfectly still, whatever the weather is doing elsewhere. Moss grows up to that distance and stops in a clean collar. Midges have found the stillness, and every post wears a tall, slowly turning column of them from dusk onward, with spider silk strung in a ring just outside where the hunting is easy. Nothing nests on the posts themselves. They are neither warmer nor cooler than the air.

Two of the nine are dead. They look the same except that rain wets them, and lichen has begun on them, orange and unhurried. Around each one is a circle perhaps sixty meters across where the basin is not held. The ground there is still warm, but the air is thin and bitterly cold, and frost forms on warm rock and melts and forms again. These circles are alpine islands inside the jungle, covered in cushion plants and dwarf gentians. A colony of pikas lives in them and darts out across the edge to steal fern fiddleheads, and then goes back in to cool down.

A third post, near the tarn, leans. It is cracked along one side, and the inside is neither hollow nor solid. It is laminated, with layers too fine to count, and it sheds a gray dust that the stream carries into the tarn. The dust settles there in ripples that do not match the current.

Outside the line, on the north side, tree-fern trunks stand in the snow. There are hundreds of them, rooted in ice and bleached silver, polished by wind and frozen too hard to rot. They run eighty meters out from the present boundary in thinning ranks. The basin was larger once. It is shrinking at about a stride per century.

Other things have been left here. A drystone windbreak sits just outside the line with a rusted fuel canister inside it. A cairn stands on the notch. Near the second terrace an aluminum instrument mast has pitted white in the wet heat. Its anemometer cups are full of moss, and a frog lives in the housing where the logger was.

At the notch the basin's one outflow goes over the lip and crosses the line in midair. It leaves as warm water and becomes a column of blue ice hanging two hundred meters down the outer face, built from the top and thickening each year. Every eleven or twelve years its own weight snaps it. The crack goes through the rock, and for some seconds afterward the frogs are quiet.

From the cairn, on a clear evening, the next massif to the east stands forty kilometers off across the cloud. High on its shoulder, where there should be only ice, there is a small smudge of green under a cap of fog, and further along the same bearing there is a second one, fainter.
