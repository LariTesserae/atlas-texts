The saddle sits a little below the height where breathing stops being automatic. It is a shelf of grey-green stone about three kilometres long and less than one across, tipped a few degrees to the south, and at each end it climbs into a summit. On the long sides the rock simply stops. Far below lies a floor of cloud, white and lit from above.

The stone is clinkstone, jointed long ago into columns and since worn into standing pillars. There are thousands of them, most twice a person's height, set so close that in places you would turn sideways to pass. Their edges are rounded and their tops dished. Between them lie the plates that have split off and fallen flat, overlapping like dropped roof slates. Each plate carries small cups where drips have landed in the same spots for longer than anyone has reckoned.

Everything is wet. Several times a day the cloud floor bulges and comes up through the saddle from the south, and for some minutes every pillar gathers it. Then it clears, and the sun, small and white and hard at this height, strikes a field of running stone. Glare comes off every face, and more light comes up from the cloud below, so the pillars are lit on their undersides. A sheet of water a finger deep moves south across the pavement. It never freezes. Night at this altitude should lock it solid, but the water stays at the temperature of a cellar, and so does the air, at noon and at midnight alike.

Nothing grows here that could be called a plant. In the drip lines there is an orange-brown skin, slick to the touch, which thickens after cloud and thins in sun. It could be iron. But around each of the old copper pegs there is a clean halo a hand wide where the skin will not come, which suggests it is something that can be poisoned.

The place is loud. Wind in the gaps makes a low, unsteady note. Every drip that lands on a plate rings it, and after a cloud has passed there are tens of thousands of drips, so the field sounds like a yard of small bells struck out of time. Beneath that is the runoff going over the south lip, a steady roar with no bottom to it, because the water falls into cloud and is never heard to land. The cloud comes up again later. It is probably the same water.

## What was built

- **Cairns:** stacked plates, knee-high to chest-high, set in lines.
- **Ring walls:** three or four, roofless, an arm-span across. Inside each is a flat stone with a hollow worn where someone sat.
- **Hammerstones:** quartzite cobbles, rounded by a river that is nowhere near here, carried up and battered white at both ends.
- **Copper pegs:** beaten, not cast, driven into joints and now gone green.
- **A trough:** pecked into the bedrock, four paces long.
- **Tally marks:** rows of short pecked strokes at shoulder height on the pillars, grouped in fives. There are hundreds of rows, and many are abandoned partway and begun again lower down.

Someone was counting the pillars. The rest of what they built shows why the count kept starting over.

## How the place behaves

**Level and plumb are separate matters.** The trough is cut on a slope, one end a hand higher than the other, and the water stands full to the lip at both ends. From one peg a pierced stone still hangs on a strip of copper. It hangs perfectly still, a few degrees off true, leaning toward the eastern summit. At the far end of the field another leans toward the western.

**Shadows agree only with their neighbours.** At the east end they point north. At the west end they point east. Each pillar's shadow lies sensibly beside the next, and nowhere in between is there a place where they turn.

**There is one mountain.** The eastern and western summits have the same notch, the same pale scar under it, and the same three-stepped shoulder on the left. They are not mirrored. The cairn lines run toward it in both directions.

**A count is true once, for the one who made it.** Between two particular cairns it is forty paces going and fifty-three coming back. The tally rows for a single stretch of pillars sit one under another, each a different length, each ending in a stroke struck harder than the rest.

**Sound keeps its own time.** When a plate finally shears off a pillar, the crack comes first, flat and enormous. Some while later the plate is seen to fall in silence. Echoes come back early.

There is also the pecking. From somewhere among the pillars comes the sound of stone on stone, a patient tock, tock-tock, with pauses the length of a breath. No one is here. Nothing that breathes has been here for as long as the drip cups took to form. But the sound has not finished arriving, and on some pillars, among the grey weathered strokes, there are strokes that are pale and sharp-edged, with rock flour still clouding the water beneath them.

The cloud comes up again. Sometimes the light inside it belongs to another hour, low and amber, with long shadows that all point the same way for once. Then it clears to noon, the same noon as before, with the sun where it was. The bells start. The water goes over the lip. On the nearest pillar the newest row stands at four strokes, wet, waiting for the fifth to cross it.
