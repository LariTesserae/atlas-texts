The room lies under a low limestone shelf at the bottom of a wide lowland basin. It is reached by a stooping passage that bends twice, so that daylight arrives only as a gray wash on the wet walls. It is about thirty paces long and never more than three times a person's height, and you cannot see across it. Columns stand in the way, hundreds of them, where stalactites met stalagmites long ago and thickened into trunks the color of old teeth and tallow. In places they stand so close that you have to turn sideways and go through with your chest against one and your back against another.

Stone curtains hang between the columns, folded and banded in rust-orange and cream. Where there are no curtains there are soda straws, thin hollow tubes of calcite crowded like a pelt on the ceiling, each with a bead of water at its tip. No surface is bare. The walls carry knobbed popcorn crust, and in the recesses there is moonmilk, a soft white paste that takes a fingerprint like cold butter.

Water is everywhere, in a thin layer. It sheets down the columns without seeming to move and glazes every surface, so the whole room looks freshly varnished. It gathers on the floor in terraced pools held by rimstone dams, low scalloped walls of mineral no higher than a hand that step down toward the back of the chamber. The pools are ankle-deep and perfectly clear, and they are at the temperature of skin, so a foot lowered into one feels wetness but neither warmth nor cold.

The spring that feeds them comes up through a crack in the lowest corner without any bubbling. It shows only as a place where the surface is slightly domed. It brings a faint smell of struck flint and boiled egg. The air has the same warmth as the water and holds as much moisture as air can, so nothing dries here. Breath does not show, and sweat stays on the skin.

Nothing green grows. Where the gray light reaches the passage mouth there is a dark stain on the rock that might be algae and might be manganese. Deeper in, the only living film is a slick, pale mat on the spring-side stones and a few limp, colorless threads hanging from the ceiling near the crack, each dripping slowly. In the pools, small white isopods walk the bottom. You might count four in an hour. There is a long-legged cricket, nearly transparent, that keeps to the ceiling and makes no sound. On one ledge lies the skeleton of a bat, entirely sheathed in calcite, with each finger bone made into a thin stone twig.

People have been here, though not many and not recently. Along the route in from the passage, a line of shallow footholds has been pecked into the flowstone with a harder rock. Their edges are rounded now under a clear skin of new mineral. Two of the rimstone dams have been notched to lower a pool. Beside the largest pool sits a basin, a natural hollow deepened by hammering, with a rim that still shows the pocked texture of the work.

A lamp sits in a niche at shoulder height. It is a cupped stone with a blackened lip, and it is cemented to the shelf, because the room has grown a collar around its base. Above it a tongue of soot runs up the wall, and the soot lies under calcite like a stain under glass. The lower third of a notched pole leans against one column. It was a ladder once, and the wood is now a core inside a white crust that keeps the shape of every notch. There is also a single copper pin, bent, which has bled a green streak down the stone beneath it as long as a forearm.

At the back, where the lowest pool laps against a bank of red-brown clay, there are handprints. They were pressed in deliberately, with the fingers spread. The clay is as wet now as when the hands left it, so the prints have neither hardened nor slumped. The ridges of the palms are still sharp. There is no way to tell from looking whether they are an hour old or older than the lamp. Next to them someone dragged three fingers through the clay in a wavering line and stopped.

The room is quiet in a close way. There is dripping, but it is spread across so many points and falls such short distances into such shallow water that it makes a soft, even ticking without rhythm. The crowded stone breaks up any echo, and a spoken word seems to stop a few steps from the mouth.

The pools do not rise or fall. The spring has no seasons, since the water has spent too long underground to know what the weather is doing. In the basin lie a few dozen cave pearls, smooth concretions grown around grains of grit, the largest the size of a thumb joint. Someone once sorted them by size into a rough curve, and they are still in that order, each sitting in a small socket it has worn for itself.

When the gray wash at the passage mouth fades for the night, the room becomes fully dark, and that is the only thing that happens in it. The warmth and the ticking stay the same, and the film of water goes on running down each column.
