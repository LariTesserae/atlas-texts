The valley lies under forty meters of cold sea, and it has been a valley for longer than it has been underwater. Ice ground its shoulders down to low whalebacks of rock, a river worked the middle, and then the sea came in over everything and stayed. There is very little left to wear away. What remains gets moved, twice a day in each direction, and has for eight thousand years.

It is dark. At midsummer noon a brown-green dimness comes down from above, enough to tell up from down, not enough to cast a shadow. The rest of the time the water is black and full. Silt, shell dust, shreds of weed, the cast skins of small animals and flocs of something grey and soft are all travelling sideways in it together, so thickly that a hand at arm's length would be touched before it was seen. When the current throws sand hard against a boulder, a few blue-green sparks sometimes go off in the wake, and that is the only light the place makes.

The tide runs through the valley like a wind through a pass. Near the bed it carries a skin of moving sand about knee-deep, grains hopping and streaming like spindrift over snow. The floor underneath is packed. Along the valley's flanks lies a pavement of cobbles, flint and quartzite, rolled down to the shape of eggs and wedged shoulder to shoulder with every gap between them plugged by smaller stones and the gaps between those by shell hash. In a strong run the top layer chatters, a dry ticking that carries a long way. That, and the hiss of sand over sand, is most of the sound there is. A boulder shifts once a season with a single knock.

Across the middle of the valley stand the sand waves, ridges four and five meters tall with crests running a kilometer or more, steep on one face and long on the other. They look like something caught mid-stride. They hardly travel. The small ripples on their backs reverse with each tide, dissolving and rebuilding to face the other way within the hour, while the great ridges under them lean a little north, then a little south, and end the year where they began it. This is the valley's settled form, and it consists of motion. Dig anywhere and the hole is gone by the next tide. Pile anything up and it is spread flat.

The old river channel still winds down the center, filled to the brim with stiff grey mud. Where the current cuts into its bank a ledge of peat stands exposed, black and fibrous, with the stumps of oak and pine still rooted in it. The wood is soft as cheese now, bored through by piddocks, the roots spread exactly as they grew. Hazelnut shells weather out of the peat face and bowl away downstream. In winter gales, when the swell far overhead reaches down and adds its own back-and-forth to the tide, the ledge calves whole blocks. They tumble off into the dark, shedding leaves that fell before the sea arrived.

Almost nothing grows. On the crowns of the largest boulders, where the sand cannot quite reach, there are thin pink crusts of coralline alga, living at the very limit of the light. The tufts beside them that look like moss are animals. Most of the plant matter in the valley arrives already dead. Kelp torn from the shallows comes cartwheeling through with its holdfast still clamped around a stone, and rags of wrack snag on the peat for a tide before moving on.

Animals are few, because the sand scours off whatever tries to settle. A wolffish holds one crevice in the boulder field and has held it for years, its grey head filling the entrance. Sand eels lie buried in the flanks of the ridges and burst out all at once when something disturbs them, then are gone back in. A hermit crab works along the lee of a cobble. Now and then a seal comes down from the surface, reading the bottom with its whiskers, and leaves with nothing.

And in the pavement, among ten million rounded flints, there are a few that are not rounded. One is a flake with a bulb of percussion on one face and an edge still keen enough to cut. One is a core with the scars of six blades struck from it. Near the peat a hazel stake lies on its side, its point showing the short facets of a stone axe, and somewhere under the mud is a length of antler with barbs carved down one side. Nobody built anything here that lasted. They walked the riverbank, dropped what was blunt or broken, and went uphill when the water rose. The tide uncovers these things and buries them again on no schedule at all.

Four times a day the current stops. For ten or fifteen minutes the knee-deep skin of sand sinks out, the cobbles go silent, and the load in the water begins to fall straight down, coating the stumps, the flints and the wolffish's brow with a fine grey film. The temperature is five degrees, as it nearly always is. The whole valley holds still and total in the dark.

Then a grain on the crest of a ripple rolls over, toward the direction it came from, and the rest follow.
