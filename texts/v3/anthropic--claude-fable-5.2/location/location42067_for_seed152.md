Nothing here has been rained on yet. The founding pair went into the ground at the end of the last wet season, and what they started has only now come up through the surface: a cone of dark earth about shin-high, with three blunt spires. It stands on the low bank of a streambed that is currently a ribbon of dry sand.

A thicket has closed over it. There is a raisin-bush gone leggy, a creeper with leaves like open hands, and last year's grass standing dead between them. The daylight that reaches the cone is brown-green and second-hand, and by mid-afternoon it is hard to tell the wet clay from its own shadow. The air under the leaves is warm and does not move much.

Up close the walls are granular. Every pellet is the size of a mustard seed, rolled in a mouth, pressed into place and left, and no weather has yet blurred one into the next. The age of any part can be read by its colour. This hour's work is nearly black and has a shine. Yesterday's is chocolate. Last week's is the pale tan of the surrounding soil and hard as a fired pot. The spire tips are always black.

The tips are also open. Fresh work is lace: pellets stacked into pillars, pillars bridged into arches, the gaps not yet filled. Through the gaps a little of the thicket's light gets inside and falls down the central shaft in threads. It is enough to show the curve of a wall, a floor, an opening onto another room. The builders are blind and have no use for it. To them a hole that admits light also admits dry air and ants, and they close each one as they come to it. Light inside the cone is therefore an exact map of what is unfinished, and it retreats upward by about a finger's width a day.

Most of the rooms are empty. The colony builds ahead of itself, a few thousand inhabitants with space for ten times that. There are vaulted cells joined by ramps, and galleries that run a hand's length and stop at a blank wall where the work went elsewhere. A worker crossing one of these halls is the only moving thing in it, pale and soft-bodied, tracing the wall with one antenna. Two rooms away another passes in the opposite direction. They may not meet anyone before the gardens.

The dampness is all carried. The ground around the cone is dry far deeper than a root-hair can reach. Beneath it a shaft no wider than a grass stem goes down through the bank to where the old stream lingers as dark, cool sand. Workers descend empty and climb back swollen, then give the water away mouth to mouth until it ends up in the building clay, the walls and the air. Inside, the air is as wet as breath. Just before dawn, when the outer shell is coolest, beads form on the inner faces of the spires and hang in the pores. For a few minutes each one holds a point of grey light, and then the beads are drunk.

The gardens lie lower down, where the threads of light give out. The inhabitants cannot digest dead grass themselves, so they chew it, pass it once through the gut, and build the result into combs. These are brown, folded and spongy, each filling its chamber the way a walnut fills its shell. On the combs grows a fungus. Its mycelium covers every surface in a grey-white nap, and out of the nap rise the nodules, round and white as pinheads, hundreds to a comb. These are the crop. The garden rooms are the only crowded places in the city, with workers grazing, workers adding fresh material to the comb's upper edge, and the smallest workers carrying nodules down to the nursery. The rot keeps the rooms a little warmer than blood, and they smell of mushroom and cut hay.

The thicket has noticed the gardens. Fine white rootlets from the raisin-bush come through the ceilings toward the humidity. Each is nipped off and plastered over, and each comes back.

Beneath the gardens is a cell with a floor flat as a coin and a ceiling low enough to brush the backs of those inside. The queen is still small, the length of a fingernail. Her abdomen is only beginning to stretch, with pale skin showing between the brown plates. The king stands alongside, and a ring of attendants faces inward. Every few minutes an egg is taken from her and carried out.

Others have already moved in. There are silverfish the same ivory as the workers, moving a little too fast. There is a rove beetle with its swollen abdomen curled over its back, which taps a passing worker and is fed as if it belonged. Mites ride on the soldiers' heads, and springtails graze the damp walls. In an abandoned gallery on the east flank, where the clay keeps the same warmth night and day, a skink has left four eggs the size of beans. At dusk a gecko takes up a position on the outside, below the spire tips, and waits to see what shows itself.

There is almost no sound. Jaws work wet clay without any click a larger animal could hear, and all those feet on packed earth add up to nothing. When a wall is breached the soldiers strike their heads against the floor and the alarm travels room to room through the earth itself, felt through the feet. An ear laid on the cone might catch a dry rustle like sand poured on paper. Outside, the creeper leaves tick as they cool, and a dove calls from some distance down the streambed.

The day has a shape. As the sun gets through to one flank, the air inside that flank warms and rises, the air on the shaded side sinks, and the stale breath of the gardens seeps out through the pores of the shell while fresher air seeps in. By noon the builders have pulled back from the tips and the black shine dulls.

After dark the foragers leave by tunnels that run just under the soil and surface beneath the dead grass. They plaster each stem in a skin of earth and eat it out from inside the sleeve. By morning there are hollow earthen tubes standing where stems stood, and these crumble at a touch. The grass is thinning in a rough circle around the cone, and the circle is a little wider each week.

Some afternoons a column of large matte-black ants comes down the bank three abreast. They break into the foraging tunnels or into the lace at a spire tip, and each leaves with a bundle of workers held crosswise in its jaws. Soldiers with rust-red heads fill the breach and block it with their own skulls. Behind them the workers wall the gap shut, leaving the soldiers on the outside. Within an hour the patch is black and shining. By the next day it is chocolate, and a week later nothing distinguishes it from the rest.

Nobody directs any of this. A pellet set down still carries the taste of the mouth that set it, and the taste is the instruction to put another here. Two pellets call louder than one. A pillar goes up where traffic happened to pause, an arch forms where two pillars happened to lean, and a room is whatever resulted. A dry wall is left alone. A draught is answered with clay.

Late in the day the air over the streambed thickens and the light under the thicket goes from brown to something nearer slate. Nothing comes of it. The west spire is one course of pellets taller than it was this morning, and its tip is still wet.
