The basin sits high in a range that is still being made. The rim is black basalt, cooled so recently that its edges will cut a hand. The columns have not rounded and the scree has not settled, and lichen has barely started on the rock. Somewhere below the southern lip, a lava flow closed off the valley's only exit, and the valley filled. It is still filling, by about the thickness of a leaf each day.

The forest that was in the valley did not die.

From the rim it looks like a green plain, several kilometres across and slightly domed, with steam lying over it in a layer about as deep as a person is tall. There is no open water anywhere. The plain is canopy: the crowns of trees whose trunks go down forty or fifty metres through warm, clear water to a floor that still carries ferns. The trees have kept pace with the flood. Each year they add height at the top and give up nothing at the bottom, so every tree is its own full history, leafed all the way down.

The surface is hard to find. Going down from the rim, the air thickens first, body-warm and so wet that breathing feels like drinking. Then come the hanging roots, fine as hair and pale pink, trailing from every branch into the steam. Below them is a floating mat of small-leaved plants, moss, and seedlings rooted in nothing. Somewhere under the mat the wetness becomes continuous. There is no moment of entering the water, only a point where one realises one has been under it for a while.

Below the mat the water opens and the light comes back. At this altitude the sun is strong, and the water is clear enough to carry it down in slow shafts that slide across the trunks through the day. The space is crowded. Trunks stand an arm's length apart, with branches meshed between them. Every surface is sleeved in something: ribbon-weed, soft green felt, translucent ferns that unfurl over weeks. Leaves touch leaves at every level. Nothing sways, because nothing moves the water. Fed by seeps from below and by rain from above, the lake loses water only to steam and to a slow weeping through the dam, so there is no current. A dropped thing falls straight.

Seen close, the trunks are not quite wood. The water carries dissolved silica up from the vents, and it settles on everything that holds still. The bark has a glassy pale skin, one layer per year, thin as a fingernail. Deeper down, the oldest wood is banded like agate and rings faintly when touched. The forest is turning itself to stone from the bottom up while it goes on growing at the top, and it seems to be the youngest stone in the range, younger even than the dam. A broken branch does not rot. It hangs where it caught, whitens, and in a decade it is a spar of opal with leaves still printed on it.

The ferns on the valley floor grow among the old leaf litter, which never decayed and now lies as a pavement of thin stone leaves, each vein intact. The vents are in the deepest part: a field of low, sharp-edged chimneys, none taller than a knee, breathing warm water that shimmers like air over a road. Nothing grows within a body-length of them. Beyond that margin everything grows at once.

Animals are few. A pale salamander as long as a forearm, with frilled gills, can be found resting on a branch and found on the same branch days later. There may be one for every hundred trees. Something small and glassy grazes the felt on the trunks and leaves clean, winding trails that take a season to green over. Above the steam, swifts sometimes cross the basin without descending. Nothing sings and nothing hunts in any way that could be watched.

The place is very quiet, and its one regular sound is the pearling. By mid-morning, when the light is well down among the trunks, every leaf in the forest starts to bead with oxygen. The bubbles are silver and pinhead-sized, and they hold to the leaf edges until they are too large to hold. Each makes a tick as it lets go, too faint to hear singly. A whole valley of them, under water, is a fine dry crackle at the edge of hearing, like a fire a long way off. By noon the forest is silvered top to bottom and the bubbles rise in slow threads through the shafts of light. They gather under the mat, and the mat lifts a finger's width across the entire basin, as though the valley were taking one breath a day. At dusk it settles back.

In the afternoon cloud comes over the rim and it rains, heavily and warm, and falls straight down. Rain here makes almost no noise. It lands on steam, then moss, then mat, and is absorbed before it can strike anything. From below it shows only as a dimming and a faint dimpling of the green ceiling.

Other things happen over longer spans. Every few years a vent clears itself with a single soft gulp, and a dome of warmer water rises through the trunks, bending the light as it passes. Seedlings in the mat send a root downward, and it takes a decade to reach the stone floor. When the root touches bottom, the seedling begins to become a tree, and by then the surface has risen above where it started, and it climbs after it.

At the dam the forest stops against a wall of fresh black rock, glassy in places and still showing the ropy folds of its flowing. The lake presses against it without hurry. On the far side the wall is damp, and a few ferns have started in the cracks. The level is perhaps thirty metres below the lowest notch in the rim. At a leaf a day, the water will reach it in something over a thousand years. Until then the basin keeps on as it is, rising and greening above and hardening below.
