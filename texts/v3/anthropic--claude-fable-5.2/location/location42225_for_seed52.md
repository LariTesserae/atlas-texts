The plain is younger than some of the people who live on it. It lies a few metres above the tide, a shelf of black basalt running from the old sea cliff, now stranded inland with its dead tideline still showing, out to an edge that is still being made. The rock has not had time to become soil, or even gravel. It is ropy where it flowed slowly and clinkered where it tore, pocked with blisters whose roofs have fallen in, leaving glassy rims that will open a hand. In places it is still warm underfoot, and after rain it steams.

The city stands on this at wide intervals. Its structures are slabs, fins, and a few shallow domes, none tall, each a kilometre or more from the next, with nothing visible holding them in place. Their surfaces are a seamless matte dark, and their edges are hard to look at, because the light itself is sliding there. Each structure draws light toward itself out of the surrounding air, along a gradient that begins far out on the plain. Noon here is a brown-gold dusk with the sun a smudged coin, and every building sits in a pool of deeper dimness.

It is also why shadows point the wrong way. A person's shadow falls toward the nearest structure, and so does a fern's. Halfway between two buildings you cast two shadows, both faint.

With the light taken, the skins of the buildings run cool. Sea air comes in warm and wet and condenses on them, so each building sweats. Before dawn the water sheets down the walls and spreads across the rock for a few dozen metres, sometimes a hundred, before the porous basalt drinks it. There are no streams anywhere on the plain. There are only tea-coloured pools in the blisters at each building's foot, and beyond them dry black rock.

So the green grows in rings. Close to a wall there is water but almost no light, and what lives there is pale, long-stemmed, and threadlike. Far out there is light but no water. In the band between, maybe forty paces wide, everything grows at once: tree ferns, aroids with leaves like serving plates, vines, moss so deep that the sharp rock goes soft beneath it. All of it leans outward, leaves turned away from the building toward the brighter dimness of the open plain, like a crowd with its back to the thing it has gathered around. From the old cliff the whole city is visible as a scatter of dark shapes, each wearing a green halo, with bare lava between.

The roads are strips where the basalt has been persuaded smooth, a wet black gloss that reflects the brown sky. They do not run straight. They curve along the lines where the pull of two structures balances, and the rule for walking them is to keep your shadows twins. If you stray, one shadow darkens, and soon you are pushing through wet fronds in someone's halo.

The residents are few and seldom outside. A door, when one opens, is a rectangle slightly less dark than the wall. Now and then someone crosses a road a long way off, unhurried, carrying no lamp. Lamps are little use here, because a flame's light leans and stretches toward the nearest wall like hair in a draught.

The animals are scarcer still. Pale geckos lie along the warm cracks. A grey moth works the halo flowers by scent, flying low. Small frogs live in the blister-pools and call one at a time, never in chorus. Once in a long while a heron stands in a pool for an hour and then leaves.

It is quiet. Water drips from the leaves, and the rock ticks as it goes on cooling, decades after it was laid. Around midday, when the intake is strongest, the structures give off a note too low to be sure of, felt in the sternum more than heard.

A few times a month rain comes in off the sea and hisses on the warm flows. The steam rises in columns that look bent, though it is only the light in them bending. For a week afterward the dry ground between halos shows a film of green, which then dies back.

The halos themselves do not die back. Each year the moss makes a little more soil and the rings widen by a pace. In one place two have met, and the road there runs through a dripping tunnel of fern.

At the seaward edge the plain is still being extended. Something under the last row of structures pushes new rock out beneath the surf. At night it is the only frank light for miles, a dull red line under the water with steam standing over it. Overhead, the stars above each roofline are dragged into short arcs.

The newest shelf has no buildings yet. It already has roads, polished and damp, curving carefully around empty places. On that bare rock a walker's shadow already tilts a little toward those places.
