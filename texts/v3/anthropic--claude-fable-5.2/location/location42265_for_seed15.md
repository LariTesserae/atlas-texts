From above it is only a rectangular hole in a paved yard, four paces by six, with a noise coming out of it. The yard is limestone flags, the coping is limestone, and the walls going down are stone under lime plaster so new it is still the white of the inside of a shell. At midday the sun stands straight over the opening and reaches the bottom. The whole tank lights up like a lamp, and looking into it makes the eyes water.

Eleven steps go down. The first four are dry. Their edges are sharp enough to scrape a heel, and the chisel left its marks in neat slanted rows, narrow and close together, the work of a soft blade that was often resharpened. A line of charcoal still runs along one riser where someone marked the level. On the plaster by the sixth step a palm print has set into the surface, with the sweep of the trowel visible around it. Below that the steps continue underwater, and from above they seem to bend and shiver.

The water comes in at the far end. A run of fired clay pipe, its joints packed with lime putty, emerges from the wall at about head height. It ends in a stone lip, and from the lip a stream as thick as a forearm falls straight into the tank. It arrives warm from the hillside, warmer than skin but bearable. The fall is why the place is loud. In a chamber of hard parallel walls the sound has nowhere to go, so it becomes a steady drumming roar with a slapping edge on top. A person on the steps has to raise their voice. Now and then the pipe swallows air and coughs, and the stream stutters, thickens, and resumes. At the opposite corner a notch cut in the top course lets the overflow out into a covered drain, which gulps and sucks irregularly beneath the roar.

Most of the place is under the surface. The tank is two body-lengths deep at the spout end and holds perhaps twice as much water as there is air above it. The light is even stronger down here, because white walls and a white floor bounce the sun back and forth through clear water, and nothing in it casts a real shadow. A net of bright lines crawls over the floor and up the walls, stretching and knotting as the surface moves. Where the stream enters, a column of bubbles drives down an arm's length, flares, and climbs back as silver froth. Underwater the roar is a deep continuous rumble felt in the chest, with a fine fizzing layered on it.

The warmth sits in layers. The top is bath-hot near the spout and cooler by a degree at the notch. At the floor a thin sheet stays almost cool around the ankles, since the stone below still holds the temperature of the ground. The water feels faintly slick between the fingers and tastes of chalk, because the new plaster is still giving up its lime. A pale dust of it lies in the floor's corners, combed into ripples by the slow turning of the tank. The circulation is easy to see. A single dry leaf, blown in from somewhere beyond the yard, rides out from the spout along the left wall, crosses at the steps, comes back along the right, and pauses at the notch. It has been doing this for some time.

Few other things interrupt the white. A wooden wedge, forgotten by whoever set the last coping stone, bumps along the waterline. A length of plied cord lies sunk on the ninth step. On the stone lip of the spout the spring has begun laying down its own mineral, a cream-colored crust thin as eggshell and only weeks old.

Growth has barely started. Along the shaded south wall, exactly at the waterline, runs a green stain no wider than a thread. In the joint beneath the spout, where spray keeps the mortar wet, one fern has opened three fronds, each the size of a thumbnail. A few grass seeds have lodged in the silt at the mouth of the drain, and two have split.

No animals have come. There are no skaters on the surface, no wrigglers under it, and no wasps drinking at the edge. The water is too warm, too new, and too sharp with lime, and no bird has yet learned that the hole is here.

The day runs on a schedule. At dawn the tank steams, a low white breath that fills it to the coping and spills across the flags. By mid-morning a bright wedge starts down the west wall, and at noon it covers the floor. In the afternoon the wind brings a film of yellow dust, which drifts toward the notch and is swallowed in rags. Toward evening shadow climbs the steps one riser at a time, and the roar seems louder as the light goes. All night the water keeps falling at the same rate, and the level, held by the notch, stays the same to a finger's width.
