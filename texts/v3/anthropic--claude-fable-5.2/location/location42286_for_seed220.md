The sound arrives before the ramp levels out. It is a low sustained tone that comes up through the soles of the feet first and through the air a moment later, and by the bottom of the ramp it is loud enough that a shout would carry about an arm's length. The air is as warm as a room where laundry has been drying, and it smells of wet stone and faintly of hot ceramic.

The chamber is not large. It runs perhaps three hundred meters from the foot of the ramp to the far wall and half that across, with a roof forty meters up that is felt more than seen. It holds a town. Sixty or seventy buildings stand in terraces down a gentle slope, two and three stories high. Their walls are a single pale material without joints, the corners eased as though the whole thing had been poured. The streets between them are wider than streets need to be, and nothing stands in them, no carts, no rubble, no furniture left out. The buildings are shells around empty rooms. Most of what the chamber contains is dark, humid air, shaking.

There is almost no light. Markers the size of a thumbnail are set at ankle height along the street edges, one every ten paces, and perhaps one in four still gives off a dull amber glow. Each lights a hand's breadth of pavement and nothing more. From the top terrace they are a scatter of dim points sketching the street plan. Past them, at the low end of the town, is a red so deep the eye takes several minutes to find it. That is the rim of the well.

The well is the source of the sound. It is a circular shaft twelve meters wide, cut flush with the plaza at the bottom of the slope, with no railing. Something stands in it that is hard to call an object. It is a column where the air is thicker than the air around it, and it bends the red rimlight the way heat bends the view over a road. It does not sway and it does not rise.

Conduits as thick as a torso run from the shaft across the plaza, up the streets, and into the wall of every building. They are warm to the hand. Rapped with a knuckle, they ring like stone, solid all the way through, so whatever moves along them moves through solid material. At a dozen points they meet the chamber's rock wall and continue into it without a collar or a seam.

The tone has a fundamental near the bottom of hearing and a stack of partials above it, and the town sorts these by its geometry. Each street has its own pitch, and crossing an intersection changes the chord. In certain places, such as the corner of a doorway or the fourth step of a particular stair, the sound nearly vanishes. One pace further on it is back in the chest.

No stream runs here. The water is on the ceiling, where warm air rising from the well meets cooler rock and beads. It would drip slowly if left alone, but every eleven minutes or so the column vents. The tone climbs by about a third and holds. Then comes a crack like a slab breaking, and a roar that lasts several seconds and presses on the eardrums. The whole ceiling lets go of its water at once. For half a minute it rains warm, heavy drops that fall forty meters through the dark and strike the pale roofs hard. Then the rain stops, the gutters run, and for about a minute the tone sits at half its strength.

In that lull the small sounds come through. Water drips from every eave and runs in the channels. The animals, which have been waiting, begin.

- **Geckos:** They are white, and they lie along the conduits for the warmth, one every few buildings. Each gives a few clipped notes and is answered from streets away.
- **Crickets:** There is a kind that has given up singing. It drums its abdomen against the pavement in quick dry rolls.
- **Moths:** They are small and gray and circle the working markers, three or four to a lamp. Their wings make no sound that survives even the lull.

Plants grow where the lamps are. Each live marker wears a collar of moss the size of a palm. From some collars a single fern has put up one or two fronds, bent over so far toward the light that they rest on it. Where a marker has died, the collar is brown and keeps its shape.

The plaza floor is ruled with lines. The vibration drives the film of water on any flat surface toward the still places, and the silt goes with it. This leaves a lattice of thin dark seams a hand apart, bowed into curves around the well. A colorless slime lives in the seams. Close to a marker they turn green for a few centimeters. Isopods the color of candle wax walk the seams in single file and are never seen off them.

The pale material does not wear, but everything laid on top of it has had a long time to settle.

- **Walls:** Calcite has skinned the lower courses in a cream glaze.
- **Stairs:** Each step has grown a small rimstone dam and holds a shallow pool, so a flight of stairs is a ladder of basins spilling one into the next after every rain.
- **Eaves and lintels:** Soda straws hang from all of them, and all are about the length of a hand. At that length the venting snaps them.
- **Streets:** The broken straws fall and crunch underfoot. Over the years they have cemented into a second pavement, gritty and pale, a few centimeters above the first. The gutters have cut clean channels through it.

The growing and the breaking came to terms long ago. Nothing in the chamber is getting thicker or thinner anymore.

The doorways have no doors. Step through one and the sound drops to a murmur and the damp stops at the threshold. The floors inside are dry, and the rooms are warm, clean, and empty. A stair leads to another room, with a window opening the rain does not enter. From inside a house the rain can be seen falling past the opening in the faint amber, and almost none of it can be heard. Stepping back out puts the full tone in the chest again.

The interval is not fixed. It runs eleven minutes, then ten and a half, and over many hours it stretches to nearly fourteen before it comes back. It may be following a tide, or the day's heat on the ground far overhead, or a demand from wherever the conduits go.

The animals keep track of it. At the lowest edge of the plaza the gutters end in a sump, a shallow black pool. It spills over a lip into the well, where the water never audibly lands. It turns to vapor in the column and goes back to the ceiling. A dozen fish live in the sump. They are finger-length, translucent, and eyeless. Some seconds before each venting, as the tone begins to climb, they all sink and lie flat against the stone.

After the roar and the rain, when the lull is ending, the geckos stop first. The tone fills back into the stairs and the walls. The fish hang in mid-water again, and on the plaza the isopods have started walking.
