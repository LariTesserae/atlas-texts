You hear the place before anything else, because there is nothing to see. A roar comes down the approach valley from several kilometres off. It sounds at first like a waterfall, then like a stadium, and close up it turns out to be voices, millions of them, layered over running water and a low mechanical chord that you feel in the sternum.

**The ground**

The city sits in a saddle between two peaks that are younger than most languages. The rock is dark, glassy in places, and still sharp. Nothing has rounded its edges, and no soil or scree softens the slopes, only fracture planes as clean as the day they cooled. The peaks are still rising, about a hand's width in a year. The rock ticks and sometimes cracks like a rifle, and the sound carries through the streets and is ignored.

The air is thin enough that a newcomer climbs stairs in stages. It is cold enough that anything not near the city wears a skin of hard, clear ice.

**The heat and the water**

At the centre of the saddle is a shaft the residents call the Tap. It goes down to where the mountain is still molten and brings heat up by a method that involves no fluid and no visible moving parts. The column of rock around it simply stays cold while the top stays warm. The city spends some of that heat and sheds the rest.

The shed heat is why everything is wet. Every wall weeps. Fog condenses on every surface a few degrees above freezing and runs down in sheets into gutters, then channels, then covered cisterns the size of lakes. The air is saturated; your sleeves are damp within a minute. Above the saddle the warmth and wet build a permanent cap of cloud, so even in the brief months when the sun clears the horizon the city gets only a faint grey, like the inside of an eyelid. The rest of the year it gets nothing.

**The city**

It fills the saddle from wall to wall in terraces, tight stacked blocks and towers. The spans between them are held by something that isn't quite tension; press a hand to one and it presses back before you touch it. The building material is dark, faintly warm, slightly rough, and grown rather than poured. The footings lengthen themselves as the rock rises, so the city is always marginally under construction at its base. Its lowest courses are raw and unfinished, its upper ones old. There are no windows as such, and no lamps.

That is the first rule here: no light. It isn't a law. Strike a light on an open terrace and within seconds ten thousand bodies come at it. They aren't hostile, only helplessly drawn, and they will put it out with their own weight and may put you over the rail. People who live here learned that generations ago and stopped needing light soon after.

They navigate by sound. Every junction holds a steady tone, and every street has its own interval against the Tap's chord. A resident walks home by pitch the way you'd follow signs. Because the towers lengthen, the tones go slowly flat, a fraction of a step in a lifetime. Old people complain that their street used to sing higher, and they are right.

**The animals**

Nothing here is a plant, and nothing photosynthesises. A grey-pink bacterial film coats every warm wet wall. It is slick as the inside of a cheek, grows on heat and mineral seep, and feeds everything else.

Thumbnail-sized shelled grazers cover the walls so thickly that the walls feel pebbled, and they rasp audibly as they move. Translucent crustaceans crowd the gutters until the water runs thick with them. Clinging to the walls above the channels, long-fingered animals with wet fur rake them out by the fistful. Flightless, barrel-bodied things walk the streets in files, licking the stonework, and will not yield the way. You shoulder through them and they grumble.

On every ledge, span, cornice and roof are the fliers. They are neither bird nor bat exactly. They have dense oily down and blunt wings, and each has a voice like a struck bottle and uses it constantly, to find its mate, its ledge, its chick, and its way. They nest shoulder to shoulder on bare material and breed without season, because there are no seasons to breed by. Their droppings would bury the city if the weeping walls didn't carry them down to feed the film. The smell is ammonia and wet stone, and it gets into bread.

In the air above the terraces they are thick enough that you feel wingbeats on your face as pressure. They click as they fly, and they have learned the city's tones. Colonies sort themselves by street, and each colony pitches its calls against its junction's note. Each district therefore has an accent in its roar, and a resident can tell where they are by which animals are shouting.

Something larger hunts them. It is rarely touched and never seen. People know it by a sudden hole in the noise that travels across a tower face and closes behind itself.

**The lip**

Nothing here changes quickly. The populations don't swing, the dark doesn't lift, and the heat doesn't waver. The city has added perhaps one terrace in living memory.

At the low end of the saddle the overflow from the cisterns goes over the edge. It runs warm and alive for a few hundred metres, steaming and noisy with crustaceans, and then the cold takes it. Below that it becomes an icefall, blue-black, studded through with tiny shells, and longer every year. Its weight has begun to press a shallow groove into the new rock beneath it. That groove is the first mark that water has left on this mountain.
