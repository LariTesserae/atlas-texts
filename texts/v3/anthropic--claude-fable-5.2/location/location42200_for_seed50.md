Just under the ridge crest there is a saddle of grey-green rock about the size of a threshing floor, and rain falls on one part of it only. The sky above is clear. Some forty body-lengths up, the drops simply begin, along a flat underside you can see from the side as a faint trembling line. From there they come down in a column a dozen paces across, so heavy and close-packed that it is more a standing body of water with some air in it than weather. Choughs cross above the line with dry wings.

The sound reaches you long before the sight. In the valley it is a hiss that people mistake for wind. On the last scree slope it thickens into a roar, and at the col it is physical: in the teeth, behind the breastbone, pressing the ears flat. Nobody speaks here. The people who climb up use their hands, and the few signs they need have worn down to almost nothing: stone, rope, deeper, enough, go.

The rock is new. Its edges will open a boot sole, and it has no rind of weather on it and no soil in its cracks. Water has not yet worked out how to leave. There is no stream bed below the col. The overflow sheets off east for part of an hour, then a surge tips it and it runs north, then it splits three ways and starts channels a finger deep that it abandons by evening.

Across the saddle are four shallow dimples, each the width of the column, each marking where the rain stood for a while before it moved. The fifth is the basin.

It lies directly under the fall, round and steep-walled, the size of a small room and deeper at its centre than a person with an arm raised. The rain did not cut it alone. Seven stones did, fist-sized to head-sized, driven around the floor by the churn and grinding the bowl downward as they knock. A notched pole wedged into a crack at the rim once touched bottom. It now hangs short by a forearm.

Under the surface everything changes. Overhead is a white, hammered, seething ceiling. An arm's length down the bubbles thin out and the water is startlingly clear, because there is nothing in it. With no soil above, there is no silt, and there is no weed and no fish. Full sun comes straight down through the turbulence and breaks into nets of light that slide over the pale walls. The roar becomes a weight on the skull, and through it comes the clack of the stones, slow and separate, each one distinct. The cold takes the feeling from your hands within sixty breaths.

Very little lives here:

- **Moss:** a ring a hand wide grows exactly at the spray margin, a complete vivid circle. It dies back on one side and creeps forward on the other as the column leans with the wind.
- **Algae:** a dark film slicks the basin walls below the churn.
- **Cushion plants:** a few sit tight as fists downslope where the overflow most often goes, among the grey husks of others where it used to go.
- **Larvae:** they glue themselves into cracks with silk and trail in the current.
- **A bird:** one small grey bird, or perhaps a pair, walks in under the edge of the fall to pick the larvae out. It is the only thing here that seems unbothered.

Goats will not come within hearing.

People have left a few things:

- **A wall:** a knee-high crescent of dry stone stands on the windward side, begun from both ends and never joined.
- **A gutter and trough:** split slabs lead to a hollowed trough, which is dry most days because the overflow has moved.
- **Pegs and a rope:** antler pegs are hammered into cracks, and a rope of twisted hide, swollen and black, is tied round a boulder and runs down into the basin for whoever goes in.
- **The stones:** on a ledge out of the spray, rough stones wait on the left and finished ones sit on the right. The finished ones are nearly perfect spheres, sorted by size, with gaps where some were taken. The smallest are the size of a bird's egg and have sat long enough to gather grit behind them.

The one rule is that the basin is never left empty. When the rain strikes bare floor the sound climbs to a note no one can stand near, and within days the column begins to wander. So whoever takes a round stone out puts a rough one in. The four dimples are the places nobody fed.

Now a surge heaves over the rim, the overflow swings back west, and the trough fills, shuddering, with no one there to see it.
