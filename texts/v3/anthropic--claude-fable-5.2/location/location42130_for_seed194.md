You find it by ear first. Across the floor of the old pan, where the ground is worn so flat that walking tells the feet nothing, a chord stands in the dark. It is low and reedy, like an organ with every stop held down, and it does not waver. It grows louder for the last few hundred paces, and then your outstretched hand meets the rind.

The rind is leaf: stiff, cool scales the size of thumbnails, overlapping like shingles and dry as paper. They cover a hummock no higher than a barn roof, which can be walked around in a quarter of an hour. Nothing else grows on the pan. There is no light here, outside the mound or in it, and what the plant lives on is an open question. The best guess is that it lives on very little, very slowly.

On the low side is the throat, a shoulder-wide hole lipped with smooth callus wood. Air pours out of it at walking speed, cold, smelling of resin and pepper and the inside of an old cupboard. The sound comes out with it. You enter stooped, against the draft.

Inside, the floor is dust, a mix of stone flour, cork crumb, and shed scale. It is ankle-deep and takes footsteps without a sound, so all the noise in here is the plant's. The walls are bark in plates, like rough tile, a hand thick. Knock anywhere and it answers hollow. The mound is mostly air and weighs far less than its size suggests.

It takes some time with the hands to understand that the passages are streets. The corners are square. Raised sills come at regular intervals, and you trip on the first few. The doorways are the height of a short person.

Whoever lived here built low and close, in stone and mud brick, and trained the plant over the lanes for a roof. You can feel where they worked it. Cut stubs have healed into knuckles and adze bites have closed into raised scars. A lashing of fibre cord has been swallowed so completely that only a spiral ridge remains in the wood.

Then they left, and the roof kept growing. It came down the walls and through the doors and lined every room like a sleeve. After that the stone itself went, brick to grit and grit to flour, and the flour settled on the floor. What remains is the plant holding the shape of what it grew against, a town cast in bark with the original poured out of it. In places the wall still carries the print of coursed masonry, joint by joint, with nothing behind it.

The rooms are empty. One has a shallow ring in the floor where a hearth was. One has a quern worn to a saucer, among the few stone things hard enough to last. And there are the jars, fired clay set into the walls at head height with their mouths to the street, gripped at the neck by the wood. Every one of them hums. Whether they were put there for storage or for the sound, they belong to the instrument now.

The instrument is the whole town. Hollow stems as thick as a torso run the length of the main street, open at both ends, and they make the drone. Narrower reeds line the lanes, each lane at its own pitch, so that a person who knew the place could navigate by interval. The main street is the lowest note, each turning sounds a step above it, and the dead ends beat against themselves.

From the ceilings hang gourd-pods, one to a room at most, hard-shelled, with loose seeds inside. The draft swings them and they rattle. Nothing eats the seeds or carries them off, and there is no wet to wake them. In time a pod drops and lies on the dust, and the one plant goes on being the only one.

At the central crossing, where the pipes of four streets open onto each other, the sound sits in the sternum and the teeth. Speech would be pointless there.

The mound breathes with the weather of the pan. When the pressure outside falls it exhales for hours, and when the pressure rises it draws in. Between the two comes a slack few minutes. The chord sags and drops out pipe by pipe, the high reeds last, and the pods go still.

In that quiet you can hear the wood working. It ticks, gives one long creak, and somewhere in the far quarter something cracks like a snapped stick. Then the draft touches the back of your neck instead of your face, and the chord rebuilds from the other end. The notes are the same but arrive in a different order, a shade flat, because the incoming air is colder.

That incoming air, at its coldest, leaves something on the lip of the throat. It is not enough to feel as damp. The callus there is only a little less brittle and gives slightly under a thumbnail. This is the town's entire supply of water.

On the side farthest from the throat the lanes narrow. Your shoulders touch both walls, then you turn sideways, then you stop. The plant adds to every inner surface, about a finger's width in a long lifetime, so each room shrinks evenly and keeps its proportions. The pipes close in the same way, which means the pitches rise. The town has been tuning itself upward for as long as it has been roofed.

In the oldest quarter the closing is finished. The rooms are solid wood, cool and dead to the knuckle. Here and there a clay rim sits flush in the surface where a jar was swallowed mouth-first. It is almost quiet there, and the chord comes from behind you.

The rest will follow in its order, and the main street will be last, its note climbing as it goes. For now it holds low and the jars answer it. The dust is deep enough that you could walk the whole town and leave without having made a sound of your own.
