Before anything can be seen, there is the noise. It arrives through the soles of the feet, climbs the shins, and settles behind the breastbone, where it stays. It is clatter, drone, shriek, and a low continuous grinding, layered so thickly that the ear stops sorting and reports only pressure.

The place is a hollow under a desert pan, several kilometres across and seldom higher than a tall tree. Its roof is a crust of gypsum, thin enough to pass daylight. The light that comes through is the light of an overcast noon, white and even, almost shadowless. Where the roof holds clear blades of selenite, harder patches of sun slide across the canopy as the day turns.

Under the roof, everything is plant. There is no soil and no bare floor. Hollow, jointed stems as thick as a torso fuse wherever they touch, so no one can say if this is a forest or a single body. The leaves are scales, bronze-grey and hard as fingernail, and they clack against each other in the moving air. The stems are gritty with mineral, and a struck one rings.

Nothing here is wet. A cut stem bleeds warm amber oil that skins over to wax within a minute. Dead growth does not rot. It dries, shatters, and becomes chaff, which lies ankle-deep in the low places and lifts in pale sheets on the rising air. That air is skin-warm and smells of resin and hot dust.

The animals are everywhere, and all of them are loud:

- **Beetles:** They are the size of a fist, and they drag their wing-cases across ridged stems.
- **Lizards:** They are flat-bellied and slap the hollow trunks like drums.
- **Fliers:** Clouds of small things with rattling wings pour through the gaps between stems.
- **Borers:** They live inside the stems and chew holes that turn each stalk into a pipe, so the warm draught plays the whole canopy in chords.
- **Roof-hangers:** Leathery and long-fingered, they hang under the crystal and scream down at intervals.

None of these animals appears to have ears. They listen with their feet.

They are loud because loudness is what the plant feeds. Where a stem is shaken hardest, it swells, buds, and sets fat pods of oil. Where it is left quiet, it goes grey and brittle within days. So every animal eats by shouting, and the richest ground is the most unbearable.

The plant makes its own use of the noise. Its hollow stems carry the din down to the root-tips, which press against the walls of the hollow. Under that steady humming the rock crazes, flakes, and falls. The roots close over the rubble, and the stems take up the grit. At the edges, where the freshest rock shows raw and pale and still sharp, the sound is a physical wall.

The hollow is young. It is wider this month than last. It is being shouted into existence.

Nothing holds still. Trunks fatten until they split with a crack like a snapped beam. Pods burst. Whole stands go grey and fall in on themselves when a swarm moves on, and green surges up in the place the swarm has moved to. Chaff storms rise without warning, turn the white light brown, and vent through holes in the roof as plumes over the desert above.

Predators here hunt by hush. One of them is a long, low animal felted in chaff, and it swallows sound as it moves. Its routes can be read afterward as brown, withered streaks through the green.

People live in this, a few of them, thinly spread. Their ears are stopped with wax and chaff. They speak with their hands and by tugging cords tied wrist to wrist. They cut with flaked chert from the rubble and scrape with beetle wing-cases. They lash platforms of split stem with slip-knots, because any fixed knot will be swallowed by growth within a week.

They keep no fire, since oil, wax, and chaff would burn through the whole hollow. Their sleeping-houses are baskets packed thick with chaff felt, the only quiet places they have. The quiet kills the growth around each house, so it sits in a widening ring of grey, dying stalk. After about nine days, the ring will no longer bear weight, and the house is moved.

The old sites heal quickly. Here and there a trunk carries a basket half-engulfed in its wood. A row of tally notches may be stretched by growth to the length of an arm.

In the southern lobe is a black place where something once did catch fire. The stems there are glazed and standing, and the silence is complete. The plant cannot go back in alone. But beetles have begun working the edge of the black, rasping at it, and behind them, a hand's width at a time, comes the green.

At night, the roof goes blue with moonlight. The shrieking thins, and the drumming deepens. The walls keep falling.
