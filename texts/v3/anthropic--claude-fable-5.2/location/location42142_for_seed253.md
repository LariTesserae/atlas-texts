The mountain was once much taller. What is left is a broad stump of a massif, its shoulders ground down into rounded slabs, still standing higher than anything else for a long way in every direction. A permanent cap of cloud sits on it, and it rains there without pause. This is not a storm, just a steady, cool, vertical rain that has not varied in longer than the slabs have had their present shape.

On top of the stump is a lake, and the lake is not held by rock. The last stone ends forty meters below the waterline. Above that the basin is a wall of plaited water-moss, felted so tightly it holds a lake's weight. It weeps evenly around its whole circumference, so the flanks of the massif are streaked with a thousand thin falls. As much seeps out as rains in, and the level has not moved by a finger's width.

The surface is covered too. A mat of the same moss roofs the whole lake from rim to rim, three or four kilometers across, a meter thick, green-black and sodden, dimpled everywhere by rain. It is the only part of the place that sees light, and that light is the grey of late dusk at noon. The mat makes what food it can from it and passes the food down.

Frogs live on the roof in their tens of thousands. They are small, cold to the touch, the color of the mat, and they call into the cloud without stopping. Between the frogs and the rain, the top of the lake is a continuous roar.

Under the mat there is no light at all. The water is steeped brown as strong tea and would be dark even without the lid. It is six degrees at the roof and six degrees at the floor ninety meters down. It does not turn over or carry a current, apart from the slow outward drift toward the weeping rim.

Most of the lake is empty water. A swimmer could go several minutes in any direction and touch nothing. Then there is a column, a trunk of plaited moss as thick as a house, running from the roof to the floor under tension and tying the lid down against the wind. Its surface is living strands laid in a braid that repeats every arm's length, wet-furred and firm as rope. After it comes another long stretch of open black water before the next.

The columns were built, like the rim, the floor and the roof's underside. The builders are fish the length of a finger, pale, eyeless, with soft muscular lips. They work in pairs: one holds a growing strand in its mouth while the other draws the loop through. The moss grows about a hand's length a year, and every length is tied down as it comes. No strand in the lake hangs loose. There are millions of these fish, though at any one spot on a column there may be only two.

Other animals live in what the weavers made:

- **Snails:** They are as big as fists and graze the film on the braids. Their rasping can be heard from a body-length away.
- **Shrimp:** They drift between the columns in loose clouds, crackling like wet kindling.
- **Salamanders:** They are as long as an arm, grey and gilled, and cling head-down to the trunks. Each gives a short cough, like a bark, every few breaths and waits for it to come back.
- **Drumfish:** They are heavy and slow, and cruise the open water thumping their swim bladders. Each has its own interval and uses the echo to find the next column.

Everything here locates itself by sound, so the dark is as loud as the roof, only lower. Under the thumps, coughs, crackle and rasp is the lake's own note. The rain drives the mat like a drumhead, the bowl beneath answers, and the columns hum with it. The note is too deep to seem pitched, and is felt in the chest and along the jaw. Because the rain never varies, the note doesn't either.

That note decided where the columns stand. The weavers can only tie where the water is still enough to hold an open loop, and the water is stillest where the lake's hum cancels itself. So the trunks grew in the quiet places. Mapped, they would form rings, concentric and evenly stepped, around a single trunk at the center that is wider than the rest. The pattern is too regular to look like a forest.

Inside the columns are galleries, knotted chambers the size of a cupped hand, where the eggs are kept. Weavers, snails and shrimp each have their own depth. Dead animals are tied in where they settle, and the braid closes over them within a season.

When a gust lifts a flap of the roof, a shaft of grey stands in the water for a while. The weavers cannot see it. They come because the rain sounds sharper there, and by the next day the tear is closed with a braid identical to the braid around it.

The floor is the oldest work. Its top layer is alive and soft as a sleeping animal's flank. A hand pushed in finds the same braid underneath, browner and tighter, and under that the same again, pressed dense as wood. The rock is somewhere below, and nothing in the lake has ever touched it.

At the foot of the central column, a pair of weavers is tying a loop. One holds the strand and the other pulls it through and draws it snug against the loop below.
