The plateau has been wearing down since before there were flowers. What remains is a low dome of pink-grey quartzite two thousand metres up, planed almost level, every edge rounded off. Its surface is pocked with shallow pans where rain has sat and worked for a few hundred million years. Most of the pans hold a skin of white sand and nothing else. One of them, near the eastern rim, can be heard from a long way off across the bare rock.

It is about thirty paces across and waist-deep at the centre. The air inside is warm and close, because the stone takes in sun all day and gives it back all night. The floor is coarse quartz sand that squeaks underfoot, and the seams in the rock hold black peat a finger deep, which is all the soil there is. The rock gave up everything soluble long ago, and nothing that grows here gets much from the ground.

So the plants stand apart from one another, each in its own crack, with clean stone between them. Each one is fat and glossy. There are bromeliads the size of washbasins, their tanks full of brown water. Pitcher plants stand in clumps of five or six, lids cocked, and a mat of red sundew is so thick with caught midges it looks furred. Three trees grow here, none taller than a person, with leaves as thick as rind. They feed on what falls into them, and a great deal falls into them.

Thumbnail-sized orange frogs live in the bromeliad tanks, several hundred of them, each with a voice like a struck bottle. Cicadas sit on the three trees in such numbers that the bark seems to crawl. A cricket drums its abdomen on the rock, and the rock carries it. Lizards lie on every sunlit surface, grey ones and a smaller green kind, and they slap their tails flat against the stone, which their relatives on neighbouring plateaus do not do. Small parrots nest in a crack under the north lip and quarrel without pause. Above everything the swifts come up from the cliff in thousands and scream as they turn. The pan is a bowl and focuses all of this toward its middle, where the noise can be felt in the teeth.

At the middle is the plate. It is an oval the length of a person lying down, matte, the colour of wet slate. It sits on a pedestal of rock a hand's breadth higher than the floor around it, because the floor has worn away since it was set there and the stone beneath it has not. No lichen grows on it. Its edges are still sharp, and nothing else on the dome has an edge. On the rim of the hollow stand three pegs of the same material, each as thick as a thumb and knee-high, also on their own small pedestals. A fourth lies in the sand where it fell, unweathered.

The plate sweats. A film of water a millimetre deep stands on it at noon in the dry season, when the morning cloud has burned off and the rock is too hot to kneel on. The plate is neither cool nor warm to the touch, and the water does not seem to come from the air. It is there in proportion to the noise. When the hollow is at full cry the film brims and spills over the sharp edge and darkens the pedestal. The swifts skim it with open bills and shriek as they come.

When a hawk's shadow crosses the pan, everything stops within half a second. The wind on the rim becomes audible, along with a dry ticking from the pegs, like metal cooling. On the plate a dry margin appears at the edge and widens inward. After a minute or two one frog tries a note. Others answer, the cicadas come back in, and the margin closes.

Everything living here is louder than it ought to be. Quiet species presumably lived here once. In the driest weeks the calling runs all day and all night without a break, hoarse and frantic, and the plate stays wet.

On the side where the fourth peg fell, the arrangement leaks. Beads of water creep the wrong way there, a little distance up the slope of the rock and out of the hollow, before they dry. In the sand nearby lies a loose arc of grey beads, perfectly round, each the size of a pea. The parrots carry them off to their crack. By morning the beads are back in the arc.

Real rain comes a few times a year, in storms that build below the cliff and climb. The plate answers thunder more than anything. After a close strike the hollow floods ankle-deep in minutes and water sheets out through the gap at the fallen peg, running across the dome until the hot stone takes it. For a day afterward the sand holds pools. Shrimp hatch in them from eggs that have waited in the dry grains, and they grow, breed, and are eaten by the lizards before the pools are gone.

At dusk the bats come out of a seam in the south wall, a few thousand of them from an opening no wider than a hand. The frogs reach their peak, and the plate runs over on all sides at once.
