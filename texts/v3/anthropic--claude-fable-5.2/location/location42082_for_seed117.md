The pit sits below the level of everything around it: a chamber cut into chalk at the bottom of the low country, perhaps forty paces long and fifteen wide. It is roofed by an overhang of the same chalk and three iron joists that no longer carry anything. Light comes down one square shaft at the far end, the old hoisting well, and arrives as a grey column that reaches the floor and spreads thinly from there. Away from it, the walls are visible mainly because they are wet.

Everything is wet. The chalk weeps along every bedding plane, from thousands of small seepages rather than streams, and each surface wears a moving skin of water a hair thick. Black flint runs through the white in bands, and the water beads differently on it, standing in drops where on the chalk it sheets. The air is cool and saturated, and breath hangs in it. Nothing here has leaves. What colour there is belongs to minerals and to things simpler than plants: orange floc where iron bacteria feed on the pipework, a black manganese varnish at the waterline, a grey-white felt of fungus on the one surviving timber, and, in the shaft's dim spill only, a stain that may be algae or may just be copper.

The floor is a sump, ankle-deep at the edges and deeper than an arm in the middle, clear as glass over pale silt. At the uphill end a spring comes in through a brick throat, and directly in its path stands the machine, a hydraulic ram, cast iron, the height of a child. It has no fuel or fire. The falling water slams a valve shut, the shock drives a spoonful up the delivery main, the valve drops open, and it happens again. *Clack.* Four seconds. *Clack.* It has done this since before the roof joists rusted through. Tufa has grown over its air vessel in ribbed curtains until it resembles a small stone organ, and the maker's plate is half swallowed, showing only *—SON & Co.* and *No. 4.* The moving valve is the only bare metal in the chamber, kept bright by its own motion.

The delivery main is bolted along the ceiling for the whole length of the room, and it has become the spine of the place. Each stroke of the ram sends a thud down the pipe, and each thud shakes loose every drop that has ripened on the stone teats beneath it. So the dripping here is not random. Every four seconds a single straight line of water, forty paces long, falls all at once, a brief bead curtain that strikes the floor with one soft, wide sound. Otherwise the chamber is nearly silent, because the wet chalk absorbs sound.

Under the line, generations of drops have worn a row of shallow cups in a ledge just above the water. Each cup holds a cave pearl, a bead of calcite the size of a pea or a marble, which turns a fraction with every fall and so never cements down. There are several hundred, all white, rotating a few degrees every four seconds.

The life is dense, mostly pale, and mostly small. Blind isopods graze the bacterial film on every wall in such numbers that the surface seems to crawl at the edge of vision. Springtails raft on the sump's meniscus in grey drifts that the ripples herd into the corners. Flatworms slide along the undersides of things, and snails with translucent shells rasp the iron floc from the pipe flanges, leaving clean rings around each bolt. Midges hatch from the silt in slow continuous smoke and rise toward the shaft. Most do not get there, because the ceiling between the joists is hung with gnat larvae in mucus hammocks, each trailing a dozen beaded threads and each giving off a blue-green point of light about as bright as a star seen through cloud. Away from the shaft, these are the room's only illumination, and they make a second ceiling just beneath the stone one.

Salamanders work the walls, dark and slick, the length of a finger, and eat the isopods. Eels come up the outfall culvert from the ditches beyond, lie in the deep middle of the sump like dropped cable, and eat the salamanders that fall. At dusk the grey column in the shaft flickers as bats drop through it to skim the midge-smoke, then return to the flue to fidget and tick.

All of them keep the ram's time. The isopods grazing the pearl cups scatter a half-second before each curtain falls, because they feel the thud through the rock before the water arrives, and they flow back the moment it has passed. The salamanders cross the drip line only between beats.

There is a slower cycle over this one. The outfall is a siphon, a bent iron pipe through the downhill wall. The sump fills for some hours, climbing an enamel gauge board whose numerals are readable up to 7 and buried in tufa above that, because the water has never gone higher. At 7 the siphon primes with a long swallowing sound, the loudest in the chamber, and the level drops a forearm's depth in a few minutes. The exposed margin gleams, thick with stranded springtails and film, and everything comes down the walls to feed: isopods first, salamanders after them, and the eels nosing along the new shallows. Then the water starts to climb again.

Rain on the land above shows up here a day and a half later as a quickening of the seeps and a slight milkiness. Some of what weeps from the walls has been here before. The delivery main leads up through the roof to a cistern that cracked long ago, and what the ram lifts leaks back down through the chalk and returns to the spring.

*Clack.* The line falls. The pearls turn.
