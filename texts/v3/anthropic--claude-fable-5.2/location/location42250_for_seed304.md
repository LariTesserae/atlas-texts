The valley floor is flat, sedgy and brown, and standing water shows wherever the ground dips a hand's depth. Out of it rises a mound of pale stone, about two hundred paces across and no taller than a big tree. It is the color of bread crust, cream where it's dry and rust-streaked where it isn't, and it's dry almost nowhere. The whole mound is covered in walls.

At the top is the eye, a pool a few strides wide, cold and faintly milky, blue in the middle where the water wells up from below. The water tastes of chalk. Wherever it runs thin over a surface and meets the air, it leaves a skin of stone, perhaps a fingernail's thickness in a season, more where it splashes. The mound is the eye's own deposit, and it is young. Its rims are crisp and scalloped, and the edges of the terraces will cut a bare heel. Nothing has had time to round off.

People have packed themselves onto it wall to wall. There are no streets, because the gaps between houses belong to the water. Every lane is a channel, and every channel is split and split again by clay dams and wooden tongues. Each share is measured where it divides by a notched alder board that the neighbors on both sides have watched being cut. You cross the town on wall-tops and on planks laid over the runnels, or along the edges of roofs. The roofs are reed thatch, steep and black with smoke, the only dry surfaces on the mound and guarded as such.

The walls are grown. A builder weaves a hurdle of willow and hazel, stakes it upright, and leads a share of water over it from a trough of split log. In the first year it grows a grey fur of crust. By the third the weave is buried and you can lean your weight on it. By the tenth the wood inside has rotted out and left the wall full of fine hollow tubes, light for its bulk, so that it rings a little when you knock on it. Hurdles stand at every stage along the lower terraces, some raw and yellow, some furred, some half stone with twig-ends still poking out. They are crowded close, and the dripping troughs overhead are packed closer. A share of water is the right to build. Quarrels here are about a thumb's width of flow.

The trouble is that the stone doesn't stop when the wall is done. Thresholds rise. A door cut at knee height by a grandmother is a window now, and her floor is somewhere under the present one, sealed in. So half the town's labor goes into cutting stone away. Antler picks and flint adzes tap at channel beds, at sills, at the lips of pools that have crept too high. A few households own a hammered copper chisel and lend it for a price. The tapping is the main sound of the place. The water itself is nearly silent, running in sheets too thin to make noise, and it only ticks where it drops from a trough-end. Voices stay low. Fog comes most mornings and takes what sound is left.

Anything left in the flow is kept. A dropped basket becomes a stone basket in two winters, and people set them out on purpose, along with nets of twine, knotted cords and bundled reeds, to harden into bowls, sieves and lamp-stands. Alder leaves blown in from the valley edge leave perfect casts of their veins on the pool floors. In the lowest terrace, where no one is permitted to cut, the shapes under the crust are longer. Each is laid on its side with its knees drawn up, and the oldest are just smooth swellings in the rimstone.

Little grows. The stone coats a seed before it can root. Moss manages in the splash zones by climbing its own corpse. The cushions are green at the tip and already rigid a finger below, so they stand out from the walls like stiff sponges. Dark liverwort lines the shaded channels. One crack near the eye has held a single stunted fern for longer than anyone has bothered to count. Withies, reed, firewood and peat all come from off the mound, carried along a causeway of hurdles that is slowly becoming a causeway of stone.

Animals are few. Small snails graze the algae film, their shells thickened and chalky, heavier each year until the old ones can barely drag them. A grey wagtail works the troughs. A heron sometimes stands in the last pool at first light and leaves when the first smoke-hole opens. Eels live in the outflow where the water has dropped its stone and turned brown, and boys wade in with wicker traps and come back with their shins crusted white.

The weather sets the pace:

- **Rain:** Rain thins the spring's water and hurries it through, so nothing deposits. These are rest days, when the picks are quiet and people mend thatch.
- **Dry wind:** On cold, bright days with wind the stone grows fastest. You can see the new skin on a trough by evening, and everyone is out shifting dams to send the flow where they want it.
- **Frost:** In hard frost the eye steams, since it is warmer than the air, and the mist rolls down the channels and stands between the houses at roof height. Frost lifts the freshest crust in flakes thin as fish scales. In the morning the walkways crunch, and children sweep the flakes into the pools, where they are cemented back in.

At the bottom, the last terrace ends in a rim as sharp as a broken pot, the water goes over it, and the sedge begins. Each year the rim is a little farther out. Someone has already staked a line of fresh hurdles past it, standing in the marsh, with a log trough propped and waiting.
