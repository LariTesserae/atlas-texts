The sea stands five kilometres above the rest of the planet. It covers a plateau the size of a continent, and the plateau is still being made underneath it.

Out in the middle the water is slate blue under broken cloud and runs three ways at once. A long swell comes from the south-west, a shorter one crosses it from the north, and the wind throws a steep chop over both. Where the trains meet they pile into peaks that jump straight up, hang, and drop back on themselves with a flat report like a slammed door. Nothing absorbs any of it. There is no beach anywhere to take a wave apart, so a swell raised a month ago is still travelling, crossing the basin for the fourth or fifth time. The whole sea rings like a struck bowl.

The air is cold and thin and full of spray, and it doesn't smell of the sea. That smell comes from things dying in water, and nothing here has died yet. It smells of wet rock and, after lightning, of ozone. The light is ordinary daylight from a white sun, with cloud shadows sliding across the swell.

Below the surface the water is nearly empty. It holds no silt, no plankton and no drifting debris, so the light goes down in swinging blue shafts far past the depth where an older ocean would have gone green and then dark. The floor shows a hundred metres down as a darker blue with edges. There is no machinery to be seen anywhere, no platform or pylon or cable. The work is done by something carried in the water itself, spread so thin that a litre drawn anywhere would test clean.

What it does shows on the floor. Along fronts hundreds of kilometres long, new basalt comes up in courses. Each course is a rank of six-sided columns, every one about a metre across, rising a hand's breadth an hour with orange light in the joints between them. As each column's skin chills, it cracks. One crack is a sharp tick. Millions at once make a roar like gravel poured on sheet steel, and it carries through the water for a thousand kilometres. Directly over a front the surface shivers with it, a fine standing ripple laid over the backs of the swells.

A front that size ought to boil the sea above it, and it doesn't. A few metres over the glowing seams the water is seven degrees, the same as everywhere else. The heat is taken somewhere. Hundreds of kilometres off, a patch of open sea with nothing beneath it will begin to steam, a flat warm disc a day's sail across, and a tower of cloud will go up from it into the cold air. By afternoon there is a squall line.

The squalls are the other loud thing. The rain arrives as a wall with a clean leading edge, dry air on one side and a grey mass on the other. Inside it the chop is beaten flat and the surface turns to a white fuzz half a metre deep, where it is hard to say which part is sea and which is air. The noise is a single sustained hiss that covers even the floor's cracking. Lightning goes into the water. Then the wall moves on, the light comes back, and the old swells are still running underneath as if nothing had crossed them.

Where the floor has risen far enough it breaks the surface. The new land is black and glassy, a pavement of hexagons barely awash, stepped where one course stands above the next. There is no sand on it, no gravel, not one rounded stone. Every edge is as sharp as the day it cooled, and that day was recent. Waves hit the steps and burst straight up, white against black, and the water sheets back off each tread in a hundred small falls.

Most of these islands do not last. The floor comes up unevenly and is corrected, so a ridge that surfaced one week may be drawn down the next, its columns sinking in rank the way they rose. The sea closes over it with a long sucking boom.

A few have been let stand, and on those, in the joints where spray collects, there are rosettes of something grey-green. They are flat and leathery, and the largest are the size of a hand. They are the only things on the planet that hold on to rock.

The only other growth is at sea. It is an olive-gold film that the wind combs into parallel streaks a few metres wide and tens of kilometres long. Storms shred the streaks and in a day or two they knit again. Inside one the chop is damped and the water looks oiled. There is a faint sulphurous smell there, the first trace of what a sea is supposed to smell like. Between one field of streaks and the next lie days of bare water.

Then there is the edge. For most of its length nothing holds the sea in. The water simply stops in a vertical face, and waves run up to it and rebound as if from a harbour wall. From the air beyond, it is a cliff of blue-green glass with the swells passing along its top in profile and sunlight coming through the upper hundred metres. Below it the plateau's raw flank drops five kilometres to the lowland basins, which are tan and grey and dry.

In one sector at a time, the hold is let go. Across a front two hundred kilometres wide the sea pours over the rim. None of it reaches the bottom as water. By two kilometres down it has torn itself into mist, and the mist feeds a permanent bank of cloud that rains on the basin floor. The sound is low and total, a pressure in the chest that can be felt across a hundred kilometres of open water before the edge comes into sight.

Over weeks the open sector migrates along the rim. Each time it shifts, a long shallow wave runs back across the whole plateau as the level settles.

The level is the rule everything else follows. The surface is kept at one height while the floor rises beneath it, and the excess is spilled. The sea is being decanted, slowly, off its own bed. The water is here to quench and to carry, and when the columns reach the air for good it will all be below, in the basins, with shores.

It has started. At the foot of the cloud bank, brown lakes are spreading across bare rock, and the gullies that feed them have begun to cut. At the mouth of one gully is a fan of grit a few hectares across. It is the only sand on the planet.
