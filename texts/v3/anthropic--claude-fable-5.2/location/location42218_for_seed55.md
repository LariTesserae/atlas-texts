Above it all lies ice, a meter or two thick, with snow on the ice, and above the snow a winter that lasts most of the year. For a few weeks in summer a grey-green dimness gets through, enough to tell up from down. The rest of the time the water is black from the underside of the ice to the bottom thirty meters below. The bottom runs on in every direction, farther than anyone could have walked in a month, back when it could be walked.

It was walked. The whole shelf is a made plain. Before the sea came up this was low wet ground, and people raised it by hand. They cut drains with antler picks and wooden spades and carried the spoil in baskets. They heaped it into long rectangular beds, faced the beds with fieldstone, and staked the banks with alder and hazel. They kept at it for so many generations that the works bury one another. Under any bank is an older bank. Under any lane of edge-set cobbles is a backfilled drain, and under that a hearth, a floor of trodden clay, a layer of carried gravel. The ground the first of it was built on has never been reached. From the old shoreline to the shelf edge, every surface was put where it is by someone's hands.

All of it has finished falling down. The stone facings slumped long ago to the angle where stones stop moving, and silt has filled the gaps between them. Mounds that once stood above floodwater, with houses on top, are now broad swells a meter or two high and a hundred across. The field banks are rounded ribs. The drains are shallow troughs with soft floors. Across the whole shelf the relief is hardly more than a standing person's height, and it repeats as bed and drain, bed and drain, mound, causeway, in a grid that bends slightly wherever one generation's surveying met another's.

The materials are few. There is fieldstone, unshaped, chosen for size. There is clay, puddled and packed into pond linings that still hold their bowls. In the anoxic mud, black wood has kept its form: stake rows, wattle hurdles, a plank trackway with its pegs in place, the cold having preserved even the adze facets on the post tips. Quern stones lie dished and face-up. Boundary stones carry pecked cups and rings that are now only shallow dimples.

And everything is overgrown. Kelp holds to stone, and the stone here lies in lines, so the kelp stands in lines. Along every wall-top and causeway grow double rows of thick stipes with blades four and five meters long, leaning together over the lanes. Seen whole, which is impossible, the forest would be a ruled diagram of the field system, with hedges of weed wherever there had been hedges of thorn. In the dark months the kelp grows on what it stored during the weeks of light, pushing out pale new blade at the base while last year's tatters at the tip. On the silted beds between the walls, where barley or flax once stood, eelgrass has taken the plots and fills each rectangle to its edges. Deeper down, thin red weeds cover the cobbles, and the stones themselves wear a hard chalky crust of coralline algae, layer over layer, slowly cementing the walls into single objects.

Nothing eats any of it. There are no fish in the lanes, no urchins on the holdfasts, no snails on the blades. Old shell lies in the middens beside the mounds, thick lenses of mussel and periwinkle, so there was a time when the shore here fed people. Those animals are gone, and the weed is unbitten. Blades wear out only by age. They come loose and settle into the drains in drifts, where the cold lets them rot very slowly, and the troughs are filling with a dark, fibrous muck that is partway to peat.

The plain is wide enough to have weather. When the surface freezes, the ice sheds its salt, and the heavy brine sinks in sheets, finds the beds, and runs off them into the drains. The drains still fall toward the sea, as they were dug to. So the brine goes down the field ditches into the collector ditches and from there into the great trunk canals, which are forty paces wide, with banks now only gentle shoulders. A slow, dense river runs in them under the ordinary water, with its own surface. That surface shows as a shimmer when there is any light and as a sudden cold on the skin when there isn't. The tide crosses the shelf as a long, low pulse, half a day late at the inner fields. It turns the kelp one way and then the other, and all the blades in a lane lean together.

That leaning is most of the sound here, a faint leathery slide of blade over blade. The ice above ticks and sometimes gives a long descending groan that travels for miles and comes back off nothing. Every so often a stone settles a finger's width with a small, dull knock.

Change here is measured in crust thickness and silt depth. The beds are a little lower each century and the drains a little fuller. At the shelf edge the trunk canals end in outfalls, stone-lined throats built wide to pass spring floods. The brine pours through them slowly and steadily and drops away down the slope into deeper water. The drains are still draining the fields.
