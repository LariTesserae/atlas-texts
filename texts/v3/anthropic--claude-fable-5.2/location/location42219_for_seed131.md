The pump house sits at the lowest point of a country that was made by hand. The fields around it were lakebed once, the soil exists because water was taken out of it, and the drains run straight because someone ruled them. None of that can be seen from inside. Inside is one room, and the room is mostly air.

It is a brick hall perhaps twelve metres long and eight wide, open to iron trusses and a glazed lantern in the roof. It was built for three engines and holds one. The two empty beds are still there, granite blocks with their holding-down bolts standing up in rows, upholstered in moss so thick the bolts look like stems. Between the beds and the walls there is nothing but a wet floor and the height of the room. A person standing in the middle is a long way from anything they could touch.

The machine is at the east end: a drum-shaped electric motor in green enamel the size of a garden shed, turning a vertical shaft that goes down through a collar in the floor to the pump in the well below. The sound comes from it and from under it. There is the hum of the windings, the higher note of the gearing, and beneath both the water, several tonnes a second dragged through a cast-iron volute and thrown up over a sill into the high drain. White glazed tiles line the walls to shoulder height and send all of it back. Speech is impossible. A dropped spanner lands in silence. The noise is felt in the breastbone and the back teeth, and after some minutes it stops seeming like sound and becomes a property of the room, like its temperature.

The temperature is that of a hand held near a cup of tea. The motor has been giving off the same waste heat for longer than anyone has kept count, and the water moving below keeps the air saturated. Everything sweats. The discharge pipe is beaded from flange to flange. The west wall weeps along its lower courses, where the high drain presses against the other side with its surface above head height. The gland on the shaft lets go a steady thread of water, thin as string, that has never been observed to break into drops. On the floor a film of water lies in a fine standing ripple, a mesh of tiny diamonds held there by the vibration, and it creeps along channels cut in the concrete back to the sump it came from.

The ferns live on this. Maidenhair spleenwort grows in every mortar joint above the tiles, and hart's tongue hangs in clumps where the wall weeps most. Liverwort has plated the lower tiles in overlapping green scales, and algae tints the lantern glass, so the daylight arrives even and faintly green, like light under a hedge. Ivy has come in through a broken louvre and hangs down six metres in a curtain that sways in the motor's updraught. Nothing dies back, because there is no winter in here to tell it to. The tips of the fronds tremble all the time, at mains frequency.

Around the motor the floor is dry. The warmth holds the film off in a rough oval, and the moss stops at that boundary in a clean curve, as if trimmed. It has not advanced or retreated. The oval is the only bare ground in the building.

Everything made here has finished wearing. The brick edges have gone round like used soap. The steps down to the well are dished in the middle, and the brass handrail is thinned to an oval at the height a hand takes it. The maker's plate on the pump casing has been wiped so many times that only a raised *& SONS* survives. Paint is gone from the ironwork, which has settled under its oil into a black skin that no longer rusts. Across the one long crack in the north wall someone cemented glass tell-tales, each scratched with a date. The dates are very old and the glass is whole.

In the sump the water stands at one mark on the gauge board, and the stain on the wall is a single line. Inflow from the land equals what the pump lifts. A float recorder in a glass-fronted box draws the level in ink on a circular chart, one revolution a week. A keeper comes to change the chart and grease the bearings, wearing ear defenders, speaking to no one. The caged lamps are never switched off, so night in the hall is only a yellower version of day.

Almost nothing else lives here. There are midges over the sump. In the window frames a few spiders keep webs that shiver constantly and catch little. The trusses would suit swallows, and none have come. There is one toad, under the grating by the well stairs, always on the same bar.

The used charts go in a drawer under the recorder. There are hundreds, and on every one the pen has drawn the same circle.
