Twelve meters under a gravel plain, below the depth the seasons reach, there is a room in the frozen ground. Nothing leads to it. It is a lens-shaped void in the permafrost, five paces long and three across, the roof at shoulder height in the middle and curving down to meet the floor at every edge. It is full of plants.

There is no light, and never has been. The place is known by touch, smell, and sound. A hand put out would first meet cold, the still, mineral cold of ground that has not thawed in a very long time. Then it would meet fronds.

**The growth**

The floor is moss, in cushions deep enough to take a hand to the wrist. Under the living layer is a forearm's depth of peat, damp as a wrung cloth and threaded with fungal strands. Out of the moss rise ferns, small, stiff, and leathery, packed so closely that their fronds interleave like fingers. Club mosses trail between them. The walls are scaled over with liverworts, lobe on lobe. Filmy ferns hang from the roof, their fronds one cell thick and wet to the touch, each holding a skin of water.

All of it is green. The color is a fact of the chemistry, though nothing here has ever seen it.

Everything reproduces by spore or by creeping. Nothing flowers, because there is nothing to carry pollen: no mites, no springtails, no worms in the peat. Nothing eats or burrows. Dead fronds stay where they died, hanging in grey skirts beneath the living crowns for decades, until fungus slowly takes them down into the floor.

**The thread**

The room is crossed diagonally at knee height by a taut filament about the thickness of fishing line. It enters the frozen gravel at each end with no hole and no collar, as if the ground had been poured around it. It is neither warm nor cold. It takes the temperature of whatever touches it, immediately and without lag, and gives nothing back to the skin.

The garden is arranged around it. Close to the thread the ferns are tallest and the moss deepest, and a sleeve of moss has climbed onto the line itself for most of its length. A pace away the growth shortens. Two paces away the moss is frost-stiff and thin, then gives way to a dry crust. At the rim, where roof meets floor, there is only ice-cemented gravel and hoarfrost in feathers as long as fingers, undisturbed since they formed.

The thread emits nothing a lamp would recognize as light, but the plants near it live as though they stood in a dim forest understory at midday. The air beside it is also a fraction colder than the air at the walls, which is the wrong way round. The room as a whole is slightly colder than the surrounding ground should allow. The thread seems to gather the faint heat that seeps up through the rock, and the heat the plants shed as they respire, and hand it back in a form a leaf can use. No rule known at the surface makes that accounting close, but here it has closed, quietly, for longer than the peat is deep.

**The other marks**

There are two other made things. Near the narrow end, set flush in the floor, is a plate the size of a palm, smooth and slightly grainy like unglazed ceramic. No frost forms on it and nothing has ever grown on it. The moss stops at its edge in a line clean enough to feel with a fingertip.

In the roof above the room's center is a bore the width of a wrist, perfectly round, plugged with clear ice. This is presumably how the thread came down. The ice column is the room's only connection to anything else, and its only source of water. Its lower face sublimates a little at a time, and the vapor drifts until it condenses on the filmy ferns.

**Water, air, sound**

There is no pool and no trickle. Water exists as films on fronds, as dampness in the peat, as frost at the margins, and as single drops. A drop gathers at the tip of a hanging fern over several minutes, then falls. That is the main sound of the room: a soft tap on moss, then a long gap, then another from somewhere else.

Beneath that, the roof ice ticks as it contracts. A few times a year the frozen ground shifts with a creak felt more in the chest than heard.

The air turns, very slowly. Chilled beside the thread, it sinks, spreads across the moss, rises along the walls, and returns across the roof, taking about an hour for one full roll. This is enough to swing a hanging frond the width of a hair. When a fern sheds its spores they ride the current and settle in a faint oval on the floor, which is why the young ferns come up in arcs.

The smell is cold peat, wet stone, and a trace of mushroom. Where a frond is bruised there is the sharp green smell of crushed fern, sweeter than expected from the sugars the plants hold against freezing.

**Time**

The room is in balance, but it moves. The peat thickens by a hair's width each year. The roof gives up its ice at about the same rate. So the whole void climbs through the ground, perhaps a centimeter in a century, and the floor rises toward the thread. The filament once crossed at chest height. Now it is at the knee, and the moss has begun to climb it.

Directly below the tip of the longest roof fern, the moss cushion has a dimple the size of a thumbprint, worn there by the same drop falling in the same place.
