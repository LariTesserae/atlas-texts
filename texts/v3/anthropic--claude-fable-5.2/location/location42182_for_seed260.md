The range has summits and no base. It was begun from the top, and the work has come down only so far: three peaks, a basin slung between them some four miles across, and beneath that a ragged underside where the rock thins to a crust, then to grit hanging in place, then stops. Below that there is no lowland and no cloud. The dimness continues downward, and there are stars in it, the same as overhead.

It is very high here. The sky is nearly black at noon, and the sun is a small white disc that gives a thin, violet-grey light and warms nothing. Shadows have hard edges. The cold is steady and completely dry. No snow lies in the couloirs, no ice has ever split a ledge, and no rain has touched a single face of this stone. Nothing here has ever been wet.

And it is loud. One tone fills the basin, low and enormous, with a grinding carried on it, and above the grinding something like cloth tearing without end. It has no direction. Hands over the ears make no difference, because it comes in through the jaw, the breastbone, the soles of the feet. A shout cannot be heard by the one shouting. The sound is older than the rock. The rock is what it leaves behind: stone comes out of the roar the way a crust of salt comes out of brine, slowly, thickest where the roar stands strongest.

So the stone is new and looks it. Every edge is sharp enough to cut cord. There is no scree under the cliffs, because nothing has fallen yet, and no sand or soil, because nothing has worn. Much of the rock is black and glassy and breaks in shells. Elsewhere it has not settled on what to be, a pale stuff between pumice and ash that takes a thumbprint and keeps it for years.

One of the three peaks has a north face only. The walk around it is a full day along the basin wall, and from every side it shows the same chimney and the same white seam, lit from the same angle. The second peak is solid to two-thirds of its height. Above that it is a line drawn on the sky, a shade darker than the black, with stars showing through the middle.

Things grow here, without water and almost without light, and they grow thickly. They root where the sound is strongest. There are felted cushions a yard deep, bronze-black, and stiff fronds stacked like plates. Runners as thick as a wrist cross the rock, and at the hearts of the oldest cushions stand boles as dense as horn. There is no sap in any of it. A snapped frond breaks like a wafer and lets out a puff of dust, which hangs shivering in the air, drifts to wherever the roar is thickest, settles there, and takes.

Nothing eats the growth. There are no insects in the felt, and no tracks, droppings, or bones anywhere on the mountain. Nothing rots either, so the dead growth stands where it died, grey and whole.

From the rim it is plain that the growth follows an order. The basin floor carries a figure: rings, spokes, and long curved bands of dark growth, with wide lanes of bare stone between them, the whole of it symmetrical around a point somewhat off the basin's centre. It is the shape the sound makes standing in the basin. Most of the floor is bare.

The figure moves, a hand's width a year, as the tone sinks. Bands die along one edge and creep forward along the other, leaving grey behind them. The live figure lies on top of older figures, each a little tighter than the last, back to faint ash-coloured ones that hardly show.

In the bare lanes, where the sound cancels itself, there are places it does not reach. They are small, a few paces across. In them the mountain has not happened. They are not pits, since a pit has sides and darkness. These have no depth to judge and no colour, and the eye crossing one arrives at the far side without having seen anything in between. Dust that drifts over the edge of one does not fall in. It is gone at the edge. These places migrate with the figure, and the stone they leave behind is the pale thumbprint kind.

Someone lived here, or tried to, and what they wanted was quiet. A dozen dry-stone cells stand across the basin, hours apart from one another. They are beehive-shaped and shoulder-high, laid from knapped slabs, the gaps packed with dead felt, each low doorway hung with a mat of twisted frond fibre. Inside the ones still standing, the roar is cut to something bearable, and a person might have slept.

Those are the poorly made ones. The well-made ones are gone. What remains of them is two jambs and a lintel standing on bare rock, and behind the doorway a patch of ground the eye cannot cross. Where it is quiet enough, nothing holds the floor up. The builders learned this. Their later cells have a slot left open on one side, the width of a hand, to let enough of the mountain in.

Their tools lie where they were set down, undecayed: flakes of the glassy stone, mallets cut from cushion-bole, coils of cord. There are no hearths. The growth does not burn. It only crumbles.

On the eastern floor a shadow lies across the bare stone, long and tapering, the shadow of a pinnacle. There is no pinnacle. Someone outlined the shadow in fist-sized stones, and it still swings with the sun and returns to fill the outline at the same hour. At the spot where the pinnacle would stand there is now a stub of black glass, knee-high. The cells nearest it are older than it is. Shadows arrive first here, as the sound does.

At the basin's lip, cords hang over the edge, knotted at arm-spans and weighted with holed stones. They were lowered to find the underside. The oldest weights are sunk in rock now, the cords running down into solid stone that has grown past them. The newest swings free in the dim, with stars behind it.

Under an overhang on the west wall is a row of forty-one stone bars on bearers, each chipped shorter and shorter until it answered the roar of its maker's time. A struck bar cannot be heard here. A bar is in tune when it shakes on its own. Forty of them lie dead, the tone having sunk below each in turn. The forty-first is blurred at its edges with trembling and has walked a finger's breadth along its bearers.

Beside it lies a forty-second, roughed out and still too long, with a mallet resting on it. It does not move. The roar is coming down toward it at its own pace, and in some number of years the bar will begin to hum untouched, finished or not.
