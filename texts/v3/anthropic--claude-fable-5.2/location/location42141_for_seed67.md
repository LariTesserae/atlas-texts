The ground is steel plate. Each plate is twelve paces on a side, blue-black with mill scale, and stencilled in one corner with a heat number in white paint that has not begun to chalk. Between plates runs a gap two fingers wide, and in every gap, a hand's depth down, water moves at a slow walking pace. The water is as warm as a bath left standing ten minutes. So is the plate, and so is the air, which means nothing steams and nothing chills. Moisture simply arrives on surfaces, beads, joins other beads, and slides over the plate edge into the gap.

The plain is flat to within what a surveyor's level can find. Brass datum studs are welded in at intervals, each stamped with a height, and the heights are all small: a metre above some zero, two metres, here and there a minus sign. Everything that rises from the deck was put there. Most of it is radiator banks, finned panels four storeys tall standing in rows, the spangle still bright on their zinc. Between the rows run pipe racks in five tiers, carrying lines that range from wrist-thick to wide enough to drive a truck through. At each crossing sits a concrete plinth with the grain of the form boards still printed on its faces and corners sharp enough to skin a knuckle. The weld beads are unground. The bolt threads still shine with cutting oil. All of it looks as if it was commissioned last week.

It has looked that way for a very long time, and the reason is on every surface. A grey-pink film, thinner than a coat of paint and slick under a fingertip, grows wherever warm water sheets over metal, which is everywhere. It is the only thing here that grows. Left alone it would thicken, turn orange, and start to pit the steel. It is never left alone.

On the radiator fins there are snails the size of lentils with shells clear enough to show the dark coil inside. They sit a thumb's width apart, and that spacing holds from the bottom of a panel to the top, on every panel, in every row. On the deck plates flat isopods the colour of mill scale travel in sheets several animals deep. They part around a boot and close behind it without altering their pace.

In the gaps the water looks cloudy until you kneel. Then the cloud resolves into colourless shrimp, each with a visible thread of gut, packed so closely they touch. Below the grated main channels lie eels as thick as a forearm. Every one points upstream and holds station with a slow sculling of the tail, so the channel floor appears paved with them.

Spiders have strung a web across every space between fin panels. Each web is loaded with fine water, and the rows look glazed. Small furred drain flies rise from the gaps into a haze that stops at knee height as if at a ceiling. Geckos the grey of fresh concrete sit on the plinths, one to a face.

On the pipe racks stand the birds. They are tall grey waders with heavy bills, one to each pipe support, on every tier. Along a single rack there are thousands in view before the perspective closes, and there is a rack every sixty paces.

None of them calls. The frogs that sit along the lowest pipe runs have throats that never swell. The birds do not clatter their bills or shift their feet. What sound there is comes from water: a drip off the lowest tier onto plate, and a steady trickle in the gaps. Under that is a faint dry hiss, which is the feet of the isopods, millions of them, on steel. Under that again is the machinery, well below the deck. It is something very large turning slowly in oil, and it comes through the soles of the feet as a faint pressure.

The light is the light of an overcast noon. A cloud lid sits a couple of hundred metres up, flat-bottomed and bright, formed where the plain's warm wet air meets the cooler air above it. There is presumably a sun beyond it, and presumably nights. On the top tier of every rack are lamps on photocells. When the lid dims they come up, and when it brightens they fall back, so the deck holds one illuminance. Shadows are soft and lie directly beneath things, and they have never moved.

The rows run to the vanishing point in both directions, and in the cross direction they do the same. At the horizon the lid and the deck close to a single pale seam. The plain is big enough to have its own climate and has settled on one. Heat comes off the fins and lifts into the lid. The lid gives it back as a drizzle too fine to see, felt only as a dampness on the face that never becomes drops. The drizzle runs to the gaps, the gaps feed the channels, and the channels feed pump galleries somewhere below, which send the water back up through the fins.

There is no wind, because no place here is warmer or wetter than the place beside it. Someone once tied a strip of orange surveyor's flagging to a handrail. It hangs straight down with the knot still tight and the colour unfaded.

The big lines come up through the deck hot and go back down less hot. Whatever they serve lies below and elsewhere, and its load does not vary.

The animals do not vary either. At a sump, a square pit with a ladder and a float switch, sixty or so pale crabs sit around the waterline. Some carry eggs under their tails. A wader on the rail above bends at long intervals and takes one shrimp from the gap with a single unhurried stab. A snail drops from a fin, and the isopods have the shell emptied before the next bead of water crosses the plate. The shell goes into the gap, softens in the warm flow, and is gone. The lime ends up in other shells. No young are ever seen in surplus, and nothing lies dead for longer than a minute. The number hatched matches the number taken, row by row and plate by plate. The film is grazed exactly as fast as it grows, and the steel underneath stays at its first week.

Near the foot of one plinth a weld has been circled in yellow paint crayon, with OK written beside it in a quick hand. A snail is working along the edge of the circle. It clears the film right up to the yellow and goes around.
