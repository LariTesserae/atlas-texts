The way in is down, and the first thing that registers at the bottom is the ceiling: cast-iron plates bolted in arcs, each plate the size of a barn door, the joints caulked with lead that still shows bright where the caulking iron bit it. The arch springs from columns set so far apart that from the foot of one, the next is a grey suggestion with a lamp at its shoulder. There are perhaps forty columns in all, and the hall runs for the better part of two miles. No rock shows anywhere. The floor is concrete poured in bays, each bay stamped with a number while wet, and the arrises of the drainage channels are still sharp enough to skin a knuckle.

Those channels are open launders a forearm wide, laid out in a grid, and they are never dry. Black water moves through them toward a sump somewhere in the middle distance, where pumps lift it out in long strokes. The pumps are one layer of the noise. Under them is the beat of the compressor houses, low iron sheds spaced down the hall with flywheels turning behind wire glass and a smell of ammonia and wet cement drifting from their doors. Over everything, at irregular intervals, water hammer goes through the mains like a sledge struck against a rail, and the blow can be heard travelling away down the pipe for a long time afterward.

The north side of the hall is not a wall in the ordinary sense. It is ground held frozen. Ranks of brine pipes stand against it floor to ceiling, thousands of them, each furred with frost a hand thick, so the whole face is white and soft-looking, like something upholstered. Behind it is whatever the hall was dug to keep out. Where a sheet of frost has come away, the frozen ground shows like dark bottle glass, with gravel hung in it, mussel shells, and in one place the curved rib of a small boat. High up near the springing of the arch, the dark thins to green, and for an hour or so around midday that band glows faintly, the only light here that does not come from a lamp.

The lamps are arcs on lattice pylons, one to a column. Each makes a ball of lit fog about thirty paces across, and between the balls the hall is nearly dark, the floor reading only as a wet shine. Around the foot of every pylon the concrete has gone green in a circle. First there is a film of algae, then cushions of moss toward the centre, and where a lamp has burned longest, a few hart's-tongue ferns have rooted in the expansion joints. The circles end as cleanly as if they had been drawn with a compass, and beyond them the concrete is bare. Midges hang in the lit fog above each one. Nothing else is common.

The hall has weather, and it runs to a schedule. A siren sounds from one of the compressor houses, two notes, the second arriving back from the far end noticeably flat. Then the brine in one bank of pipes reverses. For some minutes nothing happens. Then the frost lets go, in whole curtains, forty feet of it sliding down the pipes and breaking on the floor with a noise like a coal delivery. The launders fill to their lips with slush and meltwater, and the fog thickens until the nearest lamp is a smear.

When the cold comes back on, the fog goes up, condenses on the iron, and falls again as rain. It drips only from the bolt heads, so the rain comes down in straight rows a yard apart, in big slow drops. The puddles form in rows too, and after enough cycles they have begun to wear the first shallow dimples into the floor. Those dimples are the only soft edges in the place.

There are eels in the launders. They must have come in through an intake while they were thin enough to pass the screens, and they have their ordinary river colouring, olive and brass. One may show as a slow flex against the current and then not be seen again for a mile. A small grey bird, a wagtail or something close to it, works the channel edges under the lamps, picking midges off the moss. It appears to be the only one. When the siren sounds, it walks without hurry to the lee side of a column and waits there.

The bay numbers climb toward the east, and the concrete changes as they do. It runs from pale and lime-streaked near the shaft, through grey, to a damp greenish colour where the pours are recent. At the far end it is still curing. That concrete is warm to the hand, the only warm surface in the hall, and steam stands over it in a low bank. The moss circles there are larger than the lamps alone would account for. The eels gather in the channels alongside, a dozen at a time, lying still in the tepid water.

Beyond the last numbered bay the formwork is already up for the next: clean boards, oiled, with tied steel waiting inside. A skip of wet concrete stands on the rail, ticking as it cools. No one is in sight. On the nearest column is a chalked tally in bundles of five, and the last stroke is fresh enough to come off on a sleeve. On the north side, the newest brine pipes have only begun to whiten.
