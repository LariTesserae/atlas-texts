The land here finished wearing down a long time ago. What remains is the root of a mountain range planed to a floor of banded gneiss, pink and grey and bone-white, smooth as the inside of a wrist. Nothing on it rises higher than a knee. The floor is roughly oval, perhaps four hundred paces on its long axis, and forest stands around it on every side, stopping at the edge of the rock the way trees stop at a shoreline. Inside the oval there is stone, water, sky, and a few islands of green, with a great deal of room between them.

Water sits a hand's width under everything. The rock is pitted with pans, some the size of a bowl, some the size of a bed, three or four wide enough to swim across in six strokes. The joints weep. At dawn the whole floor is filmed and shining; by noon the higher swells have dried into pale maps with dark wet borders. Every afternoon it rains, warm and hard, for about forty minutes. The pans overflow and join into one sheet a finger deep, which creeps south and slips into the forest without ever making a channel. There is not enough slope left here to make a stream.

Plants grow where the stone permits. Moss fills the joints in long straight lines, so the floor looks ruled. Sedges stand in tufts where a pan has silted. Lilies cover the deeper pools with pads the size of plates. Every thirty or forty paces there is an island: a single fig sitting directly on bare rock, its roots poured outward like wax. Each fig has ferns in its crotches, orchids on its limbs, and a skirt of red sundews where its shade ends. Between islands the stone is open and bright, and the light comes from above and below at once. By midday the whole floor is hard to look at.

And everywhere there are animals. Every pan holds fairy shrimp in clouds dense enough to tint the water rust. Tadpoles blacken the shallows. The wet rock carries frogs no bigger than a thumbnail, tens of thousands of them, green-gold, so that a stretch of stone seems to change color as you approach and change back behind you. Butterflies mat the damp margins in sulfur-yellow and white, wings shut, drinking. Dragonflies hang over every pool in stacked layers, the small red ones low and the big blue-black ones above. Stilts and ibis and small pale herons work the sheet-water in loose lines. Skinks run the dry swells. Swifts cut the upper air by the hundred, and kites turn above them. At any moment, somewhere on the floor, something is being eaten. A heron's neck goes out and comes back. A dragonfly takes a midge and is taken by a swift.

All of this happens in near silence.

It takes a moment to understand. The birds have their bills open. The frogs' throats are pulsing. But sound here does not travel. It dies in the air at about two arm's lengths, with no echo and no fade, simply ending at a distance, as if each creature stood inside a small room. Walk through a flock of stilts and you hear the nearest three, thin and sharp. Then you hear those three no longer and a different three instead. A wingbeat past your ear is loud. Thunder directly overhead is a flash and nothing after.

The rock still carries what the air will not. The thunder arrives through the soles of the feet as a long low shudder, and the animals have arranged their lives around that. The thumbnail frogs do not call for mates. They drum, one hind foot tapping the stone, and they wave, lifting a leg and spreading a white-webbed foot like a small flag. The herons stand on one leg and seem to be listening with it. The ibis display instead of crying out, wings snapping open to show a red underside visible across the whole floor. Courtship here is done at close range, in whispers, or at a distance, in color.

The quiet is made. Three things on the floor were built, and none of them has weathered.

The first is a post, waist-high and grey, near the northern edge. Rain does not land on it. The drops bend a little as they fall and strike the rock around its base, leaving the post dry in the middle of a downpour, and a ring of moss around its foot grows thicker than anywhere else. Skinks crowd its top during the afternoon storm, dozens of them stacked dry while the world pours.

The second is a ring set flush in the stone near the center, about as wide as a room. Inside it the water stands a finger's length higher than the water outside. No wall or lip holds it there; it has a clean vertical edge you can put a hand through. The shrimp swim to that edge and turn back. Fish washed in by the afternoon sheet-flow sometimes tip out over the side and lie flapping on the lower water, where the herons have learned to wait.

The third is a line in the air, running shoulder-high between two points where nothing stands. You cannot see it. You see the dragonflies perched along it, a row of forty or fifty, evenly spaced, clinging to nothing, all facing the wind. When one lifts off, another takes the gap.

There are fainter marks. A shallow groove runs straight for sixty paces, too straight for a joint, and holds a line of moss greener than the rest. A rectangle of slightly darker stone is the size of a small house, and there the frogs for some reason do not sit. Whoever worked here used very little and left less.

The day turns on water and heat. Morning is film and glare, the butterflies opening, the waders spread wide. Noon is the dry hour: the pans shrink, the shrimp concentrate, the birds pack in around the pools, and the kites come lower. Then the cloud builds from the west, tall and white and unheard. The rain is felt before it is seen, a prickling hum in the stone underfoot, and when it comes the whole floor turns to hammered silver. Fish leave the deep pans and travel overland through a finger of water, backs out, heading for other pools. The frogs double in number, or seem to. After forty minutes the cloud is gone, steam stands off the rock in low sheets, and the light is brighter than it was before.

At dusk the raised water in the ring does not darken with the rest. It holds the day for about an hour, a pale disc lit from within, and every flying insect on the floor goes to it. Then the bats come out of the figs in thousands. You hear only the ones that pass within reach, a papery flick now and then, while the rest go round and round above the lit water. Under your feet the stone is ticking with small drumming feet.
