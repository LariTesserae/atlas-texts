The chamber lies some forty meters under a waterworks, in limestone, and it is full of water to within a hand's width of its roof. It is one room, about thirty meters long, half that across, and a dozen deep at the middle. The water is so clear that every wall is visible from anywhere inside it.

Light comes from a single place. Near the high end of the roof a natural joint in the rock, widened by long solution, runs up into the basement of the pump hall, where the lamps are never switched off. Their light comes down the fissure and stands in the water as a pale column, white with a little green in it. It lands on an oval of floor and a strip of the near wall. The rest of the chamber is lit by scatter from that column, an even grey-blue visibility like an overcast afternoon seen from indoors. Shadows are soft and do not move.

One made thing shares the fissure. A steel riser pipe as thick as a thigh descends through it and ends in a slotted strainer two meters above the floor. Its flanges are ringed with bolt heads, and rust has raised small orange blisters along the seams. The chamber is otherwise as the rock made it, with no ladder, no cut wall, and no concrete. The pipe draws water constantly, but so gently, and from so wide a volume, that the silt beneath the strainer has never been disturbed.

The rock records older conditions. The walls are covered in scallops, shallow overlapping hollows the size of a palm, cut when water once moved through here fast. From the roof hang stalactites that grew in an even earlier age, when the room was dry and dripping. They are drowned now, blunt and finished. The water is saturated with the stone it sits in, so it neither dissolves the walls nor adds to them.

The floor is fine tan silt, level as something poured. A few blocks that fell from the roof long ago rest in it, each with a smooth cap of the same silt on top, like snow on a fence post. There are no tracks or burrows in it, and no small pits or trails. Nothing has ever walked across it.

Nothing lives here that moves. There are no fish, and none of the pale shrimp a cave like this might be expected to hold. The water arrives filtered through forty meters of rock and carries almost nothing to eat. No bubbles rise.

What does live here lives in the lamp's footprint and nowhere else:

- **The lit wall:** a thin film of green algae lies over the rock, shading to brown where diatoms have settled. Nothing grazes it, so it has grown to an even thickness and stopped, limited only by the light.
- **The lit floor:** a dozen strands of stonewort stand upright from the oval of silt, brittle and widely spaced.
- **The air gap:** a cushion of moss holds the lip of the fissure at the waterline, and two small ferns are rooted in the crack, pale and thin-fronded.

The edge of the green follows the edge of the light closely. A meter outside it the rock is bare.

The water is eleven degrees from roof to floor, the yearly mean of the country overhead. There is no warmer layer and no colder one, and no current that can be felt on skin.

The sound is the motors. Their hum comes down the pipe and through the rock as one low, even note, felt in the chest more than heard. Close to the strainer there is a faint hiss of water entering the slots. Away from it there is only the hum, and the chamber is quiet around it.

The room stays as it is because of an arrangement. Somewhere up the bore a sensor reads the level, and the pumps take exactly what seeps in through the walls, liter for liter. The surface under the roof stays at the same line on the rock, a line marked by a faint pale band a finger wide. The pumps hand off to one another in rotation without the note changing.

Weather does not reach here. Rain that falls on the hills takes years to come down through the stone and arrives already averaged, with no storm or drought left in it. The lamps make no day or night. In the air gap the fern fronds hang over the water, and their reflections lie on the surface under them without a ripple.
