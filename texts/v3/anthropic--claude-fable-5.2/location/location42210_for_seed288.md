Above, there is only a plateau of pale limestone, worn so flat that the horizon is level in every direction. The city is underneath it. Long ago someone hollowed out the soft rock below the caprock, street by street and hall by hall, until a whole town stood inside the stone under a roof held up by the pillars they left in place. The roof has since fallen through in six or seven places. Those holes are the only windows.

The light comes down through them in grey columns and does not travel far. Beneath each hole is a mound of collapsed rubble, and on each mound a grove of fig and stunted laurel with something thorny beneath. The trunks lean up into the shaft, and the crowns crowd the opening like hands at a hatch. Away from the holes the green fades within thirty paces to twilight and then to brown darkness. The city continues anyway, with terraces, galleries stacked four deep and plazas wide enough that the far side is only a slightly different shade of dimness.

Everything that was carved has gone soft. Stair treads have worn into ramps and door frames into ovals. The pillars are waisted like old bones where centuries of dust-laden draught have rubbed them at shoulder height. No tool mark survives. There is no metal, glass, or timber here, and perhaps there never was. A hollow in a sill may once have held a lamp, and the grooves across a threshold may be writing or may be the tracks of roots. The city no longer looks made. It has reached the shape it will keep until the roof comes down altogether, which happens at a rate of one block every few hundred years.

It is dry in a way that preserves things. The floor is a fine flour of limestone and old guano, ankle-deep in the streets and knee-deep in the corners. Fallen fig leaves lie on it for decades without rotting, curled and intact. The cisterns, great bottle-shaped chambers under the plazas, hold only dust and beetle husks, with a tide-line of salt far up the walls. The air is cool and constant, the same at noon as at midnight, with a sharp reek of ammonia.

Water exists here only as something inferred. The roots must find it somewhere. They are the city's real vegetation, more than the groves. They hang through every crack in the ceiling from scrub on the plateau that no one below will ever see, in ropes, curtains and pale beards thirty metres long. Where they touch the floor they thicken into columns and keep going down through the paving and past the cistern floors, into fissures too narrow to follow. Some halls are so full of them that they stand like a second forest of thin grey trunks with no leaves. They have lifted lintels and pried one colonnade a hand's width out of true.

Then there is the noise, which fills the place more than anything else in it.

The swiftlets are in the galleries by the hundred thousand. Their nests are glued along every cornice, and they navigate the dark by clicking. Each bird makes a dry, rapid rattle. The hollowed chambers act as resonators and the straight streets carry the sound, so that together it becomes a solid, textureless roar like gravel pouring endlessly down a stone chute. Under it is the chitter of the bats that roof the deeper halls in a living, shifting pelt. The rock pigeons along the lit ledges add a throb that is felt more than heard. In the groves the cicadas saw away in the leaves at the one pitch that cuts through everything else. A clap of the hands at arm's length cannot be heard. Footsteps make no sound at all.

The floor is alive too. Crickets with antennae longer than their bodies cover the walls, and roaches and hide beetles work the guano drifts so thoroughly that the surface seems to simmer. Pale centipedes hunt among them. Snakes hang by their tails from the root curtains near the openings, mouths open, and let the traffic come to them. An owl sits in one of the upper windows of what may have been a granary, and a fox uses the east ramp. Every animal here drinks somewhere else, out on the plateau from dew or from one another.

The one event of the day is the changeover. At dusk the swiftlets pour in through the holes as the bats pour out, two spiralling columns passing through each other in the shafts for most of an hour. The roar becomes a pressure on the chest and on the teeth. Dust lifts from the streets in the wingbeat, and the fig crowns thrash as if in a wind. At dawn it happens again in reverse.

Between changeovers the grey columns of light creep across the rubble mounds, a leaf drops, and the dust deepens by about a finger's width in a century. In the largest plaza, where no roots have reached and the ceiling is too smooth for nests, there is an acre of bare, level floor. Nothing crosses it except the noise.
