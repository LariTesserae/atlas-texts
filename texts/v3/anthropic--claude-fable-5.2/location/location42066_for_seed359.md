The floor comes first, because everything else stands on it. It is a shallow basin about two hundred paces from rim to rim, lower in the middle by the height of a person, and tiled throughout in unglazed red clay. The tiles are hand-cut and none is square. Each is fitted to its neighbours with a seam of black pitch, and the edges are sharp enough to catch a thread. They are warm all through, like a kiln opened a day early, and they tick now and then as they give the heat up. That ticking is most of what there is to hear.

There is no soil. Lift a tile, which is easy because the pitch never quite sets, and beneath it is another tile, slightly paler. Beneath that is one paler still, and so on down the reach of an arm, until the lowest are the colour of dough and soft, with edges that have not been decided. Below those the hand meets nothing that refuses it and nothing it can enter.

Overhead is a roof of gut membrane, scraped thin and stitched in panels with fine cord. It is carried on peeled poles lashed at the joints, as high as a great hall. Daylight comes through it plain, even and noon-coloured. One panel is scraped thinner than the rest and serves as the sun. The roof is stitched down at the rim to a chest-high wattle fence, and the air inside is as dry as a granary, smelling of pitch, tallow and fresh-struck flint. No rain has ever tested the membrane, because nobody has made rain.

The plants grow from coiled baskets of ash and chaff set into sockets in the tile. The stems are plaited rush, thick as a wrist. The leaves are bark-cloth beaten thin, veined with stitching and oiled until they shine. The knots are visible, yet each stem is one plait-row longer after every crossing of the bright panel. The newest leaf is always pale and stiff, waiting for its oil. The plants lean toward the bright panel and follow it. Toward the middle of the basin they close over completely, leaf on leaf in four or five layers, and the oiled cloth squeaks faintly where it touches. The fruit is a sealed wax gourd with seeds of knapped chert that rattle. A fallen gourd does not break, and by the next look it has been carried off.

The basin is crowded, and nearly every tile has something on it or over it:

- **Lizards:** they are fired clay jointed with sinew, the same red as the floor, and lie so flat they look like tiles that have raised their heads.
- **Beetles:** they are obsidian flakes that walk the pitch seams in single file, each carrying a crumb of pitch.
- **Birds:** they are folded bark-cloth with bone-needle beaks.
- **Moths:** they are ash, and hold their shape only while flying. They settle as a grey smear and rise from it whole.
- **The browser:** it is a slow wicker animal the size of a goat that eats bark-cloth leaves and leaves behind neat coils of rush cord.

All of them work. The beetles re-pitch the seams and the birds stitch the leaves. At the low point of the basin the lizards pry up tiles and dig out the pale clay beneath. They press it into small lizards and carry these in their mouths to a warm tile to bake. There is no kiln. The warmth bakes the clay and the baked clay holds the warmth, and neither came first.

On a low bench at the north rim lie bone awls, antler tines and a chert scraper with a hide grip. They are warm, polished by handling, and sized for a hand. No hand comes, and the lizards walk around the bench.

Every tile carries the same thumbprint in one corner, the same whorl with the same small scar across it. The thumb can be found. It is a soft grub of unfired clay, the size and shape of the top joint of a thumb, which crawls over new tiles and presses itself into each corner. There are dozens of these grubs, and the lizards make them too.

The tiles are lizards. An old one chooses a gap and lies down. Over a long while its legs draw in, its back flattens and its edges sharpen, because things here grow keener with age. The beetles pitch it in and the grub signs it. Later it is covered by another and sinks, and as it sinks it pales and softens until it is dug up again as clay. The clay cycles the way water would in a place that had water. The floor neither rises nor falls. The oldest thing in the basin is younger than whatever made it.

The quiet is thrift. When a bird opens its beak, what comes out is a span or two of thin twine, which drops. Other birds gather it. Every stitch in the leaves and the roof was sung. A loud noise would come out as rope, so nothing here is loud.

The bright panel moves when the birds unpick it and resew it one place west, at about the pace of a plait-row. When it reaches the west fence they carry it back across the underside of the roof. The light does not change while it is carried, because the light never came from it. The plants turn to follow it back anyway. There is no night.

Through the gaps in the fence the outside is visible. The floor there is one red surface without seams, and the membrane above it has no panels. With no joints to count by, the eye cannot find a distance on either. A beetle that walks out through the east fence comes in at the same moment through the west, still carrying its crumb. There is only one fence, and the outside is the thickness of it.

At the lowest point, under the thickest leaves, a wide shallow bowl is set into the floor. It is black clay, the only burnished thing in the basin, and it is dry, with no stain line. The birds keep the ash off it. The animals come in ones and twos, lower their heads into it and stay a while, and the wicker throat of the browser can be seen working. Then they leave.

The rush nearest the bowl is green. Nothing else in the basin is green, and nothing in the basin could have been used to make that colour. The rim of the bowl is rounded, the one edge here that has worn instead of sharpening. A lizard has lain down beside it and begun drawing in its legs.
