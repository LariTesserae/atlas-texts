No light arrives here. The tableland lies on the side of the world that faces away, under a lid of cloud several miles thick, and nothing that lives on it glows. Colour is a rumour. The place is known by its heat, its wetness, and above all its noise.

The rock is old and worn into long shallow domes and pans, with nothing sharp left on it anywhere. Over the rock lies a springy mat, knee-deep, soaked through and as warm as a sleeping animal's flank. Water stands in every hollow and moves between them in slow sheets, having finished cutting its channels long ago. At the rim, which runs for thousands of miles, the sheets go over the escarpment. That fall is the lowest note in the country, a floor under every other sound, felt in the breastbone hundreds of miles inland.

The wind comes up the escarpment from the lit side. It is warm and loaded with water, and with dust, spores, husks and the dried bodies of small things that lived in sunlight. It climbs, cools, and drops its rain in surges regular enough to serve as a clock.

The forest eats this wind. Combwood stalks stand taller than a house, hollow, bearing tiers of stiff fringed combs where leaves would be, every comb canted the same way into the weather. A hand drawn along one comes away gritty and greasy with what the daylight sent. Beneath them grow the secondhand feeders:

- **Drum-leaves:** taut skins stretched flat to catch the drip from the combs.
- **Felt-rope:** thick cords that wick along the ground.
- **Bladders:** sacs that fill, sour, and burst with a wet cough.

The growth is exactly as thick as the wind can feed. The balance is so old that when a stalk falls, one stalk replaces it.

All of it sounds. The stalks hoot across their open tops, each at its own length. The drum-leaves take the rain. During a surge it is like standing inside a mill.

Through this runs a pipe of riveted iron, wide as a cart and warm to the palm. It rests in saddles its own weight has worn into the rock. Every thirty miles or so stands a draw-house, a stone shed furred over with felt-rope. Inside, a beam engine driven by the ground's own steam lifts hot water from under the tableland and shoves it onward toward cold cities beyond the far rim, which no one here has visited. A dynamo on each flywheel feeds a wire that runs beside the pipe and carries only a pulse, one governor speaking to the next.

There is no firebox and no lamp. The engines were built for hands: one valve handle is a ball, another a fluted bar, another a ring, and the gauges are read by raised pins under the thumb.

Each engine was set to its own speed, so each has its own beat and its own pitch. There is perhaps one keeper to every ten houses. She walks the line with one hand on the iron and knows her place by the chord of the engine behind against the engine ahead. Distance is counted in strokes still audible.

The pipe is the warmest line in the country, and life crowds it. Warmth leaks from every joint, and the combwood stands closer there, a hedge a thousand miles long. The animals find their way by sound:

- **Combers:** goat-heavy climbers that strip the fringes with rasping tongues and place themselves by a bleat with a click folded inside it.
- **Tickers:** fliers that swarm thickest in the surge, competing with the combs for the drift. They strike the face like thrown grain.
- **Grounders:** wagon-sized beasts that move in herds. They boom a low note into the mat and wait for what comes back, reading the shape of the rock through their feet.
- **Tallow-eaters:** small nesters in the draw-houses that lick the bearings. The keepers leave tallow out for them, because they also chew back the felt-rope that would otherwise bind the flywheels.

One animal makes no sound. The hush is long and low, with a pelt that swallows noise, and it is known only by its effect. Combers stop mid-rasp and the tickers part. A patch of silence a few yards wide moves upwind through the racket. Where everything is located by its voice, the one quiet thing is the predator.

Something else sits out in the mat and hums an engine's note, slightly flat, a little way off the line. The keepers' rule is never to leave the iron for a sound.

Over the ages the animals have tuned themselves to the engines. Grounders boom in the key of the nearest draw-house, and combers pitch their clicks against its beat. Along the pipe the dialects change house by house. When an engine is stopped for repacking, the country around it frays within the hour. Herds drift, calls sour, and the hush feeds well. Keepers work fast, and while the engine is down they whistle its note themselves, which helps a little.

Overhead, storms the size of provinces turn inside the lid. Their thunder comes down, but whatever flash made it is spent in the cloud long before it reaches the ground. Thunder here has no visible cause, like the surge and the far falls.

When the surge crosses a draw-house, the pressure lifts its whistle. So the rain can be heard coming down the line as one note, then the next, then the next. The sound travels a wet front wider than nations and passes out of hearing, still going.
