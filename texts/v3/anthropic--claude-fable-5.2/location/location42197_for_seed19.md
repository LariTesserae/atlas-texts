The tableland lies above everything else. It is the stump of a mountain range that was worn down grain by grain until it had no peaks, no valleys and no scree, only a pavement of pale stone running level to every horizon. Somehow the wearing never brought it any lower. The air is thin enough that the sky deepens toward indigo overhead. Past the rim, far down, a floor of cloud sits without moving, and none of it has ever come up. There is no frost here, no dew, and no channel or stain where water once ran. The rock is polished into broad shallow swells, like a table worn by centuries of forearms, and a fine mineral dust lies in the hollows with no print in it.

The cold is mild and constant, about the temperature of a cellar. The light is ordinary afternoon light, clear and a little pale, and it has been afternoon for as long as the stone has been flat.

Lichen grows in rings on the pavement, orange and grey-green. Some rings are wider than a person's reach, with centres long gone to bare rock. In the cracks sit cushion plants the size of a fist, as hard as what they grow from. None of them has been bitten.

The lack of grazing is strange, because the tableland is full of animals. Grazers stand shoulder to shoulder across the whole of it, dun and felt-coated, with split lips and pupils like slots, their flanks touching. Between their legs crouch hares with their ears laid back. Lean dogs lie among the hares with their chins on their paws, and nothing near them is afraid. Grey birds stand in rows on the grazers' backs, knee-high and all facing the same way. Moths lie flat against the wool beneath the birds. The lee side of every stone is scaled over with beetles. Flies hang in the air at head height without drifting, wings blurred. Higher up, raptors are fixed against the indigo with their wings spread, none of them circling. A hand set down anywhere touches fur or feather or wing-case.

For all that, the place is almost silent. There is breath, an enormous number of small breaths that have fallen into step, so that the whole plain seems to draw in and let go about once every four seconds. Now and then a beetle's leg ticks on stone. Sound doesn't carry far in air this thin, and no wind moves it.

The animals do not eat or drink, and they leave no dung and no tracks. None of them is young and none is old. Their eyes follow anything that moves among them, but their heads stay still. A walker always finds a gap exactly as wide as their own shoulders. It opens without any animal seeming to step aside and is closed again by the time they look back. Each animal's shadow lies at its own slightly different angle, as if each stood in its own afternoon.

Someone was here once, and stayed long enough to build. There are low windbreaks of stacked slab, curved like cupped hands, though there is no wind to break. There is a hearth ring with ash still in it, cold and grey and perfectly heaped. Beside it is a knapping floor where flakes of dark stone lie around a hollow the shape of someone sitting. A scraper and a hammerstone are there too, and one copper awl gone green, the only metal on the tableland. A hide is pegged out with bone pegs, half cleaned.

One windbreak is roofed with ribs set as arches. They are grazer ribs, though the plain holds no carcass, no skull and no place where anything has ever fallen. The ribs rise and settle very slightly, in time with the breathing.

Behind the hearth, the builders tried to count. Rows of pebbles are laid out on the pavement, hundreds of rows, each one a tally. Sinew cords are knotted in groups of five and tied off. The rows come to different totals, and every total is right. Counted from the hearth, the grazers come to one number. Counted from the windbreak ten paces off, they come to another, though none has arrived or left. One grazer with a torn left ear stands beside the hearth. The same grazer stands a hundred paces on, and again at the edge of sight, and each time it is the animal nearest to hand.

The rim behaves the same way. It is visible from everywhere, a clean line where pavement stops and the cloud floor begins far below. Walking toward it puts more animals behind and exactly as many ahead.

There are no graves, no second hearth, and no path leading out. The scraper lies with its edge toward the hide. The ash has not shifted. A fly hangs a hand's width above the awl, and the grazer with the torn ear watches it with one slotted eye.
