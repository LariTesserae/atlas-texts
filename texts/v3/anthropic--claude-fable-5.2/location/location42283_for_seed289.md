**The Pocket**

It lies under a dry plain, about the depth of a well below the grass roots, and it is the size of a pantry. There is no way in. A fissure thinner than a fingernail runs up through the caprock, enough for air to seep and for a few roots to find their way down, and that is the pocket's whole connection to anywhere else.

Inside it is as bright as noon.

The walls are not quite stone yet. They are a pale mineral paste, half set, the colour of milk glass, and from every part of them crystals are budding: blunt, clear, finger-length and shorter, crowded shoulder to shoulder like teeth in a jaw too small for them. The light comes from the budding itself. Each crystal gives off a cold white spark as it sets, and since all of them are setting all the time, the sparks blur into one steady glare that falls from every direction and casts no shadow. The light has no warmth in it. The air is cellar-cool and smells of wet chalk.

There is almost no empty space. Crystals reach in from the floor, the ceiling and the walls until their tips nearly meet in the middle, leaving a gap you would have to enter sideways with your breath held. The gap is occupied too. White spiders have strung it with webs, and each thread carries a row of water beads that catch the glare, so the open air is a lattice of bright points. On each crystal tip sits a cricket, colourless, long-feelered, eyeless. There is one to a tip and never two. On the film of damp that coats everything, springtails stand as thick as the nap on velvet.

The noise is enormous. The walls crackle as they set, a sound like fat in a pan or hail on a tin roof, and the crickets sing over it, all at once, without a rest between strokes. In a space this small the two sounds pack together into a single solid tone. There is no echo, because there is never a silence for an echo to fall into.

Water is present only as condensation. A bead hangs from the underside of each crystal, fat and trembling with the sound. The beads never fall. The floor is damp but holds no pool, and the paste of the walls is wet to the touch but nothing runs.

Where the roots come through the ceiling they hang in a white fringe the length of a hand, and because of the light they have done something roots are not meant to do: their tips are faintly green. Moss grows in thumb-wide cushions wherever a crystal has left a patch of wall bare, which is not many places. A grey-green film of liverwort covers one ledge. That is all the growth there is, and it does not spread.

That ledge is the only flat surface in the pocket, and it holds the only made things. Nine cups of pinched clay stand in a row, unfired, each holding a spoonful of water. A tenth is half-formed, its rim still open on one side, with a thumbprint pressed into the wet clay. The print has clear whorls and is the width of a lentil. Beside the cups, three courses of flat stone flakes are stacked across a notch in the wall that leads nowhere. The flakes for a fourth course lie sorted by size, ready. There is no sign of who was building, and no sign they have left.

The rule of the place is that everything in it is beginning and nothing gets any further.

- **The crystals** dissolve at the root exactly as fast as they bud at the tip. They have been finger-length for longer than the plain above has had grass.
- **The crickets** die, and an egg splits at the same moment in the paste below. The springtails take the husk apart before it settles. The count does not change, nor does the tone.
- **The water beads** swell with condensation and lose the same amount to the air.
- **The moss** grows at one edge and yellows at the other.
- **The tenth cup** has not dried.

Break a crystal off and the stump sparks a little brighter, and when you next look it is the length it was. The pocket has no memory of the injury, because it has no before. It is one moment, the one in which a rock starts to become a rock, held open like a held breath, lit by its own onset and roaring with it. The cups wait on the ledge with the water level in the nine of them exactly even.
