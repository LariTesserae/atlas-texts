The mountain's top is not mountain. For its last few hundred feet it is carried stone, river cobbles and split slabs hauled up from somewhere lower and laid dry, course on course, by hands that had no metal. It has sat so long that the pile is taken for a summit. Inside the pile is a hall, and the hall is full of water.

It is about the size of a large barn. A warm spring was walled in at the center before anything else was built, and it still comes up through a throat of fitted stones at roughly the temperature of a held hand. The water fills the room to within a palm's width of the roof slabs. In that thin air gap condensation gathers and falls, one drop and then, a long while later, another. That is most of the sound there is. Above the slabs there is snow and thin wind, but neither comes through.

There is almost no light. Two roof slabs have settled apart by the thickness of a finger, and on bright days a grey seam lies on the surface there and reaches an arm's length down before the water takes it. Everything else is known by touch, and everything here has been touched by water for so long that it has no edges left. The pillars are stacked drums of stone, set so close that a swimmer would turn sideways to pass, and they have been waisted smooth like old bone. The stair that descends from a sealed doorway is no longer steps but a single ramp with ripples in it. The walls once had pecked marks in rows, perhaps tallies. They are shallow dimples now, readable only as a regularity under the fingertips.

Between the pillars, on ledges, on the floor, and stacked mouth-outward in banks four and five high, are jars. They are fired clay, coil-built, thumb-smoothed, and no two are quite round. There are thousands of them. They fill the hall the way comb fills a hive, and the open water is only what is left over, lanes and gaps and one slow vertical shaft above the spring. The lids were stone discs seated in puddled clay. Most have long since slid off and lie on the floor like dropped coins, each wearing a soft skin of mineral.

The jars are occupied. There are no weeds, no algae, and no green of any kind. The only thing growing on the surfaces is a pale bacterial felt that thickens near the spring, and it is the bottom of everything. Snails the size of barley grains graze it. Shrimp without eyes, white as the inside of a shell, walk across the jar mouths in such numbers that a hand lowered anywhere would be investigated within a breath. Flatworms move over the pillars. Fish hang in the lanes, finger-long, colourless, their heads smooth where eyes would be. They hold station like sleepers and adjust with one fin-stroke at a time. And in the larger jars along the lowest tier lie the salamanders, forearm-length, the pink of a tongue, with gills like wet feathers. There is one to a jar, each facing outward. They move perhaps once a day.

What is slightly strange is the order of it. The fish keep to the lanes the builders left and do not cut across the stacks. Each salamander's jar is polished inside by a body, to a gloss no water could give it, more bodies than one lifetime accounts for. When one dies, another of the right size is in the jar within a season. The small jars hold shrimp broods and the middle tier holds the snails' egg-masses, row after row, as though sorted. Whether the hall was built as a larder, a nursery, or a kind of shrine, it has become what its shape suggests, and the animals fit it the way a hand fits a worn tool.

The water turns as a single slow wheel. It rises warm over the spring, spreads beneath the roof, cools a little against the walls, and sinks and creeps back along the floor through the jars. A shed scale takes most of a day to make the circuit. A disturbance, such as a slab ticking as it settles or a lid finally sliding, sends a puff of silt that hangs for hours. The shrimp flinch once, together, and then resume.

Over years the spring swells slightly with the snowmelt that soaks down through the cairn, and the air gap narrows by a fingernail. Late in the year it widens again. Over much longer spans the mineral in the water lays itself down, a paper's thickness in a generation of salamanders. It is narrowing the jar mouths, welding the stacks to each other, and filling the dimples on the walls. The steps are still wearing away while the pillars slowly thicken. Nothing in the hall behaves as though this matters.

Near the spring's throat, where the felt is thickest and the warmth is strongest, the floor cobbles have been pressed into a shallow bowl by nothing but traffic, ages of small feet crossing to feed. One jar sits at the center of it, larger than the rest, with its lid still seated. The shrimp walk around it and the fish do not hang above it. The clay seal has long since turned to stone.
