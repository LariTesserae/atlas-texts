The plateau is a lava field too young to have valleys. The flow came out a few centuries ago, spread across the high ground, and stopped; nothing has yet cut into it. There are no streambeds, no gullies, no soil worth the name. The rock underneath is clinker and broken crust, sharp enough to open a boot. But you see almost none of it, because the whole field, rim to rim and a day's walk across, is covered in moss.

It is one kind of moss, mostly, a woolly grey-green species that grows in cushions as deep as a forearm. It has rounded every edge of the lava into pillows and domes, so the plateau looks upholstered. There are no trees or shrubs, and nothing stands taller than a knee. In the hollows where a lava tube has collapsed there are a few other things: a creeping berry plant, crusts of pale lichen, a sedge or two. Everywhere else the moss is the ground. You do not walk on rock here but on a plant, and it gives under you like a mattress.

It is wet through. Cloud crosses the plateau most nights and the moss combs the water out of it, holding many times its own weight. Press a hand in and water wells up around the fingers, very cold. There are no streams, though. The lava is so porous that everything the moss lets go drops straight down and runs away in the dark through tubes and rubble, to come out as springs somewhere far below the rim. The only standing water is in the collapse pits, small still pools with moss growing down to the waterline.

By mid-morning the cloud is gone and the light is hard. The air is thin at this height, the sun close and white, and it is cool enough that you want a jacket while your face burns. Wet, the moss is a vivid, almost yellow green; as the surface dries it turns silver-grey from the tips down, so the whole field changes colour slowly through the day, and changes back with the first drizzle. There is no shade anywhere, and every drop of water glints.

Then there is the noise. Steam lies under the plateau, and people have drilled for it. The wells are far apart, a kilometre or more between them, each one capped under a small aluminium dome like an upturned bowl. A single insulated pipe links them, raised on short legs and kinked every few hundred metres into a square loop so the metal can expand. It runs to a separator station at the eastern rim, and from there a line of pylons goes over the edge and out of sight. One road of crushed red scoria follows the pipe. That is everything built here, and it occupies almost none of the space.

One well is always being flow-tested or bled, open to the sky. It throws a white column a hundred metres straight up, and the sound is like a jet engine that never takes off. It is a broadband roar with no pitch and no rhythm, felt in the sternum and the teeth before the ears sort it out. The moss, which absorbs sound the way it absorbs water, gives back no echo, so the roar arrives from exactly one direction, flat and total. Yellow posts mark the radius inside which ear defenders are required. Inside the next ring of posts, the crews wear plugs under the defenders and speak with their hands. Even at the far rim it is still there as a steady hiss, like a tap left running in the next room. It has been there for decades.

Animals are few and you see them rather than hear them. A pair of ravens works the road. A plover stands on a moss dome with its beak opening and closing, and no call reaches you. Midges hang over the pools on still days. Sheep were tried once and taken off again.

Change here is slow and leaves marks. Downwind of each well the steam condenses and falls as a fine warm rain, and the moss there has grown thicker and greener in a long tongue that points the way of the prevailing wind. In winter the same tongue is a field of rime, ice built out sideways from every pipe leg. The moss gains perhaps a centimetre in a year. A footprint in it stays for twenty years, and a tyre track longer. So the first rule everyone learns is to keep to the red road. There is one line of old bootprints leading out from the third dome toward nothing in particular, each one a neat dark hole in the green. They were made before any of the present crew arrived, and they are still filling in.
