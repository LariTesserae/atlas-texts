**The Crown House**

The stack rises from a mountain that was itself made. Its lower slopes are slag terraces and courses of brick, and above them the flue climbs alone, banded with stiffening rings, until the clouds are a floor far underneath it. At its top sits the crown house, a drum of riveted plate some seventy metres across and forty high, roofed with louvres. An enamel sign by the hatch still reads CROWN HOUSE No. 4, though the lower half has been swallowed by liverwort.

Inside there is almost nothing. A ring gallery of chequered plate, four metres wide, runs around the wall. The rest is the throat, a round hole straight down into the flue, eight kilometres of dark with a warm wind coming up out of it. No catwalk crosses it. The gantry crane was parked against the wall long ago and has since become a hedge. The middle of the room holds only rising air, which smells of hot oil, wet iron and leaf.

The air is loud. The flue is an organ pipe the height of a mountain range, and the fans that drive it, somewhere out of sight in the first kilometre, hold it on one low note. The note is felt before it is heard, in the sternum, the teeth and the fluid of the eyes. Speech is not possible here, and a dropped wrench lands in silence. The note has not stopped within the memory of anything that lives in the room.

Because the note stands still, the room is striped. Every three metres or so up the wall there is a level where the air barely moves, and between those levels it shakes hard. The quiet levels are green. Moss lies there a hand deep, with ferns rooted in the old pipe lagging and tank-plants crowding the cable trays. Small figs have poured themselves around the handrails and set like castings. Between the green bands the plate is bare and shaken clean to a brown polish, the rivet heads worn round as river pebbles. There are thirteen green bands and thirteen bare ones, stacked to the roof.

Nothing here hangs down. The figs and creepers send out aerial roots, and the breath of the flue lifts them, so they stream upward from the gallery edge and sway toward the louvres.

All the water is breath-water. Rain has never reached this height. The exhalation carries damp up from the works, and it condenses where warm air meets the cold louvre blades. From there it runs down the wall in channels it has cut into the paint over generations, and it stands a finger deep in the upturned webs of the ring girders. It tastes faintly of coin.

Some of it never lands. When daylight comes through the louvres in level bars, faint shelves of mist show in the empty middle of the room. Each is a sheet of fine droplets held at a still level of the note, one above another, with clear air between them. They are thin enough to read a gauge through.

The animals keep to the walls and have given up on sound.

- **Frogs:** They live in the girder pools and do not call. Each raises a hind foot and spreads it to show the pale webbing, and from across the throat the far wall can be seen flickering with these small signals.
- **Swifts:** They nest between the louvre blades and are mute, but they flash white underwings at each other. They are nearly the only things that enter the void. They slide out into the updraught and hang there without a wingbeat for minutes.
- **Spiders:** They string webs only in the quiet bands, since a web anywhere else is shaken to lint by morning.
- **Geckos:** They lie flat on the warm casings of the louvre motors.
- **Moths:** They crust the sodium lamps, which are still lit, in such numbers that the light comes through them brown.

The machinery goes on working at its own pace. The louvre actuators shift a degree or two in a day. The needles on the gauge board lie steady behind glass fogged from the inside, where a small fern has got in. The bus bars are furred green except where they are warm.

The lushness ends at the louvres in a line. Leaves that push between the blades meet air that is thin and far below freezing, and they come back as a brown fringe. From the outer gallery the plate is rimed with frost, the sky is a darker blue than it should be, and the horizon bends. The whole garden is a few metres of warmth around the mouth of the flue.

The room does change, slowly. As the works below warm and cool across the years, the note drifts by less than a semitone, and the still levels drift with it by perhaps a hand's width in a season. The moss follows. Above each green band is a margin of bright new growth edging onto bare metal, and below it a margin of dead felt where the quiet used to be. Further down, older stains on the plate show where the bands once sat.
