**The Tableland**

The tableland is old sandstone capped with ironstone, and it is so flat that a walker could cross it for days without climbing or dropping more than the height of a house. It ends on every side in an escarpment a kilometre deep. The surface was finished long ago. What remains is a pavement of fitted stones, low tors worn round as loaves, and shallow pans that the wind scoured out and then abandoned. Nothing here is still being shaped.

It is dark here at every hour. A few hundred metres overhead there is a ceiling of smoke, flat-bottomed and slowly billowing, held in place by the warm air under it. The smoke comes from the basin below the escarpment, where the works are: furnaces, coke ovens, rolling mills, and a rail yard that takes an hour to cross. None of that is up here. The rock of the plateau is worth nothing, which is why the plateau still exists. Somewhere near the eastern rim a brass survey pin is driven into a tor, and that is the entire record of anyone's interest in the place.

At noon the light is the brown of strong tea, enough to tell stone from not-stone. At night there is the red seam along the eastern rim, which brightens whenever a furnace is tapped. There is also the lightning. The soot charges itself as it rises, and the pall discharges inward in wide, silent sheets, with at most a dry crackle like paper.

The plateau is warm in the way a room next to a kitchen is warm. Heat climbs the escarpment and the lid keeps it in. Water arrives on the works' schedule rather than the sky's. When the coke is quenched below, steam goes up in a column, and about an hour later the rock begins to sweat. The pans fill a finger deep, and the water tastes of coins and carries a faint iridescent skin. By the next quench most of it is gone.

Everything is varnished black with fallout. Animal trails show as pale threads where feet have polished the soot off the stone. There are thousands of them, braiding between pans and tors, and they are the only map the place has.

Little grows here:

- **Lichens:** grey-black crusts on the rock.
- **Cushion plants:** tight mounds the size of a fist.
- **Grass:** a wiry kind, rooted in the joints of the pavement.
- **Plate plants:** a single broad-leaved species that lays its leaves flat on the ground like dishes to catch what light there is.

The vegetation could not feed what lives here, and it does not have to. The stacks do that. Their thermals lift insects out of the whole basin, among them midges, aphids, mayflies from the settling ponds, and moths drawn to the yard lamps. The insects are carried up through the warm column and dropped, stunned or dead, across the tableland. They come down like thin grain an hour or two after each shift change, and the ground is waiting for them.

Beetles work the fall first, then webless spiders that simply walk and eat. Geckos wait at the lips of cracks. Jerboas sieve the grit with their forefeet. Nightjars sit flat on the pavement with their mouths open. The tors are hollow with bats, which pour out at each tapping of the furnaces because the red glow pulls a river of moths eastward and the bats know the route. Above them are owls, and on the ground a small pale fox with ears like cupped hands.

These are all animals of the night, and here they never have to stop. Owls hunt at midday. Nothing sleeps by the sun, and everything keeps the hours of the works.

For all this the place is nearly silent. The pall muffles sound. From below comes a hum that is felt in the soles more than heard, and once in a long while a whistle, very small. The rest is wingbeats, the tick of beetle feet, and a fox's nails on stone. The bats are in fact screaming continuously, so the plateau is loud in a register that nothing without a bat's ears can hear.

Twice a year the furnaces are banked for relining. The lid thins and tears, and for four or five days a brown-gold light reaches the ground. The plate plants lift their leaves. The cushions flower all at once, small and white, and set seed before the smoke closes again. The hares, which have waited for this, eat almost all of it.

On ordinary nights the lightning is the only thing that shows the place whole. A sheet goes off inside the pall, and for half a second the plain is lit to the horizon, with its black pavement, its pale threads of trail, and its loaf-shaped tors. Across all of it, in every direction, are the paired green points of eyes turned upward. Then the dark returns, and the animals go on eating.
