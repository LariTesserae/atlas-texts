The notch is forty paces across, a gap in the ridge at a little over three thousand metres. On both sides the ground falls away into air. It is night and the notch is inside a cloud, and the cloud is moving through it fast enough to hurt the face.

There is almost nothing to see. A hand held at arm's length is a guess. The place arrives through the ears and skin: cold near six degrees, and a wind that never steadies. It comes up the western slope in slabs, hits the two rock shoulders, squeezes between them and accelerates. Each gust has a front that can be heard coming, a roar climbing the slope through the canopy below and growing until it lands. The whole notch leans east. A second of slack follows, and everything springs back and knocks against everything else.

The rock is young. A lava flow crossed this ridge a few centuries ago, and its surface is still rubble: clinker, fist-sized and head-sized, with edges that will open a palm. The two shoulders are the chilled walls of the flow, blocky and fractured in columns, their corners square. Nothing here has been rounded and there is no soil in the usual sense. A mat of roots, moss, and dead leaf lies over the clinker, a hand deep in most places and an arm deep in the hollows, with air beneath it. It gives underfoot like a mattress. Through the gaps it is possible to reach down past the roots into loose dry stone that has never been wet for long.

On this mat stands a forest three metres tall. The trees are old and stunted, with trunks as thick as a thigh and crowns planed flat by wind. Their leaves are small and leathery, and their branches are so interlocked that the canopy is a single lid. Under the lid every surface is occupied. Moss sleeves the trunks to twice their thickness, and filmy ferns hang from the moss. Orchids no bigger than a fingernail sit in the forks, liverworts plate the twigs, and beard lichen streams from anything that sticks out. Dwarf bamboo fills the spaces between the trunks so tightly that its canes squeak against one another, and tree ferns shoulder up wherever a branch has failed. To move through it is to be touched everywhere at once by cold, wet, yielding things.

Water comes only from the cloud. It has not rained in weeks, and no stream or pool or trickle runs anywhere on the ridge, because the lava swallows whatever reaches it. But the cloud is full of droplets, and the forest combs them out. Every leaf tip and lichen strand collects a bead, and when a gust shakes the canopy the beads come down together as a brief false rain. Then the dripping stops until the leaves reload. The moss holds the water like a sponge. Squeeze a handful and it runs down the wrist. A metre down, the stone is dry.

Overhead, heard before it is understood, a power line crosses the ridge. Its towers stand somewhere out along the shoulders, beyond the notch and out of reach. Only the span passes over, three bundled phases sagging through the gap twenty metres above the canopy, carrying a dam's output from one watershed to a smelter in the next. In the wet air the conductors fry. A steady crackle of corona lies under a hum at twice the mains frequency, low enough to feel in the teeth. The wind adds a thin whistle that slides in pitch with each gust. When the bundles start to gallop, the spacers clack and a long groan travels down the aluminium toward a tower that answers with one flat metallic note. The air under the line smells faintly of ozone, cut with torn leaf and wet stone.

The line is also the only light. When the cloud is thickest, the corona shows as a string of dim violet beads at the spacer clamps. They sway in arcs and light nothing but the fog immediately around them, three dotted curves hung in black. They vanish when the cloud thins.

Nothing in the notch moves except what the wind moves. No moths come to the violet glow and nothing calls. The mat does not rustle with anything small. All of the noise is weather, wood, stone, and current.

The notch is coming apart as the night goes on. Branches go every few minutes with a wet crack, and then comes the scrape of a limb with its load of moss dragging across the canopy until it snags or sails off the eastern lip. Near the north shoulder a tree has begun to rock. Its roots never entered the rock, only spread flat through the mat, and with each gust the mat lifts around it like a rug with a draft underneath. It lifts and settles for a long while. Then a gust holds a second longer than the others and the tree goes over slowly, taking a disc of mat four metres wide with it and standing the disc on edge. Bamboo, ferns, and moss still cling to it. The exposed clinker rattles and shifts, and some of it starts downhill, clinking like crockery. The smell of freshly broken root is sharp and almost sweet. The wind finds the new hole in the canopy at once and starts working on its edges.

The shoulders shed too. The columns are cracked through, roots have been in the cracks, and tonight the trees are levering at them. Now and then a block lets go with a hard clap and bounds through the forest. The crashing fades down the slope and does not so much end as drop below the wind.

Twice the cloud tears. For a few seconds there are stars, hard and many, and the three wires show as black lines ruled across them. Far down to the east, under a lower deck of cloud, lies a smear of orange where the valley's sodium lamps light the cloud from beneath. In that glimpse the notch has a shape. It shows the flat pale lid of the canopy heaving like a sea, the two square shoulders, the tilted root-disc and the dark hole beside it. Then the next slab of cloud arrives, and the violet beads come back on one by one.

By morning the notch will have the same forty paces and the same two walls, but the lid will have new holes and the mat new tears. A scatter of clean grey stone will lie where there was green. The cloud will keep delivering water and the moss will keep catching it. Fern spores will already be settling on the upturned disc, and the line will go on humming over all of it.
