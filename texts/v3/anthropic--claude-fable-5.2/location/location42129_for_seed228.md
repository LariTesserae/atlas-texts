You hear the room before you see it. In the last stretch of passage, a stooping tube worn as smooth as the inside of a shell, the sound arrives as pressure in the chest: a roar with a slower pulse inside it, like something very large breathing through water. Then the passage ends at a lip of rock and the chamber opens, and it is bright as noon.

It is perhaps sixty meters long, forty across, and thirty to the roof at its highest. The limestone is cream and grey, scalloped everywhere into shallow overlapping cups by water that has been doing the same thing here for a very long time. The floor is mostly pool. A cold river enters through a slot high in the north wall and falls in a white rope. A second source wells up from below, blood-warm, visible only as a doming of the surface and a shimmer where the two temperatures refuse each other. The air is bathhouse air. Condensation drips from the roof steadily enough to count as rain.

The pool never rests. The exit is a sump at the south end, a throat too narrow for what is fed to it. Every few minutes it chokes on its own trapped air and the whole pool climbs, a meter or two, fast, slapping up the walls. Then the sump clears with a boom you feel in your teeth, and the level drops and drags everything loose along with it. As the water rises it pushes air through the side passages, and the room sounds a low, wavering chord. As it drains, the chord comes back a different shape. Speech is useless here.

The stone itself is finished with changing. The walls have been cut to exactly the shape this violence requires and now simply hold it. The floor is pocked with potholes, each with one or two tenant stones grinding round and round. The gravel is less a deposit than a population: the same few thousand pebbles in permanent circulation, heaped into a bar on the east side this hour and the west side the next, each lap leaving them a little closer to marbles.

The light comes from a seam in the roof. It is not an opening, since there are hundreds of meters of rock overhead. It is a filament a hand wide and some twenty meters long, laid into a ceiling joint, and it gives off daylight. This is the full flat white of a clear midday, without flicker or warmth, and it does not change with whatever the hour is above. Under it the mist becomes almost solid. Rainbows form in the spray and are torn apart, then form again a little to the left. Everything in the room moves except the light and the walls.

There are two other made things. In the deepest part of the pool stands a ring, upright and half-submerged, three meters across, of a dull grey material that is not metal and has not worn. Water that passes through it comes out smooth. For about a body's length downstream there is a tongue of glassy, silent flow, and then the turbulence takes it back. Whether the ring is a gauge, a filter, or part of something long since removed, there is no telling. The limestone around it has gone down by a hand's width, and the ring now stands on a low pedestal of the rock it sheltered.

Along the east wall, above the highest reach of the surge, runs a row of seven studs, fist-sized and a stride apart. In a room where nothing is dry, they are dry. Spray bends around them. The air between each pair has a faint firmness, like a hand pushed into a strong draught. It may be a walkway whose deck was never matter.

Life is thin. On the high ledges directly beneath the seam there is a band of liverwort, a few maidenhair ferns, and an algal film that turns the cream stone green-gold. It stops at the surge line as sharply as paint. A few small flies work the ferns, descended from whatever came down with the builders. Pale amphipods cling in the potholes. Now and then the cold river delivers a white, eyeless fish, and it finds its way to the tongue of calm behind the ring. Three or four of them may be holding station there, the only still animals in the room. They wait until the next surge clears them out.

The ferns have never had a night. They grow slowly anyway, leaning in the wind the water makes.
