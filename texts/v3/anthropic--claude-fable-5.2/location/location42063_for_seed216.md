There is one kind of tree here and it is the ground. A root of it, thick as a longhouse and warm as a flank, leaves a knot of other roots and crosses forty miles of dim air to the next knot. It was led there by hand. Its upper curve still keeps the flattened shape of the guide it was started in, a split and hollowed trunk laid like a trough, though the guide rotted off before the root was thicker than a wrist. Every few paces the bark rises in a ring, the way a finger grows around a band. Under each ring is a lashing of bark cord, rolled on someone's thigh, long since swallowed.

Spans like this one go off at every angle, up and down as well as across, each furred with moss and fern and tufts of dark oval leaf. They meet in knots where six or sixty roots were pressed together and bound until the bark gave up and they became one wood. Trunks stand out of the knots and climb to the lid, a roof of the same leaf some miles overhead, shingled so thickly that noon comes through as a brown-green dusk. Underneath lies a floor of cloud that has never opened. The lowest spans sag into it and come back out dripping.

No root has been followed down to rock. The trunks stand on knots, the knots hang from spans, and the spans pull on other knots. The weight goes round. It can be felt going: every few hours a span draws tight, the moss along it lifts a little, and then it slackens as the load passes down the grain toward somewhere else.

At this height the air should be thin and cold. It is neither. The wood gives off its own heat, and within a few arm-lengths of the bark the air is thick, damp, and smells of fig sap.

The wood tells things what to do. Down is toward the nearest root, so the animals walk the top, the flanks, and the underside without distinction. From a neighboring span they are a file of small backs moving along the bottom of a line. No rain crosses the open. The wood's warm breath condenses in a sleeve of mist around each span and rains inward from all sides, softly, most of the time. At a big knot the sleeves merge into real cloud, with warm squalls that circle the knot for days. Once the water is on the bark it runs along the grain toward the growing tip, uphill if the tip is uphill, in channels worn glassy by its passing.

Sound goes into the wood as well. A hoof set down makes almost nothing in the air. An ear against the bark can hear a herd a day's walk off as a faint ticking, and under that the long creak of the load going round. Calls do not carry across the gaps, so the animals have mostly stopped making them. They drum, or they show a pale throat or the underside of a wing.

Evening obeys the grain too. The light does not fail everywhere at once. Along each span a darkness some miles long travels tipward at about a walking pace, and from a distance it shows as a stretch where the moss holds no shine. Some animals keep inside it their whole lives, and others graze just ahead of it.

A span is as crowded as a hedge. Frogs sit in the old guides where water pools on its way to the tip, and their spawn drifts slowly uphill. Moths drink from the sleeve. Narrow-hoofed walkers, goat-sized and mottled like bark, go in single file along a groove their kind has worn knee-deep into the living wood. The groove has healed and been worn again so many times that its lips are rolled like scar. A long, low animal lies flat to the grain with moss rooted in its back and takes the last walker in the file without the first one knowing. The fliers never leave the sleeve. Their straight line is a spiral round the span, and they are careful at speed.

They are careful because past the sleeve nothing has been told anything. A drop shaken off a frond too hard coasts out through the mist and stops. A leaf stops, and a moth stops with its wings open. Every span is wrapped, just beyond the reach of its weather, in a thin shell of stopped things. Most of them are drops, millions of them, each holding a grain of the brown light, so that a far span shows as a faint beaded outline, and that beading is the brightest thing in the country.

Farther out the stopped things are fewer and larger. A walker that slipped at a run hangs with its legs half folded. Nothing out there has told it to fall, or to rot. Between one span and the next there are forty miles of this, a few feathers and one animal, and then nothing.

The work goes on. Fresh guides appear at the tips, split with a wedge, hollowed with a stone adze whose bite marks are still pale, and lashed tip to tail with cord that is still green. The twist of the cord is uneven in the way a tired hand's twist is uneven. Tools are found wedged in forks: the adze, a bone awl, a mallet of root-wood with the grip worn dark. Nothing here is made of metal or has ever been.

No one is seen. The files do stop sometimes, a mile of animals all at once, and every head turns the same way and holds there for about as long as it takes to tie a lashing. Then they walk on, and behind them there is a new band on the root.

Every guide is aimed at something hanging in the open. A root takes a human lifetime or several to get there. When it arrives and its sleeve closes over the stopped thing, the thing resumes. The drop runs. The leaf settles onto the bark. The walker gets its legs under it, stands, and joins a file of animals many generations its junior, and they let it in.

What cannot get up is grown over. Where old knots have opened with heart-rot, each has something at its center: a nest, an adze head, a coil of cord gone to dust in the shape of a coil.

The farthest guide on the newest span points at a thing the size of a crouched person, one arm out. It has been aimed there since before the span behind it existed. The tip in the trough is pale and wet, and there is a frog on it. Water arrives uphill, beads at the end, and drops off. Each drop travels a hand's width past the mist and stops, so a dotted line of them leads out from the tip into the dark, the oldest farthest out. The root grows along the line, and each drop it touches runs.
