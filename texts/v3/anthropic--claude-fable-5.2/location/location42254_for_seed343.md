The sound arrives before the place does. On the last pitch of the escarpment, still inside the cloud, there is a pressure in the air that seems at first to be altitude, the blood in the ears. Then it resolves into a pulse, and then into millions of separate pulses that never quite fall into step. At the rim it pours over the edge like water over a weir.

Then the cloud is below, and there is nothing to see.

The plateau is what was left when a mountain range finished wearing away. It is a table of pale quartzite three days' walk across, so high that the country around it is only a floor of cloud-tops and the sky at the zenith is a darker blue than it should be. The rock has no peaks left to lose, no scree, no gullies. Over a thousand paces it rises or falls by less than a hand. Every part of it, except a few whalebacks of bare stone rounded like knuckles, is covered by a single plant.

The few people who come here call it the hide. It is a cushion plant, with resinous rosettes no bigger than a fingernail packed so tightly that the surface is hard, dry and slightly springy, like the flank of a large animal. It grows less than a grain's width in a year, and it is four metres thick, ten in the swales. Only the top hand's depth is alive. Below that is its own past, tan stems as fine as straw, and over the ages these have slumped and been tunnelled into galleries, chimneys and chambers the size of a fist or a room. The plateau is one continuous green roof over a hollow, and nothing on it stands higher than the ankle. A person is the tallest thing for a day in any direction.

The hide is also warm. At this height there should be ice, but the dead interior ferments slowly, like the middle of a hay pile, and the heat comes up through the soles. Snow that falls is gone within the hour. On cold mornings the whole plateau smokes from its pores, thousands of thin steam-threads standing straight up in the still air until the sun cuts them off.

The pores are where the noise comes out. Everything that lives here lives inside. Thumb-sized frogs the colour of the dead stems sit in the damp galleries, each calling a single knocked note, and the galleries work as pipes. There are enough of them that the note is felt in the sternum. A stridulating beetle lays a higher band over the frogs once the day warms, a dry sizzle with no gaps in it. Tailless grey animals the size of two fists, the whistlers, keep watch at the pore-mouths, and their shriek passes from one to the next, so that a walker's presence is announced a half-day ahead. The din is hard to credit against the emptiness. The surface is blank green to the horizon, the sky is bare, and the ground is shouting. There are no echoes, since there is nothing for sound to strike, so it simply goes up.

Speech is useless here. So is clapping, or calling a name.

Quiet, when it comes, is a warning. Falcons work the updraft along the rim and now and then slide inward over the table. Under each one the whistlers drop and the frogs nearest the pores stop, and a patch of quiet a few hundred paces wide travels across the plateau beneath the bird. A hawk can be tracked with the eyes shut by the hole it makes. Inside the galleries a long-bodied hunter, narrow as a wrist, makes smaller silences that wander and go out. It is almost never seen, though its scat lies on the bare stone, full of small bones.

Water follows the same rule as the animals and goes inside. Before dawn the cloud climbs the escarpment and slides across the table at knee height. The rosettes comb it, and it runs down the stems into the dark. By mid-morning the surface is dry enough to sit on. Rain comes on some afternoons, briefly, and each drop sounds on the hide as on a drumskin. Open water stands only where the rock shoulders up and makes a basin. There are a dozen such eyes on the whole plateau, each a few paces across and black with tannin, with a ring of brighter green around it and a margin trampled by small feet. Paths that the eye cannot find on the dry hide show up near the pools as a faint polish.

Twice a day the emptiness breaks. A dark burrow-nesting bird, short-winged and heavy, breeds in the upper galleries in numbers nobody has tried to count. One of each pair spends the day far out beyond the rim, feeding in the cloud forests below. At dusk they come back, and their mates leave. For the time it takes to boil water, which is not long up here, the air from ankle to as high as the eye can follow is solid with wings. The roar of them is one of the few things that drowns out the frogs. The falcons kill until they are too full to fly well. Then the sky is empty again and the first stars come out hard and unblinking. After a night of rain something else comes up, a pale winged insect that rises from the pores in columns like the morning steam. It mates in the air and falls, and by noon the hide is dusted with wings and the whistlers are fat and slow.

The human marks are few and far apart. On each whaleback stands a cairn of stacked quartzite, set so that the next one is just visible. Beside some of them is a low ring of stones roofed with stitched pelts, big enough for four people lying down. The shelters are built on bare rock, never on the hide, because the hide does not forgive. In the oldest-used parts of the plateau there are rectangles of slightly paler green, an arm long, where blocks were once cut for fuel. The resin burns hot and black. The newest of these scars is older than any story told about it, and the skin has closed over it by perhaps a finger's width. Cutting is rationed now to one block a season for each hearth, taken from a swale edge with a cold-hammered copper blade, and the wound is packed with wet crumbled stem.

The people climb in the warm months for resin, for whistler pelts and for the fat young birds. They take them with hair snares set at the pores and with hooked sticks of bone, since there is no wood they have not carried up themselves. They wear plugs of resin and wool in their ears and talk with their hands. Across distance they talk with light, using a copper disc rubbed bright that flashes from one cairn to the next. In cloud they lift a boulder and drop it on the rock, and the blow carries through the stone under the hide and is felt in the feet a long way off. On the hide they walk in soft soles or none.

At night the fires are small because the fuel is dear. From one whaleback another can sometimes be seen, a single orange point a day's walk away across a plain that is black, warm and steaming a little. The frogs have the whole night to themselves, and the knocking comes up through the stone into anyone lying on it.
