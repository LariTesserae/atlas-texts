Three meters under a lava field younger than most trees, there is a bubble in the rock. It was a gas pocket when the flow was liquid, and when the flow stopped, the pocket stayed. It is a room shaped roughly like a loaf, four paces long, and low enough at one end that the ceiling comes down to meet the floor. Nothing wider than a finger connects it to the surface. A crack in the roof lets water in, and a crack in the floor lets heat in.

The rock is new and has kept its edges. The walls are lined with a rind of black glass, formed where the trapped gas held the surface molten a little longer than the stone behind it. The glass is still glossy, and in places it has chipped into curved flakes sharp enough to cut. From the ceiling hang the last drips of the lava, each a centimeter or two long, pointed, frozen partway through falling. The only signs of age are thin ones: a white frost of silica starting along the seams, a yellow crust of sulfur at the lip of the floor crack, and a few gypsum needles finer than hair.

It is very hot. The floor crack breathes air close to boiling. The far end of the ceiling is cooler, about the temperature of a bath too hot to stay in. Everything in the room sits somewhere on the slope between those two.

It is also as bright as open ground at noon. The light comes from the walls themselves. Over the glass lies a film thinner than the skin of a soap bubble, with no color of its own. It arrived as dust in the rain, as it does everywhere, and it settles wherever heat is moving through a surface. There it stays, spreading sideways and turning part of that heat into plain white light. No one placed it here, and it is not alive in any way the slime at the pool's edge is alive. It is brightest near the floor crack and thinner toward the cool end, though even there the glass shines like a lamp.

Because every surface gives light, nothing in the room casts a shadow. The lava teeth look flat, like drawings of themselves. Depth shows only in the glints off the glass and in the water.

The roof crack weeps. Rain from the surface takes a day or two to work down through the flow and arrives already warm. It beads along the ceiling, gathers at the tip of each lava tooth, and drops. The film cools the rock it sits on slightly, since it carries heat away as light, so vapor condenses on it first. The brightest stretches of wall are therefore the wettest, running with a sheet of water that carries the light along inside it.

All of this collects in a pool at the low part of the floor. It is the size of a tabletop, a hand deep, and perfectly clear. The glass bottom shines up through it. The edge nearest the floor crack simmers with small bubbles, and the far edge is merely hot.

About every twenty minutes the pool rises far enough that a tongue of it reaches the floor crack and spills in. There is a faint sizzle, and then the crack exhales. Steam rolls across the ceiling and fills the room from the top down. The light, which had no direction to begin with, becomes a solid whiteness, and for a minute or two there is nothing else in the room.

Then the fog settles onto the walls. The ceiling rains briefly, a few dozen drops falling almost together, and the air clears. The pool, lower by a finger's width, starts filling again.

In wet weeks the cycle shortens. After a long dry spell it stops altogether. The pool shrinks to a ring of salt, and the room is left with dry heat and steady light until the rain comes back.

At the pool's cooler margin there is a stain, a band no wider than a thumb, orange on the side toward the heat and dull green on the side away from it. It is slick and has no height. It lives on the light from the walls and is the only thing here that grows.

Small dark flies of the kind found at hot springs come down the roof crack, following the damp. There are rarely more than three or four. They walk the edge of the stain, feeding, and lay eggs in the green part, where the larvae show as pale commas in the slime. When the fog comes they stop where they are and wait it out, beaded with water.

One spider lives here, grey and flat-bodied, the size of a lentil. It keeps to a chip in the glass above the cool end of the pool. It spins no web, because a web would be drenched every twenty minutes. It hunts by rushing, and it catches a fly perhaps every few days.

The rest of the room is empty. Most of the floor is bare bright glass under a film of water.

It is quiet. Drops strike the pool in two or three different notes, depending on where they land. Before each exhalation comes the sizzle, then a hiss like breath let out through teeth. The fog muffles everything, and after it comes the short patter of the ceiling's rain.

Now and then there is a single tick in between. The glass takes up heat unevenly, and a flake lets go of the wall and clicks onto the floor. It leaves a dark patch of bare rock, hot and dry, the only dark thing in the room. Over the following days the film closes back over the scar from its edges. The advancing margin shows as a slightly brighter line until the patch is gone and that part of the wall is wet again.

The flakes collect along the base of the walls. Cut off from the heat that fed it, the film on a fallen flake soon stops giving light. A flake that lands near the floor crack keeps shining. One that lands at the cool end goes dark within the hour and lies there, black, with water pooling around it.
