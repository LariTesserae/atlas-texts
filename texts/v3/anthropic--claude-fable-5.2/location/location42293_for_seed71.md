The mountains that were here are finished. What remains is their floor, a tableland of banded gneiss lifted some four thousand metres and planed so level that water set down on it has no way to choose a direction. It is wide enough to curve. From any point on it the rim lies below the horizon on every side, and the weather is made on the premises. Fog breeds in the hollows, travels a few hundred kilometres across the top, and settles again without ever having come from a sea.

The cold is steady, a few degrees either side of freezing all year, and the air is thin enough that a breath feels half-poured. The rock is grey with pink feldspar seams, rounded into whalebacks no higher than a doorway and pocked with basins that each hold a hand's depth of brown water. Between the whalebacks lies black peat. Wherever there is lee, the green on it is immoderate:

- **Cushion plants:** domes the size of carts, tight as upholstery and hard enough to stand on.
- **Moss:** a dozen textures of it, heaped knee-deep along every seep.
- **Dwarf willow:** pressed flat against the ground like dropped netting.
- **Lichen:** rust, sulphur and a pale mint, drawn in maps over anything that has held still.

On the open ground stand tall rosette plants, each a woolly trunk with a head of silver leaves. They are about a body's height and a body's width, and they stand in loose groups like people waiting. Nothing eats any of this. No flies hang over the pools, and the peat holds no tracks. The sky has nothing in it but cloud. Pollen goes by wind, or travels wet in the fog.

The fog comes up out of the ground twice a day, at dawn and again in mid-afternoon. It fills the plateau to roughly the height of a roof and then moves. It is most of the water there is, apart from an occasional fine rain that hardly falls at all, and a city has been built to meet it.

The city is mainly fences. Chest-high pylons of dry-laid stone are strung with nets of twisted grass cord. The fog combs through, beads, and runs down into fired-clay troughs. The troughs feed cisterns lined with puddled clay and lidded with slate. The cords are pinned with cold-hammered copper, long since gone green and weeping green down the stone. The fence lines run dead straight for distances that stop making sense as fences.

Between them, causeways of flat slabs cross the peat. The houses are round, with double stone walls packed with turf and turf roofs a metre thick, still growing. Each has a hearth, a peat stack, a cistern and a threshold slab. Tools lie where they were set down: hammerstones, wooden wedges, a mallet of willow root, a length of cord half twisted.

There are enough houses for many thousands of people, with drying racks, cistern yards and paved squares to match. They are spread across an area a nation would fit inside. From any one door, two others might be visible as low bumps. The squares are the hardest thing to look at. Each is a kerbed circle fifty paces across with a single standing stone at its centre, wind-swept, and nothing else within a morning's walk.

The city works because of a rule the plateau keeps. Every door is one day from every other door. The walk to the neighbouring house takes from dawn to dusk. The walk to a house on the far rim, a thousand kilometres off, also takes from dawn to dusk, and the legs are no more tired. The causeway does not hurry and the ground goes by at walking pace, yet the arithmetic comes out the same. In the sense that matters to a town, this one is compact. It has only been allowed to keep all its emptiness.

Frost does the surveying. Each night's freeze and each day's thaw shoulder the loose stones outward into rings and polygons, the way cold ground does anywhere. Here the rings are house-sized, and the houses stand on them. Some rings are one course high and some are three. No line can be found where the frost's sorting stops and laid wall begins. The ground proposes a foundation and something takes it up, though the taking up is never seen. A wall passed at one course on the way out stands at four on the way back, and its top stones are lichened exactly as deeply as its bottom ones.

This is the other rule, and it goes further down than the first. The plateau reached the end of wearing long ago, and the wearing did not stop. It had nowhere left to go but ahead. New work here is never new:

- **Nets:** a freshly knotted one is already frayed at the pins.
- **Thresholds:** in a house not yet roofed, the slab is dished by feet.
- **Causeways:** the slabs carry two worn tracks a shoulder's width apart, across stretches where the peat on either side has never taken a print.
- **Peat:** a brick cut from the bank has rounded corners before it reaches the stack.

Sound gets the same treatment. Frost splitting a boulder at dawn ought to crack. It arrives as a soft knock, like a knuckle on a full cistern, with the edge already rubbed off. A spoken word would lose its consonants within arm's length. What is left is small and continuous: water dropping from net to trough along thousands of kilometres of fence, wind drawing one low tone from the cords, and slate lids ticking as they warm. The quiet is made of processes.

The hearths are warm. Peat stacks go down and are built up again. The nets are mended, and the knots are in different hands, some neat and some impatient. No one is ever in view. No figure shows even at the distance where the rosette plants get mistaken for figures and then turn out to be plants. The best account is that the spacing that governs the doors governs whoever uses them. Each is a day from each, in every direction, including the direction of a turned head. The city is fully occupied, and no two presences in it have ever shared a view.

None of this happens in strange light. The sun is a white coin behind high cloud. Shadows are soft and colours are true. Afternoon looks like afternoon. The light is the one thing here that behaves, and it leaves nothing to blame.

Further in, toward a middle that the one-day rule makes impossible to locate, the proposals outrun the things proposed. Fence lines are staked out across hollows where fog has not yet begun to rise. Basins are dished into the gneiss with no water in them and no stain of any. Against certain whalebacks lie aprons of scree, every piece already rounded, piled as if at the foot of a cliff, with only sky above them. Whether those heights have gone or have yet to come, the rock shows the same wear from either side.

In the last square the kerb is unfinished. A third of the circle is still loose stone, which the frost is nudging into line. The standing stone at its centre is up, though, and in the paving around it a shallow groove runs in a clean arc where the tip of its shadow passes each day. The shadow has worn it there, and the groove is deepest at mid-afternoon. When the fog comes up through the joints of the slabs, the groove fills first.
