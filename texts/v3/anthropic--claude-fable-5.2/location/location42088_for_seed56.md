Under some fifteen metres of glacier ice, down where the ice meets the ground, a plant is making a room.

The note comes first. It is a single pitch, a little below the middle of a speaking voice, held without breath or waver. It is loud enough that the water on every surface stands in fine fixed ripples, and a body feels it in the sternum before the ears have sorted it out. A hand's width into the wall it is gone. On top of the glacier there is nothing to hear. The lining of the room returns the whole sound to the room.

The room is thirty-odd paces long and a third as wide, shaped like an overturned hull. It is tall enough at the old end for a mature tree and tapers at the new end to a crawl. Every surface is plant. The floor is a black-green mat, dense as wet felt and deeper than a knee, laid over glacial till. Ribs rise out of the mat, horn-hard and glossy, and arch to meet overhead. Between the ribs a clear membrane, also plant and one cell thick, lies flat against the ice. Through it the glacier shows blue and bubbled, and it is moving. The ice slides over the vault a few centimetres a year, and the ribs all lean a few degrees downhill.

Low between the ribs, and again along the crown, the plant grows in pages. Thousands of thin dark leaves are stacked face to face, with gaps too narrow to admit a hair. The floor gills are warm, which here means a degree above freezing. They sit where the roots bring up the small heat of the ground. Air shuttling between the pages takes that warmth and gives it back a fraction late, and the lateness is the note. The plant is an engine whose one moving part is the air in the room. The crown gills run the same trick backwards. Sound goes in and cold comes out along one edge, and that keeps the roof frozen. Frost grows on their upper margins in ferns.

That much could be drawn on paper. Other things could not. The gradient is far too slight to yield so much sound. None of the sound escapes. And at the far end the plant gathers the note to a point.

There the mat thins to a pale leading edge, nearly white, pressed against raw ice like a palm on a window. Between tissue and ice is a film of water, and the film boils cold. Bubbles open and collapse in it by the thousand. Each collapse knocks loose a grain of ice and gives off a spark of blue light, so the whole front glimmers. A hiss like frying rides above the note.

Meltwater, milky with rock flour, runs back down a groove in the mat to a pool. Nothing at this end is finished. The ribs are soft green stubs and the membrane has gaps, through which the roof weeps steadily. Till shows through the floor in patches of grey, angular stones with fresh faces that have never been rained on.

By day a blue-grey light comes down through the ice, enough to tell rib from gap. At night there is only the glimmer of the front and its double in the pool.

The floor is striped. Pale ridges cross the mat a stride apart, with dark bare lanes between, and the ridges are animals. The note stands in fixed places, in bands where the air rushes back and forth and bands where it holds still. Everything light enough to be pushed ends up in the still bands. Springtails lie there in grey drifts deep enough to scoop, among wingless midges, shed skins, and beads of condensate rolled into lines.

Larger animals work the loud lanes. Flat-shelled grazers the size of a thumbnail crawl the gills and rasp off the frost, which keeps the pages clear. Pale hunters with a hand's span of leg stand with their feet in two neighbouring ridges, their bodies slung over the lane, and pick from either side.

Black ice worms thread the membrane overhead like stitching, passing between the plant and the glacier. The note has drawn the surface of the pool into a lattice of standing wavelets. Under each still point hangs a translucent amphipod, its gut a grey thread of rock flour.

Nothing here can call across the note, so the animals call beside it. The grazers stridulate a hair flat of the room. One alone is inaudible. A few hundred together set the tone throbbing, once a second and then faster, until the room pulses like a slow heart. While it lasts the ridges blur at their edges, and the hunters fold their legs and wait.

Along the walls are blisters. These are closed chambers the size of a skull or a barrel, each sealed around its own higher note, which can be heard only with an ear against the membrane. They are rooms that have not yet joined the room.

The front gains about a forearm's length a year. As the room lengthens the note sinks, a semitone every four years or so. The stripes spread with it, and the animals are carried apart without ever having moved.

Once in several years a blister opens into the main vault, and both notes fail. For some minutes the room is silent. The drifts of springtails slump and spread across the lanes, and the pool goes flat. Drips from the roof fall straight. Then a lower pitch takes hold, and the stripes assemble in new places.

At the old end the ribs converge on a hard black boss no bigger than a walnut, where the plant began. It has no casing, seam or mark. Whatever came down the meltwater to this spot came as a grain.

At the new end the roof is still weeping. Each drop is shoved sideways as it falls, and they land in a line.
