The mountain is too new to have rubble. Its upper slopes are still the shapes they cooled in: fins of black basalt with edges like broken bottle, rinds of glass curled back from the rock beneath, bubbles frozen open with rims that would cut. Nothing has been rounded. It rose fast and a long way. The weather is all below, a pale floor of cloud with the curve of the world showing in it, and near the top the sky is almost black at midday and the air too thin to carry a sound more than a few paces. Far down there is a collar of ice. Above the ice the rock is bare and warm, and it gets warmer as it climbs, which is the wrong way round.

The summit is a blister. Under a crust about as thick as a forearm there is a hollow, and the only way in is a seam a hand's width wide, closed by something that looks like a slightly darker piece of air. It holds the pressure in. Daylight that crosses it arrives grey and reduced, so that noon inside is one dull stripe crawling over the floor, no brighter than moonlight.

The hollow is perhaps fifteen paces long and eight wide, low at the edges, just high enough in the middle to stand. Its walls are fresh glass, scalloped where they fractured. Along one wall runs a fissure two fingers wide, and from far down in it comes the color of an ember under ash. That is most of the light, and all of the heat. The air is kiln air. The floor is hotter than the air, dry black glass that would take the skin off a palm.

The lines are the exception. The floor is ruled with blocks of unequal size, a few diagonals and one long curve, in lines thinner than a fingernail, and the lines are wet. Each is a thread of water lying on glass that should have boiled it off, cool, with clean edges. Between them the floor is bone dry. It takes a while to accept that these are streets. What settles it is the intersections. At each one sits a point of pale light the color of the sky just before it turns blue, and the light has an edge. It reaches a finger's width and stops, as if cut out. It illuminates nothing.

Above the streets hangs the rain. The ceiling, with its outer face turned to the black sky, is the one cold surface in the chamber, and the fissure's breath is mostly steam, so the ceiling sweats. Drops gather on its teeth and fall, then stop at about knee height. Thousands of them hang in a single level sheet, not touching, trembling slightly, each holding the fissure upside down inside it as a red thread. This is the town's reservoir and also its sky. When a street runs thin, the drop above it is let go and descends at the speed of something sinking through oil, lands without a splash, and lengthens along the line. Nowhere in the chamber does water fall at its own speed, and nowhere does it touch bare floor.

There are nineteen buildings on a floor ruled for thousands. None is taller than a forearm and most are smaller than a fist. There are slabs, needles, a wedge, and one ring standing on edge. They are a matte grey with no grain, their edges true. They have no doors or windows. One has been split by a crack in the floor and is the same grey all the way through. Each stands square in its plot with a street running to it, and each has a shadow on the side away from the fissure, a small fan of damp where it blocks the heat. The ember-light is too weak to cast shadows of its own.

Nothing lives here. There is no film on the water and no stain where it has been. The nearest thing to growth is at the fissure's lip, where sulfur comes out of the vapor in yellow needles and a white crust of sublimate creeps across the glass. The town clears this from its streets. In one block, with wet lines on all four sides, the needles have been left alone. They stand thick as a brush, a hand high, their tips all at one level. It is hard not to call it a park.

Roughly once an hour, on an interval that wanders, the mountain exhales. The glow in the fissure climbs from ember to orange. There is a hiss, the loudest sound the chamber has, and it is not loud. A front of heat crosses the floor and its progress is visible: the nearest streets go to steam and vanish block by block, the lights go out in order as the dryness reaches them, and the hung rain lifts a finger's height and shivers. For some minutes there is no town, only scratches on a black floor and nineteen grey objects.

Then the glow sinks. The ceiling ticks as it gives its heat to the sky. The drops are let down, hundreds at once, slowly, and the lines fill from the far end back toward the fissure. As the water runs, each street gives off a note like a wet finger on a glass rim, at the limit of what the thin air will carry. Together they make a chord faint enough that it might be imagined. The lights return, in a different order.

There is no machinery in any of this, and no moving part. The only power is the difference between the fissure and the sky, a forearm of crust apart. Either the hollow was placed where the mountain's heat comes nearest to that cold, or the mountain was pushed up until the two met. It is still being pushed. The floor tilts by hairs and new cracks open. When a crack crosses a street, by the next exhalation the street crosses the crack as a thread of water spanning the gap, resting on nothing and running level.

The ceiling is also shedding. Young glass under strain spalls. A flake lets go and drops through the rain, the drops drifting aside for it, and for one cycle a jagged black splinter lies on the floor, the only irregular thing in the town. After the next exhalation a grey needle stands where it lay, plumb and true, with a wet line arriving at its base.

All nineteen came this way. Nothing in the town was built. Each piece fell, was corrected, and was given an address. The count is low only because the mountain is young. At the far end of the chamber the ruling continues dry, faint as a scratch, for hundreds of plots across the rest of the floor and a short way up the end wall.

One of those plots is already watered. It has four wet sides and a light at its corner, and nothing stands on it. Above it, a blade of ceiling glass as long as a hand shows a pale hairline across its root, and the drops beneath have moved apart.
