Just under the ridge crest, where the cloud forest leans into a wind it has leaned into for longer than the trees have had their shapes, the limestone opens into a pocket. It is about twenty paces long and twelve wide, tall enough in the middle for a person to stand with one hand raised. The roof is the underside of the forest: old trunks fallen across the gap, roots woven through them, and a metre of peat and moss laid on top. The way in is a slot between two buttress roots, taken sideways and downhill.

Grey daylight follows you in for about an arm's length. Past that, some rotten wood in the roof gives off a green so faint it disappears when looked at directly. Eyes do not adjust here, because there is nothing to adjust to. The cold is the cold of the mountain's interior, the same in every season.

Every wall carries an unbroken film of water from roof to floor. Every root-tip holds a drop. The floor is fluted stone, the flutes worn as deep as they will ever go and every edge long since rounded. They lead to a shallow pool at the low end, rimmed with a smooth lip of flowstone. The pool overflows by a thread into a crack, and its level does not move.

What grows here grows without needing to see. Filmy ferns one cell thick hang in overlapping sheets, and liverworts scale the rock like wet lizard skin. Moss cushions swell as large as sleeping dogs. Roots come down in pale, haired curtains, and shelf fungi step up the fallen trunks. A few white shoots, sprung from seeds that dropped through the roof, have grown long and thin toward the slot.

And on all of it, in all of it, animals. Moths cover the driest wall, wings shingled like bark, thousands deep. Harvestmen hang in mats with their legs interlaced, and a mat shivers as one body if breathed on. A lungless salamander sits in every flute of the rock, head out, skin shining. Glass-shelled snails ride the ferns. Cave crickets cling to the roof with their antennae trailing down among the roots. Bats pack the root-hollows shoulder to shoulder like small furred fruit. Thumbnail frogs line the pool's lip in a row, and under them the water holds pale amphipods, flatworms, and gilled salamander larvae hanging still. A dozen wrens roost in one root pocket, balled together, tails outward. A weasel sleeps in the moss within reach of them.

Nothing eats here. That is the one strange thing about the place, and everything else follows from it. The frogs do not take the crickets. The weasel does not wake for the wrens. Nothing calls, either. For so many bodies the room is nearly silent: one drip into the pool, evenly spaced, and under it a hush of breathing and small leg movements, like cloth being folded somewhere. The ridge wind is felt at the slot as pressure, and it does not come inside.

People from lower down know the place. On a ledge outside the slot lie a chert blade, a horn scoop, and a bone needle, set down because the rule is to enter with empty hands. From a root just inside hangs a knotted cord, one knot for each cluster of bats. It has been retied in fresh fibre whenever the old one frayed. No knot has ever been added or taken away.

There is one unoccupied place on the floor: two shallow hollows in the stone just inside the entrance, a stride apart, worn to the shape of feet. A visitor stands in them, counts by touch along the cord, and backs out the way they came.
