Behind the tipping bank of the works, where the narrow-gauge track runs out over the river flats and simply stops, there is a hollow in the slag about the size of a kitchen. Two tongues of molten waste overlapped and left a pocket between them when they cooled. You would not find it unless you walked the rail to its bent end and looked down.

The walls are new. The slag is bottle-green glass in some places and blue-grey stone in others, and all of it is honeycombed with cells the escaping gas left, from pea-sized to fist-sized. Nothing has been rounded yet. Every edge would open a palm. The only weathering is a first orange bleed of iron under the drip line and one channel a finger wide where condensation has found a way down.

The heap is still cooling from the inside, years on, and the walls are warm like bread an hour out of the oven. At dawn the river air slides in cool and wet and beads on every surface. At the bottom lies a pond no bigger than a door laid flat. It is brackish and blood-warm, and it rises and falls a hand's width with the tide, an hour late, through the heap's porous base.

Little grows here. There is a green-black skin of algae at the waterline, liverwort where the drip lands, moss in cushions the size of coins, and one fig seedling with four leaves rooted in a crack.

Everything else is occupied. Each cell in the lower walls holds one banded snail, its shell thick with slag lime. Above the snails are woodlice, one to a cell. Firebrats keep to the hottest band beside the vent. Moths cover the shaded wall with their wings overlapped like slates. A chain hangs from the rail-end, left behind by a tipping car, and mud wasps have capped it link by link. In the pond, thumb-length fish lie parallel as if tinned, all with their heads to the warm seep. They may be descended from something emptied out of a tank. Three toads sit in three cups of slag that fit them exactly.

The hollow is full, and it holds exactly one empty place.

Nothing here can move except into that place, and whatever moves leaves its own place empty behind it. So the vacancy travels. You can watch it go as a small adjustment passing across the snail wall, up through the woodlice, along the moths. Whatever is crossing the gap is briefly exposed, and the toads know it. They do not hunt animals. They face wherever the emptiness is about to arrive. A wagtail that works the rim follows it too, head cocked.

When a toad takes a snail, another of the same size is already coming down the finger-wide channel before the toad has swallowed. Around the lip there is always a loose fringe of beetles, spiders, and sometimes a small crab up from the flats, all facing inward and waiting.

Sometimes the young slag cracks with a tick and opens a new cell. For a moment the hollow has two absences, and something from the fringe comes over the edge in a hurry. Put your hand in and a hand's worth of woodlice spills quietly over the far lip. Take it out and they come back.

The tide works the same way. As the pond rises, the lowest row climbs, each row above it shifts up, and the top row is pushed out onto the rim. As it falls, they all return.

Every few hours a car tips on the far side of the heap. Here the pour is felt as a thud more than heard. Twenty minutes later the warmth comes through, the vent breathes, and the gap quickens on its rounds. When rain falls on the upper heap it hisses, and the hollow fills with white steam and then clears.

It is quiet otherwise. Overhead a cable strung from a leaning post to the pump house hums on its porcelain insulators. The drip falls, and if you lean close the woodlice make a dry whisper. One cricket sings. When it stops another starts, and there are never two.

Someone has been here with chalk. A flat plate of slag carries a tally, a total, and the same total written again below it, and again, in different stages of fading.

Toward evening the gap comes down the wall to the waterline, and a single fish turns.
