**The Hollow at the Bleed Valve**

The hollow is a collapse pit in a lava flow maybe forty years old, about the size of a large room, where the roof of a shallow tube fell in and left a floor of tilted slabs. The flow sits low, not far above the sea, on a plain of black rock that hasn't yet learned to be soil. From the surface you could walk within twenty paces of the pit and miss it, except for the column of steam standing over it and the sound.

The sound comes first and stays. At the center of the pit, on a square concrete pad gone orange at the edges, stands a wellhead: a stack of flanges and a master valve with its wheel removed, a stub of ten-inch pipe going nowhere. One small side valve, the bleed, was left cracked open or has failed that way. Through that thumbnail of an opening, the ground lets out its pressure in a white, ripping roar, steady as a held note and as loud as a jet idling on a runway. It has no rhythm or pulse. The walls of the pit, glassy and close, throw it back on itself until the air seems made of it. After some minutes it stops being a noise and becomes a condition, like the heat.

And it is hot. The slabs underfoot are warm through boot soles. The air sits somewhere above body temperature and is saturated. The steam jet shoots sideways a few meters, loses its nerve, and blooms into cloud that rolls against the far wall, where it condenses and runs down in sheets. That wall weeps constantly. The water gathers in the low corner as a pool a hand deep, milky blue-grey with dissolved silica and too warm to keep a hand in for long. It drains somewhere through the rubble, since the level never changes.

The rock is young and shows it. Edges are still sharp enough to cut, and the broken tube roof shows its cross-section cleanly: ropy skin on top, a layer of stretched bubbles, drip-spines of lava on the old ceiling, some still tipped with a bronze glaze. Nothing has been rounded. The only softening is chemical, where the condensate runs. There a pale crust is forming, a rind of silica and sulfur salts, white going to yellow going to a rusty green nearest the wellhead where iron joins in. It grows perhaps a millimeter a year. The bolts on the lower flange are already half-buried in it, like something being slowly iced.

A few other human marks remain:

- a length of chain-link fence, fallen flat and fusing into the crust
- a steel sign on a post, its paint steamed off, leaving a blank plate with four rivets
- a pressure gauge on a short gooseneck, its glass fogged from inside, the needle resting against the pin at the top of the dial, where it has presumably rested for decades
- a ladder of bent rebar rungs set into the pit's one vertical wall, each rung furred with orange

Nobody finished whatever this was. The pad was poured for a larger machine that never arrived.

Life is thin here and keeps its distance. Nearest the jet there is nothing but mineral. One step out, where the rock stays wet and merely hot, a film of algae appears in a dark olive slick, then a brighter green, banded by temperature as neatly as a contour map. Beyond that, in the cracks of the cooler wall, grow a few small ferns and a cushion or two of moss. Their fronds tremble continuously, though there is no wind in the pit; the sound is shaking them. No insects come, and no birds cross overhead. The steam column and the roar seem to mark out a space that animals read as occupied.

The one odd thing is the pool. Its surface should be either still or disturbed. Instead it carries a fixed pattern, a fine lattice of ripples like the checkering on a pistol grip, standing in place and never travelling. The roar holds the water in that shape. If the pitch of the valve drifts, as it does over years while the crust narrows the opening, the lattice tightens with it. Someone who visited a decade apart could measure the change with a ruler. That is the pace of everything here. The valve seat wears, the crust thickens, and the ferns gain a frond. The note climbs a fraction of a tone and the water redraws itself to match.

Daylight comes down ordinary and unremarkable through the gap in the steam. It lights the wet wall and the blank sign, and the needle pinned at the top of its dial. Climb the rebar rungs and walk away across the flow, and the sound thins quickly, going up rather than out. At a hundred meters it is a hiss. At three hundred there is only the white column above the black plain.
