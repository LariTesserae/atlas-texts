The mountain was a range once. What remains is its root, a table of pale quartzite lifted so high that the air is thin, worn so long that it has run out of ways to fall down. There are no peaks, no scree, and no fresh breaks anywhere. The surface rolls in low hummocks, and the harder ribs of rock stand out of it as tors, rounded like loaves and smooth as river stones, though no river has touched them. Between the tors the softer stone has rotted in place over an unthinkable time into white clay, forty and fifty metres deep. The town is here because of the clay.

It is a town of kilns. They stand in rows along the lee side of the tors, bottle-shaped and brick-built, banded with iron, each with a blower house attached because a fire will not draw properly on its own at this height. They bake porcelain: insulators mostly, ribbed white bells and spools, stacked by the thousand in drying sheds. The town uses its own product. Every roofline carries wires on white knuckles of porcelain, and the wires sag from street to street under a growth of grey-green lichen that has to be stripped off twice a year.

Everything has to be stripped, and that is the first rule of the place. The cloud comes up the escarpment most mornings, climbing the cliff from below rather than descending, and once it is over the rim the town is inside it. Nothing falls as rain, but everything gets wet. Moss takes a slate roof in a season. It furs the gutters, the ropeway pylons, the north faces of the kilns up to the line where the brick gets too hot. It fills the joints of the paving so the streets look quilted. Scraping gangs work through the districts with hoes and wire brushes on a rota, and the green has closed over again behind them before they come back round.

Where no one scrapes, the growth is head-high and solid. There is dwarf forest on the old ground, with trunks no thicker than a wrist, twisted, sleeved in liverwort and hung with lichen beards that drip. There are cushion plants as firm as upholstery. Rosette plants each hold a cupful of clear water at the heart, and since no stream runs anywhere on the plateau, those cups are most of the standing water outside the town. The rest is film, bead, and seep. The town gets its own supply from mesh: fields of wire netting on frames along the windward edge, acres of it, woven in the wire mill by the ropeway head. The cloud combs itself through the nets, runs down into troughs, and goes into cisterns cut in the clay.

The second rule is that nothing is built on a tor. Nobody remembers a reason. The tors are numbered in white paint, and the numbers are repainted when the lichen takes them. The town packs itself into the spaces between, with roofs touching, alleys an arm's width across, pipes and cables overhead, and ferns in every downspout. The stone is finished changing and everything around it changes weekly.

The light is poor. On a clear hour, and there are a few most days, the sky is a hard dark blue and the sun has a thin, high-altitude bite without warmth. Mostly there is cloud, and the day is a grey that never commits. Lamps burn from morning on, each in its own globe of lit vapour. The brightest thing is usually the ground. The clay pits are open terraces of dead white, with milky pools on their floors, and they throw the dim daylight back upward so that people working near them are lit from beneath, chins and the undersides of hat brims pale. On overcast afternoons the town seems to get its light from its own diggings.

It is quiet, more than a place with blowers, pug mills, and a wire-drawing shed has any right to be. The moss absorbs the noise. Sound goes into the green walls and the padded streets and is not returned, and nothing on the plateau echoes. A blower house is audible from its own yard and not from the next. People stand close to talk. Whistles and bells were given up long ago, and the works signal with coloured lamps: amber for a kiln coming up to heat, red at full fire, green when it is drawn and safe to open. On a night of thick cloud the kiln rows show as a slow drift of those three colours. The only sounds that carry any distance are the drip, which is everywhere, and the ropeway.

The ropeway is the road. It goes over the rim at the south end and down the escarpment in one long sag after another to a railhead far below that is never visible. Buckets come up black with coal and go down white, crated in straw. It runs day and night, and its soft clank is the pulse of the town. At the rim station there is a strangeness everyone has stopped remarking on. The updraft is so steady that in wet weather the rain there rises. Drops lift off the cliff edge and travel upward past the platform, wetting the undersides of things, and the station's gutters are fitted upside down under the eaves.

The plants know where the heat is. In this cold, a few degrees above freezing most days with rime on the mesh on clear nights, the kilns are the only warmth, and the vegetation leans toward them the way growth elsewhere leans toward a window. The dwarf trees along the kiln rows all bow inward. The moss on a kiln's flank grows in bands, a different kind at each temperature, up to the bare scorched brick. When a kiln goes cold for relining, the bands creep upward within the month. When it is relit they brown off and retreat. Gardens are kept in the kiln lees, with cabbages and a sour blue-leafed kale, and their beds curve to follow the warmth.

There are few animals. A dark swift with long narrow wings nests in dead flues and hunts the rim in the clear hours, never calling. A small black toad, rough as a cinder, lives in the rosette cups and walks rather than hops, one deliberate limb at a time. Pale moths circle the lamps, and something unidentified eats the moths and leaves the wings on the sills. Nobody keeps dogs. The few cats stay on the kiln shoulders and rarely come down.

Worked-out pits are left alone, and they go green from the edges inward: a skin of algae on the white first, then moss, then the rosettes with their cups, then the first thin trunks. A pit abandoned ten years ago is a bowl of dripping forest with terraces still showing faintly under it. One abandoned forty years ago is hard to tell from ground that was never touched, except that the tors around it carry numbers.

By mid-afternoon the cloud usually thins and draws back down over the rim, and for a while the whole plateau is visible. It has the tors in their ranks, the mesh fields shining with caught water, the white pits, the kiln lamps amber and red, and the dark pelt of forest right up to the edge. Then the next bank comes up the cliff and closes it over.
