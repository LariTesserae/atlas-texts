The scar is two winters old. A slab came off the east face just below the ridge, at an altitude where breath does little good, and left a pale rectangle of granite the weather hasn't touched yet. The surrounding cliff is grey-brown and pitted. The scar is nearly white, its feldspar still bright, its quartz edges sharp enough to open a glove. It has no lichen and no stain, only rock dust lying in the creases where it fell.

Near the scar's lower corner a crack runs up at a slant, about four metres long, a palm wide at its widest and an arm deep. There is a town in it.

From a step back it reads as a grain in the rock, a faint regular texture along both walls. Closer, it resolves into terraces cut into the granite in level courses, each no deeper than a fingernail is thick. On them stand structures the height of a rice grain on end. They are the colour of the walls because they are made of the walls. The quarry faces behind them are the only smooth surfaces in the crack, and they shine a little when wet.

They are wet most of the day. A snow patch sits on a ledge above, and when the sun reaches it in late morning a film of meltwater comes down the crack's upper lip and spreads. The town was laid out for this. Its streets double as gutters, hair-fine channels that split the film and carry it along the terraces. They fill cisterns the size of lentils and pass the overflow down to the next course. The water in the cisterns stays liquid well below freezing because their linings give ice nowhere to begin. It sits clear and still while the rock around it goes hard with frost, until the town wants it frozen.

Between the walls run spans, filaments finer than anything a spider makes, visible only when frost is on them. They come clear before anything else in the morning, each line shedding its frost from the middle outward. Whatever heat the town has moves along them. Smaller things move along them too, in short runs with long pauses. They are motes at the edge of what an eye can follow, and their paths are too straight for dust. They are few. Watch one thumb's width of terrace for a minute and perhaps a single mote crosses it. Most of the town stands empty at any hour, with bare granite between districts and whole courses of terrace cut but not built on.

The light is ordinary mountain daylight, hard and clean. It comes into the crack at a slant until noon. On the upper terraces are fields of plates, each smaller than a mica flake, that turn to follow it, so that over the morning a dull glint travels slowly down the north wall. After noon the crack is in open shade, lit blue from the sky, and the plates lie flat.

Then the afternoon weather comes up from the valley. Cloud climbs the face and the air in the crack goes white and damp. At the mouth a mesh too fine to see beads all at once along its whole length and begins to drip inward. Some days it is graupel instead, rattling on the scar and bouncing, with a few pellets lodging in the crack. The channels have them melted and carried off within the hour. When rime grows on the windward lip, the structures there flex in one small shrug and the rime falls away in a single curved piece.

It is quiet. The mouth of the crack ought to sound a note in a crosswind, and the neighbouring cracks do, but this one has been shaped so it doesn't. What is left is dripping, and at dusk a ticking. As the rock gives up the day's warmth, the town lets its water freeze, chamber by chamber from the top down. Each chamber is a press. The ice swells against something that takes the push and keeps it. Each makes a tick like a fingernail on glass, a few seconds apart, the whole length of the crack. It takes about twenty minutes, and then there is nothing until morning.

The waste goes out at the bottom, where a spout no wider than a pencil lead lets the last water onto the open face. Overnight it builds an icicle, perfectly clear and as long as a hand. Around ten the next morning the spout warms and drops it. The icicle breaks on the scree forty metres down, where a small heap of clear shards never quite melts. That heap is the only sign of the town that can be seen from any distance.

Almost nothing lives here. Wind carries up a fallout from the valley, midges and a moth scale and now and then a springtail still moving, and these stick in the wet film. By the next day they are gone, drawn along the gutters toward the back of the crack. Nothing rots here and nothing takes root. No green or black film grows in the cisterns.

One animal is a regular. A jumping spider, dark and peppercorn-sized, keeps to an older crack off the scar and comes across on still days to work the film for whatever has landed. Its body covers a district. As it walks in, the spans ahead of it go slack and drop against the walls. Behind it they rise and tighten again. It takes a midge, sits a while in the slanting light, and leaves. Now and then a chough lands on the ledge above, looks down the face, and goes.

The rock is still settling. Other cracks from the same fall have already widened and spat out blocks, and at night the scar ticks and creaks in a way that has nothing to do with the presses. This crack has not moved. Hairline fractures leading off it are crossed at intervals by tiny pale sutures, and no water gets into them.

At the bottom, near the spout, the newest terraces are still raw. Their cuts are bright and the dust has not been cleared. There are three courses with nothing on them, and a fourth has been scored into the granite but not yet cut.
