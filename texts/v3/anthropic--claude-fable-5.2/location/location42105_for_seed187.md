From the fields it looks solid: a low, dark mound of foliage on ground so flat that a walker crossing toward it never feels the rise. There is a rise, though. A swell of very old, hard rock comes within a hand's depth of the surface here, the last trace of hills that were worn down before anything walked on them. The plough goes around it, and that is the only reason the yews are still here.

The grove is a ring about forty paces across. It began as one tree. The outer boughs sagged to the ground, rooted where they touched, and stood up again as new trunks. Those did the same, and the original tree rotted away from the middle so long ago that there is no stump left. From outside, the foliage comes down to the grass like a skirt, a wall of needles that are dark above and paler yellow-green beneath. You get in by stooping where a bough has lifted.

Inside there is almost nothing. The shade has killed every inner limb below about four metres, and no seedling, bramble, or fern can live on the floor. The trunks stand far apart, fluted and flaking in reds and purples, some of them hollow enough to step into. Between them is still, cool air. The floor is needle litter, brown and springy, built up over centuries faster than it decays, so that what you stand on is plant matter for a forearm's depth before the root mat begins, and the roots are locked straight onto the rock. Everything that bounds the space is yew: the floor, the pillars, the walls, and the roof. In the middle, where the parent stood, the canopy has never closed. Ordinary grey daylight comes down through the gap onto a patch of moss the size of a cart, and the light spreads sideways from there, so the grove is evenly lit rather than dim.

It is damp without being wet. Rain rarely reaches the floor. Mist catches in the needles and drips slowly from them, and under the thickest parts of the roof the litter stays dry as dust all year. The standing water is in the trees themselves: tea-brown pools in the forks and rot-holes, a few cupfuls each, where birds drink. The air stays a few degrees above cold. On most mornings breath just shows.

The quiet of the place is all in its look, because the grove is loud. Half a mile across the fields, the same old rock is quarried for roadstone, and the crusher runs with a steady grinding roar. The screens add a rattle on top, and the loaded conveyor adds a long hiss. The noise comes over the top of the canopy and down through the central gap, and the ring of trunks holds it the way a bowl would. A power line crosses the next field on steel towers, and when the air is wet it fizzes and hums a single low note. Several times a day, a train of hopper wagons drags past on the branch line, and the litter trembles faintly underfoot.

The animals live inside this noise and add to it. Jackdaws nest in the hollow trunks, dozens of pairs, and their hard, chipped calls go on from first light. Woodpigeons leave the canopy with a clatter of wings like boards being slapped together. The small birds have settled on songs pitched high enough to carry over the crusher. Goldcrests sing as thin as wire in the needles, a wren is astonishingly loud for its size, and a robin on the moss patch holds notes above the roar. In autumn, thrushes strip the red arils and squabble over them, rattling. Bank voles work through the litter for the dropped seeds and leave small heaps of opened husks at the trunk bases. A badger path, worn smooth, enters under the north skirt and leaves under the south. At night a tawny owl calls from a hollow bole, and the crusher answers it by simply continuing.

Little here marks the passage of time. The yews keep their needles, so there is no bare season, no leaf-fall, and no flush of spring green beyond a slightly brighter tip on each twig. The light under the roof is the same in June as in January. Twice a year the trees make a small show: a smoke of pollen on a dry day in late winter, and the arils later. The trunks widen too slowly to measure in a lifetime, the ring has not moved outward in any recorded way, and the rock below finished changing long ago. A needle comes loose and falls through the empty air to the floor, where it will lie for years before it loses its shape.

Nothing in here is made. The nearest wire is the field fence, a stone's throw out, and the nearest wall is at the quarry. At dusk the jackdaws come back in one flock and take a long, loud time over who sleeps where. Then they settle, and the plant runs on through the night.
