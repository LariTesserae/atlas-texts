From the plain it does not look like a town. At the bottom of the basin, where the ground has stopped sloping because there is nowhere lower to go, a patch of crust some two hundred paces across lies flatter than the rest, pierced with rows of holes the width of a fist. A few squat brick funnels stand up from it with their mouths turned southeast, toward the wind. Nothing else shows. The roof is the same clay as the pan, plastered over so many times and baked through so many summers that the seam between town and ground has closed.

A ramp goes down at the north edge, its steps worn into a single slumped chute. Below, the whole town sits under that one roof. The lanes are corridors two shoulders wide. Rooms open off them through low doorways, and more rooms open off those. Where the floor dips there is a second storey squeezed in beneath the same ceiling, reached by notched poles. There are perhaps three hundred rooms, all built of mudbrick. Every corner is rounded now and every threshold dished, and the walls lean on each other as they have for a very long time.

The light comes through the roof-holes in thin rods, brown with hanging dust. At noon they stand vertical and each puts a hot coin on the floor. By afternoon they lean and climb the walls. Between the rods it is dusk at every hour, and the eye adjusts to make out the mouth of a jar or the edge of a step.

It is hot the way the inside of a kiln is hot the day after firing. The roof takes the sun all day and gives it downward. The bricks are warm to the palm at midnight. Nothing here has ever been wet. The clay was puddled with something else, and the smell says what: pitch.

The town is here for the pitch. Under the basin floor the rock leaks it, and the builders cut fired-clay troughs along every lane to lead it from the seep-heads to the collecting pits. The troughs still run. Each morning the pitch is a dull, wrinkled solid that will take a thumbprint and no more. By midday the heat has loosened it and it creeps, slowly enough that the movement only shows against the tally marks pressed into the trough rims. It overtops the pits no one empties and has built black terraces down the low lanes, lip over lip, each day's flow a finger's thickness on the last. This is the only thing in the town that flows, and all of it eventually reaches the sump at the southern wall. There a fissure takes it back down, so the town never fills.

A vine lives on the pitch. Its stems are grey and thick as a wrist, and its roots go into the troughs and lie in the pitch like fingers in a bowl. The leaves are small, waxy and nearly black, and the sap, where a stem has split, is amber oil that sets hard in a day. There is usually one stem to a lane. It runs bare along the wall for thirty paces, then throws a tuft of leaves up toward the nearest roof-hole. When a shoot reaches a hole it spreads a single broad pad across the opening and takes the whole rod of light for itself, so that the room below goes dark. After some days the sun cooks the pad brittle and it cracks and falls in pieces, and the light comes back. Across the town holes are always closing and opening this way, and the dim patches shift from week to week.

The vine has also become part of the building. Its stems have grown through doorways and hardened into lintels where the old lintels gave out. The mortar in many walls has long since gone to powder, and there the roots hold the bricks in place.

Nothing eats any of it. The seed pods, long and ridged and hard as fired clay, hang until the heat splits them with a report like a snapped stick. The seeds skitter across the floor and lie where they stop. The dust on the lane floors holds only the drag-lines of fallen leaf pads and the pocks of seeds. There is not a track or a dropping or a gnawed edge in the place, and no wings sound under the roof.

The rooms are nearly bare. One has a quern, its hollow polished, and another a row of pegs, with a coil of vine-fibre cord on one of them. Storage jars stand in wall niches, sealed with pitch plugs, each plug stamped with a pattern of pressed dots. Against one wall leans a stone adze, its haft bound with cord, the binding still tight. Between each of these things lies a great deal of empty floor.

The town is loud. It begins at sunrise with ticking, as brick after brick takes up the heat and shifts against its neighbours, and by mid-morning the ticking runs through every wall. The pods start going off around then, singly, somewhere down a lane. At noon the roof groans as it swells. Then the afternoon wind arrives across the basin and finds the funnels. They were built to pull air down through the lanes, and they still do. The whole town draws breath at once and the corridors sound, each on its own low note according to its length. The jars in their niches take up the note and hum. The dry pods still on the vine rattle. In the eastern quarter a stone door-slab on a worn pivot knocks against its jamb, twice, a pause, twice, for as long as the wind holds. Sand comes in with the air and hisses along the floors toward the sump.

At the centre is a round room larger than the others, with a roof that corbels up into a low dome and a vent at the crown. Here the main seep comes up through a collar of fired clay, and with it gas, and the gas burns. The flame is a hand tall and blue at the root. It is the one light in the town that does not come from above. No one tends it. In the afternoon, when the heat raises the pressure in the rock, it stands up to arm's length and roars, and the roar goes out through the five lanes that meet at that room and joins the notes of the wind. The dome above it is glazed black and glossy with soot. No vine grows in this room. The stems come to its doorways and turn back.

Toward evening the wind drops and the corridors go quiet one by one, the longest last. The flame sinks to its hand's height. The rods of light redden, thin, and go out. Then the ticking starts again, slower, as the bricks give the heat back. The pitch stops in its troughs wherever it happens to be and its skin wrinkles as it tightens. At the lip of the lowest terrace a tongue of it hangs halfway to the floor, stiff until tomorrow's noon.
