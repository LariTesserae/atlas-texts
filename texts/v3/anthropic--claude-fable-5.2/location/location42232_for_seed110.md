High on the mountain's shoulder, above the last wind-flattened shrubs, there is a crack in a young lava flow about the width of a turned shoulder. The cold stops a body-length inside. Past that the rock is warm, and the passage opens into the blister.

It is a bubble the lava blew and never burst, a room two arm-spans across and not quite tall enough to kneel upright in. No light has ever reached it. Eyes open or shut, nothing differs, and after a while they stop asking. Everything known about the room is known by hands, knees, and the skin of the face.

What the skin reports first is heat, and the heat is exact. Air, rock and water all sit at the temperature of the inside of a mouth, so it becomes hard to say where the body ends. Wet can't be told from dry by coolness, only by slip, and nearly everything slips. The walls sweat. The air is so full that each breath feels half drunk, yet it is mountain-thin too, and the lungs work for it. It smells of struck flint, hot iron, and a faint egg-edge of sulphur from below, where the warmth comes from.

The stone is new, and fingers keep finding this out. The floor is ropy, cords of lava lying where they folded, still skinned with glass, and the glass still has edges because nothing has rounded them. There is no sand in the low places, no silt or grit. The room hasn't begun to make soil of itself. On the walls there is a first mineral bloom, a roughness like a cat's tongue, thin as a nail paring. The rock is froth all through, thousands of small cells from pinhead to thumb-socket, each holding a bead of warm water. They swallow sound, and a cough goes nowhere.

The ceiling is the crowded part. It hangs with lava drips, teats of stone formed in the single hour the roof ran molten. There are thousands of them, each a finger long, packed close as the bristles of a brush, so that a raised palm touches twenty at once. Each carries one drop at its tip. Because they are all the same size and the air feeds them at the same rate, they fill together and let go together. About every forty heartbeats the whole ceiling releases at once, a single soft pat across the room like a hand laid on damp cloth. Then there is quiet, and a slow reloading that can't be heard. Under it, at the edge of attention, runs a low steadiness that may be the mountain or may be your own blood.

The drops never pool. The froth of the floor takes them in and the heat gives them back to the air. Nothing accumulates, and the level never rises.

Very little lives here. In places the walls wear a skin thicker than water, gelatinous, smelling faintly of pond. Near the entrance, where a seam runs up to the surface, a few roots come through from some cushion plant overhead. They are hair-fine and hang limp and wet, and they end in open air as if they had decided to go no further. Once in a long sitting, something crosses the back of a hand on legs like threads, pauses, feels about with antennae longer than itself, and goes. It may be the only one, or the same one each time.

Someone has been here, though:

- **The cleared patch:** Against the far wall the ceiling drips have been snapped off so a person could sit upright. The stubs are still sharp. The broken pieces lie along the base of the wall, laid side by side and sorted by length.

- **The basin:** Beneath the cleared patch a hollow the size of two cupped hands has been pecked into a floor cord. It is brim-full and loses over its lip exactly what it gains.

- **The cobble:** Beside the basin lies a river stone, egg-shaped and battered at one end. It is the only smooth thing in the room, carried up from some valley far below, and after so much glass its roundness is a small shock to the palm.

- **The cup:** A shallow stone cup with a groove in its rim might be a lamp. But the ceiling above it leaves no grease on the fingers, and the cup smells of nothing.

- **The marks:** On the wall, at the height of a kneeling person's hand, fingertip-sized pits have been pecked in rows of nine. There are eleven full rows, their edges as crisp as the day they were struck. The twelfth stops at five.

Whoever worked here worked in the dark and made marks for hands to read. The cobble rests directly beneath the fifth pit, as far away as an arm would set it down. Overhead the ceiling fills and lets go.
