The town sits in a saddle between two wooded summits, at the height where cloud forms. The saddle is small, three hundred paces from lip to lip and less across. East, the ground drops into one gorge; west, into another. Whatever weather passes between those valleys passes through here, and so does everything with wings.

You come up by stairs. They are stone and older than anyone's account of them, each tread worn into a shallow basin that holds a finger of water and, usually, a frog. Oak and laurel close overhead, and rhododendron with trunks like twisted rope. Every limb is sleeved in moss so thick that it looks twice its real girth. The air is cool enough to show your breath at dawn and never warm enough to dry your shirt.

Then comes the hedge, which is the town's wall and its oldest structure. Figs and alders were planted in a ring many generations back. Their limbs were lashed together until they fused, and their hanging roots were led down through split bamboo into the soil to become new posts. It is now thicker than a house is wide. The gate is a tunnel through it, floored with roots trained into a ribbed ramp and polished black by feet.

Inside are some sixty houses, packed eave to eave on stone footings. Their corner posts are living trunks, pollarded at roof height and sprouting each spring. The walls are split bamboo and fern-fibre matting, replaced panel by panel as they soften. The roofs began as thatch and have become turf, deep in moss, ferns, orchids, and tree seedlings that children hook out with sticks. The lanes are two people wide. Above them runs a second set of streets, root-walkways with woven handrails, because for half of any day the ground level belongs to running water.

Rain falls often, but most of the water is combed out of the cloud. Cloud moves through the hedge and the leaves strip it, so it drips from every surface. Bamboo gutters carry it to stone troughs with lips worn into spouts, and from the troughs it runs to cisterns. From there it goes down the lanes and off both edges of the saddle in thin white falls. Ducks work the gutters. Small crabs live in the culverts, and tadpoles in any pot left out for three days.

Every house keeps a hearth smouldering, without a chimney. The smoke seeps up through the roof, so that from the stairs the whole town appears to steam. Smoke is the only preservative available. Nothing here is ever dry, only smoked. Anything that has to last is either stone or still alive, and what is neither is replaced on a schedule everyone knows by heart.

The animals outnumber the people many times over. Pigs live under the house floors, and goats are put up on the roofs on purpose, to graze them down. Their bronze bells clank overhead all day. Hollow-log beehives hang under the eaves, and dogs lie in every doorway. Macaques come over the hedge in gangs when the grain racks are full. Squirrels, rats and martens use the root-walkways as freely as anyone, and slugs as long as a hand cross the lanes at night.

The saddle brings more, because it is the low door between two valleys. Swifts cut through the lanes at head height. Flocks of laughingthrushes spill over the hedge, shouting. In the right weeks pigeons cross in thousands, and on certain days so do butterflies, a steady yellow-white drift going one way for hours. At dusk the bats come up out of the east gorge and pour through the gap. At the narrowest point the fowlers keep tall bamboo masts strung with nets, raised and dropped by hand. Smoked birds, honey, bark-cloth and dye go down the stairs in baskets. What comes back up is salt and soft iron. The knives are resharpened daily until they are thin as leaves.

It is loud. There is the water, constant and from all sides, and frogs in every basin and bromeliad. Goat bells, pig squeals, mallets beating bark into cloth, and the alarm calls when a hawk comes through all carry over the town. People speak at a near-shout out of habit. A slit-gong by the gate is struck whenever the cloud thickens, so that anyone on the stairs can walk toward the sound.

The cloud thickens often and with little warning. The square can be in full sun, every leaf glaring with water and vapour lifting off the roofs. Then the front edge of a cloud slides through the hedge like smoke under a door. Within a few breaths the far houses are gone, the temperature drops, and hard rain rattles the bamboo. Ten minutes later it has blown through and the light is back. This happens a dozen times a day.

Every fifth day is market, and the population doubles. Porters with tumplines crowd the walkways, stalls are lashed up by mid-morning, and by dusk the goats are pulling them apart. Growth is just as restless. A path left uncut for ten days closes, and a basket left on the ground overnight comes up with a fringe of mushrooms. Someone is always weeding a roof, clearing a gutter, or tying a new root into a railing. The town is kept the way a boat is bailed.

The shape underneath all this does not move. The stairs, the footings, the ring of the hedge and the two spouts where the water leaves were all settled long ago. At night the cloud sits down in the saddle, the hearth-glow shows through the wall mats, and the frogs take over completely.
