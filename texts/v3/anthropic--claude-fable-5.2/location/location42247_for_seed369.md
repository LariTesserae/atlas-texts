**The Ringing Bowl**

The bowl sits two-thirds of the way up the mountain, on a shelf below the ridge where the slope gives out for about forty paces before going on climbing. On three sides there is a low wall of broken columns, shoulder-high at most. The fourth side is open to the drop, and the water leaves there, sliding over the lip in a sheet as thin as skin.

The floor is the tops of stone columns, packed side by side like the cells of a comb. Each top is a grey-green tile, five- or six-sided and roughly a stride across, worn slightly dished. The stone is clinkstone, which rings like a pot when knocked. The seams between the tiles hold the only soil in the bowl.

Everything grows from the seams. The tiles themselves are bare and wet, so the vegetation follows the lattice in lines a forearm thick: filmy ferns, liverworts, and red-stemmed creepers whose leaves are no bigger than fingernails. Where three seams meet, there is sometimes enough depth for something large. There are perhaps thirty tree ferns in the whole bowl, each standing well apart from the next. Among them grow a dozen or so of a broad plant with leaves like shallow basins, each ending in a long, down-curved tip. A person can walk the bowl without touching a leaf, though the green is thick wherever it has taken hold. The air between the plants is open and warm, smelling of wet stone and of something sweetish from the fern crowns.

Cloud comes up the mountain every night and stands in the bowl until mid-morning. It doesn't rain here so much as condense. The basin-leaves fill, tilt, and empty through their tips. The fern fronds bead and let go, and the back wall weeps down its joints. All this water falls a body-length or more onto bare clinkstone, and every tile it lands on rings.

The sound is the first thing a visitor notices and the last thing they stop hearing. Close up it is a clatter, bright and uneven. From the middle of the bowl it sorts itself out. The tiles near the lip, where the columns stand half-free above the drop, give long low notes. The tiles toward the back wall, locked tight among their neighbors, give short high ones. Two people standing an arm's length apart have to raise their voices. The sheet of water going over the lip adds a hiss, and when wind comes through the notch in the eastern wall the fern crowns rattle. On a still morning the mountain reflects the sound back, and it can be heard from the trail below long before the shelf comes into view.

Each drip has worn a cup into its tile, thimble-deep or deeper, and smooth as the inside of a shell. Some tiles carry two or three cups in a short row, marking where a leaf-tip has moved outward over the generations as its plant grew. A few of the cups are dry, with nothing overhanging them any longer.

One rule here is visible to anyone. A tile that rings stays bare. A tile that has cracked through, or settled so that it has gone dull, grows a skin of moss within a few seasons and a full cushion within a few more. There are perhaps twenty of these green tiles scattered through the bowl, and stepping on one is like stepping on a wet sleeping-mat. Whether the shaking keeps spores from taking hold, or the moss simply prefers the quiet stone, the sorting is complete.

There is one exception. Near the center is a tile that gives no sound at all, yet nothing grows on it. Knuckles on it make a noise like knuckles on packed earth. It stays dry on mornings when everything around it is running, because the leaves that ought to overhang it have grown off to either side.

On that tile sits a river cobble the size of two fists, rounded and dark, of a stone that does not occur on this mountain above the valley floor. It rests in a hollow that fits it. Around the bowl, on perhaps a dozen of the best-sounding tiles, there are pale bruised patches where stone has struck stone many times in the same place. One tree fern carries an old cut low on its trunk, made with something not very sharp and long since healed to a ridge. A twist of bark cord hangs from another, rotted to a few fibers. Nothing else has been left here. No one has stacked a stone or cleared a seam.

By late morning the cloud has lifted and the light is ordinary highland daylight, clear and a little hard. The tiles steam for a short while. The dripping thins from a clatter to scattered notes, and by afternoon there is a silence broken about once a breath. Small frogs call from the seams then, three or four of them at most, with a dry ticking that is easily mistaken for another drip. Sometimes a hawk crosses overhead without slowing. Snails the color of the stone leave trails across the tiles that dry before they can be followed anywhere.

Toward evening the cloud returns and the leaves load again. There is a stretch around dusk, not every day, when the drips fall into step. For a few dozen beats the whole bowl sounds together, low notes and high, like one struck thing. Then the leaves drift out of time with one another and the clatter resumes.

Over years the plants grow and the drip-points creep, so that new tiles are woken and old ones are abandoned. What the bowl plays now is not what it played when the deepest cups were begun. In the short dry weeks at the turn of the year it plays nothing, and the cobble sits on its silent tile in the sun.
