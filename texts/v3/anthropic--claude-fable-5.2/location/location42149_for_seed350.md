The noise comes first, and it doesn't get louder as you approach, because there is no approaching it. It is the same at the rim as on the floor: a ground-tone from the pumps, below hearing, felt in the teeth and the long bones, and over it the dredge chain, the shriek of cavitating impellers, rock splitting along the eastern fault, and the water itself, which roars like a weir though nothing here is falling. Sound in this basin does not thin with distance. It lies at one thickness through the whole valley, the way the warmth does.

The valley is a graben, a block of crust that dropped between two faults. It is about nine kilometres long and two across, with its floor well under the level of the sea that came in over the western sill. All of it is new. The scarps are black basalt with edges no season has dulled, the fracture faces glassy and the columns still square at the corners. Talus lies where it landed, with no silt yet in the gaps. Along the eastern fault, vents push out pillows of lava that crust, crack, and push out again, and the heat comes from there. The water is bath-warm from floor to canopy and tastes of iron and struck flint.

It is never still. Whole bodies of water roll over one another without reference to any tide. The dredge's plume goes north, then west, then stands in a column. Visibility is twenty metres, then an arm's length, then twenty again. At noon the light is a brown-green dusk, because overhead the surface is under permanent downpour and under the rain is the weed. The only other light is man-made: sodium lamps on the pump houses, each a smeared orange ball, and the blue-white stutter of arcing from the switchyard, which lights the fronds for an instant like an X-ray.

The weed is everywhere, in strap-blades as broad as doors, bottle-green and oxblood, and in ropes of bladdered stipe as thick as a wrist. Mats of fine filament fill the gaps between them, and a soft turf covers every surface more than a week old. Moving through it is like pushing through a cloakroom of wet coats. Nothing eats it. There are no bite-marks, bore-holes, or crusts of shell, and no slime trails. Each blade is perfect from edge to edge until the turbulence tears it, and the torn pieces take hold wherever they settle. There is no fish in the valley, no worm in the silt, no fleck of anything swimming in the lamp beams. The weed has the place to itself and has filled it.

It grows toward sound. The stands are thickest around the machines, and they arrange themselves in the standing waves of the pump-tone, in dense ranks where the pressure swings hardest. Where the tone cancels there are bare lanes of clean black rock, a metre or two wide, running straight for hundreds of metres. These are the only paths. When a pump changes speed, the lanes move, and the weed is already standing in the new ranks.

The machines are few. There are lift stations, concrete pump houses the size of barns, with cast-iron volutes and riveted mains two metres in bore, each stencilled LIFT and a numeral. Their motors turn underwater in finned housings too hot to touch. Oil-filled transformers hum in a fenced yard where the fence is furred solid with weed. A bucket-chain dredge, moored to nothing, works the centre of the valley, lifting rock that is younger than its own buckets. A line of pylons carries cable from one fault to the other, and at each end the cable goes into the basalt without a fitting, as if the rock had closed on it.

They were built to drain the valley. The discharge mains climb the western sill, cross it, and come back down into the basin at an outfall which is, when followed, the intake. Even so, the pumps have removed something. There is a volume of absent water, a room of air about the size of a rail yard, that wanders the floor. Its walls are water standing vertical, trembling with the pump-tone, and its ceiling is water too, with rain falling out of it. Inside, the weed doesn't collapse. It stands upright in the air, dripping, held by the noise. When the hollow moves on, the silt behind it lies in undisturbed ripples and the turf is unbroken, as though the water had never left.

The surface behaves the same way. Rise through the canopy and you come out into air and rain. Keep rising and the rain thickens until it is water again, warm and brown-green, with another canopy of weed overhead and lamps glowing orange somewhere above that.

Other things fail to hold. From the north there are six lift stations, but from the south there are four, and two of them carry the numeral 3. New scarps come up along the eastern fault with the rock still ticking as it cools, and the weed on them is already full height. It forms canopy first, a raft of blades hanging in the water, and the stipes grow down from it over an hour until they find the rock.

In the doorway of LIFT 4 there is a time clock, its case green with turf, and a rack of cards. Every eight hours the clock drives its stamp down on a card, and the card slides out and drifts into the weed. At the same instant every pump in the valley drops out, for about four seconds. The roar of the water goes with them. Every frond in nine kilometres goes slack, the lanes close, and the walls of the hollow lose their edge and begin to lean. Then the motors catch, the tone comes back up through the rock, and the weed stands again in its ranks. The lanes run in new directions, and the scarps along the eastern fault are no longer where they stood before the stamp.
