**The Saddle**

Between two old peaks there is a saddle about the size of a village square, and its floor is new. The peaks are ordinary mountain, grey and frost-split, with lichen on the scree and cushion plants in the lee of boulders. Then the scree stops at a clean line, and beyond it the ground is pale, smooth, and warm as the flank of an animal. Frost comes down the slopes every night and halts at that line. In the morning it has left a white margin a hand wide, steaming where it touches.

The floor is stone, though it didn't come from lava or silt. It arrives out of the wet air in layers about a fingernail thick, one a day. No weather has got to it yet, so there is no grit, no soil, and not a crack anywhere. Where a slab has been broken, the inside shows a grain of small six-sided cells, too regular to be mineral habit, and the broken edges are sharp enough to open a boot sole.

Five masts make it. They are thin, dark, and taller than a house, and they stand unevenly spaced across the saddle. None of them touches the ground. Each tapers to a point that hangs a hand's breadth above the floor, and under each point the stone rises in a low collar, like wax around a wick. They have no moving parts and no seams.

Rain slows down near them. Within a few paces of a mast the drops fall at the speed of drifting seed, and you can pick one out of the air with your fingers. Light slows too, or is taken. Each mast stands in its own dusk, and the shadows of everything on the saddle lean toward the nearest mast, whatever the sun is doing. With the cloud that sits in the pass most of the day, noon here looks like the end of evening.

Water is everywhere and has no place to go yet. It beads out of the cloud onto the warm stone, weeps down the mast collars, and slides across the floor in sheets a finger deep. There are no channels, because nothing has had time to cut one and the floor rises unevenly under the water anyway. A pool that was beside the hut yesterday may be thirty paces east today. The sheets hiss as they move. Where they reach the open sides of the saddle they go over a lip and drop into cloud.

The lip grows too. It pushes outward over the drop as a shelf, and its edge shows each day's layer like the pages of a shut book. Dripstone fringes hang beneath it, already as long as an arm.

Not much is green here. A black-green algal film grows where the water moves slowest. One liverwort survives by outgrowing the stone, and each patch sits on a small pedestal made of its own buried earlier growth. Three ferns are rooted in the hut's gutter, the only place where anything like dirt has collected.

Animals are another matter. This is the only warm ground for a day's climb in any direction, and everything that can reach it has come.

- **Frogs.** They are small and grey, there are thousands of them, and each has a voice like a struck pipe. They can't sit still for long, because the stone skins over whatever rests on it. They call, shuffle a body-length, and call again, and the whole floor shifts with them. A frog that dies leaves a smooth bump by the end of the week. They lay their eggs in rafts of whipped foam that float on the wandering pools, since anything laid on the bottom would be sealed in by morning.

- **Midges.** They hatch from the pools every afternoon in columns thick enough to lean on. They drift toward the masts, slow as they near them like the rain, and hang in the dusk as a visible grain in the air.

- **Martins.** They have learned to hunt the slowed midges, flicking through the edge of each mast's dusk and out again, screaming. They nest in cups of the unset paste that gathers at pool margins. It hardens in a day, and by the next spring it is buried to the rim, so they build on top of it. The saddle is dotted with the result: chest-high chimneys of stacked nests, with a live nest on each and the fossil ones beneath.

- **Snails.** Their shells grow so thick that the old ones can barely drag them.

- **Wild goats.** They come over from the cold side to lick the fresh layer for its salts. Their hooves are glazed white and clack on the floor like crockery.

- **A stoat.** It hunts the frogs along the frost line and takes its kills back onto old rock to eat.

For so small a place, it is loud. The frogs alone fill it. Under them is the hiss of sheet water, the martins, the goats' hooves, and thunder most afternoons, which the two peaks pass back and forth until it wears out. Underneath all of that is the stone itself. Each new layer pings as it sets, in single bright ticks like a kettle cooling. At dusk, when the day's deposit hardens all at once, the ticks run together into a rattle that sweeps the floor from mast to mast. The frogs stop for it. Then they start again.

The day keeps a schedule:

- **Morning.** The air is clear overhead, the floor steams, and goats stand at the frost margin.
- **Midday.** Cloud climbs out of the valleys and fills the saddle, and the midges rise.
- **Late afternoon.** Rain comes, warm by the time it lands, falling fast in the open and slowly near the masts.
- **Dusk.** The stone sets with its rattle.
- **Night.** The cloud drops away below the lip. Cold air slides down off the peaks and meets the warm shelf, and fog stands at the boundary in a wall. The frogs go on calling until dawn.

People were here, though they left little. There is a hut of some grey laminate with its door on the downhill side. The floor has risen to its windowsills, and martins use the gap under the eaves. A row of survey stakes crosses toward the nearest mast, and only the top finger-width of each still shows, with an orange cap. There is a bench, of which the backrest remains above ground.

By the hut stands a gauge post, notched and numbered by hand. Whoever kept it stopped cutting notches some time ago. The last one is at ankle height now, and a frog is sitting against it. In a minute the frog will have to move.
