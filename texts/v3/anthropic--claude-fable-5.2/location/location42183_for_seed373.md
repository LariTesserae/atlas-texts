The hollow sits just under the ridge crest, a scoop out of the mountain's shoulder, open to the sky and about two hundred paces across. You hear it from the valley long before the climb. By the time you reach the lip, the sound has become something you lean against.

Snow lies on the ridge right up to the lip and stops in a clean line. Inside there is no snow, ice, or frost, though the air is cold enough for all three. Flakes blown over the edge go out in mid-air, each with a small tick. The light is plain high-country daylight under a hard blue sky, with sharp ordinary shadows. Nothing glows.

The rock is pale, hard, and fluted. Long ago the wind cut every wall into vertical pipes, some a finger wide and some wide enough to stand in, and then it ran out of grit. Nothing has been carved here for a very long time, and the edges of the flutes are glazed. What the wind does now is play them. It comes over the ridge from the west, drops into the bowl, and breaks up differently with every gust. One bank of pipes sounds, then another, then the whole eastern wall at once, in a chord that never resolves. Beneath it lies a lower note that does not change, felt in the teeth and breastbone more than heard. That one belongs to the pins.

There are four of them, standing in the bare floor at roughly the corners of a square. Each is taller than the walls and no thicker than a wrist, dark grey and seamless, and enters the rock without a socket. In wind that throws birds sideways they do not sway or whistle. Dust swerves around them. A hand held close finds a sleeve of dead-still air a palm thick, colder than the rest. Whoever set them left no explanation.

What they do can be worked out from what is missing. Water in the hollow exists only inside living things. The pins take it from everything else: the air, the snow, the breath as it leaves the mouth, the surface of an eye held open too long. They put it into tissue somewhere on the walls. Nobody's breath fogs here. A cut leaf does not drip, because the sap is withdrawn before it can bead, and the wound dulls over as you watch. A bird killed against the rock is a husk within minutes, light as a seed-head. The next gust carries it over the eastern lip, where a long drift of husks and leaf chaff is slowly being snowed on.

The total amount of water is small, a fixed sum. It is not shared evenly and it does not stay put.

Most of the wall, at any moment, is brown crust. This is the scurf of dormant plants: mats, cushions, hanging beards, flat rosettes pressed into the flutes. Then the allocation arrives and a patch the size of a house front takes water all at once. The crust swells, splits, and goes a dense, wet-looking green. Fat leaves stand up and beards thicken into ropes. Flowers the colour of egg yolk and rust open before the leaves have finished. For about the length of a meal, that piece of wall is as rich as a ravine in rain country.

Then the water is recalled. The leaves slacken, fold, and scab back to crust, and some shatter and blow away. The green is already elsewhere, climbing the north wall or broken into a dozen plots across the bowl. There is no rhythm to learn. Sometimes a band sweeps around the walls sunwise, steady enough to walk beside. Sometimes it stutters between two spots a hundred times in an hour. This may be design. It may equally be a regulator that overshot once, ages back, and has been hunting for its balance point ever since, so that the oscillation is now as settled a feature as the flutes.

Everything here drinks by eating, so everything follows the green. The grazers are rust-coloured, fist-sized, and tailless, and there are hundreds of them. They run the ledges in files, nose to rump. When the green jumps, the file breaks and pours down one wall, across the bare floor, and up another. They eat fast, standing. Beetles hatch out of the crust along with the leaves, and small pale moths arrive from nowhere visible. The grey wall-birds, thousands of them, nest deep in the flutes where the wind cannot reach, and take beetles, moths, and each other's eggs. A larger, narrow-winged bird hawks above them and never seems to land. All of them are built dry and carry no more water than they must. A wall-bird in the hand weighs about as much as a wad of paper.

All of them are loud. The pipes leave one narrow gap in pitch, high and thin, where the chord of the walls never reaches, and every voice has moved into it. The birds' call is a single cracked note at that pitch. The grazers' alarm sits there too, and so does the beetles' rasp. The gap is as full as everything else, and there is no quiet in any register. Near a nesting wall when the green comes in beneath it, the noise is a pressure in the sinuses. Anything that matters gets said another way. Birds flash white underwings, and grazers stamp so that others feel it through the rock.

For all that, the hollow is mostly empty. Life is a thin skin on the walls. Nothing roots or rests on the polished floor, and no loose thing stays there. The middle of the bowl is only air, moving violently, with a bird flung through it now and then. Someone standing at the centre would have the nearest living thing eighty paces off, screaming.

On the south side, steps are cut from the lip down to the floor, each tread worn into a shallow dish. They lead to a low room built into the foot of the wall, the same grey as the pins, with one doorway and no door. Sound stops at the threshold. It is not muffled, it simply ends, and one pace inside you can hear yourself swallow. There is a bench along one wall and a shelf at shoulder height. On the floor sits a shallow round basin a forearm across, polished and dry, the only object in the hollow shaped to hold water. The grazers pack into the room at night, flank to flank. They leave a clear space around the basin.

The northeast pin leans a few degrees off true. Its sleeve of still air is thinner than the others' and flickers. The sun leaves the corner behind it early, and on some afternoons a patch of rime forms there on the rock, a hand's breadth across or occasionally two. It is the only water in the hollow that belongs to nothing. The wall-birds come down and stand around it in a ring without pecking. After a few minutes the pin catches up, the frost goes with its tick, and the birds lift back into the wind.
