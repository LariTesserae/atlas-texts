Near the top of a cone younger than its own weather, above the last cloud, a gas blister in the lava has cracked open to the sky. From the rim it is a bowl the size of a room, four paces across and waist deep, with walls of glassy black basalt that still tick as they cool. The air over it trembles. The rock is too hot to rest a hand on, heated from below and by the high sun together, and it is entirely dry. There is no steam, no seep, not even a mineral stain where water once stood.

What is wet here is oil. The basalt sweats it at the seams: a thin amber mineral oil, clean, smelling of a warm machine shop. The plants live on it. Each one is lavish. There are squat crowns of leaves like stamped leather, a creeper with coin-round leaves so glossy they show the sky, and a moss as dense as felt. Break a stem and it bleeds lubricant. They grow dark and heavy and very far apart.

The distance is the first thing wrong. From the rim there are seven plants, perhaps eight, all within reach of a long stick. Climb down and the nearest stands across a plain of bare black floor, small with distance. Walk toward it and the room supplies more floor. Arrival is possible, but it takes the better part of a morning, and the pocket keeps emptiness between its things the whole way. Look up at any point and the rim is an arm's length overhead, with ordinary blue sky beyond it.

Nothing here was made. The basalt has simply crystallized into function, the way salt grows into cubes. A drum of magnetite the size of a barrel turns in a socket of its own rock, one revolution from noon to noon. Veins of native copper leave it and branch across the floor like roots, faintly warm with current. A slab of denser stone rises and falls a finger's width over a season. At one vent, ore reduces itself to beads of bright iron, which gather, dull, and sink back in. No tool has touched any of it. These are the mountain's own habits.

All of it is unfinished. The drum's shaft ends in air. A hand's width farther on the next section resumes, turning in time, and the piece between has not yet formed. The torque crosses the gap anyway. Wheels whose teeth have not yet crystallized mesh on schedule. The moss grows thickest at these gaps, packed around them like gasket material laid in ahead of a part.

In places the rock has not settled on a substance. It has weight and heat, but no grain and no color the eye can keep hold of. Elsewhere a crisp edge stands with nothing behind it. Only the daylight is complete. It falls straight and white and makes proper shadows, including a shadow for the missing length of shaft, which turns slowly on the floor.

It is quiet. There is a hum so low it is more pressure than note, and the ticking of the walls. At long intervals a drop of oil falls from a leaf onto hot stone with a brief hiss.

No insect has ever been here, and nothing crawls in the moss. At the driest hour, pollen lifts from the leather crowns in a pale thread, drawn by the charge in the copper. It travels along the vein to the next plant, hours away or a few paces.

The slab comes down on nothing, and nothing visible leaves the room. But the air thins on the way down from the rim. At the center of the floor, the lowest point, breath comes shortest and the sky is darkest. It is the highest place on the mountain. What the pocket makes is height, and the summit outside rises a hand's width each year toward where the floor already is.

At noon the drum completes its turn. The shadow of the absent shaft comes round to where it started. A leaf lets go of one drop.
