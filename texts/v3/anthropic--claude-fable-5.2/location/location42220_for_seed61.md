The noise arrives before the place does. In the crawlway that leads down, a limestone tube polished by bellies and knees until it shines like a river cobble, it starts as a pressure behind the breastbone. By the last bend it has a top end as well: a hiss, a clatter, and a thin continuous ringing laid over the bass. Then the tube opens at the lip of a room.

It is only one room, perhaps thirty paces across at the floor, narrowing as it rises like the inside of a jug. A whole town is cut into its wall. Five tiers of cells ring the chamber, each doorway an arm's length from the next. The ledges between them are so narrow that two people pass by turning chest to chest, and the corners have been buffed glossy at shoulder height. There are no ladders. Toe-notches run between the tiers, each worn to a deep cup, the left-foot cups a little deeper than the right. Up in the neck, where the walls lean close, slabs of stone lie across the gap as bridges, their undersides furred with soot.

Nothing here was ever green. Everything is stone, bone, clay, hide, sinew, or fat. The door-flaps are scraped skin gone stiff and translucent with damp. The pegs are antler, and the cord is gut.

**The water**

Water enters through a single hole in the roof, a column as thick as a thigh, and it has never been seen to thin or swell. At the top tier a stone trough catches part of it and feeds a gutter chiseled into the ledge. The gutter winds down through the town in one long spiral, passing every door. At each cell a notch spills a finger's width of water into a household basin, and each basin overflows onto the tier below. The rest of the column drops straight down the middle into a hollowed block on the floor, and that impact is the bass.

Under every household spout hangs a ringing-stone, a thin blade of dark, hard rock propped on bone pegs so the falling water strikes it. No two give the same note. In the dimness a person finds home by ear, walking the ledge until the right pitch separates itself from the rest.

The floor is a sump, ankle-deep at the edges and waist-deep under the fall, draining through a slot in the far wall exactly as fast as it fills. A single crust of white calcite marks the waterline around the room. There is no second line above or below it.

Everything else is wet too. The walls weep and the air is saturated. Breath shows, hides never fully dry, and every surface holds a skin of beads that merge and run. The cold is the mild, unvarying cold of deep rock.

**The light**

Lamps are stone cups of rendered fat with wicks of twisted hair, set in niches cut for them, about one to every four doors. The first rule of the place is that a lamp is never moved. It is fed where it sits. Above each one a tongue of soot runs up the wall, as thick as felt.

Because the lamps have not moved, neither have the shadows. Where the light has always fallen, the stone is tanned amber with smoke and grease. Where it has never fallen, behind a jamb or under a bridge or on the lee side of a pillar, a soft white mineral paste called moonmilk has grown undisturbed. The town is two-coloured, and the pattern is a map of its own lighting.

**The people**

There are perhaps two hundred of them in sixty cells. They sleep in turns, so the town is always about one-third asleep and has no morning. Nobody speaks, because speech is useless under the fall. Talk is done with fingers on a forearm, taps and presses and a drawn line, or with a hand passed in front of a lamp to send a flicker across the chamber. The sign for the fall is a flat palm laid over the ear.

Their work adds to the noise. Chert nodules stud the limestone, so households quarry their own back walls with antler picks, and the cells with good chert have grown deep and crooked. Knappers sit in the doorways striking flakes. Others grind bone on wet sandstone or scrape hides. The clacking has no rhythm, but its density never changes.

What comes down the crawlway arrives already dressed: bundled hides, bladders of fat, marrow bones, dried meat. Nothing goes back up except people.

**The quiet cell**

On the fourth tier there is a cell where the room's echoes cancel each other. Stepping through its doorway feels like having hands clapped over the ears. A drip can be heard there, and a swallow. It is the smallest cell in the town and no one will widen it, since carving might move the silence. Newborns spend their first sleeps in it. The dying are carried there if they ask.

**What else lives**

Very little else lives here. White crickets with antennae longer than their bodies sit on the ceiling near the lamps and are left alone. There are three olms in the sump, pale as tallow, eyeless, with pink frills at the neck, and they hang motionless in the slack water behind the basin block. The count has been three for as long as anyone has counted. They are not eaten. Children are lowered to touch one, once.

**The stone's own work**

The lowest tier is the oldest, and flowstone has been closing it slowly. Calcite hangs over the lintels in smooth folds and narrows the doorways. Beneath it, old carvings are still visible, rows of tally cuts and a spiral that mirrors the gutter, blurred as if under ice. Whoever notices a door narrowing chips it back with a hammerstone. The stone grows about a fingernail's thickness in a lifetime, and about that much is removed, so the doors stay the width they have always been. The chips go into the sump, and the water carries them out through the slot in the far wall.

Up in the neck, condensation gathers in the soot and falls as slow black drops. Where one lands on a ledge, someone going by wipes it off with a thumb, without breaking stride, and feeds the next lamp along.
