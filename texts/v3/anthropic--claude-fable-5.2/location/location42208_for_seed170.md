The tableland gets hotter as it gets higher. At the foot of the escarpment the air is only warm. On top it is the air over a stove lid, thin and dry, smelling of struck flint and hot iron. The summit is a plain of desert pavement about sixty kilometres across: fist-sized stones fitted close, varnished black-brown on their upper faces, each wind-cut to three facets, lying on pale dust that has not shifted in a very long time. Nothing stands on it. There is no wall, no cairn, no post. The sky is an ordinary blue, a shade dark for the altitude, and the sun throws ordinary short shadows. The light is the one thing here that behaves.

What you see first are the plumes. On a clear morning there are nine of them, sometimes eleven, kilometres apart, each a straight grey column rising in the still air to the same height and stopping there, cut flat, with clean sky above. Under each plume is a speck.

What you hear is all of them. The plain roars. It is the sound of a forge district at full shift: drop-hammers, the scream of a relief valve, a grinding like millstones, chain rattling over a drum. It arrives at the same volume from every direction, with no source. Sound on the tableland does not weaken with distance. Every blow struck anywhere on the flat is delivered everywhere on the flat, entire. Each beast strikes perhaps once an hour, and the roar is continuous. That is the only reliable census: by eye there are nine, and by ear there are millions.

Up close they are the size of boxcars, low and long, carried on six short legs that work like connecting rods. The hide is overlapping plate the colour of mill scale. The rivets are growths; they come in like teeth, and the calves have soft grey plate with none. A stack rises from the shoulders. Along the belly the seams glow dull orange from the fire inside, fed on oil shale rasped from the pavement with a tongue like a file. No one made them. They calve, they moult plate, they die and are eaten down to the firebox by the others. But they are engines, and what they do is manufacture.

Stand beside one and it is silent. The plates slide, the jaw works, and the great boss of the skull lifts and drops on the workpiece with a blow you feel through your boot soles and cannot hear, while the air around you thunders with everyone else's. Its noise is being heard somewhere else. You can hear every animal on the tableland except the one you are with.

All the water here is inside them, in closed circulation. Sometimes a joint lets go a white feather of steam, which is gone within a hand's breadth. Sometimes a bead forms at a seam and dries before it falls, leaving a small ring of salt. On the lee side of certain stones there is an orange rind a millimetre thick. It could be lichen, or rust, or the egg-stage of something. The calves lick it.

The ground records the work. Each beast walks in a groove, a pair of ruts worn knee-deep into the bedrock and polished to a gloss by the same feet in the same places. The ruts look laid, but they were only walked. Every kilometre or so along a groove lies an anvil-stone, a slab dished like an old stair tread. Where the shale has been grazed there are shallow pans a hundred metres long. The pans do not deepen. What is rasped away is there again by the next season, and it does not come back fresh. It comes back with its varnish and its three wind-cut facets, already old. The plateau is worn down at the rate it is worn in, and has been this height for as long as it has been.

A beast moves about one body-length a day. The workpiece is a loaf of dark metal, swallowed warm at one anvil and brought up at the next to be struck again, and it takes most of a year to finish. Finished loaves go to the rim and are nosed over. Nothing accumulates. There is no stockpile, no slag heap, no yard.

The rim is where the sound ends. Along the whole edge of the escarpment the rock is crusted with pale shells no bigger than thumbnails, packed solid, every mouth open inward toward the plain. One step past them the roar shuts off like a door. Below is brown-white haze, level as a floor. The loaves go into it without a sound, and no one has heard one land.

If you follow a groove you find that the beast at the next anvil has the same chipped scute over the left shoulder as the last one, and the same hitch in the third leg. The same holds at the anvil after that, where it is a little further along in the work. If you turn back, the one behind you is a stranger. The herd has been counted many times. Each count is correct, and no two agree.

Every groove runs outward to the rim. If you walk one the other way, inward toward where it must begin, you still come to the rim, with the shells and the silence and the haze. The tableland has an edge in every direction and no middle. The sixty kilometres is a measurement taken across it. No one has walked that distance.

Twice a year, something whistles. It is one long note, and it is the only sound here that has a direction, though the direction is different for everyone who hears it. Every hammer stops for the length of a breath. When the roar resumes, each beast is one anvil further along its groove, and none of them has taken a step.

After dark the plumes can't be seen and the fireboxes can. They are nine or eleven dull orange seams scattered across the black flat, like the lights of a town where no town is. The heat does not ease. The stones tick. The roar goes on at the same volume it had at noon, made by millions of hammers on a plain that looks empty.
