**The roof**

The roof is ice, and the sun is somewhere above it. This is young ice, refrozen clear and nearly free of bubbles, so the light comes through hard and white, with blue in it only where the ice thickens. Underneath lies a hollow as wide as a country. In any direction the floor runs out until the brightness swallows it. No far wall is visible. The air between floor and roof is deep enough to hold its own fog, which hangs in flat bands about halfway up.

**The plant**

One kind of plant holds the hollow open. It rises from the floor in columns, pale and jointed like cane. Each is a few hundred paces around at the base and so tall that the top fades into glare before it reaches the ice. Where a column meets the roof it spreads into a fan, dark green going to black, pressed flat against the underside of the ice to drink the light. From below, the fans look like lily pads seen from the bottom of a pond.

There are not many columns. From the foot of one, the next is a pale line half a day's walk away, and the one beyond it is a thread. Everything between them is cold bright air.

**Movement**

The ice moves. It slides across the hollow in lanes, some slow and some covering a body-length in an hour, and it drags the fans with it. Every column leans the way its piece of roof is going. It leans further through the day, then splits along a joint and comes down. Most hours, one is falling somewhere in view. The fall is slow at that distance: a line tilting, a white burst of rime shaken loose, a cloud of frost that hangs glittering for a long time afterward. The sound arrives much later as a single soft thump, or does not arrive at all.

The plant answers at the same pace. Shoots come up through the floor wherever light lands, and they stand taller than a person by the next day. They grow from the base, the way grass does, each joint pushing the older ones upward. A column is always oldest at its top and newest at the ground.

**The floor**

The floor is the same plant, dead and living together: fallen columns lying across older fallen columns, bound by a mat of root and frozen pith. It gives slightly underfoot. No one has dug down to soil.

Fresh breaks are everywhere. There are stumps with splintered rims higher than a house, long shards of cane standing upright where they speared in, and slabs of roof ice with edges still sharp enough to cut a hand. Nothing here has had time to round off. Frost covers all of it and throws the light back up, so shadows are thin and pale blue. They crawl, because the fans that cast them are travelling.

**Water**

Water exists only as a film. The dark fans warm a little in the light and sweat the roof, and the melt runs down the outside of each column in a sheet too thin to see except as shine. Partway down it freezes. The lower third of every column is cased in glaze that cracks off in plates as the cane lengthens beneath it.

Hollow stumps catch the drip and hold small round pools, skinned over with ice a finger thick. That is all the standing water there is. Sometimes a frost-fall drifts sideways on the wind and passes over like a brief snowstorm with the sun shining through it.

**Sound**

It is quiet. The mat swallows footsteps, and the wind has nothing to whistle through. Close to a column there is a tick every few breaths, a joint letting out another finger of growth, along with the drip and the occasional clatter of shed glaze. Now and then the roof shifts its whole weight and gives one low note, felt through the feet more than heard.

**Animals**

Animals are few. Pale flies breed in the stump pools and can be seen moving under the skin ice. White fliers nest on the undersides of the fans and are seen mostly as specks crossing the fog bands, rarely lower. Something long-bodied and solitary browses the new shoots. It leaves bitten tips and a line of prints in the frost, and the prints are usually all there is of it. After days of walking, the count might be one animal, far off, already moving away.

**Stone**

The only stone here comes from above. The ice carries rocks inside it from wherever it began, and lets them go. They lie where they landed, each in a small crater in the mat, black and unweathered.

**People**

Someone gathers the stones. Heaps of them stand at certain stumps, sorted by size. Beside the heaps the frost is littered with flakes where edges were struck, along with an adze head or two, bound into split-cane hafts with cord twisted from fan fiber.

These people sheltered inside stumps. A few still have roofs of lapped fan-leaf and soot on the inner walls from pith fires, and in one or two the soot smells recent. A sled of split cane lies with its runners worn. All of it is small, scattered, and days apart.

They cut steps into the living columns to reach the wet zone above the glaze, where the water runs unfrozen. The steps have not stayed where they were made. The lowest notch on one column now sits forty feet up, and each is stretched tall and narrow, with gaps between them that no leg could span. Rows of tally marks have been pulled into long scars.

They also marked a route once, with cut poles stood upright in the mat. The poles rooted. The route is now a straight line of young columns, each already leaning with the ice.

**The margin**

At the margin of the hollow, where the roof comes down to meet the ground, the mat thins and stops. Bare rock shows there, freshly broken and angular, with the first shoots already coming up between the blocks.

Out in the middle, a new set of steps has been cut at hand height into a column that was not there a season ago. The bottom step is already at the height of a shoulder.
