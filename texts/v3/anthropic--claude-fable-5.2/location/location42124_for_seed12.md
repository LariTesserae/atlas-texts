The lake has no top. It sits in the bowl of a young volcano, high enough that the air outside the rim is thin and bitterly cold, but the water is warm as blood and gives off vapor so heavy that the two never properly separate. Deep down it is plainly water. Higher up it is water with air in it, then air with water in it, and nowhere in between is there a line you could lay a hand on. A dropped feather descends at the same unhurried pace through all of it. People here do not say *on* the lake or *in* it. They say how deep they are standing.

The caldera is perhaps an hour's paddling across. Its walls are new rock, black and glassy, and the edges still cut. There are no gullies, no scree, and no soil that a plant did not make for itself. At the center, where no one goes, the floor is still cracked and working. Every few hours a slow belch of gas rises from the vents, a single thick sound like a jar being unstoppered underwater. It is the loudest thing in the place.

The town occupies the shallow eastern shelf, and it was not built so much as allowed. The water is loaded with dissolved stone, and anything that holds still in it acquires a pale, buff-colored crust, about a finger's width a year. So the people lash frames of reed with twisted cord, sink them where the warm springs flow, and wait. A grandmother ties the outline of a room, and her grandchildren move in. Walls come up ribbed and slightly translucent at the edges, like the lip of a shell. Several hundred houses stand this way, close enough that their eaves have fused. The lanes between them are channels of slow water where flat reed boats ride low and the paddlers sit wet to the chest in the thick middle air.

Tools are few. The people have obsidian flakes knapped from the rim, bone awls, cord, and wooden mallets. Each household keeps a mallet by the door, because the door is always closing. Each morning someone knocks the night's new rind from the threshold, and the dull tapping moves through the town in no particular order. A house left empty for a season has no doorway, and in ten years it has no windows. Nobody fires clay. A basket hung in the flow for two winters comes out as a stone bowl with the weave still printed on it.

The rule under everything else is to keep moving or be kept. Blades are worn on the body. Children are taught not to nap in the springs. Sleepers wake with a fine grit in their lashes and rub it away without thinking. A few people in each generation do nothing but stir. They walk the channels with broad paddles and keep the water turning so the lanes stay lanes.

The plants live by the same rule and are winning. Ribbon weed grows a hand's length a day, shedding its crusted lower leaves as it climbs, and it fills the channels with long green strokes that all lean toward the spill. Moss covers every wall faster than the stone can cover the moss, so the houses are furred and dripping, green over buff over green in layers. Rafts of matted reed drift through the town with gardens on them: fat-leaved tubers, a sour floating fruit, and a creeper whose roots trail down two body-lengths into the dark. Where vegetation loses the race it stays as it was, and whole reedbeds on the northern shelf stand as stone, every blade intact.

Animals are scarce, since the water is too thick with mineral for most of them. There is a pale, thumb-long fish that grazes the fresh crust and sometimes strays upward into what ought to be air, hanging at head height before it sinks back. There are snails whose shells thicken until they cannot lift them. Children collect these, scrape them thin with obsidian, and set them down again. Once or twice a year a few long-legged gray birds come over the rim, stand in the shallows looking uncomfortable, and leave.

The light is the color of the inside of a leaf. There is never a sun, only a part of the sky that is brighter than the rest, and nothing casts a shadow. Fire is difficult here. Embers are kept under stone hoods and fed with pressed weed-cake, and at night the town is a scatter of dull orange smudges, each with a ring of lit fog around it. Cooking is done in the springs. Voices stay low, because sound travels flat and far in the wet air and a murmur can be heard three lanes over.

Under the town is the town. The lake spills at one notch in the western rim, and the notch keeps sealing itself higher with its own deposit. So the water rises about a hand's breadth in a lifetime, and the houses climb with it. Below the lived-in floors are the floors of the great-grandparents, lightless and warm and crowded with weed, and below those are rooms no one has names for, pressed into the first raw rock. Divers go down for old stone bowls and come up quiet.

Outside the notch, the overflow steams down the mountain's cold flank and lays white terraces as it goes. The lake is adding to the mountain a basin at a time.

A few times a year the cloud tears. For an hour the sky overhead is a blue so dark it is nearly black, the rim stands out sharp, and the cold comes down on wet shoulders. People stop paddling and look up until the cloud closes again.

At the town's southern edge a reed frame stands in the flow. It has four walls and a doorway, tied this year and only just frosted with stone. No one alive will sleep in it. Someone has already hung a mallet beside the door.
