The sound arrives before the place does. From below the last ridge it is a pressure in the chest, and at the rim it becomes a white roar, level and total, with one low note inside it that never wavers. A shout does not leave your mouth here, and a clap is only something your hands feel.

The plateau is marble, pale as salt, and about an hour's walk across if it could be walked. It was jointed long ago into a tight grid and has since dissolved along every joint. What remains are pinnacles, tens of thousands of them, each two or three body-heights tall and round-shouldered, packed so closely that the slots between them are barely the width of a forearm. There is no floor to speak of, only crowns to step across and the gaps between them. From the rim the whole field looks like the knuckles of a single enormous hand.

Every pinnacle weeps. The water comes from inside the rock, not from the sky, which is a hard, dark, cloudless blue. A higher range stands far to the north, a line of white on the horizon, and its melt travels under the intervening country through the marble's own seams. It rises here under pressure, to the crown of each column, and wells out of a pore no bigger than a thumbprint. From there it sheets down the sides in a film thin enough to see the stone's grey veining through.

Each film slides into its slot and falls, and each slot is nearly the same width and depth as the next. That is where the note comes from: tens of thousands of small falls in tens of thousands of matched stone throats, all holding one pitch. The roar is the water, and the note is the shape of the place.

The light is nearly as loud. The air is thin and the sun comes through it unsoftened, and every wet, pale surface throws it back. The light comes down from above, up from the crowns and sideways off the flutings, and it goes down the slots too, passed from one wet wall to the other, so that even the bottoms glow a cold blue-white with the water moving through them. Shadows are small and sharp-edged. You see everything, and after a while that is tiring.

The stone has finished most of its changing. The crowns are domed and smooth. The flanks are fluted with parallel runnels, each a finger wide, with edges long since rounded off. Whatever was going to fall has fallen and been carried off grain by grain. There is no rubble or silt, and no soil anywhere. A fleck of grit set down on a crown is gone within the hour.

In a few runnels an orange slick follows the water, a mineral-eating film thinner than paint and cold to the touch. It is the nearest thing to a garden here. Nothing has roots.

The slots drain inward. Toward the middle of the plateau the pinnacles shorten, then drown, and the water stands as a shallow sheet, ankle to shin deep, over a pavement of submerged crowns. It is perfectly clear and never still. The surface shivers with the vibration coming up through the rock, a fine, fixed, cross-hatched ripple that goes nowhere.

On the south side the sheet reaches a notch in the rim and goes over in one unbroken pane, down into air and a valley too far below to matter. That fall is the deepest part of the roar. The level of the sheet does not rise or drop. A pale line on the marble at its edge, a hair above the water, shows where it stood at some earlier time, and nothing shows it having stood anywhere since.

The water comes out of the rock a few degrees above freezing, at the same temperature all year. The air is cool and sharp, but the film keeps frost off the stone. No ice wedges the joints, and seasons pass over the plateau without getting hold of it. The only thing that moves on a schedule is the angle of the sun.

Animals are few, and they are quiet in the only way that counts here. Afternoon updrafts lift insects off the lowlands (moths, aphids, small flies) and drop them, chilled and stunned, onto the wet crowns. Slow, pale, wingless crawlers work the slot walls and eat this windfall. They are the colour of the marble and go no faster than the film they walk in.

Once in a long while a grey bird clings to a pinnacle flank and picks the crawlers off. It has no call. When two of them meet they open their wings and flash a white patch, then shut it again, and one answers the other across the glare. It is probably the only language the plateau has room for.

The rules are simple. Everything is done by sight. Water is under every surface and on most of them. Nothing collects, because whatever lands is either eaten or carried to the notch. The note holds, and when you leave you notice it mainly by its absence, a silence in your ears that takes a day to go away.
