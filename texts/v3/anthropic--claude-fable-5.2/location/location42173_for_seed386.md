The rim stands at about four thousand six hundred metres and it is cold there, as it should be: black scoria, frost in the shaded pockets, a wind with nothing in it. Then the ground drops inward, and thirty metres below the lip the frost stops along a line as clean as a tide mark. Below that line everything is green.

The heat comes up through the rock. The caldera is three kilometres across and old; its walls have slumped to their settled angle and stayed there. The whole floor sits over something that has not finished cooling, so the ground is warm to the palm on the upper slopes and too hot to kneel on near the centre. The air above it is hot too, thick and wet like a laundry room, in a place where the lungs still have to work for every breath. You sweat and gasp at once, and the body does not know which altitude to believe.

The vegetation sorts itself by ground temperature, in bands. Highest are the cushion plants, which elsewhere on these mountains grow as tight grey knuckles and here have swollen into soft mounds a metre across. Below them is a belt of ferns, shoulder-high, their undersides furred with brown spores. Further down grow the big-leaved things, with rhubarb-like stems as thick as a forearm and leaves that hold a litre of condensate each and tip it out when something brushes past. There are no real trees, since the air is too thin for that ambition, but the shrubs have gone tall and leggy in the warmth and knit together overhead. Moss covers every surface that holds still, and all of it drips. The steam rises, meets the cold coming over the rim, and comes back down as water, all day, onto every leaf.

At the centre is what could be called the works. A field of vents runs along a fracture, perhaps four hundred metres long, and over a very long time the vents have built themselves flues of pale silica, some knee-high, some taller than a person. The flues are fluted and stained orange and rust-brown where iron bleeds out. They vent in order. One opens with a flat exhalation, then the next along, then the next, down the line and back to the start. The whole cycle takes about forty seconds, and it comes with a pulse underfoot that is felt in the ankles more than heard.

Between the stacks, hot water runs in channels it has lined with its own sinter, smooth as glazed pipe. The pools boil early here, at a heat that would only simmer at sea level, and they boil without stopping. Sulphur lies in yellow drifts against the downwind side of everything. The smell is of struck matches and hot metal. Nobody built any of it. It turns out cloud, mineral and warm water in steady tonnage, around the clock, and has done so since before there was anyone to count shifts.

The runoff gathers in a shallow lake at the low end of the floor, milky green, warm as blood at the margins. Mats of bacteria in orange and olive grow where the temperature suits them, each colour keeping to its own degree. Small black flies walk on the mats in their thousands and graze them. Frogs breed in the cooler shallows year-round, having no winter to wait out, and the tadpoles hang in the water in dense dark shoals.

Hummingbirds work the red tubular flowers along the fern belt. These are high-country birds, the kind that go rigid with cold each night elsewhere, but here they roost over warm ground and wake without effort. Grey rodents with long hind legs and curled tails sit on the heated boulders with their eyes half shut. There is nearly always a raptor turning in the main plume, riding the lift without a wingbeat and watching the rodents. Tracks of some slender hoofed grazer come over the rim in several places and wear down into paths. The animals descend at dusk to stand in the warmth and climb out again before the sun is high.

The day runs to a schedule. At dawn the air is clear and the plume goes straight up, a white column visible from valleys away. By late morning it has spread into a lid, and the caldera sits under its own cloud in soft, even light, bright enough but shadowless. In the afternoon it rains. Often what falls from above is snow; you can look up and see the flakes coming down grey against the white, and see the height at which they give up and turn to water.

Before the heavier storms the air charges. Hair lifts off the neck, the tips of the rim rocks give off a thin hiss, and a faint violet fringe shows on the highest points. Sometimes at dusk small lightning flickers inside the plume itself, in short dry snaps with almost no thunder.

For all this, the place is quiet. The thin air carries sound badly and the moss takes what's left. Standing in the ferns you hear the dripping, the soft sequential breath of the vents, and now and then a frog. Hummingbird wings come as a brief pressure near the ear. The pulse in the ground is easier to notice when you stop walking.

At night the cold comes down over the rim like a poured liquid and the steam thickens to meet it, until the whole bowl is filled with white. The rodents press flat to their boulders. The grazers stand hock-deep in the warm shallows with fog to their shoulders, not moving. Along the fracture the flues go on venting in order, one after the other, forty seconds to the cycle.
