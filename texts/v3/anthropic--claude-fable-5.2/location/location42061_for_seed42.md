The lake sits in a cirque above the last trees, a bowl of grey rock with snow in its creases into August. From the rim it does not look like water at all. The whole surface, about four hundred paces across, is covered with overlapping leaves the size of shields, dull green with red undersides where the wind lifts an edge. Only at the north end, where the meltwater comes off the headwall in a single white fall, is there an open black patch, and the noise of that fall fills the bowl and never stops.

The town is underneath.

Below the leaves the water is cold and very clear. It tastes of stone and numbs the teeth. Daylight comes through the leaf-roof in shifting green and gold, bright enough to see the far side of a lane. Everything here is one plant, or behaves like one. Its stems rise from the bottom thirty or forty feet to the surface, jointed like horsetail and hollow, the old ones thick as a body and crusted white with marl, the young ones no wider than a wrist and still slick. They stand far apart. A swimmer can pass between any two with arms spread and touch neither. Most of the lake is empty water, and that emptiness is how the place is laid out: straight lanes run from the fall to the outflow, crossed by shorter ones, kept open over generations by bending each new shoot aside in its first year and tying it there until it forgets where it meant to go.

The plant breathes. Air goes down the hollow stems from the leaves to the roots and comes back up stale, and the town lives off that traffic. At the joints of the big stems hang the bells, baskets woven mouth-down from retted stem fibre, caulked with fish fat and lake clay, each the size of a small room and held in place by grooved stones on braided cord. A bone tap is set into the stem above each one, and the plant's air leaks into the bell in a thin constant thread of bubbles. There are perhaps sixty bells. They hang at three depths:

- **Under the roof:** The highest sit just beneath the leaves, where the water is a little warmer and the light is good. These are the workrooms, for net-making, fish-splitting, and the slow chewing of fibre into twine.
- **In mid-water:** The sleeping bells hang here, each alone on its stem, a long swim from its neighbour.
- **On the floor:** Among roots like knotted rope lies the oldest quarter. Its bells are so overgrown with marl and rootlet that they have become part of the plant, pale domes with stems growing through and around them. Stores are kept there in the cold, in stone-lidded pits.

Nothing in the town is metal. The blades are flakes of black glass traded up from somewhere lower, and the needles are heron bone. Every join is a knot. The knots are read as well as tied, so that a lane-marker, a bell's owner and a warning about a weak stem are all cord, tied at the height of a passing hand.

The lake is loud. The fall is under everything, a pressure in the chest more than a sound, stronger toward the north lanes until the bells there tremble with it. Over that come the animals. Water-boatmen in their thousands rasp from the stems, a dry sizzle that seems wrong for anything underwater. Char hang in the lanes in loose silver flocks and grunt when startled. The frogs are big cold-water frogs with yellow throats. They sit in the leaf-roof with only their eyes in the air and call all through the long dusk, and the roof carries it down as a rattling drone. Grebes dive through with a slap and a stream of bubbles, and otters use the lanes as the people do, and are let alone.

The stems themselves carry sound better than the water does. Strike one with a stone and the note runs its whole height and into its neighbours through the roots. The town talks this way: two knocks, a pause, three. Someone is always knocking somewhere.

The animals are part of the upkeep. Snails as large as fists are moved from stem to stem to graze the algae off the taps and marker cords, and their slow rasping can be heard from inside a bell at night. Newts winter in the floor quarter by the hundred, tucked into the old weave. Schools of small grey minnows follow anyone who swims, picking at the skin. Caddis larvae build their cases from scraps of cut twine, so the lake bed is scattered with tiny, perfect imitations of rope.

There are rules, and they are mostly about patience.

- No living stem is cut. Dead ones are taken only after they have stood dead through a full winter.
- A tap is moved up one joint every few years so the plant can close the old wound.
- A new bell is hung only where an old one has been given back to the floor.
- The black water under the fall belongs to no one, and nothing is tied there.

Change comes at the plant's pace. The stems add a joint a year. In autumn the leaves redden and sink, the roof thins, and for some weeks the town has sky above it, hard blue, the rim of the cirque wavering overhead. Then ice closes it, grey and thick, and the fall shrinks to a mutter. The stems go on breathing through winter by some means no one has needed to understand, slower, the thread of bubbles in each bell reduced to a bead at a time. The ice booms at night. In spring the first new leaves come up rolled like scrolls and open on the surface one by one, and the green light returns to the lanes a patch at a time, the same patches as the year before.

The outflow at the south end is a notch in the rock lip, worn smooth. A few leaves always go over it, turning. Below, the stream is ordinary water and nothing grows in it but moss.
