The town sits in a saddle between two rounded summits. These are old mountains with no crags left on them. The scree on their flanks stopped moving long ago and has lichened into a single gray-green skin, and the saddle itself is a shallow tilted shelf, perhaps three hundred paces across, falling away to north and south into valleys that are usually full of cloud.

There are about seventy buildings, all of the same stone, a schist that splits flat and comes out of the hill already shaped like a building course. Nothing is mortared. The walls are thick, slightly battered, chinked with slivers, and roofed by corbelling, each ring of slabs stepping inward until one capstone closes the hole. Because the shelf slopes, the roof of one house is the yard of the next one up, and in the middle of town this stacks three deep, with a stair of projecting stones let into a wall, a landing that is also someone's lintel, and a drain that passes under one floor and over another. It is organized the way a larger place would be, with lanes, courts, a common store and a cistern row, but a person can walk from one edge to the other in four minutes. At the edges there is only a knee-high wall, and beyond it the shelf continues a little way as bare ground before the slope takes it.

More houses stand than are lived in. Perhaps forty people keep hearths here, and the empty buildings are maintained exactly as the occupied ones are. Every so often a household carries its pots and bedding to a cold house a few doors off and lights the fire there, so that over a lifetime every room in town is warmed and dried in its turn and no wall sits wet long enough to slump. The effect, walking through, is of a great deal of built space with few presences in it. There is a doorway with smoke leaking from the capstone gap, then six doorways without, then a woman on a step with a spindle, then a long lane with nobody.

There is no spring, and the water comes out of the air. Most mornings the cloud in the northern valley rises and passes through the saddle on its way to the southern one. The town has set long freestanding walls across its path for this, built of open-jointed slabs stood on edge with gaps a hand could slide into. The cloud goes through them and leaves its damp on the stone, and the damp gathers and runs down to a gutter course at the foot, a line of slabs with a groove pecked along them by hammerstone. The grooves lead to cisterns, which are stone-lined pits sealed with gray clay and lidded with slabs. Rain falls too, now and then, fine and brief, but it's the cloud that fills them. By midday the faces of the walls have dried to a pale silver and only the joints stay dark. The whole town holds that pattern, pale faces with dark seams, every afternoon.

The light is ordinary highland daylight, even and a little thin. It comes more often through a high white overcast than from a clear sky, and it casts soft shadows or none. Its coolness changes little from month to month. The cloud blunts both the cold and what warmth there is, and the stone holds the temperature of the day before.

Very little grows. Along the lee side of each wall there are cushion plants, tight green domes as hard as bread crust, some of them wider than a doorway and certainly older than anyone living. A wiry gray sedge fills the unpaved corners, and it is cut and twisted into cord. Moss lines the drain grooves in a continuous thread, and the lichens on the stone are rust-orange, pale green and a black like spilled soot. On the south edge are a dozen small terraces, each the size of a blanket. They grow a knuckle-sized tuber and a bitter leaf in soil that was carried up in baskets and has been kept from washing away ever since. In the central court stands one woody shrub, waist-high and wind-combed, with a ring of stones around its foot. No one planted it and no one prunes it.

The animals are few. A small flock of goats, shaggy and short-legged, is let out onto the summit flanks in the morning and comes back down the same lane by itself. One pair of black cliff-birds nests in the corbelling of the store and walks the roofs with a proprietary air. Some small thing with a short tail lives inside the walls and is seen mostly as movement at the edge of the eye. On some days a large raptor crosses the saddle at height without circling, using it as a pass the way the cloud does.

The tools are stone, bone, antler and a little copper. There are hammerstones of a tough green cobble traded up from a riverbed far below, antler wedges for opening the schist along its grain, and bone needles. Each household keeps a few copper awls and one copper knife, worn thin and resharpened so often that they look like leaves. Pots are coiled and fired in a pit with dried dung, and they come out mottled brown and black. Fuel is dung and peat, the peat cut in bricks from a wet pocket just below the northern lip and stacked under the eaves-stones to dry. Wool is spun on whorls of pierced slate. Everything in the town is made by hand, and the marks of that are everywhere. The pecked grooves are slightly uneven, and each door-slab has a pivot hole ground into a threshold by its own turning.

It is quiet. The wind through the fog-walls makes a low continuous hush, closer to breathing than to whistling. Under that there is the drip into the cisterns, which is irregular and has a hollow note where a pit is low. A whorl ticks against a step. Sometimes there is the knock of a hammerstone, three or four blows and a pause, where someone is resetting a slab. In the evening goat hooves sound on the paving. Voices carry easily in the lanes, so people speak low, and a conversation two courts away arrives as a murmur with no words in it.

What governs the place is a balance that no one has upset. A stone that falls is put back that day, in its own gap, and a fallen stone is never used to start something new. Thresholds are worn in a shallow dish by feet, and when the dish is deep enough to hold water the slab is lifted and turned over, and the dish on the other side, started by people long dead, takes up the wear again. The peat is cut no faster than the pocket fills, and the goats are kept to the number the flanks will carry. The households stay at about forty hearths and don't seem to strain toward more. The path the cloud takes through the saddle each morning hasn't moved. The lanes have been swept so long that the paving is polished along the centerline and rough at the margins, and the boundary between the two is a clean line that follows every curve.

There are no ruins here and no new work, so the town has no visible age. A wall rebuilt last year and a wall that has stood for thirty generations look alike, made of the same slabs, with lichen grown back to the same extent. By late afternoon the stone is at its palest and driest. Smoke stands a little way above a few capstones before the air takes it, the birds are on the store roof, and the cisterns are lidded. In the northern valley the cloud has settled back to its usual level, just under the lip, where it will stay until morning.
