The plateau is what remains of a continent's worth of mountains. It is a high, warm tableland worn down to low domes of pink-grey rock, none taller than a person, with films of rainwater lying between them. The air is thin and soaked, and rain hangs in it, drifting sideways in sheets. Across half the horizon stands the sea.

It is a sea without a basin. The rim that held it wore away so long ago that the plateau shows no scar, but the water never left. It stands where it stood, a wall four kilometres high, dark green deepening to brown-black, running out of sight in both directions and bending with the curve of the world. Its face is vertical and soft. Rain striking it leaves rings that spread sideways and upward. It gives off warmth the way an animal's flank does. Fog peels from it all day and rises to join the cloud made by its top, where a level surface with waves on it lies far overhead. The cloud never clears, and noon on the plateau has the light of late evening.

The mountains the sea once drowned are gone too. Stone finished here before water did, but the slopes stayed on as a rule the water keeps. A pebble released inside sinks until it reaches the place where a mountainside used to be. There it stops, rocks once, and rolls downhill along a gradient with nothing under it. Silt settles on these gradients in drifts and scree hangs on them in fans. Beneath them, in the volume the rock once filled, the water is the same water, only a shade darker and entirely still. A stick pushed into it goes in slowly and stays where it is left.

The forests root in that. In the old valleys they grow thick, with weed-trees whose trunks are the width of a doorway and whose fronds overlap in six or seven storeys. Every gap is filled with drifting spore, shed blades, threads of slime, and silt that never quite lands. Nothing in a valley is more than an arm's length from something else. The light is amber-green at midday and brown the rest of the time. Many fronds have pale undersides, so the canopy seen from below is a ceiling of dull silver. The vanished peaks still cast shadows. Through the day, wedges of deeper dimness swing across the forest from summits that are not there, and the weed on the shaded faces grows thinner and darker, as it always has.

The old zones hold as well. Partway up each absent mountain the trees stop along a clean line, and above it there is only a felt of short red weed. Above that the scree hangs bare. Higher still lies a snowline of fine white shell-dust on the upper slopes, warm to the touch and never melting. It sometimes lets go in slow, soundless slides that take an afternoon to reach the valley.

Rivers still run in their beds. They are bands of cooler, heavier water with visible edges, sliding down the valleys through the lighter water around them and carrying silt. Where a valley once opened out of the range, the river reaches the wall of the sea and keeps going. It spouts from the face, sometimes at ground level and sometimes from a notch two kilometres up, and falls through open air onto the plateau. These cataracts are the only loud things in the country, and they are outside. They run off across the tableland as ordinary rivers. The rain on the sea's top replaces them.

Inside there is almost no sound. Silt ticks against scree. Twice a day the whole sea leans, the wall bulging out a few metres and drawing back, and at the turn there is a low note more felt in the chest than heard. Warm plumes rise from the basement rock and cool water drains down the valleys to replace them. Blooms brown a whole range for weeks and then clear overnight.

Sometimes the stone comes back. A front of it moves through like weather, grey and slow, taking hours to cross a ridge. While it passes, the mountainside is granite again, hard and cold, with the roots clenched in its joints and the fronds held rigid. Then it moves on and the trunks sway again. Each time it returns the stone is rounder and lower than the slope it fills, as if it had gone on ageing wherever it was. The slopes are wearing too. The rivers cut at gradients made of nothing, and the pebbles come to rest a little lower each age. This second erosion is slower than the first, and it is working toward the same flatness.

Animals are few. Small transparent snails live on the fronds. In each range there is perhaps one long pale grazer, seen at a distance once in a season, working its way up-valley and leaving a cropped lane that takes years to close. It turns aside well ahead of the stone fronts.

Someone was here before the stone finished, or after. Cairns of real rock, stacked by hand and bearing no tool marks, stand on the absent ridges and hang kilometres above the plateau floor as small dark piles in the dimness. One line of them marks a pass. There are rings of stones with settled dust at their centres. There is a weir of piled boulders across one of the inner rivers, with the heavy water still backing up behind it. In places the weed does not grow along a narrow strip that climbs a slope in switchbacks.

One mountain was tall enough to stand above the sea as an island. Its absence stands there now, a hill of air rising out of the surface under the cloud. Small waves break against its shore with a hiss and slide back. Rain lands on it well above the water and runs down in runnels, following gullies no one can see, to the sea. At the top a cairn hangs in the open sky with its stones dark from the wet. The drops that fall from it stop a little way below, where the summit was, and run down from there.
