The ground is brick, and under the brick there is more brick. Pry up a course (they lift easily, the mortar is still soft) and beneath it lies another, darker and wetter, bedded in grey slip. Beneath that is another, and nowhere across the whole continent of it does the digging reach soil or stone. Every brick was shaped by hand. The faces show it: the drag of a palm along the top, a thumb pressed into one corner to seat it. The thumb is never there. The prints are warm.

It is mostly floor. It runs flat in every direction until the steam closes it off, a low basin floor the colour of liver where it is wet and of rust where it has been fired. The heat comes up through the courses rather than down from above, as if a kiln were lit somewhere beneath all the layers and had been burning since before the first one was laid. The air sits on the skin like a wrung cloth. Water beads out of the joints, stands in the shallow frogs of the bricks, and steams off without ever quite leaving.

The light is brown and does not change. When the steam thins, the reason shows: far overhead is the underside of a vault, corbelled courses stepping inward from horizons that cannot be seen, unfinished, with a ragged gap at the crown where a dull glow comes through. The vault is high enough that clouds form beneath it. The rain falls from the brickwork itself, warm and thick with dissolved clay, and where it lands it leaves a skin. Enough rain leaves a layer. Before the layer dries, something scores it into rectangles, and so the rain lays the floor.

Tools lie on it, very far apart, an hour's walk from one to the next:

- a paddle of fired clay, its handle worn to a grip;
- a flint scraper;
- a mould box, also clay, its inside slick;
- a plumb-bob on a cord.

The cords are the only thing here not made of earth. They are pale, twisted, and slightly greasy, and nothing accounts for them, since nothing in this place has ever had a stem or a tendon.

One cord matters more than the rest. It is stained red and strung taut at about head height between clay stakes, and it runs out of sight in a grid of enormous squares. It marks the level where the floor is meant to be. Everything here stands below its own intended surface. The water seems to know. Pools lie with their surfaces tilted, leaning up toward the line, and in the gutters the runoff climbs a hand's breadth uphill before it gives up and slides back.

The building never stops. A wall forty courses high stands dripping where the floor was bare an hour ago. An arch hangs in the steam with its keystone set and nothing under it, and its piers arrive afterward, course by course, from the top down. Stairs descend into floor that is laid only as the last step is reached. Dull orange fronts cross the plain at walking pace, firing whatever they pass over, turning grey to red. Behind them the rain returns, and whatever the front missed softens and sits down.

Nothing lasts long enough to weather. There are no ruins, only drafts. A fallen wall is a mound by the next rain and scored into brick again by the one after.

All this happens almost without sound. Clay swallows noise. There is the hiss of rain on hot brick and the tick of fired courses cooling. A wall coming down sounds like a heavy blanket being put on a table. Once in a long while, from a great distance, comes a single knock, a mallet blow that is never followed by a second.

Measurement does not hold. A knotted cord counted twice gives two numbers. A wall is longer walked along its north face than its south. Some bricks, broken open, are fired hard at the core and raw on the outside. Others carry the thumbprint on the underside, pressed from below. In one place a single print lies across a thousand bricks, two courses deep and as wide as a courtyard, and warm water runs in its whorls.

The only thing that grows is a grey nap on the dampest courses, finer than salt and softer. It wipes away with a touch. It comes back within the hour in the exact shape of the wipe.

The plumb-bobs do not agree with one another. Across a region days wide they all lean a few degrees off true, toward the same point on the plain. At that point the floor looks like floor anywhere, except for a circle a few paces across where the rain does not fall. The brick inside it is dry and smooth, with no print in any corner. Beside the circle sits a mould, filled and struck level, not yet turned out.
