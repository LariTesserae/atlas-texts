The basin sits in a col between two summits, above the last cloud. The cloud lies below the western lip as a flat grey floor with no other peaks standing out of it. The summits on either side rise another few hundred feet in broken gneiss, yet from the basin floor they seem to be looked down upon. The inner ear insists on it. Everything here is the highest thing.

The light is the blue of the hour before sunrise. A pallor stands behind the eastern ridge, the colour of the inside of a mussel shell, and it has the look of something about to arrive. Overhead the sky is dark enough for stars, and they are out, a few hard ones. Each stone has a faint shadow, and the shadows all lie toward the east, toward the light.

The floor is patterned ground. Over a very long time, frost has sorted the stones into polygons a few paces across, with coarse rubble shouldered out to the rims and fine grey grit domed in the centres. The sorting is finished. Every rim stone is bedded, rimmed with black lichen on its weather side, and locked against its neighbours. Scree fans lean against both summit walls at the one angle scree can hold. They are old fans, crusted over with map lichen in green-yellow rosettes as wide as two spread hands, growth that takes centuries at this height. Nothing here is fresh-broken. There is not one pale face of new rock anywhere in the basin.

And the basin roars. The roar is the first fact of the place and the largest. It sounds like a river in flood heard from inside its gorge, a full, grinding, white weight of sound that sits in the chest. Under it runs a single low note, the col itself sounding like a jar blown across the mouth. Over it, every few breaths, comes rockfall from the north wall, first the crack of a slab letting go, then the long clatter down the fan and the last small stones ticking to rest. Nothing is seen to fall. The fan does not grow.

There is no wind. Rime stands on the lee side of every stone in feathers as long as a finger, built into a gale that can be heard and not felt. They are fragile enough that a breath would take them off, and they are intact. The air on the skin is perfectly still, cold and slightly wet, like the air of a cave.

The rule of the basin shows first in the sound. At this height, happening is thin, the way air is thin, and there is not enough of it to go around. Sound has taken nearly all of it. Things here occur, but they do not add up.

Water is the plainest case. A seep comes out under a slab on the south side, darkening the rock, and drips from a moss lip into a tarn the size of a threshing floor. The drip is steady, about one drop to a heartbeat. The tarn has no outlet, and its edge sits at a line of orange lichen that has never been wetted or left dry. The water is black and unfrozen, though the grit a foot from it is iron-hard with frost. Each drop lands, and no ring goes out.

Snow falls here too, in fine dry grains like sifted salt, steadily, out of the clear dark sky. It can be felt on the face. The ground is bare.

A voice does not add to the roar, which is already full. A shout is felt in the throat and ribs and is not heard, and two stones struck together give only a jar in the wrists.

What grows keeps to the lee of the polygon rims. There are cushions of something tight and grey-green, hard as a fist, no taller than a knuckle. Black moss grows at the seep. One woolly plant, a snow lotus or its cousin, stands in a crack near the tarn, its bracts closed around a flower head like hands around a coal. It is in bud. The bracts are furred with rime.

Animals are fewer. A small black jumping spider lives among the cairn stones and eats whatever is blown up from the world below, a midge or a springtail or a winged seed. It turns to face anything that comes near, lifting its front legs. Above the col two yellow-billed choughs ride a circle with their wings set, never beating, on the wind that isn't there. They open their bills. After long listening their call can be found inside the roar, a thin, falling whistle that comes round again at the same point in the circle. Once, at the western lip, a blue sheep stands with her forefeet on the last rock and her head turned toward the cloud. She is chewing.

Someone has been here, with hands and nothing else. There are cairns, each a chest-high stack of flat stones chosen for flatness, unshaped. There are eleven, counted from anywhere. From any one cairn, eleven others are visible.

Near the tarn is a ring wall, knee-high, dry-laid, open to the east, big enough for two people to lie down inside out of the wind. The grit inside has been swept clear of stones. One flat slab is set as a seat, and its top is worn to a dull polish, slightly dished. The lichen that covers everything else in the basin has not taken on it.

The basin looks a mile across. From the ring wall to the tarn is forty paces. From the farthest cairn, small as a thumbnail against the eastern ridge, to the tarn is also forty paces. It is the same from the lip where the sheep stands. Walking it, the ground passes underfoot at the ordinary rate, the polygons go by one after another, far more than forty paces' worth, and the count comes out the same. The tarn is the middle of the basin, and everything is the same distance from the middle.

Going east, toward the pallor, the ridge gets no lower and the light gets no stronger. The shadows of the stones point ahead the whole way, like fingers.

The western lip is the only edge that behaves like an edge. The rock ends there in a clean step. Below it is the cloud, and below the cloud, by the feel of it, more height. The roar is no quieter here. It doesn't come from the basin's walls or carry out over the drop. It is the same loudness at the tarn, at the lip and inside the ring wall with hands pressed over the ears.

Along the top of the ring wall, on the left side of the opening, pebbles are set out in a row a thumb's width apart. They are white quartz, picked from the grey, and there are nine. The capstone has room for a tenth. The tenth lies in the grit at the foot of the wall, directly below the gap, feathered with rime on one side.
