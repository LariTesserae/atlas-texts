**The tableland**

There was a mountain here, and it finished. Whatever rock once held this height wore down grain by grain over a span long enough that even the wearing is over. What stayed is the plant.

It is one plant, a cushion, the kind that grows on cold high ground as a tight green dome. This one is the size of a district. Its surface is made of rosettes no bigger than lentils, packed so hard that walking on it sounds like knocking on a tabletop. It runs to the horizon in long low swells with the last shape of the vanished ridges still faintly in them. The green is hard, yellowing on the crowns and beaded with amber resin that smells of pine pitch and crushed parsley. A hand's depth under the living skin the stems are dead and brown, compressed to something like peat-brick. Nobody has reached the bottom of that, and there's no soil or stone anywhere.

The air is thin and cool, a few degrees above freezing by day. The daylight is plain and even, the sort that shows every rivet at a mile.

**Water**

There are no streams. Fog comes up around the rim before dawn and combs across the swells, leaving one bead on every rosette. By mid-morning the beads have gone into the plant. Rain is occasional and fine. In a few places the cushion has dimpled, and water stands there the colour of tea, a yard across. The town hangs mesh fences on the windward side to strip the fog into tanks for the boilers. Everything else stays merely damp. The resin stiffens at night and frost whitens the crowns until nine.

**The town**

It is a works town built on brick, iron and steam. It cuts the dead underlayer into blocks, shreds it, presses it and distils the resin, and it burns the offcuts to drive the engines that do the cutting. There are beam engines, a steam hammer, a shredding mill, a narrow-gauge line, and gas lamps on poles.

Its footprint could hold fifty thousand people, though there seem to be a few hundred, seen mostly as small figures crossing between buildings. The reason is that the cushion grows, slowly and from everywhere at once, and whatever stands on it is carried apart. The two sides of the original main street now face each other across four hundred yards of open green, each with its row of lamps. The tram rails are spliced every spring. Telegraph wires go taut and sing, then part. Buildings raised wall to wall now stand alone in the open, each with green lapped up to the windowsills. The plant skins over anything left still. Old machinery has become hummocks with a flywheel's curve showing. The rails run in a groove.

**The copies**

A rosette grows by dividing, and things left in the cushion long enough take up the habit. A second chimney appears first, shorter, standing by itself a hundred yards off. Nobody laid it and nothing feeds it, yet its brick is warm and a thread of smoke comes from the top. In later years a shed forms beneath it. The ledgers list one engine house, and there are four on the tableland. They have the same maker's stamp in the brick and the same crack through the same lintel. The farthest is only a doorframe standing in open green, with the full sound of a beam engine coming through it.

**Sound**

It is loud. The hammer, the shredders and the whistles would be enough, but the cushion is also hard on top and has nothing under it. Past the last lamp the plant curls under at the rim like the edge of a loaf, and below that is air. The mountain left and the plant kept its altitude. The whole tableland is therefore a drumhead. Every hammer stroke arrives in the shins a mile away, and wind on the swells makes a low continuous boom.

Sound that goes downward meets no bedrock. It leaves through the underside, and some of it returns late. The noon whistle comes up through the ground around four, a third lower. On still nights the day's shift replays from below, faint and in order, with now and then a stroke that nobody struck.

**What lives here**

Very little does. A grey finch nests in the warm brick of the unbuilt chimneys. A beetle the colour of the resin eats the resin. In the oldest quarry the cutters went too deep, and there is a hole there a few yards wide, fenced with rope. Fog rises through it at dawn in a column. On clear afternoons the view through it is sky, pale and very far down. About once a season something broad-winged passes across the hole, circling slowly beneath the tableland, and from the rope only its back can be seen.
