The shelf is about sixty years old. A flow came down to the coast, ran out into the shallows, and stopped, leaving a black apron of basalt barely three metres above the tide line. It is still sharp. The surface is clinker, loose and glassy and full of blisters, and it will open a boot sole in a season. Nothing has had time to round it. Where the roads were cut, the bulldozer scars still look fresh, and the crushed-rock fill has the same hard edges as the ground it was taken from.

On this shelf sits the station and the town that serves it, the whole of it walkable end to end in fifteen minutes. There is a turbine hall, long and windowless, in ribbed sheet steel gone the colour of weak tea. Beside it are four separator vessels, a switchyard behind chain-link, and two squat banks of cooling towers with fans turning in their throats. Then come a dozen short streets of concrete-block houses under tin roofs, with a canteen, a pipe shop, a clinic with one bed, and a football pitch of rolled cinder that nobody uses at midday.

The buildings stand far apart, because the wellheads are scattered where the heat is, and between them is open lava crossed by steam lines. The lines are fat silver-lagged pipes on steel trestles. Every hundred metres they jog out sideways in a square loop so the metal can lengthen and shorten without tearing itself loose. Much of the town is these pipes and the empty black ground under them.

Everything is wet. The cooling towers throw a plume that never detaches. It rises, flattens against the low coastal cloud, and comes back down as warm drizzle too fine to see and too slight to bother sheltering from. Condensate ticks from every flange and trap. Each hollow in the clinker holds a puddle, and the puddles do not dry. In the deeper cracks the groundwater is a forearm's reach down, brackish and bath-warm.

The outfall channel runs from the station to the sea in a straight concrete trough. The water in it is milky blue with dissolved silica and steams along its whole length. Pale crust has built up at the waterline in scalloped ledges, and below that the channel walls are furred with mats of orange and dark green algae. The algae is the most vigorous plant life in the place.

Otherwise there are ferns, small stiff ones, rooted in cracks where a little grit has gathered. Grey lichen grows on the seaward faces of the rock. Moss covers the cooling tower basins on the side the drift falls. A row of ornamental shrubs outside the administration block has been replanted several times and is currently alive.

The heat comes from above and below. The air is thick and still under the cloud, and the ground gives back warmth through the soles of the feet. Handrails near the separators are wrapped in rope, and people learn not to lean on bare metal. The dogs know where the buried lines run and sleep on the slabs above them in a row.

The light is dim and brownish, with the sun never more than a brighter patch in the overcast. The sodium lamps along the pipe roads are left on around the clock because there is no hour that doesn't want them. At night the plume glows orange from beneath, and the town is hardly darker than it was at noon.

The sound is constant. The turbine hall gives off one low note that is felt in the sternum before it is heard. The wellhead silencers, concrete stacks packed with rock, roar like surf that doesn't break. The fans add a higher wash on top. People here talk with their hands, or put their mouths near each other's ears.

Over all of it, the birds scream. Swifts nest by the hundreds under the trestles, in the angle where the lagging meets the steel. Night herons and egrets stand at intervals along the outfall like fence posts, because the warm water is packed with tilapia that someone released decades ago. Black crabs occupy every crack near the shore and rattle away under the pipes when a truck passes. A gecko waits on the wall beneath every lamp, and around every lamp there is a cloud of moths for it to eat. Bats work the lit corridors at dusk. Kites circle the canteen roof. Flies graze the algal mats in a shimmering layer.

Nothing here changes. The station runs at baseload, and the needles in the control room sit on the green marks someone painted on the glass years ago. There are no seasons at this latitude, and the cloud holds the temperature within a couple of degrees between day and night. The horn goes at six, two and ten. One shift walks out along the pipe road and another walks in, and the herons do not look up.
