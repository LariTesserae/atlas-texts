**The lower reservoir**

The reservoir lies four hundred metres under the hills, and all of it was poured. The floor is concrete, the columns are concrete, and the vaults are shotcrete over rock bolts, so no bare stone shows anywhere. The halls run in a grid of bays sixty metres on a side, bay after bay, linked by galleries wide enough to float a barge through. From any column the lamp rows run off in four directions and blur into haze before they reach a wall. There are walls, but a boat with an outboard takes most of a day to find one.

It is young. The board marks from the formwork are still crisp on the columns, with wood grain printed in the cement. The stencilled grid numbers haven't flaked. The expansion joints are black and glossy. Where water seeps through the vault crowns, lime has started to come down in soda straws, hollow and white, the longest about a finger's length. There are no stalagmites, because the floor under them is nearly always under water.

**The tide**

The water is clear and faintly green, and it stays at nine degrees all year. It moves on a schedule set by a city nobody down here can see. When the city wakes and switches things on, the machine hall at the north end opens its valves. The upper lake falls through the turbines and arrives here through six draft tubes as broad, boiling upwellings. The level climbs the depth gauges painted on every column, a decimetre every few minutes, until the air is a two-metre slot under the vaults. Late at night, when power is cheap, the same machines run backwards and drink it down again. By four in the morning the high bays to the south stand empty, wet and shining, and the deep bays hold thirty metres of still water with lamps burning in sealed housings far below the surface.

**Sound**

It is loud nearly all the time. The discharge is a roar felt in the sternum before it is heard, carried through water and concrete alike. The trash racks hum when the pumps draw. The echo takes twelve or fourteen seconds to die, so every sound lies on top of the last.

A siren runs before each changeover. Then there is about twenty minutes of something close to quiet, with only drip and the tick of cooling steel. Then the main valves seat with a single boom that travels the length of the halls, strikes the far end, and comes back noticeably later and softer. After that the whole basin rocks for an hour. It is a slow slosh with a forty-minute period, visible only as the waterline creeping up and down the gauge marks.

**Light and weather**

The ventilation shafts are two hundred metres of lined bore. Each drops a pale disc of daylight onto the water, and they whistle. On a rising tide the reservoir pushes its air out through them, and the gratings rattle. On a falling tide it pulls the outside down.

In summer that air is warm and wet. It meets the cold concrete and turns to fog, which collects under each shaft and drifts along the lamp rows in banks, turning every light into an orange ball. The vault crowns sweat until it rains, a steady, sourceless drizzle over hundreds of bays at once. In winter the air comes down colder than the water, and the surface steams.

**What lives here**

Hardly anything grows. Each lamp above the high-water line has a green halo of algae on the concrete around it, exactly the shape of its beam. Under the shafts, where real daylight reaches, there is moss on the ledges and here and there a hart's-tongue fern rooted in a joint. Rafts of torn weed from the upper lake come through the turbines and circle for weeks, browning. Nothing roots on the floor, which is bare slab under a first skin of silt a few millimetres thick.

The animals came down the same way as the water, and they have done well. They are spread thin through all that volume and gather wherever there is a reason to:

- **At the submerged lamps:** each one holds a slow ball of perch working the plankton that the light pulls in.
- **In column shadows:** pike hang there, one to a column, and have grown heavy on roach.
- **On the silt:** crayfish cross it and leave stitched tracks that the next tide erases.
- **On gratings, ladders, and gauges:** striped mussels crust the lower rungs. None is bigger than a thumbnail yet, and all of them are already on a scraping schedule.
- **At the lamps above water:** midges hatch out of the silt in grey clouds and go to the light. Spiders have webbed every lamp cage so thickly that the cages look bandaged.
- **Over the surface:** bats roost in the shaft collars by the thousand and trawl the water at dusk, dragging their feet through it.

**The schedule**

Everything here has learned the timetable. The discharge bays are also the warmest, a degree or two above the rest, from the machine hall's cooling water. When the siren sounds for generation, eels are already stacked there in the dark water, and the pike have drifted north. They are waiting for what the turbines send down, which is small fish from the lake, stunned or in pieces, arriving in the first white surge.

When the siren sounds for pumping, the shoals turn and leave the high bays ahead of the falling water. They head for the sumps, passing columns numbered in fresh paint. Behind them the floor drains to a shine, and the rain keeps falling on it.
