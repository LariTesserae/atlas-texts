The stair goes down ninety-odd steps, and the sound meets you around the fortieth: a slow double thump you feel through the handrail, and over it a shrilling so dense it registers first as pressure in the ears. The handrail is wood. So are the treads, the wall the rail is bolted to, and the bolts. All of it was iron and brick once. A root system came into the masonry long ago and ate it grain by grain, laying down wood in the same shape as it went. The stairwell still has mortar lines and the rail still has its foundry seam, but none of it would ring if you struck it.

At the bottom is a pump hall the size of a church nave, barrel-vaulted, perhaps twenty-five metres to the crown. The ribs, the tile courses, and the arched recesses for lamps are all reproduced in pale, close-grained root, sooted dark on its face. The floor is a mat of roots, polished along the old walking lines and springy everywhere else. It is as warm as a laundry. The air smells of toasted sawdust and wet bark, with a little vinegar under it.

There is more room than you expect. A dozen structures stand on the floor with wide dark yard between them: an engine house, a boiler house, a dynamo shed, a row of six workers' cottages, and an office with a clock in its gable. Narrow-gauge rails, also wood now, run from one to the next. In the middle of the yard you can stand with forty paces of bare mat on every side and nothing near you, inside one of the loudest rooms there is.

The light comes from three places:

- **The bulbs.** A string of them crosses the hall on a sagging line. About a third still burn, brown and weak, and each sits inside a ball of moths as big as a haystack. Where the light reaches the wall the wood has put out leaves, white and thin as onion skin, the only leaves in the place. Geckos the colour of candle wax wait among them.
- **The fireboxes.** Orange bars show through the boiler-house gratings.
- **The shaft.** At the far end a ventilation shaft goes up through the vault, and at its top is a coin of grey that is sometimes daylight and sometimes not.

The engines are iron, the one thing the root did not replace. It keeps them instead, under a skin of amber lacquer renewed wherever it cracks. There are three beam engines, and two run at any time. They lift water from a sump under the floor and drive it up the rising mains. The mains are living vessels now, as thick as barrels. Put a hand on one and you feel the water go up in gulps toward the crown of the plant, somewhere overhead in the weather. Very little of that water stays. The mains sweat and the wheel ruts hold puddles, but the hall is only damp.

The fuel is frass. The vault above the boiler house is honeycombed with galleries, and in them live longhorn beetles as thick as a thumb, in numbers that make the wood look as if it is crawling. They chew, and their dust sifts down through chutes the root has grown for the purpose into hoppers. A screw driven off the line shaft feeds the hoppers to the fires. The wood grows back at the rate it is eaten.

Everything here keeps to that arithmetic. The drive belts are lianas grafted into loops, and they lengthen exactly as fast as the pulleys wear them. The bearings run in sap, and ants work them in unbroken columns, carrying off the sweet overflow and leaving the journals bright. Nothing gains on anything else, and by the look of it nothing has for a very long time.

Most of the noise is crickets. They are pale and long-horned, and they pack every fissure of the walls from floor to crown by the million. They sing on the engine's downstroke, so the din has a pulse: thump, shriek, thump, shriek. Under that come the slap of the belts, the hiss of the valve, and the dry clicking of swiftlets navigating the dark overhead.

Sometimes a hole opens in the sound, a patch of silence the size of a door that slides along the wall. That is a centipede as long as a forearm hunting under the bark, and the crickets go quiet wherever it passes. On a busy stretch of wall you can count four or five such holes drifting at once.

The hall keeps its own day, eleven hours and some minutes long, counted by the office clock, which is geared to the line shaft and has twigs for hands. All through that day the exhaust steam goes up into the vault and beads on the cooler crown. At the end of it a cam trips the whistle, and several things happen in order:

1. The blast shakes the vault loose and it rains for about four minutes, warm, hammering on wooden roofs that still carry the corrugations of the iron they replaced.
2. One engine sighs to a stop and the rested one takes up the stroke. The crickets lose the beat, and for a minute the hall is one flat roar until they find the new one.
3. Frogs start up in the gutters and cisterns. Midges hatch out of the wheel ruts in smoke-like columns.
4. The swiftlets come off the vault in thousands and feed through the lit balls of moths. Bats unpeel from the picture rails in the cottages and follow.
5. The whole mass funnels up the shaft, whatever hour it is outside. Sometimes they pour out into noon and sometimes into midnight. They are back within the hour either way, and the hall counts that as morning.

The cottage doors stand as they were left, ajar or shut, and will never move again, because the hinges have grown through. In one kitchen a wooden kettle sits on a wooden stove. In the office the ledger lies open on the desk. Its pages still lift, but they are veined now, and the last column of figures has healed over like a scar in bark.

By the engine house a grating is set in the floor. Below it is the sump, the one real body of water here, black and low, and it drops a hand's breadth with every stroke. Something long turns over in it between strokes. The water comes back from wherever it comes back from, and the engine takes it again.
