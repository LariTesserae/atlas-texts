From the crest of the old ridge the valley can't be seen, only heard. The roar stands up out of the dark like a second ridge: frogs, wings, clicking, a bass drone underneath that seems to come from the rock. Behind the crest it drops away as if a door had closed. In front there is nothing to look at. A lid of warm fog sits at ridge height most nights and takes the stars, the ground below is black basalt that returns no light, and three layers of leaves close whatever gap is left. A hand held at arm's length is a rumor.

The valley floor is a lava flow, a tongue perhaps eleven kilometers long and two wide that ran down between the ridges to the coastal flat. The grandmothers of the oldest people living watched it run. It has not had time to soften. The clinker fields are heaps of fist-sized rubble with edges that open a heel through hardened skin. The smoother sheets still carry their glassy rind, which breaks underfoot with the sound of thin ice. Pressure ridges stand cracked down their spines, and the cracks are clean, without rounding or fill. There is no soil in the usual sense. In places the rock is warm as a sleeping animal, and steam comes up through the rubble smelling of wet stone and struck flint.

Rain, when it comes, falls straight through. The flow is a sieve, and a downpour is gone into it before the leaves stop dripping. No stream runs anywhere on it, and the valley has no pond or even a puddle that lasts until morning. Everything lives on what the air gives up: fog combed out by fern fronds, steam condensing on the undersides of leaves, a steady private drip from each plant onto its own roots. Water stands only in small held places, such as the cupped bases of leaf rosettes, the crowns of tree ferns, and hollows in fallen trunks. Each holds a mouthful, a few tadpoles, and a mosquito larva twitching.

On this, a forest grows that has no business being so thick. Tree ferns stand shoulder to shoulder with their dead skirts interlocked. Figs have run their roots over the rock like poured wax, down every crack, and around each other. Climbers bind the crowns into one mat, and moss sleeves everything to the thickness of a forearm. To move is to push, and the growth springs back behind.

The forest is fed from underneath. The flow is hollow, run through with tubes where the lava drained out from under its own crust, and where the roofs have fallen there are skylights, ragged holes from the size of a basket to the size of a clearing. Cave birds roost in the tubes in numbers no one has a word for, and their droppings and their dead make the only real earth in the valley. The forest is rankest in rings around each hole and thins to moss and fern between.

The tubes breathe. When the weather outside is heavy, the flow draws air down through every opening, dragging fog, moths, and loose leaves into the holes. When the pressure drops it exhales, and a warm ammonia wind pours up out of the ground and stands as a fog column over each skylight. Each opening sounds a note according to its size and the length of tube behind it. The largest ones hum below hearing and are felt in the sternum. Crossing the flow means walking from one note toward the next.

The rest of the sound sits on top of that drone:

- **Frogs:** Thumbnail frogs call from every leaf cup at head height. There are thousands in earshot, each one a struck pebble, and together they make a solid pulsing sheet.
- **Insects:** Crickets in the clinker use the hollow rubble as a sounding box. Katydids rasp in the crowns.
- **Birds:** They navigate by clicking, and when they leave the tubes at dusk the noise is like gravel poured down a chute for most of an hour. The air over each skylight is so full of bodies that the wind of their wings rocks the ferns.
- **Bats:** They go out with the birds and through them.
- **Crabs:** Pale land crabs move across the flow in their own season, a carpet a kilometer wide. Their legs on the glass crust sound exactly like heavy rain, on a night when none is falling.

Something is always landing, on the neck, on the lips, in the ear. Ant columns pour over a foot and move on. Centipedes as long as a hand hunt the fern trunks. Nothing here is rare, and nothing is alone.

Nor does anything hold still. The trees have only cracks for anchor, so they tip constantly, with a long tearing of climbers and then a thud the rock passes along. The gap is full of seedlings within a season. Tube roofs give way without warning. There is a crack, a gust of exhaled air, and a new note joins the drone while the birds boil out of the fresh hole. Steam ground wanders too. A vent opens under a grove and cooks it grey in a week, and the old vent, gone cold, is under moss by the next rains. No route across the flow stays good for two years running.

People come anyway, from the ridges, for fledglings and crabs. They build nothing, since a shelter here would be swallowed or dropped into a tube before it paid for itself. They carry little:

- long poles with a bone hook lashed on, for lifting young birds off the skylight ledges
- basalt adzes
- net bags of bark cord
- gourds of water, because the valley will not give a drink

They do not bring fire. A flame draws moths in such weight that they smother it with their bodies, and after the moths comes everything that eats moths. So the work is done blind, by ear and by hand. Routes are marked for the fingers: notches cut in fern trunks at hip height, and cord lines strung from tree to tree and knotted in patterns that mean *hole left*, *hot ground*, *glass, go slow*. Scatters of knapped flakes lie where someone stopped to put a new edge on a tool. Heaps of crab shell lie by the routes, already furred with moss.

Toward morning the lid of fog goes from black to the dark grey of wet slate, which is as bright as it gets. The birds come back and fall into the skylights in streams, and each hole's note thickens and chokes as it fills. Somewhere in the middle of the flow a cord line runs taut from a notched trunk to the lip of a hole that was not there last season. Its far end hangs down into the warm breath, swinging, its knots still saying *straight on*.
