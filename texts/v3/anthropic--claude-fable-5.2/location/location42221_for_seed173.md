The pump hall sits under what was once a quay. The quay is seabed now, and the hall's roof is level with the mud around it, a brick rectangle with one long slot down the middle where the skylight lantern stood. The glass is gone, though the iron ribs remain. At low water the swell breaks across them.

Inside, the room is about forty paces long, fifteen wide, and as tall as a parish church. It was built to keep a dry dock dry, and every surface in it was made: glazed brick walls, cast-iron columns, plate floor, riveted galleries. There is no rock anywhere. The silt on the floor arrived through the culverts and counts as a later furnishing.

Light comes down through the lantern slot in one grey-green slab and lands along the centre line. It doesn't reach the corners. At midday you can make out the far wall as a paler darkness, and the rest of the time the hall ends wherever your eyes give up. The water is cold year-round, the cold of a cellar. In summer a warmer, hazier layer sits across the slot like a lid, and the water under it stays clear and chill.

Three pumps stand in a row down the hall with a lot of open water between them. Each is a volute casing the shape of a snail shell and the size of a cart, with a vertical shaft rising to an engine bed on the gallery above. The engines themselves are mostly absent. Steel went first, generations ago, thinning to lace and then to flakes, so the plate floor is gone except for its bearers. Under the silt the pump pit shows as black squares.

What remains is cast iron and bronze. The iron has finished changing. Seawater has taken the metal out and left the graphite, so the bedplates, columns and flywheels keep every contour they were poured with but are soft as pencil lead. A crab crossing a flywheel spoke leaves a grey scratch. The maker's name on each casing has slumped into a row of illegible bumps.

The bronze is still metal. The impellers inside the casings, the gauge rims and the valve seats have gone brown-green, polished wherever the flow rubs them.

The overhead crane came down at one end long ago and lies diagonally across the hall, its upper end still on its rail. It has been at that angle so long that the growth on it runs with the slope. Everything that could fall here has already fallen, and the room has settled into its final arrangement.

The hall is loud. In each end wall is a culvert mouth taller than a person, one leading toward the old dock and one toward the harbour, and the tide uses them as it would any pipe. On the flood the current runs the length of the hall, strong enough to keep a furrow scoured in the silt from mouth to mouth. The dock-side culvert drones, a low organ note that the brick box of the room picks up and holds.

On the ebb, with swell running, the bronze flap valve in the harbour culvert takes over. It is a disc as wide as a table, hung from a hinge pin worn oval in its lugs. Each surge lifts it and drops it back on its seat with a flat clap that is felt in the sternum before it is heard. The fallen crane answers a moment later with a knock where its girder shifts a finger's width against the wall.

At low water the surf on the roof ribs adds a rumble from above. Sound in water has no clear direction, so all of it seems to come from inside your own head.

The animals are plentiful but keep to the structures, leaving the middle of the room empty.

- **Pouting:** A shoal of a few hundred, copper-barred, hangs in the lee of the second pump and turns as one body when the current changes.
- **Pollack:** They patrol the lit slab under the lantern, looking up.
- **Congers:** Each of the three suction pipes holds one, grey head out, as thick as a thigh.
- **Lobster:** One lives under the first bedplate in a hollow it has dug to its own measurements.
- **Edible crabs:** They sit in pits in the silt with only the shell edge showing.
- **Ballan wrasse:** They work the columns, biting mussels off the iron with a crack that carries the length of the hall.
- **Smaller life:** Prawns line the gallery edge, starfish lie draped over the mussel clumps, and tube worms stand in scattered ones and twos on the dark brick.

Plumose anemones, white and orange, crowd the upper side of the crane girder where the flow is best. They are the nearest thing the hall has to foliage, and they are animals.

Actual plant life is meagre. A pink mineral crust of coralline algae covers the brick where the light slab touches the wall. A short fringe of red weed grows on the top rim of the tallest flywheel and the high end of the crane. A few kelp stalks are rooted on the lantern's lip, outside, leaning in. Below the gallery nothing grows that needs the sun.

The tide sets the schedule. While the water runs, everything faces into it and holds station behind iron. Twice in each tide comes slack water, about twenty minutes when the drone dies and the valve hangs shut. Fine silt begins to sift down through the light, and the room empties out of its shelters.

Crabs walk and the pouting shoal loosens and spreads across the floor. The congers come a body-length out of their pipes. In the quiet you can hear the fish themselves, the pollack grunting and in winter a cod drumming somewhere behind the third pump.

Some slacks a grey seal drops through the lantern slot, unhurried, and makes a single circuit of the hall. The shoal draws into a tight ball against the casing, and the seal takes one or two fish from its edge and leaves by the way it came.

Then the flap valve lifts off its seat, the furrow in the silt begins to smoke at its edges, and every fish in the room turns to face the harbour.
