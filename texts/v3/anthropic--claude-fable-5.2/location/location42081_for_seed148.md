The ground is a pan of grey silt a few feet above the tide line, and it is new. Nothing has sorted it. There are no pebbles, no layers, no cracks; press it and it takes the print and keeps it. No channel has been cut anywhere, so water stays where it lands, even on the few inclines, where it sits in beads on the tilt as if downhill had not been explained to it. The air is cold enough to feel in the teeth. Overhead the dark is flat and close, a sky with nothing yet hung in it. Eyes adjust for an hour and gain nothing.

Under the silt runs one rhizome, black, thick as a culvert, laid out in a grid. Where it runs the ground is lifted into something like kerbs. From its nodes rise the stems, green-black, jointed, fluted, and hollow from end to end. Each is a room wide and stands five or six joints high, with a solid diaphragm at every joint for a floor and a collar of dark papery teeth outside for a cornice. They grow shoulder to shoulder and fuse into terraces. The surface is glassy with silica, and a palm dragged along a wall comes away scoured. At each joint a whorl of thin green rods stands out stiffly, like railings or brackets. There are six streets, a square, and a works at the low end with one stack. The whole of it could be walked around in a quarter of an hour.

Walking through it is another matter. The interiors hold more room than their walls enclose, and so do the streets once entered. A bat leaving one side of a stem-chamber beats steadily for a long while before the far wall arrives. Nearness has not been put in yet. Everything inside the grid is kept at a great remove from everything else, and the remove is empty: cold air, and a faint smell of cut cucumber and wet slate.

The works runs on root pressure. In the cold hours the rhizome forces water up every stem, and at the low end a cluster of stems narrows into a throat that drives a wheel. The wheel turns a shaft and the shaft turns belts of plaited sheath-fibre. One belt turns a dynamo with a cast-iron housing, real iron, with a raised rectangle on its side where a maker's name would be cast. The rectangle is smooth. Wire, actual drawn copper, goes out from the works along the whorls of rods, street by street, hung on knobs of silica the plant extrudes for it. Nothing on the site says where the copper came from. The other belts turn machines that are being run without load: a row of spindles with no thread, a press that comes down on an empty bed every eleven seconds and lifts again. The stack gives off nothing. The noise of all this, close to, is a soft slap of belting and the tick of the press. Two doors away it is thinner than a watch under a pillow, because sound also has to cross the distance and gets there worn out.

At the nodes, where the wire touches, the plant has blown small glass blisters with a filament in each. They burn orange. The light stays inside the glass. A bulb can be seen from the far end of a street, a clean hard point, and it shows nothing of the wall it is fixed to or the ground below it. The town, seen whole, is a few hundred orange points in rows, a plan drawn in dots on black. The moths are known only by interruption: a lamp blinks, and blinks again, and that is a wing going between.

There are a great many animals. Snails are the most numerous, small and dark-shelled, hundreds of thousands of them, grazing the film of algae off the silica. Their rasping is the commonest sound in the place, a fine dry fret like sandpaper on a fingernail, heard one snail at a time. Their trails never cross. Shrews work the kerbs, pipistrelles work the streets, tawny owls take the shrews, and eels live in the water that leaves the wheel. Each of these is alone in an enormous amount of room. An owl drops on a shrew from a body-length above, and the drop takes minutes; the owl falls with its feet out, patient, through air that keeps being added to, and the shrew usually has time to finish what it was eating and go. When a kill is made it is made slowly and the owl arrives tired. A female moth calls from inside a ground-floor chamber for nine or ten cycles of the damp before a male, who started from the opposite wall, reaches her. Everything that lives here has adjusted its appetite to the commute. The shrews are fat. The owls are lean and long-lived and lay one egg.

The animals also use parts of the town that have not grown yet. Above the square, where no stem reaches, a row of bats hangs head-down from nothing, evenly spaced, at the height of a fourth joint. On the east street eleven owls sit along a level line in the open air, shifting their feet now and then on a ridge that isn't there. They are not hovering. Their weight is on something. Over weeks the stems come up underneath, joint by joint, and meet them, and the collar of teeth closes around a perch that was already in use. The plant seems to take its measurements from where the animals wait. At the stair-end of the north terrace no stair has grown, but the snails go up it anyway, in a diagonal file through the dark, each trail a wet thread that hangs a moment in the air and dries.

The weather is made on the premises. When root pressure peaks, every tooth of every collar pushes out one drop of water. For a short while the whole town is beaded, and then the drops let go, floor by floor, and a thin cold rain falls from the buildings onto the streets under a dry sky. The wheel quickens. The bulbs go from orange to yellow inside their glass. All the snails come out at once, and the rasping rises to a hiss, and the pipistrelles go through the falling drops after the moths that the brighter lamps have stirred. Then the pressure slackens, the press slows to one stroke in twenty seconds, the bulbs sink to the brown of a hot wire, and the walls dry rough again. This happens at intervals regular enough that the eels come up for it beforehand.

In the square the paving is splitting. Pale shoots without any green in them are pushing through, each capped with a blunt cone, and from the cones a grey-green dust is sifting down. Every grain of it carries four arms that curl when dry and straighten when damp, so with each cycle the dust walks. It moves in low drifts down the streets, a hand's width per rain, against no wind. It collects where the grid stops, along the outer kerbs, and there the silt has begun to hump in straight lines, a seventh street marked out as a raised seam with nothing standing on it yet. Two owls already sit above it.

The water that leaves the wheel runs out past the last kerb onto the open flat and has nowhere to go. It lies on the silt in a single rope, forty yards long and a foot wide, unspread, with a rounded back. The eels live in it in single file, nose to tail, with a long gap of clear water kept between each one and the next. Where they have turned and turned again at the far end, the silt has given a little. There is a groove there now, about a finger deep, with a soft lip, and the rope of water leans into it. It is the first slope the ground has.
