The mountains that once stood here are gone. What remains is a tableland the width of a small continent, planed so flat by its own age that the rock no longer decides anything. The rock lies somewhere below, a black shield seen only at the rim, where it shows as a thin dark band beneath forty metres of compacted brown fibre. Everything above that band is plant, alive at the top and dead underneath, and the dead part is slowly fermenting. A good part of the plateau's height is plant.

The ferment is why it is warm. At this altitude the air ought to bite, and above the knee it does, a little, at night. But the ground gives off a steady animal heat, the heat of a compost heap the size of a country. Sit down and it comes up through you. The warmth lifts moisture, the moisture becomes a lid of cloud, and the lid rains back onto the place it rose from. It is mostly the same water going round, and the plateau makes its own weather this way. Clear sky here is a rumour. Noon is a grey-green brightening with no shadows in it, and the rest of the day is dimmer than that.

There are no streams in the interior. Water does not run across the mat. It soaks outward through it, taking years to cross, and the journey stains it the colour of strong tea. Press a hand into the surface and the hollow fills. Lift the hand and the hollow takes an afternoon to rise back. Only at the rim does the water finally let go, along a fringe of thousands of thin falls dripping from the overhanging lip. None of them reach the bottom as anything but mist.

The surface is cushions, each one a tight dome of small leaves, shouldered against its neighbours, in every green from near-black to the yellow of old bruises. The trees stand out of the cushions, though "stand" flatters them. They are squat and broad, their trunks mostly a braid of their own old roots, and their crowns are flattened against the cloud. From every branch hang grey filaments that comb the fog and drip. Things grow on the filaments, and smaller things grow on those. No bark, soil or bare wood shows anywhere. Break a twig and the inside is already green.

It is quiet. The mat swallows sound the way it swallows footprints, and the animals have stopped bothering with voices. There are a great many of them. Broad-footed grazers move in loose herds, moss growing along their spines so that a resting herd is a field of hummocks until one of them breathes. Thumb-sized amphibians live in the cushions by the thousand, and none of them call. Pale birds walk more than they fly. Something long and low travels the tree crowns and is only ever seen as the filaments swinging after it has passed.

They speak through the ground. A grazer stamps, and the tremor goes out through the wet fibre slowly, at walking pace or a little faster. A herd's approach is felt in the soles most of a day before it arrives. The amphibians answer with a faint collective shiver, like a hand resting on a purring animal. Standing still, the listening is done with the ankles. The only sounds in the air are the dripping and, a few times a year, a long groan below hearing as some district of the mat settles.

Where a foot breaks the surface, the bruised rot underneath glows a weak blue-green that lasts about an hour. So at night the plateau is crossed with fading lines: the heavy dotted track of a grazer, the stitched trail of a bird, your own prints going back the way you came and dimming at the far end.

The rule everything here obeys is that stillness becomes plant. A season without moving is enough. Some hummocks have the outline of a shoulder and a lowered head, old grazers that stopped walking. The mat rises about a finger-width in a lifetime, and in wet centuries it swells so that the whole horizon lifts by the height of a body. In drier ones it sinks back.

Someone has been here, with hands and nothing else. Living stems have been bent and tied in plain overhand knots to make arches, and the arches have gone on growing. The newest knots sit at head height. Older ones are at the knee, then the ankle. Reach down into the cushions beneath an arch and there are more knots below, tied in stems long dead, one under another as far as the arm will go.

There are stones, too, which have no business up here. They are pale pebbles carried from the rim, weeks of walking away, and set out in a line at intervals of a few paces. Each sits in its own dimple, sinking. The line runs straight toward the interior, and its far stones are only dents in the moss.

In one place there is a ring of trees trained inward until their crowns fused, forming a dome big enough for a dozen sleepers. Inside, the floor is fibre trodden flat and nearly dry, and it is the warmest spot for days in any direction. No one is there. Above the entrance, one of the knots in the stems is still pale where the bark was creased.
