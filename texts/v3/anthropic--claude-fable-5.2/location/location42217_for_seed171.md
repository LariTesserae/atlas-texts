The first thing is the noise, and it never stops being the first thing.

Most of it comes from below. There is a continuous grinding roar, like a glacier calving without pause, and over it a higher percussion of millions of sharp reports a second. Each one is a column of water snapping inside a vessel as wide as a well shaft. Ordinary plants make this sound too, too faint and too high to hear. Here the vessels are enormous and they move rivers. The cracks overlap into something like hail on sheet metal, and beneath them is the bass of the pumping itself, a throb that would be felt in the teeth before it was heard.

The land making this sound is plant all the way down. It is a plateau the size of a continent and two kilometres thick, with its surface up in cool, thin air a few degrees above freezing. There is no forest standing on ground here, because there is no ground. Trunks press against each other and fuse. Branches meet, graft, and become beams. There are no gaps between things, only places where one tissue becomes another: bark into root, root into duct, duct into a wall of pale wood forty metres across. To move through it would mean squeezing along seams.

The leaves are black. They are thick, waxed, and stacked in so many layers that they take nearly everything the sky offers. What reaches the interior is a brownish-green dusk at noon. Above the canopy sits the cloud, which the plateau makes itself. Every morning the mass exhales, the vapour condenses in the cold, and the lid closes. It has not opened in a long time. Weather fronts form over the middle of the plateau and take days to reach its margins.

It rains almost constantly, and the rain never lands on anything but leaf. Water is caught within metres of falling, drawn in, and carried the rest of the way inside the plants. Where it escapes, it collects in the forks of the great junctions as cold, tea-dark pools. These overflow down the bark in sheets, so every vertical surface shines and drips. The air smells of cold sap, flint, and the ozone that sinks down after lightning.

Deep in the mass, the pale wood turns grey, then glassy. The lowest strata are turning themselves to stone, packing their own dead cells with silica and carbonate. They are laying down the bedrock that the layers above will eventually stand on. This stone is new in a way stone almost never is. Its edges are sharp, and nothing has weathered it. There is not one grain of sand on the whole plateau, and no soil, scree, or silt, because nothing has yet been broken down. The terrain above is provisional. Domes swell where growth runs fast, basins sag where the lower layers are still settling, and a ridge can come up in a season.

Nothing lives here that is not plant. Every leaf is whole, because nothing has ever bitten one. Nothing rots either. Dead tissue is resorbed or sent downward to be mineralised, on schedule.

That schedule is the only sign that someone meant this. There is no machine anywhere, no marker, no seam of metal. But every branch leaves its stem at the same angle. The lower strata carry loads that should crush wood to pulp, and they hold. And several times a day the whole plateau surges at once, every stem lengthening by a finger's width in a single instant. No signal moving through sap or fibre could cross a continent that fast. It simply happens everywhere together.

The sound of that instant has to travel at the speed of sound. The surge close by arrives as one detonation, a crack like a hull splitting. Then comes the same instant from a kilometre away, then from ten, then from a hundred. One moment is smeared into a roar that takes most of an hour to drain back into the ordinary thunder of the pumping. After that, a pressure lingers in the air from the far side of the plateau.

At night the pumping slackens and the stone-making takes over. It is a brittle, ceaseless crackle from below, like lake ice in a hard frost. Lightning walks around inside the cloud and strikes the canopy often. The struck crowns do not burn. They take the charge, and the leaves around them stand stiffer for an hour. Each flash shows the black canopy for a moment, swell after swell of it with no edge.

In one basin, where the canopy has thinned over a floor of fresh grey stone, the overflow from a pool has run along the same line for several weeks. It is a finger wide, and it makes no sound that can be heard beneath the roar. It has not yet cut anything, but it is the plateau's first stream.
