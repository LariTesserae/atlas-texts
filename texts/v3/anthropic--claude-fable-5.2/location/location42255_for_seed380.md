The way in is an iron door in the retaining wall behind the tram sheds, then twenty-six steps down. Daylight follows as far as the first landing. Hart's-tongue ferns grow from the mortar there in a fringe that ends exactly where the daylight does, and below that the hall has its own light.

An enamel plate over the inner arch still reads LOW LEVEL STATION, white on a blue gone chalky. The hall is forty paces long and fifteen wide, a brick barrel vault carried on two rows of cast-iron columns. It was built for four pumping engines and holds one. Where the other three stood there are granite bedplates with the holding-down bolts still upright in them, each bolt wearing a small cap of rust. Between the bedplates lies open floor of blue brick and iron chequer plate, under a hand's depth of clear, cold water. Most of the room is the space between things.

Twenty-four lamp fittings hang from the vault on runs of conduit. Six burn, yellow, each in a wire cage. Beneath each working lamp, on whatever happens to lie below it, there is a round patch of liverwort and thread moss as wide as the pool of light. Nothing green grows between the patches. At the north end a panel of pavement lights is set into the footway overhead, glass prisms in an iron grid. The glass has gone amethyst from a century of sun, and it lets down a violet-grey daylight that lands on the water as a block of faint squares. The shadows of people walking above cross the squares, but the sound of their feet does not arrive.

Water comes in through the walls. The glazed brick weeps at every joint up to shoulder height. Above it the common brick is dark and soft-faced, and the lime leached from its mortar has come down in cream-coloured curtains over the tiles. One curtain covers the lower half of a notice, so that it reads only NO PERSON SHALL. Thin stone straws hang from the vault along the joint lines, each holding a drop, and the drops fall so far apart that each one is heard separately. The air is nine degrees and saturated. The columns bead and breath shows.

All of this finished happening long ago. The rust blisters on the columns grew to a certain size and stopped. The flowstone thickens too slowly to matter. A path worn into the blue brick runs from the stair to the engine and from the engine to the office, dished to the width of a boot, and it gets no deeper.

The engine is No. 3, a vertical three-throw ram pump. It was built for steam and converted to electric drive in the same decade that someone installed the slate switchboard with its knife switches and browned copper busbars. The board has one ammeter, and its needle rests just above zero. The motor drives through a reduction gear so deep that the crank takes eleven minutes to come round. At the top of the stroke there is a long intake sigh. At the bottom a leather-seated valve closes with the sound of a book set down on a table. For the eleven minutes between there are only the drips and a hum felt through the chequer plate more than heard. The oil cups are full, and their brass lids are bright, though the film of lime on the handrail beside them is unbroken.

The water in the hall does not lie level. Each bay between columns has its own gauge staff, enamelled in feet and tenths. The staffs were bolted on by different contractors in different decades, working from different datums, and each carries a red-painted mark for the level to be kept. In each bay the water stands at that bay's mark. This leaves the second bay a finger's breadth higher than the third. Where they meet there is no sill or weir. The water steps down along a straight line, and the edge is clean and rounded like the edge of thick plate glass. A shed moth wing drifts to the line, tips over it, and drifts on.

The station was built to hold a level, and it has held one for so long that holding has become the property of the room, ahead of the pump. Instruments here sit upstream of what they measure. The pressure gauge on the rising main reads forty, and the main complies.

The superintendent's office is a glazed timber box on a brick plinth, the only dry cubic yards in the place. Inside hangs a tally slate ruled in white paint. A sign-writer lettered the first columns DATE, LEVEL and STROKES. Other hands added more in chalk: NEWTS, EELS, BATS, SNAILS (EST.). The bottom row is fresh, and its figures are correct. Count the bats and there are eighty-eight. No one is seen to write them. In very wet weeks the damp reaches the slate and the chalk in a column softens and runs. For those weeks that animal is uncertain in the hall, sometimes more of them and sometimes fewer, until the figure dries firm.

Animals far outnumber every other kind of presence here. The room still looks nearly empty, because each kind is spaced out evenly and none of them makes a sound.

- **Newts:** They lie on the floor of every bay, palmate and crested, about an arm's length apart. They rise one at a time to breathe and sink back to the same brick.
- **Eels:** They come up through the sump grating beside the engine and cross the hall unhurried. They take the step between bays like a rope drawn over a table edge.
- **Snails:** Several thousand graze the algae on the glazed walls and leave pale winding tracks. They work the gauge staffs first, so the numerals stay clean while the tile beside them greens over.
- **Spiders:** One cave spider lives in each lamp cage, with an egg sac hung beside the bulb like a smaller, unlit lamp.
- **Woodlice:** White ones walk the flowstone.
- **Frogs:** One sits on each rusted bolt cap of the empty bedplates, and none of them calls.
- **Bats:** They hang along the crown of the vault at intervals as regular as the rivets in the columns. At dusk they leave by the stair in single file, which takes a quarter of an hour and sounds like cloth being drawn slowly through a ring.
- **Moths:** The motor casing is the one warm object in the room, and moths cover it wing to wing like scale. They are the only crowd in the hall.

In the last bay, under the pavement lights, the red mark on the staff has flaked away. The water there has no instruction. It stands slightly domed, higher in the middle than at the columns. When the valve closes at the bottom of the stroke its surface shivers, and the violet squares break up and take a long time to come back together. The newts do not lie in that bay, but the snails are on the staff and have cleaned the bare enamel where the mark used to be.
