From above there is almost nothing to find. The plateau is the stump of something that was once much higher, worn down over a span that makes the word "mountain" feel like a rumor. What remains is a tableland of pink-grey quartzite planed into pavements and low knuckles, with coarse sand lying in the joints and a dark mineral varnish on every upward face. No river has crossed it in any memory that matters. The old valleys survive only as wide, shallow swales where the wind slows down.

The air is warm and a little thin. Vultures turn level with the ground, because the ground is where the sky starts.

Then there is a crack. It is eight paces wide at most and runs a few hundred paces before pinching shut at both ends. Its edges are rounded like the lip of an old bowl. Fig crowns poke out of it at ankle height, dusty green, as if a hedge had been planted in a trench. A sound comes out of it too, a steady, thick hum that you feel in the chest before you understand you are hearing it.

**The pocket**

Down in the slot, under the southwest wall, the rock has been hollowed into an overhang. It is forty paces long and twelve deep at the deepest, and the ceiling is three people high at the mouth, sloping until it meets the floor at the back. Direct sun never enters. The light is secondhand, bounced off the opposite wall, and it arrives the color of weak tea in the morning and of embers by late afternoon. The ceiling is black with a thousand years of small fires.

Into this one room a town has been packed.

It is a town of stores and hives more than of houses. Clay granaries stand shoulder to shoulder on stone feet. They are man-high, shaped like fat seeds, and each has a thatch cap and a wooden plug door tied shut with bark cord. Between them run alleys one shoulder wide. Above them, on platforms of lashed poles, lie the hives. These are hollowed log cylinders and long clay tubes stacked like firewood, hundreds of them, each end stopped with a woven disc sealed in dung and propolis. Higher still, mortared straight to the rock, are rows of round-mouthed pots for pigeons. Notched logs serve as ladders. Everything is made with stone adzes, bone awls, fire, fiber and hands. The handprints are still in the plaster, and some are very small.

The town is also built on itself. New bins stand on the stumps of old bins. The floor is a compacted pale flour of rock dust, dung, chaff, wing scales and ash, and it has risen so far that the oldest walls at the back are only knee-high now. Their doorways have become niches, where geckos lay eggs.

**What grows**

No one here has ever seen water arrive. Nothing drips or seeps, and there is no stain line on the rock. What grows does so by other arrangements.

- **The figs:** They send roots down the slot wall like poured wax, pale and fused, and the roots vanish into the joint at the bottom toward some depth nobody has followed.
- **The ledge plants:** Aloes crowd every ledge in fat grey rosettes. Candelabra spurges stand in the rubble at the slot's ends. Caper vines hang in curtains over the mouth of the overhang, and they dim it further.
- **The roof gardens:** Every granary cap has become a mat of stonecrops and small succulents rooted in old thatch and pigeon droppings.
- **The resurrection plants:** On the rock lip above, they lie curled and brown as dead fists all day. For one hour at dawn the cold stone sweats a film of dew, and they open green. By the time the light turns they have closed again.
- **The lichens:** Orange, sulfur and grey-green crusts cover whatever is left.

For a place without water it is startlingly green. All of it is fed from below, by that one hour of dew, and by the steady fall of dung.

**Who lives here**

The people are the smallest population. A few keepers sleep on a platform near the mouth, and others climb up from somewhere lower with gourds on their backs. The town belongs, by count, to everything else.

Bees come first. The hum is theirs, and the overhang is shaped like a cupped hand behind it, so the sound doubles. Its pitch climbs with the heat of the day, and the keepers can tell the hour by it with their eyes shut. Swifts glue their nests to the ceiling with spit and tear through the slot in shrieking packs, close enough to move your hair. Pigeons clap out of the pots in bursts. Rock hyraxes sun on the far wall in heaps and squabble, and their latrine ledges are streaked white and amber with crystallized urine, which the keepers chip off and trade as medicine.

Lizards with blue heads do push-ups on the granary caps. Skinks pour through the thatch. The dust floor between the alleys is pocked with antlion pits, hundreds of small perfect funnels, each with something patient at the bottom. Ant columns run the pole lashings like a second set of cords. Darkling beetles work the chaff. Every corner has a spider's web gone grey and heavy with dust, and every web has wax moths in it. A long brown snake lives among the grain bins and is stepped over, never struck, because of the mice. In the low crack at the very back, where the ceiling comes down to meet the floor, the bats hang in a packed, ticking, ammonia-smelling mass.

**The day**

- **Dawn:** The dew comes and the brown fists on the lip open. The swifts leave in one long scream.
- **Mid-morning:** The first bees go out. Soon there are thick ropes of them rising from the slot and bending toward whatever is flowering on the plateau. Bee-eaters wait on the fig tips and take their share.
- **Noon:** The plateau above shimmers, and dust devils wander across it and collapse. In the pocket the air is warm and close, and smells of wax, fig sap, pigeon and hot musk. A falcon that nests on the opposite wall drops through the slot. The pigeons explode, the hyraxes shriek, and the whole town flinches at once and then resettles.
- **Afternoon:** Most days in the hot season, clouds build. They go dark and trail grey veils of rain that fade out high in the air and never touch the rock. Thunder comes anyway, enormous in the slot. Wind follows, and it blows a low note across the crack as breath does across a jar. It brings grit, and everything shuts its eyes.
- **Dusk:** The bats come out as smoke would, a pouring that takes a quarter of an hour. The geckos start chirping. A genet comes down the fig roots, and the pigeons shuffle in their pots.
- **Night:** The rock gives back the day's heat until morning, and the bees fan through the dark, sounding like a distant river.

**How things work here**

Nothing is poured out. Water comes up on backs and is counted by the gourd. Clay can only be mixed in the cool season, when enough water can be spared, so new bins are built then. The rest of the year, cracks are patched with wax, dung and spit. Hands are cleaned with ash and sand.

Fire is kept to a fist-sized coal in a pot because of the thatch and the wax. Smoke is mostly used for talking to the bees: a little calms them, and too much loses a colony.

Nothing rots. A dead swift under the ceiling becomes a light, perfect husk. Chaff lasts for generations. Sweeping is the only way anything leaves, so little does, and the floor keeps rising slowly toward the black ceiling.
