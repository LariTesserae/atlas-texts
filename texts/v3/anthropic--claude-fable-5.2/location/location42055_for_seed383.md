The plateau above is flat and dry and has four hundred holes in it. Below them lies a single low cavern about three kilometers across, dissolved out of gypsum long before anyone cut into it. The roof hangs twenty to forty meters over the floor and is thin enough now that in places it has fallen through, so the old round light-wells sit among ragged newer skylights the size of threshing floors. Under every opening stands a column of sun. The floor is white, the pillars are white, the ceiling is white, and the light that comes down is thrown back up and sideways until there is almost no shadow anywhere. At midday the cavern is as bright as a snowfield under thin cloud.

It is cool, a cellar's temperature, and the air is damp enough to bead on skin. Water seeps along the north wall and drips at a few places from the roof. At dawn every surface carries a skin of condensation, and the sand sets into a brittle crust that breaks up by noon.

The city covers most of the floor, thinly. Its builders worked downward with antler picks, hammerstones, and a few soft copper blades. They cut streets as trenches, sank rooms into the floor, and hollowed the natural pillars into stacked chambers. That was so long ago that the trenches are now shallow troughs with rounded lips. Rooms have become dishes. In the oldest quarter the walls between houses are ridges a hand high, smooth as soap, so that what remains is the plan of a town pressed lightly into white stone. Stairs have slumped into ramps, and doorways in the pillars have widened to soft-edged notches.

The things that were cut deepest have lasted best:

- **Cisterns:** A few are stepped, and some still hold water.
- **Drip-channels:** Finger-wide runnels lead from the wall seeps across hundreds of meters of floor to the cisterns.
- **Grinding hollows:** They run in rows.
- **Peg holes:** These survive across the floor.
- **Reflectors:** Slabs of selenite stand propped on stone rests beneath certain wells. They are clouded now but still throw a second, paler patch of light down the avenues.

Tool marks survive only under overhangs, where the short choppy bites of the picks are still legible.

The buildings stand far apart. Between one cluster of dishes and the next there may be three hundred meters of bare floor or dune, and from any one place the city reads as a scatter of low features on a white plain, with pillars standing over it like the trunks of a forest that was never here.

Nothing in the cavern holds still. The cave breathes through all its openings at once, and they do not agree. By day the plateau heats and the cool air below pools and sloshes. By night the surface chills, the cave air climbs out through some wells, and outside air pours down others. At the turnovers, morning and evening, drafts cross and shear at floor level. Where dry air meets the damp, mist forms in rolls and strands and in standing pillars that fill a light column, glow white, lean, and come apart within a minute. Small whirls of gypsum dust walk the streets.

The sand is fine and cool and always slightly moist, and it moves. Low dunes cross an avenue in an afternoon. A quarter that was open yesterday is under a smooth white drift today, and beside it a row of rooms no one has seen in a season stands swept clean with its cistern exposed. The city is never all visible at once, and a map of it would be wrong by evening.

All of this happens in near silence. The sand and the soft pitted rock swallow sound, and the damp air carries little. The rock has been worn so smooth that the wind has no edges to whistle on. A footstep is a faint compression and a dropped stone makes a tick. The clearest sound in the cavern is a drip landing in a cistern, and it can be heard from a surprising distance because there is nothing else.

No plants grow here. In the light patches the damp crust carries a film, grey-green shading to rust-pink, and the film follows the sun. Each well has stained a long curved band on the floor that records its light's path through the year, darkest where the summer light lingers. Everything living depends on that film and on what falls through the wells: dust, seeds that will never sprout, and insects blown off the plateau.

The animals are many and small, and they are always moving.

- **Springtails:** They gather on the film in drifts thick enough to look like spilled ash.
- **Crickets:** They are pale and long-legged, and their wings have lost the ridge that makes a song. They signal by tapping the crust, which carries a few centimeters.
- **Beetles:** They are white and roll pellets of the film.
- **Geckos:** They are the color of the stone, with dark eyes.
- **Fairy shrimp:** Their eggs wait in the dust for years. When a dune moves off a cistern and the channels refill it, the water is crowded with translucent shrimp within days, and when a dune moves back they are gone.
- **Swifts:** They nest in the well-throats by the thousand and hunt down the light columns through the mist. They scream on the plateau and are mute inside.
- **Owls:** Small and pale, they roost in the doorway notches and hunt by day, since nothing here is dark enough to wait for.
- **Bats:** They take over the wells at dusk.

The light patches crawl west to east across the floor as the sun crosses, and the animals go with them. Each patch drags its own crowd, a slow stampede of crickets and beetles and geckos keeping to the lit ground. The swifts shuttle overhead, the owls work the edges, and the mist is forming and failing through it all. From a pillar chamber at noon one can watch thirty of these bright, teeming discs sliding across the plan of the city. They cross streets without regard to them, they climb over a dune, they fill a room for a few minutes and then leave it. The whole floor seethes like a market, and the loudest thing in it is the dripping water.

When it rains above, each well delivers its own separate shower, a standing rod of rain in a standing rod of light, with dry floor between. The channels run for an hour. The crust darkens in rings.

A few times a year a piece of the roof lets go. It makes a soft thump and raises a cloud that takes the afternoon to settle, and after that there is a new column of sun and a new mound for the dunes to work on. Within a week the film has started there, and the crickets have found it. The cavern is very slowly becoming a valley. The city has already worn down close to the level of the floor, so when the rest of the roof goes, little will be left to mark it on the open plain.
