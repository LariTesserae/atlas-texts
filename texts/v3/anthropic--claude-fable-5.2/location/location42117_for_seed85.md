The saddle sits at about forty-six hundred metres, between two peaks too new to have worn at all. The rock is dark andesite, broken along fresh planes, its edges still glassy. Nothing has rounded it and nothing grows on it. The wind that crosses the col carries grit but no dust, because there has not been time for dust.

On the saddle stands the water. It has no basin and no shore. It is a dome some four hundred metres across and a hundred high, resting on bare rock the way a drop rests on wax. Its surface is a matte black that takes no reflection and no frost. Snow blows against it, slides down the flank, and lies in a thin ring at the foot. The rock a hand's width outside the skin is dry.

Inside, the water is four degrees everywhere: at the floor, at the crown, against the skin, in the middle. It is at its heaviest and has no reason to move, so it doesn't. There is no current, no silt, no drift of particles to show a beam of light if there were a beam. The rock floor under the water looks exactly as it does outside, sharp and clean, a ridge of the saddle running through like a blade laid flat.

The first rule of the place is that things stay where they are put. Nothing sinks and nothing rises. A stone let go at shoulder height remains at shoulder height. So the settlement was not built on the ground. Its fifty or sixty rooms hang through the whole volume of the dome, thirty and forty metres apart, at every height, none touching the rock and none touching each other. Each is a hull of pale grey material without seams, its corners as crisp as if cast that morning. The openings are cut in whichever face was convenient, a wall or a floor or the ceiling. There is no glass in any of them.

Between the rooms run the rails. They are taut lines about as thick as a finger, and they serve as streets, something to pull along. They cross the dark in long straight spans and all of them trend inward.

The second rule concerns light. Each room carries a single lamp, the colour of an ember almost out, and each lamp lights a sphere about two arm-lengths across. The sphere has an edge. Light here does not fade with distance. It goes a set way and stops, and the boundary is as definite as the wall of a bowl. Inside the sphere there might be a door corner, a stretch of rail, a patch of grey hull. Outside it there is nothing. The spheres can be seen from anywhere in the dome, but they illuminate nothing beyond themselves. From any point in the water the town is a few dozen dim globes hung at different depths, each holding its small piece of architecture. They look like a constellation, though one that doesn't flicker or turn and has never been seen from a different angle by anyone holding still.

There are no shelves in the rooms. They were never needed. The contents stand in the water where hands released them: a vessel, a coil of line, a flat tool with a hooked end, a stack of thin plates not quite squared. In one room a dozen containers hang in a loose spiral around an empty space the size of a body, each one within reach of whoever worked there. In another, a single object like a comb floats at the exact centre, with nothing else.

Then there is the breath. A bubble does not rise here any more than a stone falls. Wherever someone exhaled, the air is still there: silver beads, undissolved, at head height. They cluster thickly by certain doorways where people must have stopped to talk. They trail in single file along the rails, marking journeys, with a bead every body-length or so and closer together on the long hauls. Near the crown one rail has no bubbles along it at all. Near the floor there is a place with no room and no rail and a dense cloud of them, hundreds, in a rough ring, as if a number of people had once gathered around something that is gone.

At the centre of the dome, where the rails converge, hangs a plain hoop three metres across, the same grey as the rooms. Every rail passes through it. None comes out at quite the angle it went in. The differences are small and precise, a few degrees each, and the lines stay perfectly straight on either side. Within the hoop the water looks the same as elsewhere. A lamp sphere that overlaps the hoop's plane is cut flat there, like fruit. Whatever holds the dome to the mountain, keeps the water at its one temperature, and gives the light its edge is presumably this hoop. It has no moving part and makes no sound of its own.

The dome is quiet but has two sounds. One comes from the skin, a note too low to be called a pitch, registered in the breastbone and the back teeth. It is constant enough that it only becomes noticeable when imagined gone. The other is a click.

The click belongs to the only animals. There are perhaps a dozen in the entire volume, each as long as a finger. They are pale and jointed and eyeless, with a fringe of fine limbs along the underside that never stops rowing, since an animal that stops rowing here simply stays. They live at the borders of the lamps. One will hang for hours half in a sphere and half out, working its mouthparts along the edge of the light as if the edge were a film with something on it. Perhaps it is. Then it clicks once, a dry small sound that travels the whole dome intact, and rows off along a rail to the next lamp. Each animal keeps a circuit and the circuits do not cross. As one passes a string of breath-beads, they tremble in the disturbance and settle back to exactly where they were.

Nothing else moves. Open doors stand at their angles. A length of line someone was paying out between two rooms ends in the dark with its last loop still half-uncoiled. The cold reaches a certain depth in the body and goes no deeper. It is the same cold at the crown, thirty metres above the highest room. There the skin, as black from inside as from outside, curves overhead and gives no hint of the wind going across it or of what the sky is doing.

Below the lowest lamp, a hand above the ridge of the saddle, a single bubble sits by itself. No rail leads to it.
