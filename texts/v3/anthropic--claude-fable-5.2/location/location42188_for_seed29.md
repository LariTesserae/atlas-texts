Near the top of a volcano, a little under five thousand metres, a spine of new dacite has been pushed up through the summit dome like a tooth. It is younger than most living things. Inside it, a forearm's depth behind a rind of black glass, there is a cavity about the size of a barrel's interior. It is a gas blister that never collapsed, and it has no opening wide enough for a hand. Nothing has ever seen into it.

At the bottom is the throat, a crack as wide as two fingers that runs down into rock still hot from its making. Gas comes up through it at several hundred degrees. It is dry, acid, under pressure, and loaded with metals stripped from the magma below. Deep in the throat the stone glows a dull brick red, which is the only steady light in the pocket and not enough to reveal the walls. Now and then a blue pinpoint of static snaps at a crystal tip where dust-laden gas has charged the air. Otherwise it is dark.

Nobody built anything here, but the pocket runs like a works. The throat is a furnace mouth and the cracks in the roof are flues. Lower cracks on the windward side pull in outside air at ten below zero, thin and clean, because the rising heat makes a draught. Between intake and throat, the gas cools and drops its cargo in order, like the trays of a refinery column:

- **Nearest the heat:** plates of specular hematite, steel-bright and thin as razor blades, with grey rosettes of molybdenite.
- **A hand's width out:** sulfur kept molten, creeping in amber runs. It is the only liquid here and it is not water.
- **Farther out:** sulfur frozen into needles, crusts of orange realgar, cubes of salt.
- **At the cold margin:** white feathery sal ammoniac that a breath would crumble.

There is no bare rock left. Every surface is plated, and the crystals grow into each other, shoulder against shoulder, competing for the last open space. None of these colours has ever been lit.

The noise is enormous for so small a place. The throat roars like a relief valve that never closes, and the cavity takes that roar and rings with it, a single bottle-note held in the stone. On the outside of the spine it can be felt through a boot. Under the roar are the smaller sounds of manufacture. The cooling glass ticks. A hematite blade pings as it spalls under its own growth. Grains lifted by the gas hiss and rattle before they fall back.

Water hardly exists. There is steam in the gas, but it stays vapour and leaves through the flues. Only at the lips of the intake cracks, where hot meets freezing, does a film of damp form for a few hours before dawn, and by midmorning it is gone.

That margin is where the animals are. Between rock that would cook them and air that would freeze them lies a band of tolerable warmth perhaps a palm wide, running around the intake cracks like a gasket. It is full. Mites cover the salt crust so thickly that the crust seems to have a nap. Springtails pack the crevices between sulfur needles. Bristletails graze a grey bacterial film that feeds on the sulfur itself and is the nearest thing to vegetation the pocket will ever have.

Wingless lava crickets, the kind that only ever live on rock too new for anything else, stand antenna to antenna along the cracks, facing the draught. The draught is the conveyor. Every afternoon the winds lift aphids, midges, moth scales, pollen, and spider silk from the forests four thousand metres below and drop them dead on the summit, and the pocket inhales them. The crickets take the bodies and the mites take the dust. Small pale wolf spiders take crickets, and a few pseudoscorpions work the mite fields like harvesters.

Nothing here calls. The roar makes hearing useless, so everything is done by touch, by tremor in the crust, and by reading the heat. The rules are simple. An animal that moves toward the throat dies, and one that moves out through the crack also dies. It has to hold its place in the band, and when it moults, another takes the place it left.

Change is slow. When a storm lowers the pressure outside, the throat blows harder for a day and the band of life shifts a finger's width outward. When the weather clears, it shifts back. Each magnetite grain, as it cools, locks in the direction of north and keeps it. Below the intake cracks a deposit is collecting that amounts to no more than a pinch of dark crumbs. It is made of moults, droppings, and wing fragments, and it is the first soil on this mountain.

The works is also closing itself. Every crystal laid down in the throat narrows it. Over years the note has been rising, by less than any ear could track. Eventually the throat will seal, the roar will thin to a whistle and stop, and the pocket will go cold with its stock of crystals and insect remains still inside.
