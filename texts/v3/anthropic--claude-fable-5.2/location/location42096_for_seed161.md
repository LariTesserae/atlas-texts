The plateau is what remains of a mountain range after everything that could be taken from it has been taken. It stands very high, high enough that the sky at noon is a darker blue than it should be and a newcomer's breath comes short for a month. But it has no peaks left. The top is a pavement of quartzite and old banded gneiss, rubbed by wind into low swells, and its boulders have weathered to the shape of loaves. Nothing up here has an edge. Even the rim rounds over like a worn stair tread, so you cannot say exactly where the plateau stops and the fall begins.

There is no water on it. No snow stays, no stream has run here in any memory, and the few clouds pass below. Wind and the daily cracking of stone between hot noon and freezing night have done all the wearing.

Near the middle of the tableland the rock is split by one cleft, an old joint in the gneiss about three hundred paces long and forty wide at its widest. From a distance it shows only as a dark line with birds boiling over it. The town is inside.

**The descent**

The way in is a stair cut into the north wall, each step worn into a shallow trough by feet, then recut beside the old trough, then worn again. A few steps down, the wind stops. A few more and the air turns warm. The deep rock holds an even heat that the surface never keeps, and the cleft adds to it with dung fires, animals, and some four hundred people living close together.

The light goes at the same pace. The walls lean toward each other as they rise, and whatever sky they leave is taken up by plants. Fig trees root in the cracks, their grey trunks flattened against the stone and their roots running down forty feet like poured wax, seeking the faint damp deep in the joints. Caper bushes hang in mats. Houseleeks, aloes, and fat-leaved stonecrops fill every ledge. Over all of it, strung from branch to branch and from wall to wall, are rootless air-plants and long beards of lichen, which drink only what the night air gives them. Together they make a ragged, silver-green ceiling. What comes through is a dusk that lasts all day, apart from the hour around noon when a single strip of sun crawls down the east wall and people set out their trays of figs and curds to meet it.

**The town**

The town is built up both walls in seven tiers. There is no mortar, since mortar needs water. The houses are fitted dry-stone, chinked with a paste of dung and ash, or else chambers pecked straight back into the rock with copper chisels and hammerstones. Each roof is the neighbor's doorstep. Pegged ladders and rope walks tie the tiers together, and three bridges of fig-wood poles, black with hand grease, cross from wall to wall.

Everything is close. A woman grinding seed on one side can pass a bowl to the other side on a pole. Hives made of clay cylinders are stacked like firewood under the eaves. Goats live on the roofs and on the ledges between roofs, small, dark, and belled, eating the succulents back to stubs. The people replant from cuttings as a matter of habit, the way one sweeps a floor.

The floor of the cleft is the oldest part and no one lives on it. It is a deep, soft fill of dust, dung, potsherds, and the collapsed walls of earlier towns. Here and there a smooth round cobble sits in it. These river stones are the only sign that water once ran here. They are used as weights in the market, and no one is allowed to keep one.

**Water**

On the rim above stand rows of dew-heaps, tall open cairns of loose stone with a clay basin under each. At dawn the town's warm, used air rises out of the cleft, meets the cold of the plateau, and gives up its moisture on the stones. A good heap yields a cupful, and the heaps are owned, inherited, and argued over.

The rest of what people drink comes as milk, as the bitter sap of a tuber, or as the flesh of aloe leaves. Its handling follows fixed customs:

- **Nothing is poured out.** Jars are sealed with beeswax, and no liquid goes on the ground.
- **Washing is dry.** People clean themselves with ash and fine sand.
- **Guests get a cloth.** A visitor is handed a damp cloth to hold against the mouth and is expected to give it back.

**Sound**

The cleft is a stone throat and it amplifies everything. Swifts nest in the upper walls by the thousand and scream down the length of it in packs from first light. Rock doves clap off the ledges. Choughs quarrel along the rim, where vultures wait hunched beside the platform on which the dead are laid. That is the only burial the town has, since no one can dig in quartzite.

Below the birds there are the goat bells, the querns, the steady tapping of a coppersmith, the bees, and children shouting across the gap. Beneath all of that, whenever wind crosses the mouth of the cleft, comes one low, breathy note like air blown over a jar. The people no longer hear it. Visitors hear nothing else for days.

At dusk the swifts go in and the bats come out of the same cracks. Geckos take over the walls, chirping. Dark, long-legged beetles climb to the rim to stand head-down in the night air and let the dew bead on their backs. Children are sent up before dawn to collect them, and they lick the beetles before they put them in the basket.

**Time**

Nothing here changes quickly. The year has a windy half and a still half, and little else to mark it. The figs fruit when they fruit. The cleft widens by a grain's width in a lifetime, and the floor rises by about a hand as the town sheds its dust, so the lowest tier of houses is slowly drowned and a new one is started at the top, nearer the light.

The people know they are moving upward. They speak of it without urgency. At the present rate the town will reach the rim and the wind in some thousands of years, and by then the plateau will be a little lower too.
