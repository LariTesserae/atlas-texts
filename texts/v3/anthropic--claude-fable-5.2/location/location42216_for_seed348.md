The ground here was sea once. A bank of clay faced with slag blocks keeps it from being sea again, running around three sides of a pan perhaps four hundred yards across. On the fourth side a cinder causeway carries a line of pylons in from somewhere flat and grey. Everything inside the bank sits a few feet lower than the water outside it. The floor is concrete, poured in slabs the size of rooms, and where it isn't concrete it is clinker and furnace slag rolled level. Nothing grows on any of it. There is no moss in the slab joints, no slime in the gutters, no lichen on the north face of the chimney. The only green is verdigris on the lightning strap and the enamel of one door.

There are eleven buildings, and they stand well apart, as if each had been given its own share of the noise:

- an engine house of dark brick with tall arched windows
- a boiler house leaning against it
- a transformer yard behind railings
- a water tower on six iron legs
- a long corrugated shed with a conveyor climbing to its gable
- a row of six cottages under one slate roof
- a canteen
- a weighbridge office no bigger than a signal box

Tram rails set into the concrete join some of these and ignore others. Between them the apron is wide, bare, and wet, and it takes a full minute to walk from any door to the next.

The wet has no weather behind it. Water sweats through the bank and comes up at the slab joints. A drizzle too fine to fall hangs at about the height of the gutters. The engine's exhaust condenses on every cold surface and drips. The puddles lie in the same places with the same outlines, each with one hard mineral rim exactly at its edge and no higher mark above it. The pumps lift what seeps in and nothing more. The air is cool enough that breath shows, and it smells of wet iron, coal smoke, ammonia, and cooked fat.

Nothing here is new and nothing is ruined. The slab corners are rounded. The handrails are bright steel along the top where feet grip them, and brown underneath. The engine-house sills are dished like old stair treads. Along the footings of every wall runs a channel a finger deep and polished as a spoon, worn into the concrete by rats. Rust forms at the rate it is rubbed away. Nobody maintains the place, and it keeps its shape because wear and deposit come out even.

The sound arrives before any of this can be seen from the causeway. The beam engine makes one stroke every four seconds, a long indrawn groan and then a thud that travels through the slabs into the shins. The dynamo holds a single note under it. The conveyor rattles, and the cookers in the long shed blow off steam through a relief valve that shrieks for exactly as long each time. A telephone in the weighbridge office rings, stops, and rings again.

Over that, and louder, are the animals. Gulls cover the roofs so completely that the roofs appear to be shingled in white and grey, and the shingles shift. They don't lift off in alarm or settle in waves. They trade places one at a time, constantly, and each trade is announced. Starlings sit on the pylon wires in numbers that make the wires sag to a fixed curve. They have learned the works, and they do the relief valve, the telephone, and the squeal of the conveyor's dry roller. The valve goes off, then goes off again from four thousand throats a half-second late. The telephone is answered by its own ring from every span of wire.

The culverts are full of frogs. They are small and grey and packed in the drain mouths like wet gravel, and they call on the engine's stroke, all of them, once every four seconds, so that the thud has a croak laid on it like a second hammer. Below the frogs, in the sump under the engine house, there are eels. The surface of the sump is never still and never rises. It turns over, dark and muscular, and now and then a length of it slides up the masonry and back.

Flies stand in a column over the settling tank, about the height and shape of a second chimney, and their note sits just above the dynamo's. Moths batter the lamps, which are lit, though the sky is a plain overcast afternoon and gives light enough to read by. The afternoon gives no sign of going on toward evening. The lamps burn into it and the moths go round.

Rats use the channels. They come along the wall footings nose to tail at walking pace, a brown belt running beside the tram rails. They turn the corners square. Where two channels cross they pass through one another's files without a break in either.

For all this, the place feels empty. The animals keep to the structures and the drains and the wires. The aprons between are left bare, shining, crossed only in set lines, and the eye goes over long distances of wet concrete with nothing on them but reflected sky. There is far too much life here, and all of it is at the edges of things. In the row of six cottages the doors stand open. One has a table and an enamel mug. The stair treads are worn, and what has worn them is visible on every step, grey-backed, watching the door.

The long shed is where the place explains itself, as far as it does. The conveyor runs from a low hopper at ground level up to the gable. Nobody loads it. A gull that has stood a long time on the weighbridge roof walks down, crosses the apron by the usual line, and steps onto the belt. It stands there, facing forward, and is carried up. There are usually three or four on the incline at once, spaced out, not looking at each other. Rats come too, slower ones, and climb on at the hopper. Inside, the cookers do their work and the valve shrieks.

At the far end of the shed a worm-screw pushes out a grey pressed cake, warm, onto a chute. The cake breaks on the concrete, and that is what the gulls and rats are eating. The washings go to the settling tank, where the flies breed. The frogs take the flies and the eels take the frogs. The gulls stand on the sump wall and take the eels. Under the slates of the cottage row, where the starlings go in and out, there must be nests, and the nests must give back exactly what the hopper takes, because the wires never sag any lower.

Nothing comes into the pan except current along the pylons and water through the bank. Nothing leaves except the same water, lifted over, and the smoke. The count holds. The apron has no carcasses on it, and guano leaves only one thin wash on the sills. Not a feather lies in the gutters for long before a rat carries it along the channel toward the shed.

In the engine house the beam goes up and comes down. The brass is worn pale where wings brush it. Pigeons, the only ones in the place, sit along the entablature in a row, and each lifts slightly as the beam end passes under it and settles again. They have done this long enough that the paint on the beam shows a row of faint clean ovals, one beneath each bird.

The telephone rings in the weighbridge office. The wires ring back.
