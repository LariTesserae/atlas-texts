The plateau gives no warning. Wind-flattened heath and frost-split limestone run level to an edge, and then the ground is gone. For a mile and a half in length and half that across, it has dropped eighty metres in what looks like a single piece. The collapse is recent enough that the walls are still the colour of the rock's interior, pale cream in long clean panels where slabs have peeled away. Only the oldest faces have had time to stain grey. The rim is sharp enough to cut a hand. Blocks the size of barns lie at the foot of the walls with their corners intact.

The fall cut straight through the plateau's groundwater, so the walls weep. There is no waterfall anywhere, only films and drips along every bedding plane. Each seep is marked by a line of moss, and the lines thicken into curtains lower down. Cold air slides off the plateau at night and pools in the basin, so the floor is colder than the rim. Under the shaded wall a wedge of old snow lasts into late summer, grey and pocked with birch seed. Most mornings the basin fills with mist. By midday the mist lifts to a flat lid at rim height, and an even, shadowless daylight comes through it.

Nothing down here is bare. Moss on the fallen blocks is deep enough to take a hand to the wrist. Ferns fill the joints and liverworts plate the wet faces. Alder, willow and downy birch crowd any ground that stands above water, their stems too close to walk between without turning sideways. Below them grow sedge, horsetail, marsh marigold and hummocks of sphagnum. The tree line runs upside down. Birch grows tallest high on the talus, and at the cold bottom there is only willow scrub and sedge.

From the rim, the first thing you understand about the floor is that it is stepped. Dark arcs cross it, sixty or seventy of them, each holding a level sheet of water a hand's width to a metre above the next, and they descend toward the low end like contour lines. They are beaver dams built of sticks, mud, cobbles and sod. The newer ones are still pale with peeled wood. The older ones have sprouted, so that each dam is also a hedge. Between them stand the lodges, and after counting forty it is easier to stop. Canals an arm wide run straight out from the ponds into the alder for floating cut branches home. Slides are worn slick into the banks. There are fields of pointed stumps, and beside every lodge a raft of anchored branches is stored for winter. Through the clear water of the lower ponds the crests of earlier, drowned dams are visible, an older street plan under the present one.

The quiet here is built. Beavers build toward the sound of running water, and wherever the water makes a noise they plug it. Over generations they have taken nearly all the sound out of the basin. No dam spills, and each one lets its water through the weave as a seep. The wall drips land on moss. An enormous volume of water is moving through the basin all the time, and what you hear is a tick here and there, a branch being dragged through sedge, and at dusk a dry, patient scraping of teeth on alder from several directions. A few times an evening there is a tail slap, which carries the whole length of the basin and stops everything for a minute.

At the lowest point the water once left through a throat in the rock. The largest structure in the basin sits on top of it, a mound of sticks and mud the size of a house. It is repaired every night because the throat keeps swallowing. It still leaks, but less than the walls give, so the bottom pond grows a little each year. Birches stand dead and grey in it up to their knees. The whole basin is turning into a lake.

There is a second city on the sunward talus, the only dry ground. Wood ants have raised hundreds of waist-high thatched domes of birch twig, bud scale and dried moss stem. Trails worn clean through the moss join the domes and run up the birches to the aphid herds. On cold mornings the ants mass on the lit side of each dome in a dark sheet to warm themselves, then go below carrying the heat in their bodies.

A third city, the smallest, lies on the pond beds. Caddis larvae build their cases there from sand grains and the beavers' wood chips, and drag them slowly over the silt.

The other animals live in whatever room these builders leave:

- **Fish:** Char hang in the canals, facing the faint current.
- **Small animals:** Newts rest on the drowned dam crests. Water shrews hunt along the margins, and voles keep tunnels through the sedge.
- **Hunters:** A heron stands on a dam for an hour and then takes a vole. An otter family uses the beaver slides, eats the char, and now and then takes a kit. A goshawk comes through the alders below the canopy. At dusk bats leave the wall fissures and work the midge columns standing over every pond.
- **Visitors:** Elk come down the one passable ramp, a chute of rubble at the upper end. They browse the willow to the height of their reach, so the scrub there is clipped flat on top.

The walls are not finished. Frost works behind the faces every night with small clicks. A few times a year a panel lets go, and this is the one loud event in the basin. A wave runs down through the terraces and breaches a dozen dams. By the next morning the gaps are already dark with fresh mud. The new block sits white in its pond. Within a season it greens over, and within a few years a lodge is built against its lee side.

In the evening the cold comes over the rim again and the mist forms from the bottom up. Thin ice starts at the pond edges. Wakes open across the water in V shapes, dozens at once, each with a branch at its point.
