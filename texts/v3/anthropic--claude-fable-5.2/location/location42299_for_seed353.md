The tableland was a mountain range once. What remains is a low dome of basement rock standing a kilometre and a half above the plains, its shoulders rounded off and its summits long since carried away as dust. None of that rock is visible. The works cover it from rim to rim in poured floors, slag-brick, rail-bed and plate decking, with the footings of forty-odd furnace houses and the long sheds that join them. The whole is about the size of a market town, and the growth fills it completely.

It is dark here in the way the inside of a bale is dark. The shed roofs closed first, and then the canopy closed over the roofs, layer on layer. The topmost layer is a bleached, cured hide of dead leaf, a thatch the plants made of themselves and then abandoned. Whatever sun falls on it is not used. Below, nothing photosynthesises. The leaves are black-green or simply black, thick as boot leather, some stiff enough to ring faintly when struck. They are oriented toward warmth rather than upward, and every blade in an aisle faces the nearest firebox like an open hand.

The heat is dry and total. In the outer yards it is the heat of a kiln the day after firing. Nearer the furnace rows it is the heat of the firing itself. There is no water in any of this. The boilers scaled up and cracked ages ago, and the engines that still turn are hot-air engines, big slow displacers breathing the same baked air back and forth. The pipes stencilled for feedwater carry resin now, where they carry anything. The plants run on oils. A cut stem bleeds something amber and volatile that smells of camphor and hot tar, skins over in seconds, and is hard as shellac in a minute. The roots are felted mats laid over clinker and ash, drinking lubricant from bearing sumps and tar from old sumps beneath those. The air tastes of turpentine and toasted bark, and it pulls the moisture out of a mouth as quickly as it is opened.

For something so dark and so muffled, the place is frantic. Growth here is visible, or would be if there were light. It can be felt as a stem pushing steadily through a closed fist, or a tendril finding a wrist and beginning its first wrap. Everything grows inward toward the fires and cooks as it arrives. The leading shoots brown, then char, then drop onto the stoker grates. Mechanical rakes, patient and badly worn, draw them into the fireboxes. The fire makes heat and the heat pulls more growth. Ash goes out on the conveyors to the spoil banks at the edge of each yard, and cuttings scattered by the chopper feeds root in it within the hour.

The whole town-sized mass is turning over like this, a convection cell made of wood that rolls inward along the aisles and returns outward along the belts. The machines are the only herbivore the plants have ever had, and their only means of spreading. Nothing else eats here. There are no borers in the stems and no flies in the resin, and nothing has ever nested in the thatch.

The roll is not smooth. Smoulder-fronts cross the mass without flame, as a dull orange crawl seen through ten metres of leaf, like a coal seen through a fist. Behind each front the regrowth is already knee-high in the warm ash. A firedoor trips open somewhere and a thermal gust goes down the aisle, stripping every dry leaf with it. For half a minute the dark is full of a blizzard of stiff paper that hisses once along the brick and settles. Whole galleries of stem that climbed too fast and cured too brittle let go at once and come down. Even that is quiet, a long soft crump taken up by the mass beneath, like a wardrobe falling onto mattresses.

The quiet is the strangest part. The engines have run so long that everything that could knock has worn to fit. The flywheels turn with a sound like breath through the nose. Hot plate ticks. Resin pops now and then. Under it all is the faint dry crepitation of ten thousand stems lengthening, which is less a sound than a texture in the ear.

Over the ages the machinery and the growth have stopped being separable. Leather belts rotted off the line shafts, and lianas now run pulley to pulley in their place. They are live and continuously growing, lengthening at exactly the rate the pulleys abrade them. Bearings bored oval by centuries of turning have been sleeved by wood that grew into the gap and glazed itself with its own resin. The rails in the yard are worn to ribbons, and roots have taken the profile of the missing steel. The ash trucks roll on those.

The dynamos in the power house still make current. It goes to the stoker motors and the conveyor drives, and tendrils follow the bus bars as readily as they follow heat, wrapping the copper in tight even coils like a winding. Every lamp filament burned through long ago. The only electric light left is a pilot indicator here and there on a switchboard, a red point the size of a seed, completely overgrown and showing only when a gust parts the leaves.

The districts differ, though they have to be learned by skin. The furnace rows are the hot core, where the growth is thinnest and fastest, all charred tips and fresh shoots, and red seams show round the firedoors. The old cooling halls became the densest part once nothing in them was cool. The stems there are packed so close they have fused into a single grained wall, with passages only where the conveyors keep them reamed. The gasholder has sunk into its pit and filled to the brim with a slow-churning wad of root. The stacks carry no smoke, since the plants take the flue gas before it rises. Each is stuffed its full height with a column of growth living on the last warmth of the brick.

At the rim the works end and so does the heat. The growth ends with it in a sheer face of black leaf four storeys high. It leans back from the cold night air and the bare worn slope, and not one shoot ventures out.

The place has rules, and it keeps them without anyone to enforce them:

- **Motion:** Anything that stays still is wrapped within the hour.
- **Heat:** Anything hot is fed.
- **Moisture:** Nothing wet lasts. The one place moisture ever gathers is a film on the cold underside of the rim's outermost roof plate before dawn, and it is gone by the time the plate warms.
- **The count:** The number of furnaces alight never changes. One chokes on its own clinker and goes dark, its yard cooling and its growth slackening and turning away. Somewhere across the works a smoulder-front then reaches a cold firebox packed with a century of cured stem. A new red seam opens in the dark, and all the leaves in that quarter begin, slowly, to turn toward it.
