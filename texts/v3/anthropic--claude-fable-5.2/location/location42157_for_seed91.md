The ground here dropped. Two long faults run parallel across a lava plain, and the strip between them, about a kilometre wide and eight or nine long, has sunk thirty metres below the country on either side. The walls are fresh: stacked black basalt broken along clean vertical joints, with the fallen blocks still angular at the foot and none of their edges yet softened into scree. No stream has ever run down this trough, because no water stays on the surface long enough to make one. Rain and meltwater go straight down through the porous rock.

What holds the water is the moss. It covers the whole floor in a single woolly mat, knee-deep in places, laid over every clinker ridge, collapsed lava blister and boulder. In this weather it is grey-green and stiff with frost at the tips, and it would turn a startling wet green within an hour of rain. There is no soil in the trough. The moss grows on its own dead underlayers, a centimetre a year, and the rock beneath is only a few centuries old and still sharp enough to cut a boot.

So everything seen is a rounded version of something jagged. The moss lies in pillows, domes and soft swells, and it bridges gaps it cannot bear weight across. Walking means testing each step, since the moss often lies over a hole. A handful squeezed in a glove runs with cold water. In the hollows where wind doesn't reach, a few other things get a start in the moss: crowberry, a prostrate willow no taller than a finger, grey crusts and pale coral-stalks of lichen. Nothing stands higher than the shin.

The fissures are the only place water shows itself. They run lengthwise down the trough, cracks a metre or two wide whose moss-lipped edges curl over into the dark. Fifteen or twenty metres down, groundwater lies perfectly still, clear to the bottom and a few degrees above freezing. Frost-browned ferns hang on the walls in the sheltered air above it.

And over all of it, the noise.

Near the middle of the trough is a levelled pad of crushed red scoria, and on it a geothermal wellhead: a stack of heavy valves and flanges, painted once, with a horizontal discharge pipe as thick as a thigh aimed down the length of the graben. The well is on flow test and has been for months. Steam leaves the pipe invisible for the first metre, then whitens into a plume that climbs past the rim and leans whichever way the wind is going.

The sound is a single flat roar with no pitch or rhythm. On the pad it is felt before it is understood, in the sternum, the teeth and the fluid of the eyes. The basalt walls throw it back a fraction late, so the roar arrives doubled, and the trough holds it the way a gutter holds water. Half a kilometre away two people still have to shout into each other's hoods. At two kilometres it is a waterfall heard from close by. At the far end, where the faults pinch together, it has dwindled to a hiss under the wind, but it is still there.

The other built things are few:

- **The shed:** a steel container bolted to the pad, with a pressure recorder, a logbook, and ear defenders on a hook inside the door.
- **The lamp:** one bracket light above the door, left on.
- **The pipe:** a length of insulated pipe on zigzag supports that runs sixty metres toward the eastern wall and stops at a blank flange, waiting for a decision.
- **The stakes:** a line of survey stakes with faded tape.
- **The track:** two wheel ruts cut through the moss from the pad to a ramp bulldozed up the scarp. Where the ruts crushed the moss it has not grown back, and it will not for decades. A bootprint lasts nearly as long.

Downwind of the pipe the damp becomes ice. The plume's fallout freezes onto the moss in feathered lobes of rime, building for days toward the wellhead and then sublimating slowly when the wind swings. Closer in, a fan of moss has been killed brown and glazed with a thin grey mineral crust where the hot mist lands. That fan is the only bare ground in the trough.

The light is poor. At this season the sun travels just under the southern rim and never clears it. The floor gets a few hours of blue-grey reflected sky, further dimmed when the plume drifts overhead, and then a long dusk in which the lamp on the shed becomes the brightest thing for kilometres.

Animals are scarce and silent, or seem so. A pair of ravens works the western scarp, and when one opens its beak nothing can be heard. A ptarmigan, already white, sits in a moss hollow and is invisible until it walks. Occasionally a line of fox prints crosses the rime and gives the pad a wide berth.

Every change here is slow. The faults pull apart by a couple of centimetres a year. The moss thickens. The well roars at the same pressure it did in autumn, and the needle in the shed draws the same nearly straight line.
