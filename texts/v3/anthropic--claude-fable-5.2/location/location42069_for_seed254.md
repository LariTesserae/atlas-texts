The pump hall sits eleven metres under the street, behind a steel door in a retaining wall and down four flights of open-tread stair. It is a single room, about forty metres long and half that wide, and tall enough for the travelling crane that still spans it on rails near the ceiling. It was built to push hot water through a city district, and it still does. Everything else in it has been grown.

The sound comes first, and it never lets up. Two of the three circulation pumps are always running, each motor the size of a delivery van, and together they hold a note pitched a little below a human voice. Over that lies the hiss of the return header and the roar of the extract fan in its duct. A steam trap in the north corner fires every minute and a half with a crack like a plank breaking. The street directly overhead cannot be heard at all. Water falls everywhere, from flanges and leaf tips and from the roof in long threads, and all of it falls in silence because the drone covers it. The big leaves blur at their edges. Every surface in the room, living or iron, carries the same tremor.

The light is poor and comes from three sources. In the ceiling along the south side are the old pavement lights, panels of glass prisms set in iron frames, which a century of sun has turned amethyst and which algae have filmed on the underside, so that the daylight they pass is violet-brown. At the east end a grated lightwell lets down a shaft of real daylight that moves across the wall and is gone by early afternoon. And there are the bulkhead lamps in their wire cages, perhaps half still working. Each has a ball of foliage packed around it, grown in close and scorched back to a hand's width from the glass.

The plants are the kind that need no help. No bees come down here, and nothing sets seed. The room is filled with what spreads by runner, by broken stem, by spore. Pothos is the main one. Someone's desk plant, given warmth, wet and a wall to climb, has left its houseplant form behind. Its stems are as thick as a wrist, its leaves a metre long and split like palm fronds, and it goes up every riser and column to the crane rails and hangs back down in ropes. Creeping fig sheets the glazed brick so completely that the white shows only where a tile has spalled. Maidenhair fern grows wherever groundwater weeps through the mortar, along seams of lime the colour of candle wax. Liverwort covers the floor in overlapping scales. Selaginella and moss take whatever is left.

The vegetation reads as a map of temperature. The flow mains are too hot to touch through their aluminium jackets and they run bare, each with a margin of browned leaf on either side, and beyond that margin the growth is at its richest. The cold make-up line sweats constantly and is furred in moss as thick as a sleeve along its whole length. The standby pump, idle and cool, is completely upholstered, with only its nameplate rubbed clear. Every few months the duty rotates. Over the following weeks the moss on the newly running casing dries, curls and sloughs off in mats, while the machine that has gone quiet begins to green from its base upward. This is the fastest change the room knows.

No leaf has a hole in it. Nothing chews here, and nothing burrows. No webs hang across the stair rail, which is strange in a basement. The racket and the vibration may account for it, or the amine dosed into the condensate, or the sealed door. Whatever the cause, the hall holds plants and the slow rots that undo them, and nothing else. Fallen leaves are not eaten. They lie where they land, go leathery, and break down over years into a brown fibrous felt that roots then enter. The catwalk gratings were open steel once. Now they are floors, a hand's depth of mat and root, with roots hanging beneath in curtains that trail in the drainage channel. The channel runs warm and tea-coloured to a sump at the low end, where a float switch lifts a small pump a few times an hour.

The air is close to saturation. Sleeves go damp within a minute. In winter cold air sinks down the lightwell, meets the warmth, and makes a slow column of fog that stands at the east end for months.

Under the growth the layers of construction can still be made out. There are cast-iron columns with a foundry's name on the base, riveted lattice girders, welded pipework from a later decade, galvanised cable trays, and a run of grey plastic conduit that is newest of all. On the mezzanine is a shift office with a wired-glass window, and on its sill a cracked glazed pot. A stem as thick as a forearm leaves the pot, crosses the sill, and goes out through a missing pane into the hall.

Someone still comes down, though not often. A passage has been cut from the foot of the stair to the gauge board and on to the three isolating valves. It is shoulder-wide, its cut ends brown and already putting out side shoots. The valve wheels are red, and bare where hands go.

At each end of the hall the mains leave through brick arches into the service tunnels that run under the streets to the hospital, the baths and the housing blocks. The green follows the pipes in as far as the light reaches, about ten metres, thinning to moss and then to a film on the brick. Beyond that the tunnels are bare, and they carry the pumps' note out under the city.
