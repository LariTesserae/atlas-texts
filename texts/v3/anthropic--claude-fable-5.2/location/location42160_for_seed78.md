The first thing the plateau tells you, it tells through your feet. The ground gives slightly, like the crust of a loaf, then holds. That is the plant. It is a single kind of cushion-growth, rosettes no bigger than a fingernail packed so tightly that the surface sheds wind and takes a hoofprint only reluctantly. It covers everything. On the newest ridges it is ankle-deep; in the hollows it is deeper than a standing person. For a distance that would take seasons to walk, no bare soil shows, and nothing grows taller than a knee.

Above that surface there is almost nothing. The air is thin and cold, and for most of the year it is dark. This is the long night of a high latitude, made longer by altitude and by the cloud that travels across the plateau at ground level. When the cloud opens there are stars, hard and numerous, and the mat shows faintly as a paler dark under a blacker one. For an hour around what would be noon, a brown-grey smear sits on the southern edge of the world. Nothing here glows. The palest thing is rime.

The land under the plant is young and has not finished rising. The mat drapes it the way cloth drapes furniture, with every scarp and knife-ridge still legible underneath. Where the uplift pulls the ground apart, the mat stretches over the gap for a while as a taut skin with a hollow beneath, which sounds like a drum underfoot. Then it tears. A new fissure can run a mile in an afternoon with a noise like canvas ripping. Inside the tear the rock is black and glassy, its edges sharp enough to open a palm. Within a few dark seasons the plant has begun creeping down both lips, a hand's width at a time, to close it.

There are no rivers. Water arrives as cloud and is taken directly; each rosette combs the fog and passes the wet downward into the brown felt of its own dead layers. Press a fist into the mat and water wells around the knuckles, bitter and resinous. In the trampled hollows where the herds have bedded there are shallow pools, skinned with ice by the time the animals have moved on. Otherwise the water is in the plant or in the air, moving between the two.

The mat is warmer inside than out. A hand's depth below the frozen crust the felt sits a few degrees above freezing, and this small difference drives most of what happens here. The warmth breeds fog, which lies over the plateau in a layer of its own making. It keeps the felt alive with tunnelling animals, small and dense-furred, whose runs vent threads of breath through the crust. It is why the grazers kneel and break the surface before they sleep. And it makes the plateau loud. The top freezes and contracts while the body beneath stays swollen, and the strain releases in long booms that travel outward like the cracking of lake ice, sometimes for minutes, sometimes passing under you and on.

The booms are the low register. Above them the fissures hum, because every fresh crack is a pipe mouth and the wind arrives after a thousand miles of nothing to break it. Each fissure holds a pitch set by its depth and width. The pitch drops over the years as the gap widens, then thins and goes breathy as the plant furs the edges. Rime adds its own sounds. It grows as feathers on the windward side of every rosette while the cloud is down, and when the wind sharpens it shatters and blows along the surface as a hissing glass-dust. Walking on a rimed crust is like walking across crockery.

The animals add theirs, because in this dark everything that needs to be found has to call. The grazers are stocky and heavy-coated, with flat teeth that shave the crust. They move in strung-out lines, sounding a low two-note horn down the file so the last animal knows where the first is. Birds roost in the fissures by the thousand and erupt, shrieking, at every wind shift. Something broad-winged quarters low over the mat without a sound, and its silence is the reason the tunnellers freeze whenever the wind drops. A lean, fox-like hunter stands with its head tilted over the crust, listening downward, then goes up and comes down stiff-legged through the surface. It all happens far apart. A herd may be heard for two days and never met.

People are here, thinly. Their marks are low and few:

- **Windbreaks:** These are crescents of cut felt-block, backs to the prevailing wind, enclosing a space smaller than a room. On the outer face the blocks have rooted into one another and gone green again, so an old shelter is a ridge that happens to be hollow.
- **Hearths:** A pit is lined with black stones carried up from a fissure. The fuel is dried felt, which burns slow and smoky with a smell of pitch. A fire is visible from very far away and looks like nothing, a single orange grain.
- **Blades:** The fissure glass supplies these, knapped at the lip where it was found. The debris lies there in drifts, and the oldest flakes are already overgrown.
- **Posts:** These are leg bones of the grazers, set upright in the mat and drilled so the wind sounds them. The holes are cut to pitches no fissure makes, a thin doubled note, slightly sour, that means shelter, a cache or a safe crossing. A route across the plateau is a sequence of tones to be walked toward, one after another.
- **Paths:** A cut line in the mat heals paler and softer than the growth around it. It is found by foot, a seam of give in the crust that can be followed for miles without seeing it.

The plateau runs to a pulse. The cloud comes down, the rime grows, and sound closes in until even the fissures are muffled. Then the wind hardens, the rime breaks and blows, the sky opens black and full of stars, and the cold drops straight onto the crust. The booming starts within the hour. The herds rise and move and call, the birds go up, and somewhere a drum-skin of mat finally parts over a new gap, which begins to sound a note of its own. Then the fog the plant has been breathing gathers again from the hollows.
