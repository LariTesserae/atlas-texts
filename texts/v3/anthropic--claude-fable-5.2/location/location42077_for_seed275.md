The cavern has no rock in its ceiling, or almost none. What roofs it is the underside of a root-plate: a grey-brown vault of woody cable, braided and fused, sixty metres overhead at its lowest and lost to sight at its highest. The roots made this space. They forced the strata apart and are forcing them still, a hand's breadth a year, and here and there a boulder the size of a tram hangs in the vault where the wood has closed around it and lifted it off the floor. From the plate, sinker roots descend as columns, each as thick as a gasholder and half a kilometre or more from the next. Between them the floor runs flat and open for a day's walk in any direction, a plain of shed cork packed down to the colour and give of bread crust.

It is lit as a street is lit on an overcast afternoon. The wood conducts daylight. Somewhere above, the plant stands in weather no one down here has seen, and whatever sky is on it travels down the grain. Cut a root across and the sawn face shines flat white, yellowing late in the day, and goes dark at night except when a moon puts a faint pewter on it. Every lit avenue is a row of amputations. The cuts callus over and dim within a season, and would go out but for the herds.

Nothing is wet. The plant takes all water, immediately. Tip a cup onto the cork and by the time you have set the cup down a white fuzz of root-hairs has risen through the wet patch and drunk it. Hairs fringe the lintels of rooms where people sleep, leaning in toward breath. Water is carried in resin flasks with ground stoppers and is otherwise a thing that exists only inside skins. The air is cool enough for a coat, the same in every month, and splits the lips.

The plant's own traffic is dry too. In place of sap its ducts carry meal, a pale starch flour that it moves on its breath, pneumatically, down hollow vessels wide enough to crawl through. Twice a day the whole root-mass exhales and inhales, and a slow wind crosses the plain, first toward the columns, then away. That tide is the weather. The other weather is meal-fall, when a duct somewhere in the vault sheds: a pale column sifts down for an hour without a sound and leaves a drift people come out with sacks to gather.

The city is laid out on this plain and mostly not yet there. Its streets are roots trained horizontally along surveyed lines and sawn lengthwise, the flat face up for pavement. Its buildings are grown. Bronze forms are bolted round a bud, the bud swells to fill the form, the form is struck, and a wall stands there, warm-coloured and seamless, with a window where a plate was left in. Whole terraces exist at every stage. Some are numbered stakes with a bud bound to each. Some are jigs with pale wood bulging at the seams, some are half-walls, and some are finished rows with doors hung and no one behind them. The quarters stand a kilometre apart with bare cork between, joined by a cable tram on wooden rails that hauls three cars and a flatbed of formwork along on a bast rope.

The industry clusters at the columns. Turbines sit in the throats of the breathing-ducts and turn with the tide, and the mills and shops run off their current. There are meal-works where the flour is tapped, sieved and pressed into brick and loaf, saw-halls with bandsaws three storeys tall, resin houses, and bast-walks where rope is laid in quarter-mile lengths. All of it runs under one rule, which is the rule of flour mills: no spark. Tools are bronze. Motors are sealed in casings and inspected daily. Boots are felt-shod. One quarter by the eastern column burned in a single second, years back, and the wood that grew over it came in darker and tight-grained, and nothing has been built there.

It is quiet for a place with so many engines. Cork takes sound the way it takes water. A bandsaw at full cut is a hiss at forty paces and nothing at a hundred, and the tram passes with a creak of rope. Voices do not carry across a street. People here gesture a great deal and carry small wooden clappers, whose dry knock travels a little better than a shout.

The animals have fitted themselves into all this. Borer beetles as long as a thumb work the dead outer cork and make their water from the starch they eat. Meal-moths breed in the drifts and rise at dusk when the cut faces yellow, and with them come the vault lizards, pale and flat, which drop from the ceiling on a line of spun resin and take the moths out of the air. Then there are the callus-eaters, which are hog-sized, hairless and slow, with a rasp for a mouth. They travel in herds of a dozen from one lamp-end to the next, grinding off the tender scar tissue as fast as it forms. Where they graze, the light stays bright. The surveyors have learned to lay new streets along the herd tracks rather than argue with them, and the lamp-trimmers follow behind with bronze saws to square off what the rasps leave ragged.

Past the last finished terrace of the northern quarter the stakes go on, in ranks, out toward a column that has no mill on it yet. Each has a chalk number and a bud the size of a fist, and one in ten has a fresh cut above it throwing a small square of afternoon onto the empty cork. The tide is going out. A little meal moves along the ground toward the column, and a lizard on a stake turns to face it.
