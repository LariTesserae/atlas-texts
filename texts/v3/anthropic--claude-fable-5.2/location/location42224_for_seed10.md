The mountains here finished a long time ago. What remains is a tableland about two thousand metres up, planed so nearly flat that the eye has to work to find its relief: a swell of three metres over a kilometre, a sag that holds water, a rib of white quartzite surfacing like the back of something asleep. The rock is done. It has nothing further to lose and nowhere further to go. Everything that still happens on the plateau happens in the top four metres, and those four metres are alive.

There is no soil in the usual sense. There is moss on moss on moss. Sphagnum is red-brown at the surface, yellow a hand's depth down, and below that it turns to black peat that is only moss remembering itself. Across it grow cushion plants as tight as upholstery, club moss, willow that reaches the height of a thumb and creeps sideways for decades, cotton-sedge in thin white drifts. The stones that show are furred with lichen in grey, sulphur and a rust that looks like corrosion and isn't. Even the pools are skinned with green.

Walking on it is walking on a lung. Each step presses water out and the footprint fills behind. The water table lies at the surface or a finger below it, and the cloud base usually sits right on the plateau, so the air is wet too. Beads form on every sedge tip, on fur and on feather. It is a degree or two above freezing most days and a degree or two below most nights. The ice that forms is thin and loud.

The light never quite arrives. It comes through the cloud grey-green and without direction, so nothing casts a shadow and distance is hard to judge. A mound might be forty metres off or four hundred. At dusk the sedge tips carry a faint violet discharge, thousands of tiny points that crackle if anything brushes them. The wet moss holds a charge. A hand laid flat on it tingles.

Then there is the noise. Under the wind, which is constant and has nothing to break it, the plateau hums. It is a low, steady, mains-pitched note, the note of a substation behind a fence, and it comes up through the feet. Over it runs a slower beat, a thud about every two seconds that seems to move around underfoot like a diesel idling in the next room. Here and there, kilometres apart, stand mounds where sleet will not settle. The moss on them is warm and bright, almost lime, and from splits in their crowns comes a steady exhaust of steam and marsh gas that smells of iron filings and cut grass. Sometimes a split shrieks, one held note that carries across the whole tableland.

When that note sounds, the shift changes.

There is no other way to put it. The plateau makes things, and it makes them in runs. For some hours every hummock within sight will be the same hummock, with the same lean and the same tuft of sedge at the same corner, set in rows that run to the cloud as though stamped. Then the note sounds and the rows slump, and the moss begins on something else. It may make pools, each a metre across and each with the same curl of ice. Or it may raise a long gallery of arched peat, roofed in lichen and ribbed with club moss as thick as cable, which stands until afternoon and then falls in on itself with a wet roar. Stacks go up as well, tapering green chimneys twenty metres tall that vent steam for an hour and then fold at the waist. Nothing is built, because nothing here has hands. The plant layer simply swells into these shapes, holds them, and takes them back. The peat left behind is a little deeper.

The animals come the same way. They are many, but they are not spread out. They arrive in consignments. The ground lies empty for an hour's walk, holding only moss and wind and hum. Then a band of hares four thousand strong crosses left to right, pressed flank to flank and all the grey-white of old snow, each with the same nick in the left ear. Behind them the ground is empty again. Geese come over in a single slab, honking in unison, a sound like a works siren. Voles pour along the runnels in ropes. Small dun wading birds stand in hundreds on one pool, each facing northeast, while the next forty pools hold none. Midges lift from a warm mound in a column and travel as a column. Pale foxes follow the hares, but they are loose, single, and they don't match one another. They seem to be the only things up here that came from somewhere else. They watch the runs go by with what looks like professional patience.

No one could say whether the moss makes the hares. But some lie at the margins of a run with the fur along one side still green and fibrous, and they are not breathing and not rotting. By the next shift they are gone, and the place where they lay is ordinary sphagnum.

The sequence is also unreliable. The plateau produces at a rate its own order cannot keep up with, and things come off the line in the wrong arrangement. The roar of a collapsing gallery crosses the flats, and a minute afterward the gallery rises, stands its hours, and comes down in silence. Tracks appear in the wet moss, filling with water, and the band that makes them comes through later, each foot landing exactly in its print. A sleet squall may fall on one square hectare with clean edges while the air around it holds still. One pool reflects a clear night sky with stars in it, though no such sky has been above this plateau in living time, and the wading birds will not stand in that one. Distance gets reissued. A steaming mound a kilometre off stays a kilometre off through an hour of walking, and then it is underfoot and warm, and the land behind has more pools than were passed.

A few things hold constant:

- **The rock:** The quartzite ribs never change, never move and never repeat. They are the only landmarks, and the foxes den against them.
- **The hum:** It never stops, though it drops a tone in the coldest hour before the light returns.
- **The water:** It always leaves. The tableland has no rivers, because nothing so flat can organise one. At the rim, where the old surface ends in a worn, rounded shoulder, the peat overhangs in a dripping black lip. The whole edge weeps for hundreds of kilometres in threads and sheets down into the cloud.

The water goes over brown as tea, and it carries green flecks. They are scraps of moss no bigger than a fingernail, and on the way down some of them are still curling, slowly and without success, toward the shape of an ear.
