The plateau sits on top of a mountain that has not finished arriving. It is higher than weather. The clouds are somewhere far below, and whatever moisture they carry never climbs this far. The air is too thin to do much. It holds heat poorly and carries sound worse, and it moves only in slow warm drafts that lift off the rock and give out at about head height.

It is dark. The mountain exhales a fine dry haze of gas and ash through a thousand cracks, and the haze rises, flattens, and spreads into a lid that covers the plateau from rim to rim. Under the lid there is no sky. Only at the far edges, twenty kilometres off in any direction, is there a narrow band where the lid has ended and the ground has not yet begun, and in that band the stars sit hard and unflickering. The rest of the light comes from below. The deeper fissures show a dull orange that is more a colour than a light, enough to tint the underside of the pall the brown of old iron. Shapes are known by which of their edges catch it.

The ground is black and grey-black rock, almost none of it older than a few years and much of it younger than a day. It lies in plates. Some are skinned with glass and some are ropy, and some are heaved into slabs the size of floors. Every edge is sharp. Nothing here has ever been rounded, because there is no blown sand, no frost and no running water to do it. A broken edge stays the knife it was when it broke. Rubble lies in unsorted, angular heaps exactly where it fell. There is no soil, only a grit like crushed bottle.

It is warm. Over most of the plain the rock is the temperature of skin, and hotter along the seams. A layer of warm air lies on it about shin-deep, so that a hand lowered toward the ground passes through a definite surface.

The plateau is being built from underneath, and not carefully. A level stretch of pavement will swell over some hours into a low dome until its skin splits along the crest. The split shows orange for a while, then crusts over. Blunt columns of stiff rock are pushed up through the plates at a hand's width an hour, grooved down their sides by the throat they came out of. They stand as tall as towers, then lean and break under their own weight. Fissures open, run a kilometre in an afternoon, and close again, sutured with a welt of new glass. Whole fields tilt. A hollow becomes a ridge. At any moment four or five of these things are happening somewhere on the plain, far apart, with long stretches of still, dark pavement between them. Nothing serves as a landmark for more than a season.

Almost none of this can be heard. When a spine collapses, the ground delivers the news through the feet first. The sound arrives well afterward as a small flat clap, like a board dropped in another room. The constant sounds are the near ones:

- **Cooling glass:** a steady ticking from the newest plates.
- **Vents:** a dry hiss.
- **Cracks:** now and then a low note where gas passes through, held until the crack changes shape and then lost.

The upheaval is mostly felt. A tremor passes, or the ground leans slowly, and the horizon of orange seams ends up somewhere slightly different.

No plants grow here. Around the vents there are crusts of yellow sulfur and of white and rust-coloured salts. They grow overnight in needles and frills, deposited straight out of the gas without ever having been wet. On some of the warm slabs there is also a thin matte film, darker than the rock, in rough circles a palm wide. It favours the edges of seams. It appears on slabs a few weeks old and never on newer ones. Looking at it does not settle whether it is a mineral or the simplest kind of living thing. It flakes off when the slab flexes.

There are also the stacked stones, scattered at great distances from each other. They are of three kinds:

- **Cairns:** flat plates piled chest-high, unshaped and chosen for fit, with no tool marks.
- **Walls:** a few low curved ones, knee-high, each enclosing only a patch of pavement cleared of rubble.
- **A line:** single stones set on edge. It must once have run straight, but it now doglegs where a fissure opened across it and carried one half thirty paces sideways.

These are the only things on the plateau that were placed rather than pushed, and they are the only record of where the ground used to be. Most are in some stage of being undone. Some have toppled and some ride the back of a dome. One has been split through the base and stands half on each side of a crack. All of them wear the pale salt bloom that settles on anything left out for long.

A few do not fit this pattern. They stand plumb on slabs that tilt steeply, levelled against the slope and wedged with chips. They must have been stacked after the tilt, and the clean glass of those slabs shows the tilt is recent. At the far end of the displaced line, beyond the dogleg, the last stone has no bloom on it at all. Nobody is here, and the ground does not hold tracks.
