The lava reached the sea a few years ago and stopped. The shore it made is still black and glassy-edged, and low enough that spring tides wash right across it. Nothing has softened yet. The rock cuts boot soles, and there is no lichen on it, no barnacle scar, no gull. A dozen pools sit in hollows where the lava slumped, and all but one hold only seawater and sky.

That one is four paces long and three wide, forearm-deep at the landward end and chest-deep at the other. You hear it before you find it. It gives off a held chord, about the pitch of a wet finger on a glass rim, with a fast dry ticking over it and a bass beneath that comes up through the rock into the shins. At thirty paces it is a nuisance. At the rim it is hard to think.

The water stands a finger's width above the rock, domed, and does not spill. Inside it is a town.

The tallest structures are the height of a hand. They are built from the lava's own grain and from sand fused clear, so the walls are black and bottle-green, and they stand pressed side to side with no ground left showing. What looks like a floor is the roof of the tier below. At the deep end the tiers go down thirty or forty layers, each one fully built. They are joined by ramps and by bridges no thicker than a hair, and the whole mass is threaded with tubes that carry water in one direction only. Every corner is a true corner. The edges are so fresh they catch light like broken bottle glass.

The light is the second thing wrong with the pool. It keeps noon. Under fog, under rain, at two in the morning, the water is lit the way an ordinary clear day lights a shallow stream bed. It doesn't glow or glare, and it throws short soft shadows from the towers. At night it shows from the headland as a small rectangle of afternoon on the black shore.

Nothing lives in the streets. The traffic is the water itself. Slugs of it, some faintly milky, some carrying silt, some marked only by a shimmer of different cold, travel the channels in file and turn square corners without smearing. At crossings they alternate. A milky length passes, then a clear one waits and goes. The channels are full the way a rush-hour avenue is full, with a hand's width of open water nowhere in the pool.

Building is done by sound. Over any unfinished block the water is crowded with grains hanging motionless in ranks, each one held at a still point of the chord. The ticking is the sound of them being set. A rank drops a hair's breadth and fuses, and the next rank steps down into its place. A wall goes up a fingernail's height in the time it takes to watch it. When a district is finished its note drops out of the chord and a new one enters somewhere else, so the harmony shifts all day and never resolves.

The pool sorts whatever enters it. A pebble dropped in never reaches the roofs. It is caught in the water, turned, and over about an hour taken apart from the outside in, with its grains fed away down separate channels by color. Anything alive is refused. A sandhopper washed in by spray is carried straight across the surface and set down on the far rim, unharmed. A hand put in meets a firm cool pressure at the wrist that lifts it out. The water is colder than the sea beside it, about ten degrees, and it leaves the skin completely dry.

The pool tolerates plants in two places. On the south wall a film of green algae grows inside a boundary ruled as straight as a property line, and bare rock begins on the far side of that line. Along the shallow end seven threads of red weed stand tethered upright at even spacing, each trimmed to the same length. Their purpose is not apparent. Now and then a current is routed through them and comes out the other side slightly clearer.

Twice a day the tide crosses the shelf. The sea does not mix with the pool. It slides over the domed surface as it would over a lid, and waves break on the rim and run across the top with the noon light showing through from below. For those hours the chord drops an octave and the ticking stops. One notch on the seaward rim opens, and a rope of sand is drawn in through it and unbraids into dark, pale and shell-white strands that run down to the stockyards at the deep end.

On the ebb the town takes down its oldest quarter, which is never more than a few weeks old. The towers there dull, slump at the edges, and go out through a second notch as a fine grey plume that the backwash carries off. Then the building surge begins on the cleared ground. This is the loudest hour, with the full chord at pitch and the ticking run together into a single tearing sound. Rainwater jumps in the dimples of the surrounding rock. Because of this cycle the town has always just been built, and nothing in it has had time to wear.

The pool's waste heat leaves by a third notch on the landward side, as a thread of lukewarm water that runs a body's length down the rock before it soaks away. Along that thread grows a stripe of ordinary green slime, unmanaged and ragged at the edges. It is the only thing on the whole shore that is growing on its own.

Three paces past the stripe is another hollow, with rainwater and a little sea in it. Its surface has begun to stand slightly proud of the rim. At the bottom is a single black tower the height of a thumbnail, and with an ear held close to the water you can hear a thin steady tone a fifth above the chord next door.
