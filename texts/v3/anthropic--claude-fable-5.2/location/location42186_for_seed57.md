Under the flooded meadow, three metres below standing water that arrived last winter and has not left, there is a spring-house. It was never much: a chamber cut into the foot of a low bank and roofed with two slabs, big enough for one person to crouch in and fill a vessel. Some time after the flood closed over it, one roof slab cracked across and a corner dropped inward. The gap is as wide as a forearm and is the only way in. Its edges are still sharp enough to cut. The chips lie on the floor where they fell, clean-faced, with no film on them yet.

No light comes through. The water above is brown with field soil, and inside the dark is complete. Everything about the room has to be said in terms of touch.

The room is full. There is stonewort, brittle and gritty with lime. There is water-moss in ropes, a soft plant with stems like wet thread, and a broader strap-leaved one, slick as the inside of a cheek. They grow from the floor, the walls, the underside of the roof, and from each other. There is no open water anywhere. A hand pushed in is closed around at once, with even pressure on every side. It is cold enough to ache in the knuckles after a minute, though not cold enough to numb.

None of this should have grown in one season without light. It is still growing, about a fingernail's width a week, and the growth shows what is wrong here.

There is no space left to grow into. A new whorl of stonewort extends straight through the moss rope in its way, neither pushing it aside nor piercing it. Both are in the same place. Fingers closing on that spot feel grit and softness together, in a single thickness. The water in the gap has not risen by a hair. Nothing in the room displaces anything else. That is the first rule, and the others follow from it.

The second rule is that the room is larger through than around. Traced along the wall it is six hand-spans, then four, then six, then four, and the sharp gap comes round again quickly. Reached across the middle, the far wall does not arrive. The arm runs out. A cord with a stone tied to it runs out. The middle has been filled many times over and has kept the depth of every filling.

The third rule is that down is local. Silt lies on the floor and on top of the shelf. It also lies on the underside of the shelf, in a soft even layer that stays where it is. Roof-weed hangs and floor-weed stands. In the middle, where they share their place, stems run in every direction, and each one is straight, as if each kept its own plumb line.

Someone built a few things here:

- **The shelf:** A single slab is let into the back wall. Stems cross it at the level of the stone.
- **The bowl:** A clay bowl still sits upright on the shelf. Its rim can be felt all the way round, unchipped, with stonewort running through the clay.
- **The trough:** A stone trough is hollowed under the spring's mouth. It is packed like everything else.
- **The sill:** A dressed sill sits at the mouth itself, with a groove where the overflow used to run. The spring still comes out here as a weak, steady thread of colder water. For a hand's breadth around it the weed leans away and the stone is only stone. It is the last place in the room where one thing is one thing.

One animal lives here. It is an eel as long as a forearm, of the kind people once put in wells to keep them sweet, and it lies in the trough. It moves perhaps once a day, and when it does the stems do not stir. On some days there are two lengths of eel in the trough, the same girth, facing opposite ways. Nothing about them settles whether that makes a second animal.

It is nearly silent. The spring makes no sound. About once an hour a stonewort whorl snaps under its own lime, with a tick like a fingernail on a tooth. Beads of gas form on the leaves, which they should not do in the dark, and they detach and rise. There is no pocket of air under the roof. They never arrive. Now and then a soft knock comes from the middle, like a full jar set down on wood, from farther off than the room is wide.

The change is slow, and it is moving into the stone. At the back wall a stem can be followed by fingertip to where it meets the rock. There the rock simply continues around it, flush and cold, with no crack. The broken edges of the roof slab will not be worn round. In a few years they will be just as sharp, with weed growing through them. Each season the clear patch around the sill is narrower by about a finger.
