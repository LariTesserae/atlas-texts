The ground here is all floor. From the sea intake to the innermost pans is a walk of many days, and at no point does a foot touch anything that was not put there: clay cut wet, carried, thrown down, and trodden until it would hold water. The old shore is under it somewhere, and so is the old tidal mud. What shows is a made surface divided into rectangles so large that from the middle of one, its far levee is a brown thread. Beyond that thread lies another rectangle, and another. Each holds a finger's depth of water on its clay under a hot, pale, ordinary sky.

Nothing stands higher than the levees. Most are knee height, and the main ones reach the chest. They are the only paths. Their tops are flat, a pace wide, and they keep the print of their making: overlapping ovals of heels, palm-marks along the shoulders where the clay was slapped firm, a smear where a load was dropped and smoothed with a forearm. The work is new enough that none of this has softened. Edges are still crisp. Rain has only begun on the levee sides, drawing runnels thin as a hair and a few fingers long, each ending in a small fan of silt on the pan floor.

There is no wood in it, no stone, no metal. A gate is a notch in a levee stopped with a plug of clay the size of a head. It opens by pulling the plug out and closes by pressing it back in. Water moves because the whole works tilts, a hand's breadth of fall across a day's walking. The eye cannot find that slope, but the water can. The tilt is the largest made thing here, and the only one that cannot be seen.

Following the water inland, the pans change. Near the intake they are grey-green and barely saltier than the tide, and by mid-morning they are warm as blood. Farther in, the brine thickens and takes color, first tea and then a rusty pink where algae bloom in sheets. Farther still a dull skin of gypsum forms on the floors. In the innermost pans the salt itself is just starting. It rimes the edges, and rafts of crystal thin as fish scale float on the surface until a breath of wind tips them under. No pan has yet been harvested, and most have never completed a cycle. The works is younger than its own first crop.

The heat is constant and heavy. By noon the levee tops are too hot to stand on for long, and the shallowest brine is hotter than the air above it. The air is thick, and skin stays sticky with salt and never fully dries. All morning the pans give water up to the sky, and the distance turns milky with it. By afternoon that haze has gathered into flat-bottomed cloud standing over the interior, weather the works has raised for itself. The cloud shadows cross the rectangles slowly, one pan at a time. Some days a cloud breaks and rain falls in a grey column on three or four pans and nowhere else. It sets their brine back a week and leaves a lens of fresh water floating clear on top of the pink.

The sea breeze arrives at about the same hour. It pushes the water in every pan toward the lee side and bares a wet margin on the windward side that dries and crazes before evening. When the wind drops after dark, the water slides back over the cracks and seals them. This happens daily, across thousands of pans at once.

Very little grows. On the oldest levees near the intake, where rain has leached the tops, glasswort has taken hold in jointed red-green stems, each a hand's width from the next. In a rain-freshened corner a few sedge seedlings come up and last until the brine returns. Apart from that there is only algae, as mats on the pan floors and as the pink stain in the water.

Almost nothing lives in the water or over it. The brine is too new for shrimp and too strong for anything else, and the flies have not yet found it. Birds cross high in lines, going somewhere else, and do not come down.

It is quiet enough that small sounds carry. Water laps along a lee levee. Salt crust ticks as it tightens in the sun, and drying clay gives a faint crackle. Thunder from the interior clouds is so far off that it arrives more as pressure than as noise. The loudest sound is water running through a freshly opened notch, which can be heard from two pans away.

At the outer margin the works is still being laid. The pans there are only outlines: an ankle-high ridge, a line scored in mud, heaps of clay not yet spread and still holding the shape of the armload that dropped them. The makers work at this edge, few and far apart, a bent figure every few hundred paces. Often the shimmer hides them and only the fresh heaps show where they are. Behind them the borrow pits where the clay was cut are filling with seep. The pits are square-sided and already level, and they will be pans themselves by the next season. Even the holes in this place are made.

The rules are few and practical:

- Water goes one way.
- No one walks a pan floor, because a footprint breaks the seal.
- A plug pulled is pressed back before dark by the hand that pulled it.
- Rain belongs to the pan it falls on.

At night the haze thins without clearing, and the pans hold the last light longer than the sky does. Dew beads on the levees and darkens them, and by dawn each wears a damp cap that the sun takes back within the hour. Somewhere a notch has been left open, and brine creeps through it down the tilt into the next pan.
