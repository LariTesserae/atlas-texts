The ice comes first, a lid a metre thick, grey on its underside and ribbed where it has refrozen. Below it the water is black and gets very slightly warmer on the way down, as lake water does in winter, so the works sit on the basin floor in the densest water there is, four degrees above freezing.

The town is one street of some forty roofs, two hundred paces end to end, in a bowl of its own making. The sheds stand wall to wall, with gaps between them no wider than a shoulder. Underneath is old fieldstone, a mill that was here before the water. Brick sits on the stone, and riveted steel on the brick: trusses, gantry rails, a water tower that now holds the same water as everything else. Pipes cross the street at three heights. Chains hang from the catwalks, each thickened to the width of a wrist by rust that grows downward in soft orange icicles. Nothing here can be crossed in a straight line. Every route bends around a flange, ducks a shaft, or squeezes past a hopper.

Every edge has gone round. The rivet heads are low domes and the brick has given up its mortar and stands by weight and habit. The enamel sign on the gatehouse still carries four letters and the shadows of nine more.

Twice a day, water comes through the old intake tunnel at the north end. Nobody opens the sluice, which rusted long ago at a width that happens to suffice. The flow leans on a turbine in the stone basement, and the turbine turns the line shaft that runs the length of the street through every wall. The bearings are lignum vitae, a wood that wants only water for grease, so the shaft turns without a sound. A dozen lamps on the circuit, sealed in thick glass globes, come up from nothing to the colour of weak tea. That is all the light there is. Each globe wears a beard of green algae a hand-span wide, and past that hand-span nothing green grows on anything.

The rest of the vegetation is in the street. Moss balls, dense and dark, the size of fists and smaller, lie in drifts in the lee of the doorways. When the flow comes they roll south along the cobbles, a slow crowd of them. When it slackens they stop where they are. Over years they circulate, carried back north by some eddy along the wall bottoms that has never been observed doing it.

Silt falls the whole time, fine and pale, crossing the lamplight at a slant. It should have buried the place to its ridgelines by now, and it hasn't, because the works make brick out of it.

The roofs are louvred, and they shed the silt into gutters. The gutters and the street's twice-daily current deliver it to a grate at the south end. An inclined screw lifts the slurry into the press house, and there a platen wider than a bed comes down over the better part of an hour. What comes out is a grey brick, cold and slightly soft at the corners. A chain conveyor carries it out past the last shed, where a travelling gantry sets it on the wall, moving one brick-length per brick.

The wall rings the town and stands just short of the roofs. It will not get taller, because the lowest courses are slumping back into mud as fast as the top course is laid, and the mud lifts on the next flow and falls again on the louvres. The brick stamp wore smooth an age ago. The top courses are blank. Further down, a maker's mark appears faintly, and it sharpens course by course toward the bottom until, in the rows just above the slump, whole words can be read.

In the forge shed a trip hammer is still coupled to the shaft. It rises, tips, and descends through the water so slowly that it lays itself on the anvil without a sound, though the floor shifts slightly under the weight. Nothing strikes anything in this town. The press closes without a clap and the conveyor links take up their slack without a clink. What sound there is travels in the steel and has to be felt, as a tick in the teeth with a hand on a rail, or as a pressure behind the ears when the ice overhead settles.

The works do make a little heat, at the turbine and in the press. That warmed water finds the chimney and rises from it in a column that shows only where the falling silt bends aside. Directly above, it has melted a smooth, shallow dome into the underside of the ice, and the dome's rim keeps thickening where the meltwater refreezes. In high summer, for an hour at noon, the thinned ice lets down a grey-green stain of daylight that falls straight onto the chimney mouth, so the only skylight the town has is one it made with its own exhaust.

Animals are few. A burbot, long and mottled, lies under the press bed with its single chin barbel on the floor. It moves when the platen moves, about a body length, and then settles. Pale amphipods work over the new bricks on the wall and are gone by the time the next course covers them. Now and then something comes down the intake tunnel with the flow, makes a circuit of the lamps, and leaves before slack water.

When the flow stops, the shaft coasts down and the lamps go from tea to ember to dark. The moss balls stop in the street, and the hammer rests wherever it was. The silt goes on falling straight now, and it settles on the louvres for the next flow to carry to the press.
