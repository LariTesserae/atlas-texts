The basin floor is basalt about sixty years old, and it still looks poured. The flow came out of a fissure on the northern rim, filled the low ground thirty meters deep, and stopped. Nothing has rounded it since. The ropy stretches keep their glaze, the clinker fields have edges that will open a boot sole, and the pressure ridges stand split down the middle like loaves. On cool mornings the fissure still breathes a little steam. The plain sits barely above sea level, far from any sea, and the air is thick, warm and slow to move.

Nine masts stand on the flow, eight in a rough ring and one at the center, three or four kilometers apart. Each is about three hundred and fifty meters tall, no thicker than a person's reach, the color of old teeth and warm to the palm. They have no seams and no doors. As far as solid matter goes, they are the whole town. The rest is made of sound.

The masts hold a tone near twenty cycles a second. It is too low to hear as pitch; it arrives through the sternum and the fluid of the eyes. Stacked above it are audible overtones, a chord with the grain of a ship's horn that never ends. The combined field does work that sound elsewhere has no strength to do. It stands still in the air across eleven kilometers. Where its pressures cancel, it leaves level planes eight and a half meters apart, forty-one of them between the rock and the mast tips, and the planes bear weight.

They also hold the water. Rain comes a few times a month, thin, and never reaches the ground as rain. The drops slow, sort themselves, and settle onto the planes as sheets of fog a hand thick. Dew does the same. There is no stream or pool anywhere on the flow. The water is shelved in the air, each sheet sagging where it grows heavy and dripping to the one below. Daylight has to pass through all forty-one, and noon at ground level is the brown-green of weak tea. Where the field is fiercest, along the lines between neighboring masts, the droplets give off pinpoint blue flashes, too faint to see except at night.

The gardens are on the sheets. Root-balls the size of oil drums hang in tiers with nothing holding them: ferns, strap-leafed air plants, and figs that have never touched soil, trailing roots sideways through the fog. They drift slowly along the planes and tangle into rafts. From the ground the view goes up through raft after raft, each with tens of meters of empty air above and below it and hundreds of meters to either side. The growth is dense wherever it occurs and occupies almost none of the volume. The basin is mostly air, with a little green suspended in it.

On the rock, the sound does the sorting. Glass shards, dust, shed leaves, seeds and insect husks are all walked slowly across the basalt to the still lines and left there. Soil exists only on those lines, so the ground vegetation grows in figures: concentric rings around each mast, straight hedges where two fields meet, lattices and spokes of waist-high fern and vine, with bare black glass between. The hedges are narrow enough to step over. Loose clinker that has not yet reached a line chatters in place all day.

Under the flow run the lava tubes, drained and hollow, some wide enough for a train. Where their roofs have fallen in, the openings sound. Each tube takes the masts' tone and returns it lower, and the ground near one bucks faintly underfoot.

Animals fill what structure there is. The same forces that shelve the rain shelve everything small, so midges, gnats and winged ants are gathered into the sheets by the billion, a brown haze in the fog. Swifts trawl the planes with their mouths open and never need to chase. Under the rafts hang bats that have given up echolocation, since the field drowns it, and feed by flying the sheets from memory. Thumbnail frogs live in the root-balls and call until their whole bodies blur. Geckos cover the lower fifty meters of every mast. Every bird that sings here sings at a scream, in the narrow gaps between overtones. Over generations, a cicada in the ground hedges has tuned itself to the third overtone and locked phase with it. The masts did not ask for this, but they have come to lean on it, and in the weeks after each brood dies back, the upper planes go soft and the highest rafts ride half a meter lower.

A few hundred people live in a volume that could shelve a hundred thousand. Their rooms are pockets where the field cancels in every direction at once, so that the chord drops to a murmur and a voice carries. A room has no walls to see. Its occupants mark the floor with woven mats and the edges with hanging cord, and anything set down at shelf height stays there. Stepping out through the edge is a blow to the chest and ears, and then the full roar. Between rooms people sign and do not hurry. At night a neighbor is a single lamp six hundred meters off and two hundred up, with nothing between but fog, tier under tier.

The whole structure moves with the temperature. Sound runs faster in warm air, so as the afternoon heats the basin the planes spread apart by a finger's width each, and the top sheet rises some six meters between dawn and three o'clock, lifting its gardens and rooms with it. At dusk it all settles back, and the residents feel the descent in their stomachs. Thunder is the real hazard. A close strike can knock a room loose from its place for several seconds, and mats and cups and sleepers drop a little way before the field takes hold again. After storms the sheets are heavy, the chord drops in pitch by a fraction, and water falls plane to plane all night in slow curtains.

The ninth mast, at the southwest of the ring, stopped some years ago. No one knows why. Its quarter of the sky fell in an afternoon. The fallen rafts lie where they landed, a rotted heap two kilometers long, the only deep soil in the basin. A thicket has grown out of it in the ordinary crooked way, without rings or lines, and it stays wet underneath. Animals that cannot bear the field have found it. Deer are in there, and owls, and a bird with a quiet song that can be heard only from inside the trees.
