Behind a service hatch on the collector mast, a little under three thousand metres up, there is a void between the inner and outer skins of the structure. It is about four paces across and nine metres tall, and it was never meant to be a room. It is the throat of a condenser. Nothing in it grew there: the walls are ribbed ceramic, the floor is grating, and the ceiling is a grey plate of a material that is never wet and never warm, though everything else in the chamber is both.

**The sound**

The sound comes first, and it arrives through the sternum, not the ears. The condenser works by holding a standing tone in the shaft, between the plate overhead and its twin below the sump. Neither plate visibly moves. The air does the moving, and the tone is low, total, and loud enough that a shout at arm's length is only a shape the mouth makes.

The tone has a geography. Roughly every metre up the shaft there is a shelf of stiller air, and between the shelves the air shakes hard enough to blur a held-out hand. The spiderwebs make this visible. They survive only on the shelves, so they hang in flat tiers, one above another, like floors in a building with no walls.

**The water**

The water arrives on the same shelves. Outside air is thin and cold, and it is drawn in through a louver near the top, a single blade of blue-grey daylight with a rim of frost that never advances. Inside, the tone wrings the air out. Fog gathers at each tier as a disc, then thickens into a sagging lens of clear water the width of the room, held up by nothing visible.

The heat given up in the wringing stays behind. The chamber sits at the temperature of a mouth. The walls sweat, the ladder sweats, and the only other light comes from two amber indicator lamps crusted nearly opaque.

**The wear**

All of it is old and has worn into agreement with itself.

- **The fins:** their edges are scalloped where drips have fallen from the same points for longer than the hatch label has been legible. The label is now a row of raised ghosts.
- **The grating:** mineral scale has closed the floor into a pale, rippled pan with small rimmed pools, each spilling into the next toward the sump.
- **The ladder:** the rungs were once polished in the middle by hands and have since dulled over.
- **The plants:** a black film of algae marks each drip line, a crust of liverwort rings the sump, and one fern is rooted in a failed gasket, its fronds trembling permanently.

**The animals**

The animals are everywhere, close enough to touch one another.

- **Frogs:** they sit on every fin within the still tiers, thumbnail-sized and the colour of the scale. They cannot be heard, so they do not call. Each lifts a hind leg and spreads its toes to show webbing as white as paper, a small flag slowly raised and lowered. In the dimness the walls flicker with them.
- **Geckos:** they wait around the amber lamps for moths, which come for the light and for one another's scent, the only message the tone cannot drown.
- **Smaller things:** midges rise from the sump in columns that bend sharply wherever they cross the shaking air. Snails rasp at the scale, leaving clean looping tracks.
- **Swifts:** they have glued their nests, shallow cups set close together, under the louver, where the cold blade of air keeps the mites down.

**The pause**

Every eleven minutes the tone stops. The lenses let go together, and nine metres of stacked water falls into the sump as one flat clap. For perhaps forty seconds the chamber belongs to the frogs, hundreds of them in a hard-walled stairwell of a room, all calling at once, high and piercing, a noise worse in its way than the machine. The swifts use the gap as well, dropping through the louver and out again while the air is passable.

Then the tone climbs back up from beneath hearing, the webs on the shelves go taut and still, and the white feet start to rise again along the walls.
