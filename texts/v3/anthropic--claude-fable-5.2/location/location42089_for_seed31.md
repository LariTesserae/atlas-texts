The shelf sits high on the shoulder of a young volcano, a few miles of nearly level basalt between the cone above and the drop below. The rock is new. The flows still show the ropes and folds they cooled in, and their edges are sharp enough to cut. Where the crust broke as it moved, it left fields of loose clinker, black and rust-brown, each piece glassy at the corners. Nothing here has been rounded. There are no streambeds, pebbles, or sand, because water hasn't yet had time to carve a course. It sheets across the pavement, finds a crack, and goes down.

There is a great deal of water. The shelf lies at the height where warm air from the lowlands rises and turns to cloud, and the cloud rests against the mountain most of the day, thin enough to see through for a mile or more. Rain is rare and unnecessary. The rock faces sweat, and every overhang drips at a slow, regular count. The air is as warm as skin and close to saturated, so nothing ever fully dries. The light comes through the cloud white and even, bright without a visible sun, and the shadows under things are soft and short. The wet basalt shines like a dark glaze.

The plants have come as far as the rock allows. Grey-green lichen crusts the oldest flows, and moss fills the folds of the ropy lava. Ferns stand in every crack that holds a handful of grit, with stiff fronds and hairy, copper-coloured fiddleheads. In the collapsed roofs of lava tubes, where shelter and rot have built some depth, there are tree ferns and small-leaved shrubs with red new growth, crowded together in pits thirty feet across. Between these pockets the rock is bare. From any rise the shelf looks like black stone spotted with green, and the spots thicken only where people have made them thicken.

The town is spread thin across the middle of the shelf, perhaps two hundred houses over an area that could hold ten times that. Each house stands on its own platform of dry-laid basalt, with fitted walls chest high and a steep roof thatched in fern stalk and long leaf, the eaves nearly reaching the ground. No two houses are close enough for talk to carry between them. Causeways join them across the clinker, a single pace wide and paved with the flattest slabs to be found, running straight for hundreds of yards. The slabs are the only smooth footing on the shelf, and their centres are faintly polished.

There is no metal anywhere. The tools leaning under the eaves are basalt adzes lashed to wooden hafts, digging sticks hardened in fire, gourds, baskets, and coils of twisted fibre cord. The only round stones on the shelf are hammerstones and pounders that hands have made round, and they sit on the platforms like eggs.

Most of the town's work has gone into the water. Along the windward edge stand rows of upright slabs, each taller than a person and set a hand's width apart. Cloud condenses on their faces and runs down into a channel pecked into the bedrock, a groove no wider than a palm that took someone years to make. The channel feeds stone-lined cisterns, and every cistern has a lip and a spillway, so that each one stays full to the same line while the surplus goes on. Below them lie the pond-fields, shallow walled terraces holding soil that was made here from rotted fern and crushed cinder and carried to its place by the basketful. Broad-leaved root plants stand in them in a few inches of clear water, each leaf holding a bead at its centre. They are grown from cuttings, and each row is a copy of the one before it. Nothing here flowers for the sake of a visitor, because none come. No bird crosses the shelf, nothing lives in the ponds, and there are no flies over the standing water. What spreads does so by spore, by runner, or by hand.

It is very quiet. The cloud takes the edges off sound, and there is little sound to begin with: drip from the eaves, a thread of water moving in a channel, the tick of a drop on a leaf. A voice or the knock of an adze, when one comes, seems to arrive from farther away than it is. Fires are kept indoors in stone-lined pits, and the smoke filters out through the thatch and is lost in the cloud almost at once.

Beyond the last platforms the shelf goes on, pavement, clinker, and fern pits, to a rim where the ground simply stops and the cloud lies below as well as ahead. Uphill, the cone is a darker grey within the grey, with slopes of loose cinder and a few streaks of lichen.

The shelf looks the same from day to day. The cloud arrives at the same height. The nights are scarcely cooler than the days, and there is no dry season to wait out. The thatch rots and is replaced in rotation, so the roofs are always the same mix of green, straw, and grey. The cisterns stand at their lines, and the slabs at the edge go on dripping at their count.
