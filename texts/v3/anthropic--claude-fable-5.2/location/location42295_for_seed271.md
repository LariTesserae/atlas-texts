The country around is low and plain: buff sandstone benches, shallow washes, long gaps between one thing and the next. One of these washes cuts a bend into a ledge of rock, and on the outside of the bend, where water once pushed hardest, the stone is hollowed into an alcove. It is about thirty paces across at the mouth and fifteen deep, and the roof at its highest would clear a tall tree, if there were one.

The alcove faces away from the sun. Its light arrives secondhand, bounced off the opposite bank of the wash, and has gone brownish and soft by the time it reaches the ceiling. Noon and late afternoon look much alike in here. The sky is usually a pale, warm haze, and the far corners stay in a shade that has no hard edge.

The roof is a single curve, and it has finished forming. Its lip is streaked dark with mineral varnish, which takes a very long time to grow and would be broken by any fresh fall. The slabs that came down did so long ago. They lie on the floor with softened corners, sunk to their shoulders in fine pale sand that has settled level around them. The sand holds no tracks, and there are no droppings on the ledges or webs in the cracks. The floor shows only the faint ripple of the last air that moved across it.

Along the back wall, at about chest height, a darker bed of rock meets a lighter one, and water comes out along the seam. It doesn't flow. It only wets a band of stone perhaps two hands wide, which shines faintly in the dimness and runs the whole length of the alcove. Below it the rock is black-green with algae, and below that it is crusted white where the water gives up its salts. Maidenhair fern grows along the seam itself in small, separate tufts, each one hanging from its own crack with bare stone between. They stop exactly where the damp stops.

At the lowest point of the seam the water gathers enough to drip. A drop falls every four seconds or so into a basin in the bedrock, roughly an arm's length across and a hand deep. The water is clear and slightly warm, with a floor of tan silt. The basin has one mineral line around its rim, a single crisp white ring, because the seep supplies exactly what the warm air takes and the level doesn't move. A clump of rush stands at one edge, eight or ten stems, and a mat of moss covers the stone where the overflow would go if there were ever any.

The drip is the main sound. It is a small, round note, and the curve of the roof carries it farther than seems right, so it can be heard from the mouth. Sometimes air moving down the wash crosses the lip and makes a low breath of tone that barely registers as sound. Footsteps on the sand make almost none.

The air is warm and close without being heavy. The stone gives back a steady, mild heat, like a wall that had sun on it earlier. Within a pace of the seep it is cooler and smells of wet rock and of something faintly green.

People were here, a long time ago, and left little:

- **The wall.** A line of stacked stones crosses part of the mouth. It is three courses high where it survives, laid dry and chosen for fit instead of being shaped. Sand has risen against it on both sides, and its top course is beginning to carry the same dark varnish as the cliff.
- **The soot.** On the ceiling above one end of the wall is a patch of black, sealed under a thin mineral glaze so that it no longer rubs off.
- **The hollow.** Near the basin, a flat slab has a shallow oval depression in its upper face, smooth as skin, worn by something pushed back and forth by hand over many seasons.
- **The cobbles.** Beside the slab lie four rounded stones, gray and dense. They are a different rock from anything in the wash and were carried in from somewhere with a real river.

Nothing here bears a cut mark, and there is no metal or fired clay.

Outside the mouth the wash floor is dry sand and gravel, with two gray-leaved shrubs rooted where the bank gives a little shade, well apart from each other. Beyond them the bend straightens and the low benches go on.

The alcove keeps to a simple arrangement. Water comes in at the seam and leaves into the air at the same rate. The ferns hold the damp line and stay within it. The sand sits where it settled, and the roof has let go of everything it was going to let go of. In the basin the drop falls, the rings spread to the white line, and the surface is flat again before the next one.
