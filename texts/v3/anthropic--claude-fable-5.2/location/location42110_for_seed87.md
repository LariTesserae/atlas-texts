The stone came down last summer. An iceberg carried it out along the fjord and grounded on a shelf twenty metres down, where it rotted and let the stone go. It landed on its edge against an older boulder. Where the new stone leans there is a pocket about the size of a bread oven, a hand's width high at the mouth and a finger's width at the back. That is the whole place.

The stone is granite from somewhere far inland, and until that summer it had never touched salt. Its outer face is a fresh break, with sharp crystals and feldspar still pink. The face that makes the ceiling of the pocket was ground flat by the glacier that carried it, then polished and scored with long parallel scratches. The scratches all run one way, and everything that has settled here has settled along them.

So the city had streets before it had anything else. Spiral tube worms sit in the grooves in single file, each a white coil smaller than a lentil, hundreds of them, evenly spaced. Between the rows, bryozoans spread in pale crusts. Each crust is a sheet of box-shaped chambers, and each chamber holds one animal that puts out a ring of tentacles and takes it back in. A crust grows outward from where its first larva landed, adding boxes at the rim. Where two crusts meet, they stop and raise a low wall between them. Nothing here is more than nine months old. Many of the rim boxes are finished but not yet lived in, thin-walled and empty until an animal buds into them.

All of it hangs downward. The ceiling is the ground. The mud below is where things are lost.

The mud has its own town, though. Amphipods spin tubes of silk and silt that stand upright like chimneys, packed so close that their rims touch. Among them live foraminifera, single cells that gather grains and cement them into shells. They choose their grains. One kind takes only the mica flakes the granite has begun to shed, and sets each one flat side out, so the floor is flecked with points that catch what light there is.

That isn't much. Above are two metres of sea ice and then the spring sun. What reaches the bottom is a grey-green dusk that enters at the mouth and lies on the sill about a thumb deep. A brown film of diatoms grows there, beside one pink blot of coralline alga the size of a coin and a single red thread of weed that leans in and out with the water. That is all the plant life there is. Two small snails work the film in strips and leave clean stone behind them, and the film closes over it within days. Past the sill the water goes from green to grey to a dark in which the white tubes still just show.

Water moves through twice a day with the tide, slowly, in low and out high. When new water comes, the fans open, red crowns from the worm tubes and pale rings from the bryozoan boxes. They open from the mouth inward, district by district. When a shadow crosses the mouth they shut in the same order, faster. None of this makes a sound. Far across the fjord the ice cracks, but here the crack arrives only as a tremor. The fans flinch once and reopen.

The inhabitants are many and close together. Skeleton shrimp stand on the crusts, hooked on by their hind legs. They sway upright with their arms folded, hundreds of them, and snatch copepods out of the current. A sea spider, nearly all leg, crosses the bryozoan quarter at a pace of one box a minute. It puts its proboscis into a chamber, empties it, and moves on. It leaves a line of dead boxes, and within a week the neighbours have budded into them. A sea star the size of a fingernail is working down a row of tube worms. The coils behind it don't stay empty long, because tanaids move in and spin their own silk across the doors. A brittle star keeps its disc in a crack and its arms threaded through three streets. A snailfish, thumb-length and pink, spends most days fastened to the sill by its belly sucker. A scale worm lives at the very back and comes out when the green fades. If anything touches it, it drops a scale that glows blue for a few seconds, and the thing that touched it follows the scale down.

Weather comes in sideways. Most days it is marine snow, grey flakes of dead plankton that drift in on the tide, and the whole city feeds on it. After a melt pulse, silt fills the pocket for a day and everything closes. Then it settles and the floor town digs out and builds its chimneys a little higher. On cold nights the ice above grows and sheds brine, which is heavier and colder than the sea. It sinks, runs along the bottom, and finds the low places. At the back of the pocket there is a dip in the mud where it collects. It forms a pool under the water, and its surface shows as a shimmer. Nothing builds in the dip. Amphipods that wander in slow down and stop. The tubes at its edge lean away from it.

In the middle of the ceiling there is an oval the size of a palm where nothing has settled. The polish is the same there and the scratches run straight through. Larvae drift up to it, touch it, and go on. The rows of worm tubes stop at its edge in a clean curve. The bryozoan crusts have raised boundary walls against it, the kind they raise against a neighbour. The skeleton shrimp along its border face inward. Either something already holds that ground or something is expected, and the city is being built around it.

The stone is still settling. The mud gives under it by a hair's width a month. The pocket was taller in autumn. The chimneys rise and the crusts grow down, and at the back they have already met. One amphipod tube touched a hanging crust, and the bryozoan grew onto it and down it, box by box. It is the first column in the city, joining floor to ceiling. Others are a few weeks off. The place may fill solid, a block of dwellings with water only in the doorways, or the stone may come to rest and leave the gap open. For now the tide still gets through, and everything is still building.
