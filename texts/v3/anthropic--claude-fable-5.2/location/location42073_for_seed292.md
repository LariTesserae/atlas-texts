The tableland stands some twenty-seven hundred meters above the sea and nearly a thousand above the forest at its foot. It has stood roughly this way since before there were animals with bones. The rock is quartzite, beach sand from a shoreline almost two billion years gone, pressed and lifted and then worn at until everything around it was carried off. What remains is a flat-topped remnant forty kilometers long with sheer edges. It holds no fossils, because it is older than anything that could have left one.

It is almost never dry. Wet wind hits the cliffs, rises, cools, and lets go. The summit takes eight or nine meters of rain a year, before counting what the cloud leaves simply by lying on the ground. On most days there is no clean line between air and water. Rain comes in walls, sideways, and at the rim it comes upward, where the updraft catches the waterfalls and throws them back over the edge. From inside, the place feels like the bed of a cold, shallow, fast-moving sea that happens to have air mixed into it.

The top is flat only from a distance. Up close the stone has dissolved along its joints into a labyrinth of towers, capped pedestals, shoulder-wide slots, and sinkholes a hundred meters across with whole forests growing at the bottom. The rock is black with a living crust and pink where it has freshly broken. Between the outcrops lie flats of white quartz sand and beds of loose crystals. Water stained the color of strong tea stands in every hollow and runs in every crack.

Rivers follow the joints, drop into holes, and travel through the mountain in the dark. Some come out halfway down the cliff face, falling from openings with no visible source. Others reach the rim and go over, dozens of them, and most never arrive at the bottom as water. They fray into mist on the way down and drift into the lower forest as a second rain.

Plants are packed in wherever a handful of sand and peat will hold. The rock gives them almost nothing, so they improvise:

- **Bromeliads** keep tanks of rainwater in their leaves, and smaller plants live inside the tanks.
- **Pitcher plants** stand in clumps of red-veined green funnels, each with a drain slit so the storms don't burst it.
- **Sundews** cover the wet sand in sticky red mats.
- **Low trees** with leathery rosettes of leaves lean away from the wind, sleeved in moss and liverwort that drips steadily.
- **Peat bog** lies a meter deep in the basins and quakes underfoot.

Down in the sinkholes and gullies, out of the wind, there is true forest. Ten-meter trunks and tree ferns grow there, with orchids on the branches, moss on the orchids, and seedlings rooted in the moss.

The animals are small and there are a great many of them. Swifts roost by the thousand on ledges behind the waterfalls and fly straight through the curtain to leave. At dawn they pour out screaming and at dusk they funnel back in. Black toads the size of a thumbnail walk across the bare rock, since they cannot hop. When startled they tuck in their legs and tumble downhill like pebbles. Every bromeliad holds a frog, and after dark their chorus is loud enough to compete with the water. Hummingbirds work the flowers in the minutes between squalls. A hawk hangs on the rim updraft without moving its wings. Midges rise in smoke-like columns whenever the wind drops, dragonflies hunt through them, and under the loose slabs tarantulas sit in dry pockets the size of a fist.

The weather never holds. Cloud comes over the rim like something poured upward, and a thirty-kilometer view shrinks to arm's length in the time it takes to lace a boot. Then the sun breaks through, the black rock steams, and rainbows stand in three places at once. Ten minutes later there is hail. Lightning favors the towers, and the thunder runs up and down the slots for a long time. Rivers rise a meter in an hour. A sand flat crossed in the morning is a chest-deep channel by noon and a different sand flat by evening, its bars rearranged and its pools drained through some new opening. Now and then a slab the size of a house peels off the rim, and the boom carries across the whole plateau. All of this violence is in the water. The rock takes it and stays essentially what it was a hundred million years ago.

People came up once, by cableway, to measure the rivers for power, and they left a few things:

- **The upper station** is a concrete plinth and a steel A-frame. Cable is still wound on the drum, and one strand hangs over the edge into the cloud.
- **The diesel that drove it** has rusted to an orange block, and bromeliads are growing in its cylinder bores.
- **A gauging weir** crosses the largest river. Its painted staff is still legible up to 2.0 meters, and the water goes over the top of it several times a month.
- **A riveted steel pipe** runs three hundred meters along a rock shelf from the weir to a tin shed at the rim, where a test turbine was bolted down.

The intake grate on the pipe is long gone, so it fills whenever the river is up. The wheel still turns in fits, its buckets scoured thin by sand and its bearings loose, and it drives a generator that has fused into a single lump. Its whine rises and falls with the storms. A copper rain gauge beside the shed has been overflowing for sixty years.

It is never quiet here. The falls put a low note into the ground that is felt in the chest before it is heard. Over that comes rain on rock, on leaves, and on the tin roof, then wind in the slots, the swifts and frogs, thunder, and the stuttering wheel.

Sometimes toward evening the cloud lifts off whole and the horizon appears. Other tablelands stand out there, each under its own storm and each shedding white threads of water down its sides. Then the cloud comes back up over the rim and closes in again.
