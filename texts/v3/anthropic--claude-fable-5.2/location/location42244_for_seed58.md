The way in is a lift shaft, and from the bottom of it the surface is a grey coin of daylight that lights nothing. The shaft opens into a single chamber about eighty metres long and thirty wide, with a roof high enough to hold a four-storey building and leave room for weather. A whole town has been packed into it.

The rock is young basalt, dark and close-grained, and it was opened recently. The walls still show how they were made: half-barrels of drill holes in rows, fracture faces glassy and curved like the inside of a shell, edges that would open a palm. Nothing here has had time to soften. The first mineral crust on the roof is as thin as a coat of paint, and the stalactites are hollow straws no longer than a finger, each with a bead at its tip. The rock still ticks and settles. Every so often a flake lets go from overhead and lands on a tin roof with a single flat clink.

The roofs are the first odd thing. Every building has one, pitched and guttered, with downpipes, though the whole town sits under stone. The buildings stand in two ranks against the long walls, firebrick below and riveted plate above, three and four storeys high, leaning toward each other across a street narrow enough to span with outstretched arms. Catwalks cross overhead at every level. Pipes run everywhere, lagged in grey wrapping and banded with wire, along façades, under stairs and through walls. Where two pipes meet a third there is a valve wheel, and each wheel carries an enamel tag with a number. Stencilled signs read BOILER ROW, EXCHANGE 2 and NO NAKED FLAME BEYOND THIS POINT. Screw jacks brace the upper storeys against the rock, and beside each jack someone has chalked dates in a column, recording every tightening. The newest dates are not recent, and the jacks have fallen behind the rock. Hairline gaps show where plate has pulled away from brick.

The heat comes from below. The street is iron grating over a culvert, and the air that rises through it is the temperature of a bread oven with its door left open. It smells of wet stone, hot metal, machine oil and a little struck-match sulphur. The handrails are wound in cord because bare iron would be too hot to grip. The brick is warm to shoulder height, and above that it is hot.

The roofs are there because it rains. Vapour climbs all hour from the culvert and the leaking joints and gathers under the ceiling in a dull, slow-rolling layer that blurs the upper catwalks. Then somewhere up the shaft a damper swings open on its thermostat, cooler air slides across the roof of the chamber, and the layer lets go. The rain is warm and fine and lasts about eleven minutes. It drums softly on the tin, fills the gutters and runs down the pipes into the culvert, where a small turbine takes it. During the rain the lamps brighten, visibly, from a tired amber to something close to yellow. When it stops the town steams and creaks and is dry again within a quarter of an hour. White tide-lines of mineral are left on the brick, each a hair higher than the last. There is no standing water except the sump at the low end, which is black and still and too hot to touch. Everywhere else the damp comes and goes.

The lamps are filament bulbs in wire cages, one at each corner and landing, and perhaps a third of them have failed. Their light reaches a few metres and gives out. Past that there are only shapes, the edge of a stair or the curve of a tank or a doorway. Around each working lamp, on brick or plate or raw rock, spreads a rough green disc. It is algae, thick as felt, and it extends exactly as far as the light can feed it. A dead lamp leaves a disc of grey crust.

The other plants follow the plumbing. A flat, dark liverwort grows along every weeping joint and so traces each fault in the pipework. Pale ferns root in the gutter silt and lean out over the street from the eaves, their fronds almost translucent, turned toward the nearest bulb. A thread of moss runs down the wall below each downpipe bracket. Anywhere the water does not reach or the light does not fall, nothing grows at all.

Nothing eats any of it. The fronds have no holes, the algae shows no grazed tracks, and the ferns stand whole to the tips. No moths circle the lamps. The stair angles hold no webs, and the dust on the windowsills of the upper rooms, where the rain cannot get in, carries no small prints. Enamel mugs hang on hooks inside the wired-glass window of the canteen. Coats do not hang beside them. The lift comes down on its timer, opens its gate on the empty landing, waits, closes it and goes back up.

The town is quiet without being silent, because the work goes on. The turbine holds a low note that is felt in the chest more than heard. Iron ticks as it warms and ticks again as the rain cools it. A relief valve on Boiler Row breathes out every few minutes, a long, unhurried exhalation. Drips fall into the culvert at intervals. Between the rains every sound can be told apart from the others. During them there is only the soft roar on the roofs, and under it the turbine's note rising a half-tone as the lamps come up.

At the far end the street stops at the cut face, where the chamber was still being made. Here the rock is at its rawest, and it is hotter than anywhere else in the town. A drill carriage stands on its rails with the steel still in the hole. The last building is only a frame of uprights and one course of brick, without a roof, so the rain falls straight through onto the grating. A fern has already taken hold in the mortar of that single course, under the last lamp on the line.

Overhead the vapour is thickening again, and the next rain is not far off.
