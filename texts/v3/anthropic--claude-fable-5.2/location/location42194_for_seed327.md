Just under the summit ridge, where the air is thin enough that a climber stops every twenty steps, the mountain's shoulder has fallen in on itself. The roof of some old chamber gave way long ago and left a shaft open to the sky, perhaps sixty paces across and a little deeper than that. The edges of the collapse have had time to round off. Nothing looks broken anymore, and the rim is a lip of grey rock worn smooth as a bowl's.

Everything alive here lives on the walls. From rim to floor the rock is furred with moss a hand deep, and out of the moss grow ferns, hanging sedges, and silver-leaved rosettes the size of shields that fold shut at dusk like fists. Thick-trunked groundsels lean out from the ledges wearing skirts of their own dead leaves. All of it drips. It rarely rains properly at this height. The water arrives as cloud and is combed out by leaf-hair and moss, then runs down in threads. At the bottom lies a black pool ringed with bog cushions that have the firm give of bread.

The middle holds nothing. This green is a skin no thicker than an arm's length, wrapped around a column of empty air. The floor is small and the ledges are narrow. Standing on any of them, you are aware mostly of the hollow, a room's worth of nothing that only flying things cross.

Plenty of them do. Swifts nest in the wall cracks by the hundreds and wheel through the shaft all day, always turning the same way, sunwise, as if the air had a grain to it. They scream as they go. Small iridescent birds work the rosette flowers and bicker in voices like struck flint. On the ledges live stout, tailless rock-animals the color of lichen, which sun themselves in rows and bark across the gap at one another in a rasping, ratcheting cry far too large for their bodies. Midges rise off the pool in smoke-columns and the swifts dip through them. A pair of falcons keeps the rim. When one stoops, the whole shaft knows it: the barking stops, the swifts scatter out the top like sparks, and for a breath the dripping is the loudest sound.

It is never loud for long, that dripping, because the place has a voice of its own. On the downhill side the wall is pierced by a ragged window as tall as two people, which looks out onto a drop so long that the valleys below are only a blue suggestion. Wind crossing the open mouth above and pulling through that window plays the shaft like a blown bottle, and it gives one low note, felt more in the breastbone than heard, that swells and sags with the gusts. All the other sounds sit on top of it. The rock-animals seem to bark in its lulls. The echo off the far wall comes back a shade lower than it left.

The day runs on a fixed schedule. At dawn the walls are stiff with frost and the rosettes are sealed. The sun reaches the western wall first, and the thaw begins there with a ticking that builds into a patter and then into a steady hiss of meltwater that works its way around the shaft as the light does. By midday the cloud has climbed the outer face of the mountain. It comes in through the window, rising and pouring upward over the sill like slow milk, and fills the hollow from the bottom until the far wall is gone and the swifts are only voices. Hail often follows and rattles on the leathery leaves. Late in the day the cloud drains out the way it came, the walls stand rinsed and shining, and the swifts return in a single funnel that takes a quarter hour to empty into the rock.

People come here, though not many and not often. A notched pole, grey with age, leans from the floor to the first ledge. Above it, pegs of bone and hard wood are driven into cracks in a line that climbs toward the densest nest-holes, and rotted loops of twisted grass cord still hang from a few of them. Under an overhang by the pool there is a knee-high windbreak of stacked stone, a ring of blackened cobbles, a scatter of obsidian flakes, and a drift of tiny eggshell trodden into the peat. Shallow cups have been pecked into a flat boulder, and they hold water and, sometimes, a drowned moth.

In the window itself, someone has built up the sill with courses of flat slabs, dry-laid and carefully chinked, narrowing the opening by a third. It might be a wind-wall. But it shelters nothing, and where a few slabs have fallen the note of the place wavers and sours when the gusts come from that side. Someone, at some point, cared what the mountain sang and adjusted it.

At night the frost comes back down the walls and the note drops with the wind until it is almost a breath. Then the rock-animals start, one ledge at a time. Their ratcheting cries cross the empty middle and return slightly lower, until the dark hollow is full of them.
