The rock came first and is nearly finished. It is banded gneiss, pink and grey, the root of a mountain range that was planed to within a metre of flat long before anything walked on it. It lies a little above the level of a sea that can be smelled but not seen. In one place the plain dips into a shallow basin about half a kilometre across, and the basin holds water: a hand deep, in places a forearm deep, tea-coloured and blood-warm. One whaleback of bare stone stands two metres out of it, weeping from its seams, and that is the highest ground for a day's walk.

Over the water lies the plant. It is a single organism, a felt of fleshy green stems somewhere between a liverwort and a sedge, shin-deep and floating, and it covers the basin from edge to edge. It quakes underfoot, and a step sends a slow ripple outward that takes a long time to die. Mostly there is nothing on it. The eye crosses a hundred metres of trembling green before it finds anything upright, and then another hundred before the next.

The upright things are the town.

A building begins as lines. A pale filament appears in the air, vertical, taut, attached to nothing at either end. Then come others, crossing it, until the outline of a hall or a round house stands drawn above the mat. The lines cast no shadow. Rain beads on them and the beads stay where they are. Each line hums at a pitch set by its length, so a house sounds as a chord before it exists.

Then the plant climbs. The stems go up the lines at a pace that can be watched, crackling like wet celery snapped in the hands, and in forty minutes there is a wall, then a roof, then a door left open in it. The building weighs nothing while it rises, and the mat beneath it lies level. When the last stem closes, all of its weight arrives at once. The mat dips, brown water wells up around the footing, and a thud travels across the basin through the shins of everything standing on it.

A building lasts an hour, or two days. Then its lines are gone, all together, and the walls slump into a green heap that by evening is flat mat again, a shade darker than what surrounds it.

At any moment perhaps thirty buildings stand, a dozen are going up and a dozen are coming down. No building rises twice running on the same spot. There are long halls, round houses, and a four-storey tower that is the tallest thing the lines ever sketch. There are rows of small open-fronted cells that face each other across a lane, where the stems lie pressed flat and combed in one direction as if by years of feet. The cells have counters and the halls have benches, grown in place. Every room has a basin in the floor that fills with clear water, the only clear water in the landscape. Rain stops a hand's width above every roof and slides off sideways along nothing, so the floors inside are dry. Nobody comes. The row of cells stands open through the morning and by noon it is down, and another row is being drawn in the air across the basin, at a different angle to the light.

The animals have learned the schedule, or been shaped by it. There are frogs in the tens of thousands, and their chorus is the ground note of the place. It cuts off at every thud and starts again from one side, as if passed along. Under the mat live small whiskered catfish, and each settling squeezes them up through the torn footing with the brown water. The egrets know this. When a chord starts humming they walk toward it from all over the basin, unhurried, and stand in a ring around the place where the weight will land, squabbling over position. Dragonflies perch on the lines, and they are the only creatures that will. Rails run the pressed lanes, heads down, as though the lanes had been made for them. At dusk the bats leave whichever hall they slept in and by dawn have chosen another, never one that will fall that day. Frogs sit in the clear basins. A monitor lizard lives on the whaleback and does not come down.

Squalls come in off the unseen coast three or four times a day. They are short and loud, and the rain on the mat makes a sound like cloth tearing. Between them the light is ordinary, a bright flat daylight through thin cloud, with steam lifting off the green. A hard squall will bring down a half-risen wall and the lines will stand there bare, humming, until the plant starts up them again. Sometimes it tears the mat open instead.

The tears show the bedrock. Through a foot of brown water the gneiss lies dished in shallow rectangles and rings, overlapping, each polished smoother than the stone between. They are footings. Every one has taken the arrival of a building's weight more times than can be counted, and the rock has given a little each time. The whole plan of the town is down there in stone, with every position it has ever occupied laid over every other. This explains the basin, which is the sum of the thuds. The plain was level here once, and the town has worn its own bowl into rock that rain alone had finished with.

The rim is a clean line. Outside it is ordinary swamp of pandanus, paperbark and mangrove on the seaward side, crowded and loud with its own birds. None of it crosses in. A seed that lands on the mat is under the surface by morning. No line has ever been drawn past the edge, and the egrets that follow the chords stop there as though at a wall.

The whaleback has no hollows on it. Now and then lines appear around it, a ring or a square with the bare stone at the centre, and hum for a minute. The egrets start walking. Then the lines go out, nothing rises, and the birds stand in the open green for a while, facing the rock, before they drift apart again.
