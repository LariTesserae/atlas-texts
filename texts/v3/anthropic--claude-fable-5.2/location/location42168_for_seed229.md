The col sits between two horns of rock, a saddle of bare stone three hundred paces long and a hundred wide, and the wind comes through it without stopping. It rises from the basin on the windward side, kiln-warm and carrying dust, squeezes between the horns, and falls away over the leeward lip into another emptiness. It has never rained here. There is no streambed, no stain, no memory of wet in the rock. The pavement has been polished by grit for so long that it has stopped changing; every boulder is already keeled and fluted into the shape the wind wants.

The light is brown. The dust makes a lid over the pass, and the sun at noon is a smudge you can look at directly. Shadows are soft, where there are any.

On this pavement, far apart, stand the mounds. There are perhaps forty, each alone, each with twenty or thirty paces of naked stone around it. A mound is one plant, or one thicket that behaves as a plant. Its windward face is taller than a person and dense with grey-green fronds, waxy and felted, unrolling fast enough to watch. Behind that face the growth ages within hours, goes amber, then brown and papery, and tears away in strips. Each mound trails a tail of tatters thirty paces long that thrashes like wet canvas, except nothing here is wet. The sap is an oil that smells of turpentine and hot flint.

The mounds have no roots. Their holdfasts grip the polish and creep, and each mound walks upwind a hand's breadth or two a day, feeding its front and losing its back. Behind each one a dark stripe of resin stains the stone, and the pavement is crossed everywhere with old stripes, a record of paths. When a mound reaches the windward lip, where the gusts come up unbroken, it is torn off whole. It rolls back down the col, bounding and shedding and breaking against the others. Whatever fragment lodges near the leeward end grips and begins again. The grove holds its place the way a flame does, by constant replacement.

What feeds them is the wind itself: its dust, the spore and shred of the grove's own wreckage coming round again, and the charge. The windward fronds crackle. After dark each mound wears a thin blue fringe on its upwind face, and sparks jump from tip to tip in the stronger gusts. Calm is the drought here. In the rare lull the fronds droop within minutes, the fringe goes out, and the whole col starts dying in a silence the animals plainly cannot bear.

The animals are everywhere the mounds are and nowhere else.

- **Kite-things:** Off every windward face hang hundreds of small flat creatures, each tethered to a frond by a single thread and sieving the dust. When a thread snaps, the animal is gone over the leeward lip.
- **Beetles:** The tails are full of hard-shelled beetles eating the dead paper, thousands to a mound. They hatch in bursts when a gust shakes the litter and are stripped away in bursts when the next one comes.
- **Runners:** Wedge-shaped animals, low and scaled, sprint across the bare stone between mounds with their heads down. They time each crossing to the troughs between gusts and sometimes get it wrong.
- **Grazers:** A dozen keel-backed, goat-sized animals stand nose to wind in the lee of the larger mounds, pulling down brown strips. They speak by knocking their hooves on the rock, since no voice carries.
- **Flute-nesters:** Small creatures live in the holes of the rock horns, and their coming and going changes the pitch of the stone.

The noise is total. The horns are pierced and fluted from top to bottom and give a chord that slides but never stops. The mounds' hollow stalks pipe above it, the tails crack, and the beetles rattle like gravel in a gourd. The grazers knock, the kite-things click on their threads, and the dust hisses across the polish underneath it all.

People come, though not many and not for long. Their marks are few:

- **The wall:** A curved wall of stacked stone, knee-high, stands near the northern horn. The hollow scraped behind it is big enough for three to lie flat.
- **The handlines:** Bone pegs are hammered into cracks, with plaited-fiber lines strung between them at hip height. The lines are overgrown by an advancing mound one day and scoured clean the next.
- **The scraping stone:** A flat stone is grooved where resin is scraped with flint and copper blades. Beside it are a few clay jars, sealed with the grove's own wax and wedged under an overhang.
- **The whistle-stone:** A boulder at the center has five holes drilled in it, each wider than the last. The wind sounds them in order as it strengthens.

The harvesters do not speak in the col. They use tugs on the line and flat-handed signs, and they keep one ear on the boulder. The top four holes sound most days. When the fifth begins, a low note felt in the ribs more than heard, they drop what they are holding and go behind the wall. That note means the big gusts are coming up from the basin with dust-devils in them. The devils cross the pavement, uprooting mounds out of turn and flinging grazers off their feet, and in an afternoon they rearrange the whole grove. Afterward the stripes on the stone no longer lead to anything.

Then the gusts settle back to the ordinary roar. The fragments grip where they landed and the fronds start unrolling. The kite-things that are left let out new thread.
