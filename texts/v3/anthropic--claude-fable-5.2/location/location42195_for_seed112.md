The plateau sits four thousand metres up and is the size of a continent's interior. To the eye it is flat in every direction, though it tilts a degree or two toward the west, and far past the horizon it ends in scarps where the wind pours off into lower country. All of it is covered by a single pale mat of plant, ankle-deep, silver-green, and haired like felt. There is no bare rock, no sand, and no soil in the usual sense. Under the living mat lies its own dead, tens of metres of it, dry and mummified, because nothing here has ever been wet enough to rot.

It does not rain, and the sky has no cloud to rain from. It is a hard blue that goes toward violet overhead, and the sun in it is small and fierce. The felt's hairs throw most of that light straight back, so the ground glares, shadows are black and sharp-edged, and the air stays cool, thin and absolutely clear. A dot on the horizon might be thirty kilometres off.

The water is in the bedrock. Below the dead layer the plateau is gypsum and other hydrated salts, and the roots go down to it, etch the crystals with acid, split them, and take the water bound inside. The work is audible as reports from below, single and in strings, like blasting in a distant quarry. The spent mineral comes back up as a fine white powder, pushed out through knee-high vent-stems that puff it into the wind. The whole plateau smokes with thin white plumes, each leaning the same way, thousands visible at once and none of them close together. Water never appears as liquid. It moves as sap and vapour under a waxy skin, and where the mat is cut it bleeds a thick resin that seals within seconds.

The wind arrives by mid-morning. A surface this large and this bright drives its own circulation, and the gale it makes runs all day at a speed that would flatten anything upright. Nothing here stands higher than a metre for long. The tallest things are last year's flowering stalks, dead and hollow, each snapped off at a height that gives it a pitch. There are millions of them, and together they make a chord that does not resolve or pause. It is closer to a foundry's blower than to music.

Over that come the pods. They are woody capsules on sprung stems, and they thresh themselves by beating against one another, a dry clatter like a weaving shed that runs to the horizon. The crust adds its own noise. The dead layer expands in the sun and cracks, and the cracks sound like rivets being driven. No animal on the plateau calls to another, because no call could be heard.

They signal with light instead. The ground birds here have underwings of a white so reflective that a single flick can be read twenty kilometres away, and across the felt there is a constant sparse glitter of them, flashing in ones and twos. The grazers are low, broad-footed animals that walk nose to the wind and crop the mat for its sap. They carry the same white on their rumps. A herd may hold ten thousand of them, but each keeps a hundred metres from the next, so the herd is a scatter of dots across a whole quadrant of the view, held together by flashes.

Above them the hunters hang. They are narrow-winged birds that face the gale and stay fixed in it for hours without a wingbeat, one to every few square kilometres of sky. When one folds and drops, the white signals ripple outward from the spot.

The surface does not hold still. The mat is pegged to the dead layer only at intervals, and sooner or later a gust finds an edge. Then a sheet the size of a field, or of a town, peels up with a noise like canvas ripping, loud enough to carry over everything else. It folds over itself and rolls. A big bale stands twenty metres high and may travel a hundred kilometres before it breaks apart, shedding pods and seed the whole way.

Things live in the bales. Beetles, small nesting birds and a burrowing mouse-like animal ride them across the plateau and scatter when they collapse. Behind each bale lies a brown scar of exposed dead-wood. Moths come to drink at its bleeding edges before the resin skins over. Within weeks the scar is felted in from the sides with new growth, brighter than the old.

By afternoon the air is full of chaff and mineral powder, and everything in it is charged. The stalk tips fizz with a violet corona that is hard to see in the glare. The lightning travels sideways, from ground to ground along the dust plumes, and its thunder is flat and immediate. Where it strikes living felt, a thin fire runs fast, burns off the hairs, and dies. Where it strikes a fresh scar, the fire goes under.

Smoulder fronts creep through the dead layer at a few metres a year. Some have been burning longer than any stalk or herd has existed. They bake the last bound moisture upward, and the living mat above them catches it and grows thick and dark. From a height they show as green-black bands tens of kilometres long, drifting slowly across the silver. The ground along them is warm. Burrowers honeycomb it, and the grazers come to lie on it through the cold hours.

So the plateau is a patchwork of ages: new silver, old grey-green, raw brown, and the dark bands of the fires. No two days show the same arrangement, though the proportions hardly change. The scarps at the edge show how long this has gone on. They are banded top to bottom with compressed mat, scar and ash line, hundreds of metres of it. A good part of the plateau's height is its own accumulated dead.

At dusk the wind fails for about an hour while the flow reverses. The pipes stop, but it does not become quiet. The crust contracts in the cold and cracks faster than it did at noon, the reports from the roots go on, and thunder rolls in from storms too far away to see. The stars come out hard and close, and the powder plumes stand straight up for the only time all day, pale in the starlight.

Then the night wind starts from the opposite quarter. The stalks were cut to their pitches by the day wind. Blown from the wrong side, they sound a lower, rougher chord. Across the dark, in scattered white flashes, the grazers turn around to face it.
