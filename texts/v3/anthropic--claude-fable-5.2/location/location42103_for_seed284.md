From the ridge, at a little under six thousand metres, it is a dark line in pale gneiss. It is sixty metres long, narrow enough at either end to step across, and five metres wide at the middle. There is no smoke. The only signs are a shimmer of warmer air standing over the line and choughs circling in it, several hundred of them.

The mountain split here thirty-one years ago, in one night. The faces of the break are still clean, with no varnish and no weathering rind, and their edges will open glove leather. Down the north face runs the reason for everything else: a quartz vein as wide as a doorway, set with black blades of wolframite. Four hundred people live in the crack and take it apart.

**The rim**

Along the lip squat the wind dynamos, three-bladed, guyed down with cable, shrieking. The wind tears their noise away east, and three ladders below the rim it is only a kind of ceiling, something you stop hearing by the second day. The ropeway leaves from the rim too. Its buckets swing out over a drop nobody looks at and go down toward the valley, four vertical kilometres below, carrying black concentrate.

Blue sheep come to the outer wall, to the stain under the latrine chute, and lick the salt.

**Inside**

The town is stacked rather than laid out. The transformer racks and the ice store sit at the top. The ice comes up by bucket, sawn from a glacier far below, and it is the only water there is. It is issued by weight. Nothing here drips or is damp. Lips split, and stone dust lies in a pale, floury film on every horizontal surface, marked with bird tracks.

Below that are the cells, riveted steel boxes lagged in grey felt and bolted to the south wall. Each one's door faces the working wall across three metres of air. The gap is crossed by grating catwalks, ladders, cable trays and overhead nets, and the bucket chain runs up through the middle of it all like a spine. Lower down are the crushers, wrapped and geared slow, and the magnetic separators, which pull the ore from the quartz dry and make a hum you feel in your molars more than hear. At the bottom, ninety metres down, is the face, where breath shows. At mid-level it doesn't. The engines keep the crack merely cool, and the warmest place in the town is the ledge behind the transformers, where the off-shift and the choughs sleep within reach of each other.

**Light**

The sun reaches the floor for eleven minutes after noon. The rest of the time there is a strip of sky, white by day and almost indigo at dusk, and below it the bulbs: low-voltage, yellow, each one a ball of haze in the dust. Their filaments are drawn from metal that left this crack in a bucket. The lamp glass is furred with moths, and brushing them off is somebody's job.

**The hush**

No one shouts. The rock is too new, and nobody is certain what it will tolerate, so nobody tests it. People talk with lamp shutters, with tug-lines, and with a code of knuckle-raps on the handrail that travels the length of a catwalk faster than a voice would. Boots are soled in felt. The bucket rollers are greased daily. The drills run only during the eleven sunlit minutes, when everyone is awake and watching the walls. Otherwise the rock is split with screw wedges, turned a quarter at a time, until the stone lets go with a sound like a knuckle cracking.

What you hear instead:

- the separators
- claws on grating
- gravel sifting into the nets
- a small dry snap, everywhere and constantly

The snap is static. The air is thin and dry, and the belts and felt charge everything. A brass earthing rail runs beside every walkway, and people drag their knuckles along it from habit and touch it before they touch each other. In the dark hours the top sheave of the bucket chain wears a faint blue brush of discharge. Before storms the dynamo blades wear one too.

**Motion**

For all the quiet, nothing holds still. The fracture has not finished. It widens a few millimetres a month in the middle and is closing at the east end. Thin glass slips are cemented across every hairline, and when one breaks it makes a single clear tick. Everyone in earshot stops, counts to thirty, and resumes.

So the town is re-rigged without pause. Catwalks ride on sliding joints, cells are unbolted and shifted a metre west, and the bucket chain is re-hung. Riggers outnumber miners, and a map is wrong within the week. There are three shifts and every bunk is shared. On the ladders, climbers keep to the wall side and descenders to the air side, and all of it moves at the pace of people who cannot afford to breathe hard. The nets fill with rockfall and are emptied hourly. The work is frantic and nearly silent.

**What lives here**

Nothing green lives here. Near the exhaust vents an orange lichen has started, in crusts the size of thumbnails, and a grey mould works slowly through the oldest felt. That is all.

The animals do not depend on anything growing here. The valley wind strikes the mountain and climbs, and the warmth of the crack draws it in. It carries midges, aphids, winged ants and small moths lifted from the forests far below. They arrive stunned or dead and sift down through the gratings like chaff.

- **Jumping spiders.** Small and grey, one in every bolt-hole, living on the insect fall.
- **Wallcreepers.** They work the north face for the spiders, and the crimson of their wings is the only red in the place.
- **Snowfinches.** They glean the catwalks between boots.
- **Mice.** They nest in the lagging.
- **Cats.** Lean and numerous, ears cropped by frost, they hunt the mice and are tolerated on any bunk.
- **Choughs.** They own the cable trays. They ride the empty buckets down and the full ones up, steal rations, and have learned to go still at the tick of a tell-tale exactly as the people do.

At the east end, where the walls are coming back together, a cell was left bolted in place eleven years ago to see what would happen. Its door is now a crease. The spiders are thick in it, and the choughs will not go near.
