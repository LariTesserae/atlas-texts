Just under the summit ridge, where the air is thin enough that the sky darkens toward noon, a slab of grey andesite has slipped forward and come to rest against the mountain. Behind it is a hollow about the size of a pantry: four paces deep and not quite tall enough to stand in. From the ridge you would never see it. You would hear it first.

Nothing wet has ever reached this range. Snow sublimes off the peaks before it can melt, the rock has no stains or runnels, and no frost forms at night. The scree outside the hollow is bare for a thousand metres in every direction. Inside the hollow, there is no bare surface at all.

At the back wall, set in a concrete collar whose edges have long since rounded off, is a ventilation fan. It is a riveted steel drum with a cage of bars across the mouth, the exhaust of some working far down inside the mountain. A cable as thick as a wrist comes out of the rock and enters the motor housing. Above it a single bulb burns behind a wire guard, and the bulb is so crusted with orange lichen that it gives the light of a coal seen through a blanket. The fan is turning, and by every sign in this place it always has been. It pushes out a steady column of dry, cellar-cool air that smells of machine oil, stone dust, and something faintly sweet and mineral from deep below.

The growth has built itself on that breath. Cushion plants, hard as knuckles and bright dark green, have swollen across the floor until they meet and press each other into polygons. Out of the seams between them rise wiry shrubs with scale leaves, and each stem is sleeved in grey lichen, with sulphur-yellow lichen on top of that. Papery fronds overlap along the walls like shingles. From the underside of the slab hang beards of something pale and stiff, combed into a single direction. Nothing in here is moist. The cushions feel like warm wood, and where a stem was once nicked the resin stands in amber beads that have never run. Thin air, altitude, and cool should have kept all of it at the size of a thumbprint. Instead it has filled the hollow to the walls.

None of the plants face the light. A grey seam of sky shows where the slab fails to meet the mountain, and that is all the daylight there is. The leaves have turned their backs on it. Every frond, scale, and beard is angled toward the grille, the way iron filings are drawn to a pole. They are feeding on the exhaust. They sieve from it whatever the mountain's interior gives off: fine dust, and a trace of that sweet mineral gas. Directly in front of the cage, where the stream is too strong for anything to hold on, there is a clean circle of rock polished to a dull shine. It is the only empty space in the hollow, and its edge is as sharp as a line drawn with a compass.

The noise is enormous for so small a room. The fan gives a low, wide roar with a beat in it from the passing blades, and the cage adds a rattle that never falls out of step. The plants are part of it too. The papery fronds buzz like reeds, the hanging beards hiss, and the hollow stems of the shrubs each take a note and hold it. The result is one sustained chord, like an organ with a forearm laid across the keys, loud enough to feel in the teeth and the breastbone. Three steps outside the slab it is gone, cut off by the rock, and there is only the dry wind on the scree.

There are a few other signs that people were here. Three iron rungs are set in the wall, the lowest swallowed by a cushion plant. There is an enamel plate whose lettering has worn to a paler rectangle, and a pressure gauge whose glass is intact and whose needle rests at a figure just past the middle of the dial. A grease nipple on the motor housing has a cap worn round as a pebble. No tool has been left behind, no footprint shows in the dust, and no door leads anywhere.

No insect has ever come in on the airstream either. The shrubs carry small tight buds the colour of rust. The buds have not opened and will not, because nothing here could visit them. There are no webs in the cage bars, no droppings, and no gnawed stems.

There is no litter on the floor, since there is no floor to hold it, and no leaf has ever let go. Without moisture nothing rots, and nothing rotting means nothing has died, as far as anyone could tell. The growth reached the walls, the slab, and the edge of the polished circle a very long time ago, and it stopped there, with every gap taken. The hollow is fully occupied and in balance with the stream that feeds it. The exhaust comes out at the same temperature at midnight and at midday, in whatever counts for seasons on the peaks outside. The bulb keeps its ember, the needle keeps its figure, and the resin beads stay where they formed.
