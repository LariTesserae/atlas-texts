The plateau is new. Basalt came up through fissures and lay down in sheets a continent wide, one on another, until the pile stood four and a half thousand metres into thin air, and nothing since has had time to soften it. The flow tops still carry their glass rind. Where the sheets cracked in cooling they cracked into columns, and each column head is a flat hexagon with edges that will open a hide. There are no valleys, because water has not yet settled where to run. The surface is a single black table, stepped by scarps a few metres tall where one flow stopped on top of another, and it reaches the horizon in every direction and continues for weeks beyond it.

The cold is dry and total. The sky is dark blue, nearly black at the zenith, and the sun in it is small and hard and unfiltered. Almost no snow falls here, since the air is wrung out long before it climbs this far inland.

Very little of the rock can be seen, because animals are standing on it.

The grinders stand flank to flank across the table, shoulder-high and barrel-bodied, with dense grey wool underneath and plates of dark iron scale laid along the spine. Their hooves are rimmed with the same iron, grown rather than shod, because nothing softer survives the column edges. The mouth is a rasp. They eat the crust lichen and take the top grain of rock with it, and the iron in the rock goes into plate and hoof. They are packed so tightly that a grinder wanting to turn around must wait for its neighbours to want the same thing. The herd is better measured by area than by count. It has fronts, like weather.

It also has weather. Each exhalation puts a little water into air that had none, and there are enough exhalations that a cloud stands over the herd permanently, low and flat-bottomed and lit white from above and below. All of the plateau's water comes out of lungs. At night the breath freezes onto every windward surface as rime, in feathers a hand long, on horn tips, plate edges, and the lips of the columns. By mid-morning the sun burns it loose and it drips. The drip is the rain. It runs down the joints between columns, sits in the shallow cups of the column heads, and freezes again at dusk.

Where the drip falls on rock flour and dung, moss grows faster than the cold should allow. Its cushions are a metre thick and tight as upholstery, bright green on the black rock. Dwarf willow lies flat as a rug between them, its catkins standing no higher than a thumb. The grinders carry gardens too: seed lodges in the fleece, and the old ones wear a strip of sod down each side of the spine, grass rooted in wool and lichen on the plates. The green is trampled daily and returns daily. It grows in the lee of bodies and has rarely felt wind.

Nothing here is made. The rock will not take a burrow, and no loose material exists to build a nest. Trails cannot form where the whole surface is trail. Whatever an animal needs, it grows on itself or finds on the back of another, so the place works as a factory with no walls, in which every station is a body.

The grinders are the cutting face. On their backs ride the birds, millions of them, short-winged in the thin air and better at running than flying. They sprint across the plates from animal to animal without touching ground. They swallow the iron pellets the grinders pass and lay rust-coloured eggs with shells that ring when tapped. The eggs are brooded on the feet, on a moving back. The birds eat the flies. The flies breed in the warm dung-flour and run hot to the touch, and they rise at noon in columns thick enough to throw the only shadows on the plateau.

Among the grinders, one to every few thousand, stand the kilns. These are huge, slow, nearly hairless animals whose guts ferment moss at a heat that sends steam off their flanks all day. They vent through slits behind the jaw with a whistle that carries for kilometres. At night the grinders press in around them in rings, and from a scarp the herd looks like a cloth pulled tight around studs.

The cable animals lie in the joints between the columns. They are long and low, the colour of wet slate, and they rest nose to tail in unbroken lines that run farther than the eye can follow. In the dry air, iron plate rubbing on iron plate across a herd this size builds charge, and the cable animals drain it. The current runs down their bodies toward the damp centre of the plateau, and whatever it does for them, it keeps them warm. Moss grows thickest along them. Seen from any height, green lines cross the black table, dead straight and radial, like spokes.

All of it is loud. The rasping of that many mouths makes one sound, a roar like surf with grit in it, and it never pauses, because some part of the herd is always feeding. The plates clank over it and the kilns whistle. The birds scream at each other across a gap of two wingspans, since that is all the territory any of them has. Under everything runs the hum of the cable animals, a single steady pitch felt in the hooves before it is heard. Thin air should carry sound badly, but here the sound is made everywhere at once, so it doesn't need to carry.

Shade is as scarce as quiet. The sun strikes rime, and it strikes plates worn to a mirror by the neighbour's flank and rock glass polished by a hundred passes of iron hooves, and the light goes back up under bellies and into the joints and the open mouths. At night the charge shows itself. Every horn tip wears a blue fringe, small arcs stitch between flanks, and the breath-cloud flickers silently from inside, like a lamp behind paper. The stars are hard and close, and the herd goes on glowing beneath them.

Nothing happens quickly. No predator chases, because nothing has room to flee or to pursue. The few things that eat grinders are flat, clinging animals that attach to a flank and feed for a season. The dead do not fall. They stay upright, wedged between their neighbours, and are carried along while the birds and flies take them down to plate and bone. When the plates drop at last into the joints, the cable animals eat them, so the iron never leaves the plateau.

The herd turns. Near the centre of the plateau the last fissures still breathe a little warmth, and the whole mass revolves around them like a wheel, a body-length a day at the rim and less toward the hub. One revolution takes eleven years. Every grinder spends some of those years on the cold outer edge, eating raw glass-rinded rock under the thin end of the cloud, and some near the hub, where the calves are dropped into steam.

When the herd comes back around to ground it left eleven years before, it finds the lichen re-crusted and the moss grown as deep as a mattress. The rock beneath is a grain lower than it was. The plateau is young enough that this is the only wearing it has known. Its first valley is a ring, broad and shallow and hundreds of kilometres across, cut entirely by teeth. In the afternoons the rime-melt has started to collect at the bottom of it.
