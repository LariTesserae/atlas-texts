The floor of the basin is black rock that has never been anything else. It lies a few meters above sea level and runs about ten kilometers from one low rim to the other, in ropes, folds and blisters, with the skin of its cooling still on it. The ropes have sharp crests. The blisters, some as large as hills, have split along their backs, and the edges of the splits are glassy. Nothing is rounded. There is no sand, gravel, or mud. A stone broken off stays where it fell, with all its corners.

Heat comes up through this floor. The rock is warm to the palm everywhere and hot beside the fissures, where steam leaks out in thin, steady threads. The air sits at the temperature of the inside of a mouth and holds all the water it can. The steam rises a few hundred meters, meets its own weight, and spreads into a cloud lid that covers the basin from rim to rim. The lid does not break or thicken, and it does not rain. It sweats. Water beads on every surface, joins, and creeps downward.

It never gets far, because nothing has been carved for it to follow. There are no channels, gullies, or streams anywhere in the basin. Water goes to the nearest hollow in the rock and stops. So the floor holds thousands of warm, shallow pans, from the size of a cupped hand to the size of a clearing, each rimmed in orange and green bacterial felt and each at the level it has always held. What condenses equals what steams off. The rock between the pans weeps and shines.

On this bare, wet, hot stone stands a forest. It has no soil. Tree ferns grip the crests of the lava ropes with roots like knuckles. Figs pour their roots down the sides of the split blisters and into the cracks. Moss upholsters the shaded faces, liverworts plate the weeping ones, and filmy ferns hang translucent from every overhang. In the collapsed trenches where old lava tubes fell in, the growth is thickest, with fronds overlapping fronds and orchids rooted on the fern trunks. Seen from one of the blister-hills it is a dark green pelt over the whole basin, torn here and there by a line of vents or the grey shine of a larger pan.

The forest stays on rock because of the animals. Everything that falls is eaten. A frond that drops is covered within the hour by millipedes, snails, woodlice, and small red land crabs, and by the next hour it is gone. Litter never accumulates and never rots down into earth. The plants feed on droppings, on the film of water, and on each other's living surfaces. The basin has stayed at its first stage, forest on raw stone, because its inhabitants consume the second stage as fast as it is offered.

They are everywhere. Each pan has its frogs, with eggs on the rim felt and tadpoles in the warm water. Midges stand over each pan in a column, geckos wait at the edge of each column, and pale spiders have strung the gaps between fern trunks so closely that walking means breaking webs with the face. The fronds sag under roosting birds, small dark swifts and rails that have nearly given up flying. Bats hang in the tube mouths in sheets. Crabs click across the rock in such numbers that the ground appears to shift slightly at the edge of vision. Put a hand on a trunk and something moves under it. Lift a stone, one of the few loose ones, and the underside is solid with bodies.

The sound is a single thick fabric: the frogs' continuous creak, the saw of insects, the crabs' dry ticking, the leathery commotion in the tubes, and under it the hiss of the vents. It is loud enough that speech would have to be raised, if anything here spoke. It has no dawn swell or evening hush. There is no true dawn here, and that is the one strange thing about the place.

By day the cloud lid lets through a brown-green dusk, enough to see shapes and not enough for shadows. By night the dusk remains at almost the same strength, from another source. The undersides of the overhangs and the tube mouths are crusted with glowing fungus and hung with the blue threads of glowworms. The bacterial felts give off a faint light of their own, and a dull red shows in the deepest fissures. The light changes color from grey-green to blue-green, but its level stays close to the same.

So the animals keep no day. Each kind sleeps in shifts. Half the frogs in a pan call while the other half sit silent, and then they trade. The swifts and the bats use the same air at the same time and have sorted themselves by height. The chorus never thins. The midge columns never fall. A fern uncurls a new frond as the old one drops into the waiting crabs, and the fern is the same size it was.

At the lowest edge of the basin the rock slides under a warm, still lagoon without a beach. There is only a line where the ferns stop and the water starts. The line does not move.
