**The Basin**

The basin sits just under the height where breathing stops being automatic. It is an old cirque, scooped by ice that left so long ago the scars have gone soft. The headwall has rounded off, and the scree lies at an angle it found ages back and has kept since. Lichen has grown over the fallen blocks in orange and grey-green rings wider than a spread arm. Every stone that was going to fall has already fallen.

The floor is about half a day's walk across and nearly flat. It is paved with thin plates of dark, fine-grained rock that ring when struck. They clink under any weight like crockery, and each plate has its own note.

**Light**

The sky overhead is a blue close to indigo. The sun is small and hard, and it lights everything without warming it. Shadows have edges like cut paper. The pale ground throws the light back up, so the undersides of things are lit too, even the belly of a bird passing over. The air stays cool and never turns bitter. A hand in the sun feels the sun, a hand in shade feels the stone's cold, and the difference between them is the only weather the place has.

**Water**

There is little of it, and it keeps to its places. Each morning a cloud rises from the low country, touches the eastern rim, wets the rock, and withdraws. That moisture works down through the scree and comes out at the foot of the headwall in a line of seeps, each with a green stain below it. The seeps feed a lake in the lowest part of the floor. It is wide, shin-deep, and edged with a white mineral crust. The lake loses to the dry air exactly what the seeps give it, and the crust at its margin is a single line, with no older or newer lines beside it.

**Plants**

Green cushions as hard as wood sit pressed against the lee sides of boulders. Some are the size of a sleeping animal and older than anything else alive here. Tussocks of wiry grass stand many paces apart, each in its own small circle of bare ground. Around the seeps there is a low red moss. The rest is rock and light.

**Animals**

The basin is not quiet, and it is far from uninhabited. The headwall is one continuous colony of cliff birds, dark and narrow-winged, hundreds of thousands of them, with every ledge and pocket taken. They do not leave in any season, because no season arrives to send them off. Pale wading birds stand in the lake in their thousands, sieving the brine and honking. Among the scree, fat grey rodents with long tails sit upright on the blocks and whistle. Small herds of fine-boned, tawny grazers move between the tussocks, and their hooves ring the stone plates at every step. Large scavenging birds turn slowly overhead on air too thin to hold anything smaller.

All of them live on the edges, the wall, the shore, and the scree. The middle of the basin is an enormous clear volume of air with nothing in it.

**Sound**

The air does hold sound. There is a notch in the western rim, and a wind that has never been known to stop pours through it and across the headwall. Ages of that wind have carved the rock into flutes and sockets, so the whole wall drones like an organ with every key held down. The colony screams over the drone. The waders bray, the rodents whistle, and the plates clink.

The cirque is a bowl, and the bowl gathers all of this and hands it back doubled. The noise is felt in the teeth and the breastbone. Ripples stand on the lake that the wind did not make. A shout is lost before it leaves the mouth.

The animals have settled with it. The grazers signal with their tails, white undersides flashing. The rodents whistle anyway, but they watch each other's posture. The cliff birds seem to find their own ledge among the thousands by position alone.

**The quiet place**

A little north of the lake there is a patch of floor, perhaps six paces across, where the echoes arrive out of step and cancel each other. Inside it the roar drops to a hum, and a breath can be heard. The grazers know the spot. They drop their young there, and the stone is stained from it.

The only human marks in the basin are here as well. There is a ring of fire-blackened stones, and a scatter of pale chert flakes where someone sat and worked an edge. A few of the ringing plates have been propped on smaller stones in a row and chipped to different lengths. Struck in order, they would descend a scale. Whoever comes here comes rarely, speaks with their hands outside the circle, and takes little.

**Time**

Every day here is the same day. The cloud touches the rim and withdraws. The sun goes straight over. The wind keeps coming through the notch, the lake keeps to its line, and the wall keeps sounding.
