The plateau is what remains of a mountain range once the mountains are gone. Its surface stands higher than most summits elsewhere, high enough that the noon sky overhead is a darker blue than it should be and a full breath delivers about a third of what the body expects. But there is no peak on it anywhere. The range was worn down to its roots, and then the roots were planed and polished into a floor of banded gneiss, grey and rose and black, smooth as an old stair and level out to the horizon. The folds of the vanished mountains are still in the stone. They show as stripes swinging in arcs many kilometres wide, like grain in a sanded plank, and a single dark band can be followed on foot for a day without ending.

Nothing loose on the floor is soft. Feldspar, mica and everything else that could rot or split went to powder long ago and blew off the edge. The gravel is quartz, garnet, zircon and a little magnetite, small rounded survivors all much the same size. Under and between them is the dust, the same minerals ground finer than flour. The rock is finished, and only the dust still moves.

It moves constantly. The plateau is wide enough to make its own weather and too high for anything to soften it. By day the sun heats the dark floor, the thin air lifts off it in columns, and wind pours in from every rim to fill the space. By night the floor radiates into an open sky, and the air lying on it turns cold and heavy and drains outward again. There is no rest between these two tides. At dawn and dusk the flow reverses, and for a while the wind comes from all quarters at once.

Dust devils cross the floor by the dozen, sometimes by the hundred. They are tall leaning ropes that pinch off and re-form. Crescent dunes of pale powder the size of houses travel their own length in an afternoon and leave the stone behind them swept and gleaming. Fronts arrive as tan walls reaching from one horizon to the other, with dry lightning flickering inside them. The light changes a dozen times an hour, from hard clear sun to amber and back, and the shadow of each veil slides across the banded rock.

Almost none of this makes a sound. The air is too thin to carry it. A wind that would roar at sea level arrives as pressure on the chest and a faint hiss of grains on stone, like sand poured onto paper in another room. The lightning makes a tick. A storm can take a quarter of the sky while the loudest thing present is your own pulse.

The only water is frost. In the coldest hour before dawn a white rime a millimetre deep grows on the windward faces of the pebbles. By mid-morning it has gone straight back into the air without wetting anything. There are no channels, stains or dried pans. If water ever ran here, the evidence wore away with the mountains.

What lives here lives on that frost and on what the wind delivers.

- **Crust lichens:** black and rust-orange patches in the lee of the larger stones.
- **Loose lichen:** grey-green curls the size of a fingernail, attached to nothing. They roll with the gravel and take their frost wherever the night leaves them.
- **Cushion plants:** grey and tight as a fist. One turns up perhaps once in an hour's walking, always in a seam between two bands where a crack holds a spoonful of dust.
- **Spiders:** small pale jumping spiders under the stones. They hunt the midges and aphids the updrafts carry in, dead or dying, from lowlands kilometres below.
- **Birds:** once in many days a line of them crosses very high on the rim lift, without descending. If they call, it does not reach the ground.

Then there are the masts. Each is a matte grey rod as thick as a wrist. Some reach only to the knee and others stand four times a person's height, upright on the polished stone, with no footing, socket or bolts. A mast does not move or vibrate when a dust wall hits it. A hand laid on one feels nothing of the wind, and the surface is always slightly warmer than the air. The wind here cuts facets into quartz, but the masts show no wear on any side.

A dust devil that passes over a mast loses its column for a few paces. The dust drops straight down, and the column picks up again on the far side with the same lean. Dunes part around a mast and rejoin. Each base sits in a clean disc of bare floor an arm's length across, with an edge as sharp as a drawn line.

The masts stand far apart. From one, even in a clear interval, no other is visible, and walking between them takes days. They tend to stand on the dark bands. Each has a gap between its base and the rock. On the hardest bands the gap is the thickness of a sheet of paper, and on the softer ones a finger's width. The floor has worn away beneath them, grain by grain, in this thin air, and they have stayed at the height where they were put.

Rarer still are the still places. Each is a square of floor about twenty paces on a side, with no visible boundary, where the air does not move. Dust that drifts across the line falls. Each square therefore holds a bed of soft powder, perfectly level and ankle-deep. It never gets deeper however much comes in, and where the surplus goes is not apparent.

The loose lichen that rolls in stays there, so these squares are heaped with it in grey-green drifts. Cushion plants crowd the edges. These are the only places on the plateau where a mark lasts. The powder keeps the stitched tracks of spiders and the small craters left by pebbles that bounced across the line and stopped. Inside, the pressure on the chest is gone, and the grains can be heard landing.

At the rim the floor simply ends. It has no lip, and the scarp falls away for kilometres. At night the draining air carries the dust over in long pale falls that fray into haze well before they reach the ground. From the lowlands the plateau is a ruled line above the clouds, smoking faintly along its whole length.
