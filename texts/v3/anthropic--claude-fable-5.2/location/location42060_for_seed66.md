The plateau stands two thousand metres above the lowlands and runs farther than a week of walking in any direction. It is warm because the rock is warm. Heat comes up through old faults, and the water comes with it. The water does not rise in geysers or anything else dramatic. It is a general seep, a sweating of the ground. Every cliff face weeps. Every cutting, cellar wall and quarry step has a film of water moving down it, body temperature or a little above, and it leaves a skin of pale carbonate wherever it runs. The air holds the moisture, and by mid-morning the far districts stand in a thin white haze that the sun burns through without clearing.

Nothing green grows here. The water is too alkaline, and the ground is all stone and crust. What grows instead is colour: bacterial mats, rust-orange and sulphur-yellow in the hot channels, darkening to a liverish purple where the water cools and pools. The mats are the bottom of everything, and the flies eat them.

The flies come first because nothing else here can be understood without them. Along the margins of the soda pans, which lie across the plateau in shallow shining sheets many kilometres wide, they stand so thick that the shoreline looks tarred. Walk into them and they lift in a wave ahead of your feet and settle behind you with a sound like sand poured on paper. The pans themselves are full of brine shrimp, enough to tint whole basins the pink of diluted blood.

The birds come for the flies and the shrimp. There are filter-feeders standing in the shallows in ranks tens of thousands deep, and small grey waders running the fly-line with their beaks open. There are swifts, and gulls, and black cormorant-like divers that work the deeper spring vents. There are too many to count, and the city gave up counting generations ago and now measures them by weight of guano landed per quarter.

The city covers much of the plateau. It grew along the pan edges and the seep lines and then filled in between. There are soda works and potash works, evaporation terraces stepped like amphitheatres, and kilns. There are boiler-houses that take their heat straight from the ground and send it on through iron mains to drive the crushers, the conveyors and the tram cables. The stacks are short and wide and put out more steam than smoke. Everything is built from the local stone, a honey-grey travertine, and then goes on being built by the water. A wall that weeps for fifty years gains a thumb's thickness of new stone. Sills grow lips and gutters grow tongues. The oldest quarters have rounded, swollen corners, and their doorways have narrowed until the lintels need recutting. Carved street names have been filled in, cut again a little shallower each time, and filled in again.

The pipes go the same way. A main lasts about thirty years before the scale closes its bore. Then a new one is laid beside it and the old one is left where it lies, because it has become solid rock in an iron sleeve. Some streets are walled entirely in dead pipe, stacked eight and ten high, with the rust bleeding brown stripes down the white.

Every roofline, stack rim, gantry and cable is occupied. The building code requires ledges. This was once only a concession, and the trade later grew around it. Each structure carries courses of projecting stone shelf at set intervals, and each shelf carries nests packed as tightly as teeth. The guano goes down chutes into carts and the carts go to the nitrate sheds. So the city is harvesting the lake a second time, after the birds have concentrated it. The roofs are white and so are the streets under the cable runs. The tram drivers wear broad hats of oiled canvas, and nobody remarks on it.

Other animals live under the birds. Pale geckos work the warm walls after the flies and grow as fat as thumbs. Rats follow the eggs. A lean, long-eared fox follows the rats, and it dens in the dead pipes and trots along the ledges in daylight without hurry. At dusk the bats come out of the old kiln vaults. They come in a column that takes forty minutes to pass and can be seen from the far side of the pans as a leaning smudge. Around the same time the midges come off the cooler pools in clouds dense enough to dim the gas lamps.

A single species of blind fish, finger-long and almost transparent, lives in the warm culverts. Children catch them in jars and are made to put them back.

The city is loud, and the noise comes in layers. Lowest is the plant: the crushers, the cable drums, and the long exhale of pressure valves. Above that is the trickle and patter of water coming off every surface. Above everything are the birds, a continuous clatter and bray that rises to a roar when a shift whistle sends a hundred thousand of them up off a single works at once. They resettle within a minute, each on its own shelf. People here talk with their hands at the pan edges and save speech for indoors, where the thick, growing walls keep it in.

A few customs are observed without being written down. Nobody kills a fox. Any water crossing your threshold is let through, in a channel, because damming it only means it comes through the wall instead. New buildings are given narrow rooms so they have somewhere to grow from. The dead are laid in the hottest seeps on the eastern scarp. The crust takes them in a decade, and the family visits a smooth white shape that gets a little less particular every year.

Nothing changes quickly here. The pans creep. Over a lifetime a shoreline may move a few hundred metres, and the works follow it, leaving stepped terraces behind like tide marks. The birds move into these almost at once. From the scarp roads the whole of this is visible in clear high daylight. The abandoned terraces lie in concentric white rings. The live works steam at the present edge and the pink water lies beyond them. The air over all of it is granular with wings out to a horizon that curves slightly. Clouds form over the largest pans by early afternoon, drift a few miles, and rain the lake back onto the roofs. The water runs down the walls and leaves a little more stone on them.
