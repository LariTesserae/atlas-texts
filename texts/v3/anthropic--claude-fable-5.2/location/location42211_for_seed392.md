The ground is a floor. Across the whole basin, an hour's walk from rim to rim if walking measured anything here, the surface is tamped clay skimmed with shell-lime. It is laid in panels the width of a reach, and at every seam you can see where a palm smoothed one panel into the next. The lime is white enough to hurt. Where it was left off, the clay keeps the cross-hatch of the mat it was beaten through, each strand's print crisp and damp at the bottom of its groove.

Under the floor there is only more work. Near the middle causeway one panel has been lifted and leaned against its neighbour. Beneath it is reed matting, the cut ends still green. Beneath that is a course of hand-patted bricks with straw in them, four finger-dents on each, and beneath the bricks is more matting. A digging stick stands in the hole. The hole is knee-deep and looks as if it will be deeper by evening, and there is no evening.

The sun stands straight overhead and stays there. Every shadow is a small dark puddle directly under its object. Stepping into one changes nothing, because the heat is the same in shade as in the open, the same in the water as in the air, and close to the heat of the inside of a mouth. Nothing here is cooler than anything else, and still water beads on the flint of an adze, on leaves, on the back of a hand. Press a heel into the bare clay and a ring of wet rises around it, then goes back.

The planting mounds stand far apart, with three or four hundred paces of blank white floor between one and the next. Each is a stepped platform of brick, and each is overflowing. Taro leaves sit like shields. Plantains split under their own weight, a fig sends roots down the brick face, and gourd vines run along pegged cords. Papyrus stands in a ring-trough around the base. The green is nearly black against the lime. One gourd tendril is stretched toward the next peg, a finger's width short of it, and it will stay that far.

Channels run between the mounds, clay-lined and full to the brim. They are level and there is no current, because there is no downhill. A fig leaf lies on one, and it lies there. Square pans, shin-deep, hold catfish. Along the causeways stand rows of big unglazed jars, sweating, and each has a dark skirt of damp on the floor beneath it. The edge of the damp is sharp, and it neither spreads nor dries.

There are animals everywhere, and they are the only things that move:

- **Birds:** Ibis stand in the pans and egrets on the mound steps. Weaver nests hang from the palms and the drying racks, tied with the same knot as the fish traps.
- **Reptiles and frogs:** Turtles line the brick ledges like set loaves. A monitor lizard walks the causeway. There are frogs on every trough lip.
- **Insects:** Dragonflies hang over the channels. Bees crawl on clay-cylinder hives stacked like firewood.

Their moving accomplishes nothing. An ibis strikes, lifts a frog, and swallows. The frog is on the trough lip, and the ibis's throat is still working. The count holds.

It is very quiet. The frogs' throats pulse and no call comes out, and the dragonflies make no sound over the water. What can be heard is the slap of an ibis's foot on wet clay and, at long intervals, one drop falling from the foot of a jar into a channel that does not rise.

Tools lie where they were set down. A mallet rests on the rampart ramp, its handle dark with sweat. A flint adze sits beside a half-shaped post, the shavings still white and curled. A carrying pole lies across the causeway with two baskets of wet clay.

Every brick, every seam, and the slip on the basket rims carries the same hand, with the same small finger slightly crooked. The whole basin is one person's work, all of it just finished.

The rim is a rampart of rammed earth, twice a person's height. Its lifts are visible as bands, and the individual basket-loads show as lenses of slightly different red. From the top, the basin lies on both sides. The same mounds are there, the same leaning panel, the same stick in the same hole. The mallet is at your feet whichever way you face.

Overhead, near the edge of the glare, the blue shows faint parallel streaks, all running one way, where the bristles went.
