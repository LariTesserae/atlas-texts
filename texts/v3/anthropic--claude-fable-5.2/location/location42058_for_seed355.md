The basin sits a little lower than the country around it, and everything that can settle has settled there: the heat, the water, and the dark.

The dark here has a surface. At its low stand it lies about knee-deep in the streets, flat as oil. Above it hangs a brown, sourceless dimness, just enough to tell the upper half of a wall from the air behind it. At high stand it covers the lintels and then the wall-heads, and there is nothing to tell. It rises and drains on its own schedule, toward no outlet anyone could point to.

It does not mix with the water. A channel that runs under the dark keeps running, and comes out the far side a few degrees hotter than it went in. Nothing sounds below the dark's surface. A drop falling into a covered pool makes no noise, though the same drop at low stand makes a small clean tick. So the town is quietest when it is fullest. At its loudest it offers only that ticking, widely spaced, and a faint seethe from the hot channels like breath through teeth.

The town itself would take perhaps an hour to cross, if distance held. It is made of sinter and travertine, laid down by water that comes up scalding through the basin floor. No tool has touched it. The walls rise in courses, each one the rind left by a single flooding, and the corners are square. The other features formed the same way:

- **Doorways:** their lintels formed where two curtains of dripstone met and fused.
- **Stairs:** these are rimstone terraces, each tread a shallow pan of bath-warm water spilling into the next.
- **Streets:** these are the lanes the overflow kept open for itself.
- **Waterworks:** there are cisterns, culverts and kerbs. A long conduit crosses the east quarter on arches, carrying a finger's depth of near-boiling water to a blank wall, where it stops.

The buildings stand far apart. There is more floor than wall, with wet pavement running off into the dim and squares wider than the blocks that edge them. A single doorway may face a hundred body-lengths of shining emptiness.

All of it is worn. The sills are dished. The stair treads sag in the middle, where feet would have gone. The corners are rounded to about the height of a trailing hand. The water does this as it builds, dissolving exactly as much as it deposits. The town neither gains nor loses, and it looks like a place used for a thousand years and left last week. It has never been used.

The air is at blood heat and holds all the water it can. Every surface sweats. A film a hair deep slides over the pavements, always going somewhere. The rain is only the air letting go. Large warm drops form at about roof height under no cloud and come down slowly enough to follow. The smell is of wet plaster, struck matches and hot coins.

Very little grows:

- **The felt:** a band of it, black-green in all likelihood, runs along every wall at the height where the dark's surface rests longest. It marks a tide-line for something that is not a tide.
- **The rushes:** they stand in the cooler cisterns in straight rows along the seams of the floor. They are colourless, jointed and leafless, and they never stir.
- **The shrubs:** one grows on a wall-head every block or so, low and leathery. Each thick leaf is cupped around a spoonful of condensation.

Nothing eats any of it. There are no gnats over the pools, no trails across the sinter, and no holes in the mud at the basin's margin. A dropped leaf lies whole until the mineral closes over it. The lower courses of every wall are full of leaves turned to stone, layered as closely as pages.

After each high dark the town is slightly different. There is one more street on the north side, or one fewer. The conduit ends against another wall. The new street does not look new. Its sills are dished, its felt is full-grown, and there are stone leaves in its footings. By the time it can be seen, it has always been there. The street that went has left no rubble and no gap, and the blocks on either side of it are simply neighbours now.

Every street ends at the central cistern. It is round, stepped, and the hottest thing in the basin. Following any street away from it arrives at it too. Distance is reckoned from it, by heat, so things are far in proportion as they are cool. The shrub on the wall-head just overhead, with its cold spoonful of water, is farther off than the opposite edge of town.

Other things have come loose as well.

At low stand a tick sounds. Then a ring spreads in the pavement film, and only then does the drop come down into the centre of its ring.

Steam goes down the stairs, pan by pan, and collects at the bottom.

In the west quarter the rain rises. Beads lift out of the film and climb to roof height and stay there. They form a ceiling of hung water over the empty squares until the next high dark takes it.

A room counted from inside has four walls. The same block counted from outside has only enough wall for three to a room.

The two sides of a doorway are never the same temperature and never even out.

A few rooms are dry, the only dryness in the basin. They are floored with a crust of salt. The pavement film stops at their thresholds in a straight line, standing a finger high like a cut edge.

In the brown dimness the puddles show the wall-heads the right way up.

Then the dark comes back. It takes the lowest stair-pans first, and their ticking stops. It takes the band of felt, the rows of rushes and the lintels. It fills the conduit's arches from the bottom. For a while the conduit's hot water crosses the dimness on nothing, a thin line running level through the air. Then the dark takes that too, and the wall-heads, and the shrubs, leaf by cupped leaf.

Underneath, in the silence, the water goes on laying down its courses. When the dark drains, the conduit will meet a different wall, and the stone there will already be worn smooth where the water touches it.
