The valley is a trough pressed into plate. From the gantry at its head, the far end is about four kilometres off, and nothing between here and there is ground. The floor is rolled steel, still blue with mill scale. The ridges on either side are sheds stacked on sheds, saw-toothed and glazed. The screes are spoil chutes of white ceramic, and the peaks are chimneys and gasholders, with one cooling tower so newly enamelled that it hurts to look at before noon. Everything is new. Rivet heads are still bright, cut edges still cut, and the stencilled part numbers have not begun to flake.

The air is thin and cool, and steel takes the cold deeper than stone would. Every dawn the whole works sweats. Condensation beads on the undersides of pipes and runs down the flanges into gutters, which someone has labelled CONDENSATE in letters a metre high. The gutters feed square cisterns, and the cisterns feed three cooling ponds in the valley floor. That is all the water there is, and it is enough. Orange lichen has started on the north faces of the gasholders. Cushion moss fills the seams between floor plates in thin green lines, like caulking. Along the gutters there is a hand's width of grass, cropped short.

The grass is cropped because the place is packed with animals. Ibex stand on the bottom flanges of the crane girders and sleep there. Choughs sit shoulder to shoulder on every purlin of every shed. Marmots have taken the cable ducts and hares the marshalling yard, and snowfinches nest in the open ends of scaffold tube, one nest per tube, hundreds of tubes to a rack. There are salamanders in the cisterns. At night, which is no darker than day, moths come to the furnace walls in such numbers that the brickwork grows a pale nap, and stoats work along the base of it.

The furnaces are where the light comes from. There are eleven, charged and burning white, and they give almost no heat. A chough can nest in a tuyère and does. The light goes out through the glazing and strikes enamel, wet plate and polished rail, and since nothing here has had time to dull, all of it comes back. The sun also crosses, a small pale disc, and makes no difference.

It is quiet, and the quiet has a rule. Sounds of contact have been installed, and nothing else has. A hoof on plate clicks. A drip into a cistern can be heard from forty metres. Wing brushes wing along the purlins all day, a dry continuous whisper, which is the loudest thing in the valley. But the steam hammer in Shed 6 comes down on its billet with no report at all. The blowing engines turn their flywheels in silence. The choughs open their beaks to call, their throats work, and nothing comes. Voices and engines belong to a later phase. Roughly once a season a single tone is let out across the valley for a second or so, flat and enormous, as if a line were being tested. Every animal stops with its head up, and then the whisper of wings resumes.

The machinery moves slowly. The main conveyor advances about a hand's width a day. The ladle crane has been swinging the same pour toward the same mould since the moss arrived. The animals are the quick things here, and the works goes about its business under them at the pace of a glacier.

Its business is the mountain. Under the floor is another floor: last year's valley, complete. Under that is the one before. Once a year, with no noise, the works finishes rolling out a new level beneath itself and stands one storey higher. The altitude is its output. Through any grating one can look down into the earlier valleys, each still lit by its furnaces and each a little less crowded. On the next level down there are fewer choughs and the moss lines are thinner. Four or five levels down the plate is bare and bright and nothing moves on it. Ladders descend through hatches. An ibex that takes one comes out, unbothered, onto the roof of the cooling tower.

The drawings are in the glazed office at the head of the valley, on tall sheets with wet ink, and everything is drawn. The moss is there, hatched in, with a note: *planting, by others*. The marmot burrows have dimension lines. The trouble is that the sheets are revised faster than the steel can follow, and the animals obey whichever issue they were drawn on. A catwalk between Sheds 3 and 4 was deleted two revisions ago and taken down. A hare still runs it every morning, at full stretch, eleven metres above the yard, and its feet click on plate that is not there. The choughs of the east range fly straight through a gable wall, because they were drawn before it was. The finches on the same wall, which were drawn later, perch on it. Where revisions disagree, a seam runs through the air. Along it a pipe can be seen both lagged and unlagged, and a puddle lies flat on a floor that also slopes.

The puddles are the other thing to watch. Each one left by the morning sweat reflects the same roof trusses and the same white glare, but with a few more ibex on the girders than are standing there now, and the lichen further along. A salamander crossing one walks over a valley that has not been built yet. By midmorning the puddles have drained through the seams to the gutters and the plate is dry. The loaded conveyor has come another hand's width out of a shed it has not yet been connected to.
