Under the hill, a little more than an arm's length below the grass roots, there is a hollow about the size of a pantry. It has no entrance. It is full of daylight.

The light is the oldest thing here. A piece of noon got buried, and the ground has been closing around it ever since, the way an oyster closes around grit, one coat at a time. The hollow is the inside of that work, and the work isn't finished. The walls are still soft. They have the give of cooled wax and the faint skin of milk left standing. Their colour hasn't settled: grey when looked at straight on, pinker at the edge of the eye. A thumb pressed in leaves a print that stays a while, then slowly fills from behind.

Stone forms in the wrong order here. Crystals appear first as outlines, bright edges with nothing between them, and fill with mineral later, if at all. A stalactite has begun from its tip. A bead of stone hangs in open air a foot below the ceiling, attached to nothing, lengthening upward toward the place it will eventually hang from. The bands in the wall lie with later ages beneath earlier ones.

It is cellar-cool, the temperature of the inside of a cheek on an indrawn breath. The walls sweat. Every so often there is weather: a rain the width of a tablecloth gathers a hand's breadth under the ceiling, falls for a while onto one patch of floor, and stops. The floor has not agreed on which way is down, so the water collects halfway up a slope and stays there as a tilted pool.

Little grows. There is a green film where the light is strongest and a few threads of moss. One pale fiddlehead has been uncurling for as long as the room has existed and is no further along. Hair-fine white rootlets come through the ceiling from a tree, though the hill above has only grass.

The animals are another matter. Springtails lie on the tilted pool like sifted flour. There are woodlice the colour of candle ends, and blind crickets with antennae three times their length. The moths match the walls so closely that they seem to be the walls flaking. Thumb-long salamanders are clear enough to show the dark bead of their last meal. In the upper air, things like swifts, no bigger than a thumbnail, hawk the moths in tight circuits.

All of it moves, and almost none of it makes a sound. The soft stone takes noise in. A cricket works its legs and nothing comes of it. Drops land silently, or the tap arrives a moment before the drop does. The one dependable sound is the stone setting, a tick like a glazed pot cooling. When a few hundred woodlice cross the floor together, there is something like a page being turned.

Some of the animals are unfinished. One salamander is a front half only, and it walks and feeds without difficulty. Certain beetles are just the shine of beetles, a moving gloss with no body under it. A count of the swifts gives a different number each time, and all the numbers seem right. The moths' pupa cases are found already empty and unopened, and the swifts are fat. The room keeps cause and effect no better sorted than its strata.

For all this life, the hollow is mostly bright vacant air. The animals hold to a few stations: the pool, the green film, the ceiling roots. Between them is bare soft floor, evenly lit and shadowless. Nothing casts a shadow because the light comes from nowhere. It is simply in the room, the way the damp is.

A few things here were made by hand. Seven pebbles are stacked in a ring. They are hard, finished and river-rounded, though no river has touched this place. A bowl has been pinched from the wall-stuff, with thumb marks still in the rim, and it holds the only level water in the room. Four grooves run along one wall at shoulder height, dragged by fingers. At the foot of the wall, where there is no opening, lies a flat threshold stone, dished in the middle by feet.

No one has been here. The wear has come first, as the stalactite's tip came first. The crickets step around the pebble ring. The salamanders drink from the bowl in preference to the pool.

The room is six paces across in one direction and eleven coming back. The far wall is sometimes a wall and sometimes only the place where the room leaves off. The brightness carries on a little past where stone ought to be, with moths in it.

Because the noon never ends, the animals make their own night. A few times in what would be a day, the woodlice, crickets and salamanders heap together in the low corner by the threshold stone and pack in close. At the centre of the heap a small darkness forms, the only one in the room, about the size of a fist. They take turns in it. The swifts roost on the outside of the heap with their heads tucked. Then the heap loosens, the feeding resumes, and somewhere in the wall the stone ticks as another coat goes on.
