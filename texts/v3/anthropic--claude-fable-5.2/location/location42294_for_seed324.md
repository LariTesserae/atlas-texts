The noise arrives before the room does. In the access gallery it is a pressure in the walls, and at the steel door it is in the handle. Inside it has no direction. It comes up through the boot soles and sits behind the breastbone, and the hearing-protection sign bolted to the door frame is the last thing anyone reads comfortably.

The chamber is cast into the body of the dam. It is fourteen metres long, nine wide and eight high, and a stencil on the wall gives the floor level: EL. 2314.50. Beyond the concrete on one side is the reservoir, and on the other side cold air and a long fall to the valley. Nothing in here was ever rock. The walls still carry the grain of the formwork boards, plank by plank. The tie-bolt holes are plugged with mortar a shade paler than the pour, and the corners are sharp enough to catch a sleeve. Chalked pour dates are legible near the ceiling. Deep in the mass the cement is still curing, so the walls are a degree or two warmer than the air, which stays near six.

The penstock crosses the room from wall to wall. It is a steel pipe nearly three metres in diameter, painted a grey-green that still has its gloss, with a white arrow stencilled along its flank. At mid-span sits the butterfly valve, with its counterweight arms held up by a hydraulic ram, ready to drop and close the disc if the pressure fails. Each bolt on the flange has a stripe of yellow marker across nut and thread where a fitter checked the torque.

About fifty tonnes of water go through the pipe every second, and none of it touches air. What fills the room is the steel shell being driven by the turbulence inside it. At first the roar seems to have no pitch. After a minute it separates into parts:

- **The disc:** it lies edge-on in the flow and sheds a low, steady tone.
- **The hydraulic pump:** a whine from the unit in the corner.
- **The fan:** a flat blare from the extract fan.
- **The air valve:** every so often it snaps open downstream with a crack like a plank breaking, then screams for a second or two.

The handrail buzzes under a glove. The puddles show the sound as a fine, stationary cross-hatch of ripples that never settles.

Water is everywhere in small amounts. The pipe carries water from the bottom of the reservoir at four degrees, so the steel sweats along its whole length and the drops gather on its underside in a line. They tremble and fall early, shaken loose before they are full. The construction joints weep. White threads of lime have started down the walls from them, and under the crane beam hang the first stalactites, hollow straws no longer than a finger joint. Someone has circled each weep in blue crayon and written a date beside it. All the dates are from this year, and as the reservoir rises through its first filling the circles get company. Gutters along both walls carry the water to a sump under a grating in the corner.

Six bulkhead lamps in wire cages light the room, enough for reading gauges and little else. The air is close to saturation, so each lamp has a soft halo, and the far wall is a shade or two greyer than the near one. On the control panel there is one green lamp and one amber beacon, which is dark for now. The telephone sits under an acoustic hood with a strobe on top, because a bell would be useless. A laminated board shows hand signals for open, close, stop and get out.

The lamps never go off, so each one has grown a fan of green below it on the wet concrete. It is a film of algae, thin as a stain, brightest near the glass and fading out within an arm's length. At one weep under the second lamp there is a cushion of moss the size of a coin. The spores came down the ventilation shaft or in on someone's boots. Nothing else lives here. The lamp cages have no webs and there are no flies to put in them.

The room keeps the plant's hours. In the early evening, when the valley's demand peaks, the flow comes up to full and the roar thickens. The disc tone climbs a little, and the pipe creeps downstream on its sliding saddles, leaving a few millimetres of bright scraped metal on the bearing plates. Late at night the flow drops back and the pipe returns. The noise thins to a level that would still stop conversation. Each load change sends a pressure wave up the line, and the air valve cracks open to meet it.

Weather comes in through the fan. On some days it draws down cloud from the crest, and the haloes around the lamps swell. On others the air smells of snowmelt and wet aggregate. When a front passes over the dam, ears pop.

Near the upstream wall, a pace from the hydraulic unit, someone has sprayed a pair of yellow footprints on the floor. Standing on them, the low tone drops away, since two reflections cancel there. It is the one spot in the room where a person can think for the length of time it takes to write a reading in the logbook.

Every eleven minutes or so the float in the sump lifts and the duty pump cuts in. It cannot be heard. The only signs are a second green lamp on the panel and the water in the gutters, which for a moment begins to move.
