**The Flow and Its Edge**

The flow is forty-one years old, and it still looks like something that happened last week. It lies across the coastal flat as a tongue of black clinker, barely higher than the tide line. Every edge on it is still glassy, with no rounding and no soil. Boots last a season here. Rain sinks through the rock at once, so there is no stream, pond, or spring anywhere on it. For most of its length it is dry, sharp, and nearly bare, with a grey crust of lichen just beginning.

In the middle of it is the green, a rough oval perhaps four hundred paces on its long axis, with a border you can step across. On one side of the border the clinker is bare. On the other the moss is ankle-deep, and past it are ferns to the waist, then dwarf birch and willow with wet leaves under a clear sky. The oval is the shape of the plume's drip-shadow, stretched northeast by the prevailing wind.

**The Works**

The flow is still warm inside, and the Works exists to take that heat before it is gone. Eleven wellheads are bored down into the hot core. Lagged steam pipes run from them on trestles to a turbine hall of riveted plate and brick. Beside the hall a row of condensers breathes white vapour into bright, cool air.

All the water in this place comes from that vapour. It drifts, cools, and falls as a fine drip that never becomes rain. The cold return pipes sweat along their whole length, and a line of fern grows under each one, as straight as a ruled margin. The only standing water is in the concrete sumps beneath the condensers.

The town was surveyed for three hundred people and about sixty live here. The streets were laid out as a grid of crushed cinder, six one way and four the other. Most of the lots hold only a rusted stake and a thicket. The buildings that do exist stand far apart:

- a bunkhouse
- a canteen with a tin chimney
- a machine shop
- a lamp store
- the manager's house, with its one glazed porch
- a rail spur ending at a buffer already half buried in willow

Between them the cinder streets run empty and bright through green that is higher than the rooflines.

**The Noise**

The turbine hall puts out a drone that you feel in your back teeth before you hear it. Over that comes the hiss of the condensers, the tick and bang of pipes expanding, and the bleed-valves chattering. Every eleven minutes or so the relief stack on Well Six lets go with a shriek that lasts nine seconds.

The birds add to it. Terns and kittiwakes nest on the hall roof and along the warm trestles by the thousand, since this is the only warm, cat-free ledge for thirty miles of coast. They scream without pause from April to August. Their droppings, washed down by the drip, are the only reason there is anything for a fern to root in. The soil here is thin, black, and stinking, a finger deep, and it has been made out of birds within a human lifetime.

The starlings are the odd part. They roost in the birches in the evenings, and they have learned the relief valve. They perform it badly and often, a little flat and a little short, from every quarter of the green. A newcomer will flinch and count seconds forty times a day. Residents can tell the real one by the pressure in the ears.

**What Lives by Warmth**

The animals have sorted themselves by ground temperature more exactly than any instrument could.

- **Frogs** breed in the sumps, where the water stays lukewarm, and call all night under the condenser hiss. Nobody is sure how frogs crossed a mile of dry clinker to get here.
- **Voles** tunnel in the moss over the buried steam mains, and in winter their runs show as thawed lines in the frost.
- **Wagtails** work the warm lids of the wellheads for flies.
- **A short-eared owl** sits most afternoons on the lamp-store gable in full daylight. It is fat and unbothered by the noise.
- **Seals** haul out on the black shelf where the flow meets the sea. They do not come inland, but they can be heard when the wind turns, underneath everything else.

**How People Manage**

Nobody talks outdoors. Everyone uses hand-signs, a few dozen of them, borrowed from the rail yards and added to. Children here learn them before they can read. Anything that matters is chalked on the slate by the canteen door. The dinner signal is a flag, because a bell would be lost in the noise.

The real talking gets done in the half-minute after Six blows. The birds go up in a white sheet and take a moment to settle, and in that gap you can hear a person at arm's length. People save things up for it.

**What Changes**

The changes are slow. The flow cools by a degree or so each year. Well Two went tepid and was capped, and the ferns over it have already thinned back to moss. Sooner or later the fitters will bore a twelfth well further up the tongue and the plume will shift with it. The green will follow by a few paces a decade, leaving a brown margin behind and gaining a wet one ahead.

On the windward border there is a row of survey stakes, one for each year, marking where the moss stopped. The oldest are deep in the birch. The newest stands in bare clinker, an arm's length beyond the last green cushion.
