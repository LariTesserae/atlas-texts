The city sits on a shelf of new basalt, high up, where a flow once ran down between two ridges and stopped. The rock still looks like something that happened recently. Its surface is ropy in places and clinkered in others, broken into columns along the shelf's lower lip, and every fracture is clean. Edges will cut a palm. There is a glassy rind on the undersides of overhangs that no frost has flaked off. The ridges on either side are the same age, steep, unsoftened, their scree lying exactly at the angle it fell to.

No river runs here. Water comes as cloud, which leans against the upper ridge and wets it to a certain line and no lower. Below the line the rock is dry and pale grey; above it, black and shining. Drip collects in cracks and is led down through cut channels a hand wide into cisterns, and it stands in the natural hollows of the flow as small tarns, clear to the bottom, none of them deeper than a person's waist. You can stand with one foot on moss that pushes water up around your sandal and the other on stone that has never been wet.

Where there is water, the green is thick. Moss lies in mats deep enough to lose a hand in. Ferns fill the column joints. Cushion plants, hard as bread loaves, dot the open clinker, and tussock grass stands in the lee of every wall. There are groves of a low twisted tree with red bark that peels in papery sheets, a dozen or twenty trunks to a grove, each grove sitting in its own pocket of carried soil. The terraces hold a broad-leaved tuber crop and a tall seed plant with purple heads. All the soil on the shelf arrived in baskets. Between the green places the rock is bare, and nothing is trying to cross it.

The city is perhaps four hundred buildings, and it covers the whole shelf, because no building stands near another. Each house is a single room of fitted stone, the blocks pecked to shape with hammerstones and laid without mortar, the joints tight enough to refuse a blade of grass. The roofs are tussock thatch tied down with cord. Between houses run raised causeways, paved and kerbed, straight, wider than seems necessary, crossing the clinker like lines drawn with a rule. There are stairs cut into the shelf's lip, a plaza with a floor of set cobbles, round granaries on stone feet, and cistern after cistern, each with a lid of woven grass. Fog-catchers of knotted fiber are strung on poles along the wet line. There is a little copper here, a few chisels and a handful of pins, kept wrapped in hide. The rest of the work is done with stone, bone, obsidian, cord, and fire.

People live here, though you see them one at a time. A woman carries a basket along a causeway. Someone sits in a doorway twisting fiber against a thigh. Two figures stand on a far terrace, small, bent to the same row. The city seems built for exactly this number, at exactly this spacing, and the distance between any two hearths is about as far as a voice carries without being raised.

The animals fill the rest of it. Long-necked wool-bearers graze untethered among the houses and lie folded on the causeways, chewing. Grey rock-hares with curled tails sit on nearly every wall top, eyes half shut, facing the light. Geese stand on the tarns' edges. Small brown finches work the seed heads, and there are frogs in the cisterns and black beetles in the moss. A pair of very large dark birds turns on the updraft at the shelf's lip, always the same pair, always about there. Nothing bolts when a person walks through, and nothing seems to hunt anything else.

It is quiet. The channels make a thin sound, there are hooves on paving, and sometimes a hammerstone taps for a short while and stops. The wind is low and steady from one direction, and the tussock leans a little that way and stays leaning.

The light is plain high-country daylight, even and clear, the kind that shows the grain of everything. It is cool enough that breath shows faintly in the shade and not in the sun.

After a while you notice what the place does not do. The quarry face at the north end is as bright as if it were struck this morning, but there is no fresh rubble under it. On the east side there is a wall of eleven courses, with the twelfth course's first block set and the next block lying ready beside it, lichen-free, with the hammerstone on top. The paving shows no wear from the hooves. The thatch is neither new nor rotting. The cisterns are full to a finger below the rim, all of them, and the channels feeding them run at a trickle that must exactly equal what is dipped out.

In the plaza, under a roofed frame, hang the counting cords: long bundles, knotted for the herds, the granaries, the houses, the people. A keeper adds a cord each time a count is made. Every cord has the same knots in the same places. There are a great many cords.

The moss stops at the same crack it has always stopped at. The cloud rests on its line. One of the rock-hares shifts on its wall, settles again, and closes its eyes.
