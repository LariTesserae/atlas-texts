The water is never still. It's a lake, though the word implies a calm this place has never had: a basin the width of a valley, set high where the air is thin, filled past its brim and beaten from above by a wind that does not stop. Down here the wind arrives as shoves. The whole water column leans one way, then the other. Cold plumes drop from the surface in sheets and roll out along the floor, so that a spot two degrees above freezing is, a minute later, half a degree above it. Silt lifts, hangs, and is snatched sideways. The light is a grey-green flicker with no direction to it, strained through chop and slush and whatever hail is being driven into the surface.

The floor is paved, all of it. Cobbles the size of fists and heads were set edge to edge by hand, unshaped, each one chosen to sit against its neighbors. They have been here so long that every face is worn to the same soft curve, and the joints between them are thin dark lines packed with grit. You could swim an hour in any direction and never cross a patch of mud or a shelf of living rock, because no bedrock comes through anywhere. Where a cobble has been torn loose by the current, the hole shows another course of laid cobbles beneath it, and another beneath that. Whatever hill once stood here was either buried or carried away stone by stone and put back in order.

Walls rise from the paving at long intervals. They are low now, knee-high or shin-high, slumped into rounded ridges that run straight for hundreds of meters and then turn at angles nobody alive could explain. Between them lies open floor. Here and there a ring stands, or a low mound, or a line of larger stones set upright and worn down to stumps like old teeth. These are sparse, and wide stretches of dim water with nothing in them separate one from the next. At the edges the basin climbs in terraces, hundreds of shallow steps, each a retaining wall of stacked stone gone almost flat. The rim is a wall as well, the largest, and through its gaps the inflow comes in as heavy white ropes of meltwater that fall and spread and keep the floor in motion.

Nothing grows. There is no weed and no moss. On the lee side of each wall is a grey slickness thinner than breath on glass, and every few days a current comes from the wrong direction and scours it off.

The animals are many, and all of them are moving. Pale fish no longer than a finger travel in bands of thousands. They pour through the dimness like poured grain, split around a wall-stump, and rejoin. Amphipods rise from the joints in the paving whenever a cold plume passes, a brown smoke of them, feeding on what the plume drags down: drowned insects, dust, the small dead things the storm delivers. Larvae creep along the walls in cases they have built from grit, each case a tiny dry-stone tube that copies the walls with nothing to copy them from. Flat snails clamp down when the surge comes and let go when it passes. Now and then a diving bird comes through the ceiling in a sleeve of silver bubbles, takes one fish from a band, and is hauled back up into the weather.

There are also the carriers. They are thick-bodied fish as long as a forearm, slow, blunt-lipped, and the color of the stones. Each works alone, far from any other. A carrier noses along the paving until it finds a displaced cobble, takes it in its mouth, swims it against the current, and sets it down in a gap. Sometimes the gap is in the floor, sometimes in a wall, and sometimes it is on one of the low mounds, which the carriers heap for their eggs and which the next surge spreads flat. The fish does not appear to choose which. It places the stone, turns it once with its snout, and leaves, and the water takes another stone somewhere else. It is hard to watch this for long without wondering whether there were ever hands here at all, or whether the whole basin, with its terraces and rim and ten thousand courses of fitted stone, is simply what these fish have done, given long enough.

The basin is quieter than its violence would suggest. The water absorbs its own turbulence. What remains is the tick of pebbles shifting in their sockets, a dull knock when a cobble rolls and stops, and the faint dry rasp of a carrier's mouth on stone. At long intervals, when a big wave breaks across the surface, there is a single low thud, felt in the chest before it's heard.

The rules are few. Anything lighter than a fist moves. Anything heavier stays until it wears down to a fist, and then it moves too. A stone that moves gets put somewhere, and the place it is put will not hold. The basin loses a wall's worth of order every season and gains about a wall's worth back, and it has gone on this way so long that the shapes have settled, low and blunt and stable under the storm.

A carrier sets a cobble into the floor. Silt crosses it, and a band of pale fish passes over. The cobble rocks once in its socket and holds, for now.
