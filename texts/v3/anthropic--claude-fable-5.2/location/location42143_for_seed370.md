The pass is a notch between two shoulders of a ridge, high enough that the basin below shows only as a brown floor without detail. The notch has been filled from wall to wall with made things. It is a hundred and some paces across, perhaps twice that deep, and as tall as five people standing on one another. From the windward side it looks like a dam built of mouths.

The wind comes up out of the basin warm, about the temperature of skin, at one strength and from one direction. It doesn't gust or drop. It enters the mouths, and what comes out is a sound so large it has stopped being heard as sound and become a condition of the place, like the altitude. It sits in the chest and the hinges of the jaw. A dropped stone lands in silence, because there is no room left for the noise it makes.

The front is dry-laid stone, with blocks fitted so closely the joints are hairlines, and every block is bored through. The holes range from finger-width to arm-width, and inside them the spiral scoring of a hand drill fed with sand can still be felt, though the rims have long since been rounded into soft lips. Behind the stone, everything is clay. Jars lie on their sides in banks, mudded into honeycomb, with their necks to the wind. Fired tubes in arm-length sections, socketed end to end, are bundled thirty and forty together and collared with straps of hammered copper gone brown-black in the dry air. Where a tube narrows, a slot holds a copper leaf pinned by a stone wedge, and the leaf buzzes. The makers' thumbprints are in the collars, and their palm-strokes show on the bellies of the jars.

No surface lacks an opening, and nothing has been left as it was found. The rock shoulders on either side have been dressed flat and drilled with shafts deeper than a pole can sound, and these give the lowest notes, the ones felt through the soles. The floor is sherds set on edge and packed tight as paving, laid many layers deep. The lanes are shoulder-wide and roofed with slabs, and they are ducts as much as paths. Walking one means standing inside a pipe while it speaks.

The light is poor. Outside, a low sun hangs in ochre haze and does not seem to climb. Inside, light arrives only through the sound-holes, so the lanes are dotted with dull amber coins on the walls and floor. The coins do not travel. Where each one falls the clay has bleached to the color of bone, a pale disc with a sharp edge, while a hand's breadth away the original red holds.

The sound has parts, if you stay long enough to separate them. Under everything is the drone of the bored shafts. Above that the jars give a wide, hollow middle, hundreds of them a hair apart in pitch, so that the whole mass throbs with a slow beat as regular as the wind that makes it. Higher up are the tubes and the rasp of the copper leaves. At the top, from holes no wider than a reed, comes a shriek thin enough to register as pressure behind the eyes. None of it resolves or moves toward anything. It is one chord, held.

Nothing lives here. There is no moisture to live on. A white bloom of salts has come out of the clay along the lower courses, and that is the only growth. The air smells of warm pottery.

Everything that was going to break has broken. Here and there a tube ends in a stump or a jar lies open like a halved gourd, but the edges of the breaks are as smooth as the rims, and each stump has its own note folded into the rest. The fragments were sorted long ago into the paving. The basin below has been scoured to hardpan, so the wind carries no grit, and nothing is being worn any further. The place and the wind have finished with each other and continue anyway.

At the center is the one room wide enough to stand in with both arms out. Every lane and duct opens onto it, and it is the loudest point. The floor there is fine clay dust, the only loose material in the whole construction. It lies in ridges, curved and nested and exact, a figure drawn by the standing air. The ridges are sharp-crested and they do not shift.

On a ledge along one wall sits a row of tools: stone mallets with waisted grips, a bundle of spare wedges graded by size, and a copper leaf never fitted. They are warm, like everything else. If you put a palm over any hole within reach, the chord changes, one thread pulled from it, and the wind pushes steadily against the hand. When the hand is taken away, the note returns exactly where it was.
