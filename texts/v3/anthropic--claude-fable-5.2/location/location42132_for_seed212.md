The shelf came up out of the sea not long ago, a few seasons at most to judge by its edges. It is pale marl, still crusted with salt and cracked into plates whose rims nothing has yet rounded. Two low scarps, fresh as snapped chalk, hold a shallow basin between them, some six kilometres across and barely above the tide. Old shells sit in the ground like teeth. The shelf is white, flat, hot and silent all the way to the water, and it does not carry a single blade.

In the basin there is a town, and the town is entirely plant.

Its edge is abrupt. On one side of a line there is bare marl; on the other, a wall of green culms as thick as barrels, lashed with tendril and rising forty metres. The wall has gates in it. The line is also where the noise begins. One pace outside there is only the heat ticking in the salt. One pace inside, the sound arrives as pressure on the chest before the ear can separate it into parts.

The parts are these. Culms knock against culms in the warm wind, hollow and enormous. Seed pods the size of fists go off in strings, and somewhere above, pods the size of barrels go off singly. Wood squeals as it grows against other wood under load, like a harbour full of straining moorings. Broad leaves clap in their thousands. Sap hisses in split stems. Slit reeds whistle, wet joints creak in chorus, and dry capsules rasp. The ear insists on hearing birds, frogs and cicadas in this, but there are none. Nothing in the basin crawls or flies. No gnat sits on the fruit. The flowers are opened by concussion, their pollen shaken loose by the pods and carried on the shock.

Everything is built of the same green stuff, and none of it is cut. The columns are living culms, and the walls are strangler lattice that has closed its own gaps. The roofs are shingled with leaves that overlap like scales, and the domes are gourds, thin-skinned and ribbed, swelling as you watch. Thorns pin the joints. Aerial roots drop in rows and thicken into colonnades, and buttress roots step upward as stairs. Windows are glazed with translucent bracts, each veined in a pattern that looks nearly like writing and is different on every pane.

There are streets, squares, arcades, a waterfront with no water, and a tower district. The layout is unmistakably a town's, with frontages, corners and alleys that narrow toward the middle. The light is ordinary, bright daylight falling between the roofs in hard-edged shafts, greenish white, and everything can be seen plainly.

Nothing in the town is old. There is no bark thicker than a fingernail, no litter, no humus, no brown. A leaf that falls is netted by rootlets and taken back before it wilts. The wood is wet and pale, and it smells of cucumber, tannin and snapped stalks, with salt dust underneath. Inside the walls the air is hot and close with the plants' breath, though the marl under the root mat is dry enough to powder.

The town does not hold still. A house goes up in an hour, storey over storey, and its neighbours lean in and draw the water out of it. It turns to paper, rattles and comes down with a roar, and the wreck has rooted and is rising as an arcade before the dust has settled. Streets shift like the braids of a river. Growth passes through the districts in fronts. From the scarp these look like squalls, a darker green moving across the roofs with the din sharpening ahead of it and towers pitching up and toppling in its path.

There is very little water. Fog comes off the sea at night and beads on every surface. Now and then a shower falls, and the canopy drinks it before a drop reaches the ground. What the town has it passes along like a single ration. The ration's position shows in walls that are fat and glossy, with the gourds taut. Behind it the quarters go thin and loud, all husk and clatter, until it comes round again.

The town also grows wells, shafts of woven root sunk thirty metres into dry marl, each with a curb and a lip. It grows fountains whose basins fill with seed. A channel bedded with smooth green pith winds through the middle, banked and bridged, and nothing runs in it.

The basin's central rule takes a while to notice. Here the sound comes first. A crack rings out across a square, and then a beam grows across the square and thickens until it is able to make that crack. Then it breaks, silently. A tolling begins in empty air, slow and bronze-heavy. Over the following hour a tower climbs to the height of it and hangs a gourd there, and the gourd never moves. Low down in one quarter there is a continuous roar of trade, of leaves clapping like hands and reeds calling like voices. Stalls, awnings and counters push up underneath it, row on row, laden with fruit that no one takes.

The noise is the plan, and the plants are filling it in. The town is exactly as large as its sound, which is why it stops at the line. The noise is the oldest thing in the basin, older than any stem in it and perhaps older than the shelf.

Who the plan is for is unclear. There are doors everywhere, and no two agree on a size. Some are a hand high, some would admit a ship, and one whole street is hung with doors too narrow for anything but light. Stairs climb with risers of every height to floors that were never grown. The town has the idea of being lived in without knowing the shape of an inhabitant. It keeps guessing, and each guess is torn down and replaced.

In a few places no sound falls, and nothing will grow in them. These are the plazas, clean polygons of bare marl in sunlight, with shells underfoot and greenery crowding to the edge and stopping as if at glass. Stepping into one is like going deaf. The walls around it can be seen heaving and bursting without a sound. The plazas move too. When the silence drifts off, the ground it leaves is paved with stems in minutes.

Other things in the basin fail to hold as well:

- **Colonnades:** One has thirty-one columns counted from the south end and twenty-eight from the north, and the counts never agree.
- **Shadows:** They point toward wherever growth is fastest, whatever the sun is doing. At plain noon a single row of culms throws its shade in four directions.
- **Windows:** A bract window in a ground-floor wall shows the street outside seen from high above.
- **The tower:** One tower stands in three districts at once, as a sprout, half-built and domed. When a collapse gouges one of the three, the scar shows on all of them, and on the sprout it appears before the sprout has grown tall enough to bear it.
- **Distance:** A walk toward the scarp does not shorten. The town grows more town into the gap.

Toward evening the wind drops, and for a while the hollow stems stop hooting. It is not much quieter. The fog comes over the wall in a slow white fold, every surface beads, and in the dark the whole basin drinks and swells, and the knocking deepens. By morning the streets run differently. The bell is tolling from a part of the sky with nothing under it yet, and at the foot of that sound the marl has cracked and something pale and wet is coming up fast.
