The plain has been finished for a long time. Whatever hills once stood here were taken down grain by grain until nothing was left to take, and what remains is a pavement of dark stones the size of fists, each ground by wind into three dull facets and varnished the brown of old tea. They fit together like cobbles over a bed of pale gypsum powder. The air is cool and tastes faintly of salt and iron filings. The sky is a flat, even grey until midmorning and a flat, even white after that. There is no water anywhere, and nothing green has ever been here.

In all that levelness there is one hollow. It is hip-deep and perhaps fifteen paces across, a shallow dish with a rim so gentle you could miss it at a walk. In the hollow lies a boiler.

It is a fire-tube boiler, twenty-odd feet of riveted drum, left where the nitrate works set it when the works were still a going concern. Not much else was left with it: a flywheel sunk to the hub, a few yards of narrow rail with nothing at either end, a spill of firebrick, and a heap of clinker gone grey. Someone put down a spanner and salt has since welded it to the ground. The smokebox door is long gone, so one end of the drum shows its tube-plate to the open air, a hundred and twelve round mouths, each three inches wide, in a honeycomb of iron. The windward flank is scoured to bare silver, and in places the plate is thin enough to dimple under a thumb. The lee side is white and layered like candle-wax, and the white is not paint.

Every tube is occupied. A pair of storm-petrels holds each one, small soot-coloured seabirds that weigh about as much as a letter, and by day one bird of each pair sits far back in the dark on a single egg while its mate is away over the horizon. There is a sea in that direction, out of sight, and nothing in the hollow would be alive without it. The birds are quiet by day but not silent. A low purring leaks from the plate, a hundred and twelve small engines idling, and the wind draws a breathy note off each tube mouth as it crosses. The tubes are all one length, so the notes agree. The hollow hums a single chord, and a little off the chord, never quite resolving into it, is the purr of the birds, who seem over generations to have pitched themselves to the iron.

The drum itself, above and around the tubes, belongs to bats. They hang from the stay-rods and the crown in a single packed pelt and come and go only by the firebox door at the far end, never by the manhole, though it stands open. Under them the guano has built up in terraces, dry as flour, and it moves. Hide beetles work the top layer and darkling beetles the one beneath. Silverfish run in the seams, and pale moths the size of rice grains breed in drifts of shed feather. Soft ticks lie flat in the dust by the thousand and can wait years for a warm foot. Pseudoscorpions ride from place to place clamped to the legs of beetles. If you put an ear to the cold shell you hear it, a fine continuous hiss like sand poured on paper, which is the sound of feet.

Outside, the pavement is taken too. Grey gulls have scraped nests between the stones, each exactly one lunge of the neck from the next, so that the floor of the hollow is a grid of standing birds, all facing into the wind and all complaining. Small flat lizards live on the gulls as much as among them, climbing a leg to pick flies from the corner of an eye, and the gulls permit it. Geckos own the rivet lines and bark at each other in dry double clicks. The clinker heap is the best ground in the place for anything with a sting, and it is full.

The first rule of the place is that nothing in it was made here. Every calorie arrived by air, inside something, as fish oil in a petrel's crop, as anchovy in a gull, or as the midges and moths of some distant river valley packed into a bat. It is regurgitated, dropped, shed, excreted, or it dies with its carrier, and from there it passes downward through mouth after smaller mouth until it is mineral. The works once dug nitrate out of this plain, and now nitrate is being laid back down inside their own boiler, a new scale forming on the old plates.

The second rule is that nothing rots. A chick that dies in a tube dries to a husk in a week and is a beetle nursery for a season. After that it is a plug of feather and bone that the next pair simply nests in front of. Some of the tubes are a foot shorter than they were.

The only water comes before dawn. The drum is then the coldest object for miles, and a grey sweat forms along its crown. The darkling beetles file up the rivet lines in the dark, tilt head-down, and let the droplets run along their backs into their mouths. In the first hour of light it is gone.

Dusk is when the place is loudest. The light does not redden here, only thins, and as it thins the bats begin to pour out of the firebox door in a rope that frays downwind. At the same time, at the other end, the petrels come in from the sea. They flutter over the tube-plate like scraps of burnt paper, each one screaming its churring, hiccuping call so that its mate will answer from the right hole, and all hundred and twelve mates answer. The iron takes the noise and rings with it. The gulls, changing shifts of their own, yell across the top. A pair of falcons that nest on the flywheel work the rim through all of this, stooping from the height of a house and taking fur and feather alike. Later, when it is full dark, a small pale fox comes over the edge and walks the gull grid at an unhurried pace, losing some skin for every egg. Camel spiders run the pavement behind it.

By midnight the noise has settled into a steady roar that carries on until the bats return. It dies away when the dew has formed. In the morning the plates tick as they warm, the chord comes back on the wind, and the gulls stand on their squares.

From thirty paces upwind you would not know any of this. The rim hides the drum to its shoulders, the stones run level to the horizon, and the plain looks as empty as it has for ten thousand years. From downwind you would know at once. You would smell ammonia and old fish, and you would hear a noise coming from the ground like a factory at full shift.
