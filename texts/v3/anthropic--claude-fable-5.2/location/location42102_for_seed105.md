The lake fills a hanging valley some four kilometres long, held behind a lip of bedrock high in the range, well above where trees stop. Its water is the brown of steeped bark, stained by the peat moors that drain into it. Daylight gives out a few metres down. Below that, the drowned floor lies in permanent dark at four degrees, sixty metres under at its deepest. It has slopes, benches, an old river channel and a long ridge running crosswise.

At the valley head a river comes off the headwall and drops ninety metres straight into the lake. In the air it is a waterfall. Under the surface it is the governing fact of the place. The roar spreads down the basin through the water, loud enough near the plunge to shake silt off stones and still plain at the far outlet as a low steady pressure. It gives the dark a grain, the way wind gives a moor a direction.

Everything that grows here grows toward it.

There are three main kinds. The first is a moss that builds soft pillars as high as a house, each layer packed on the last. The second is a jointed reed with hollow stems as thick as a wrist, and the third is a broad plant whose leaves are stiff, shallow dishes on short stalks. None of them has any use for light. They take what they need from the trembling of the water, and they orient to it as other plants do to the sun. The dishes face the falls. The reeds lean up-valley, every stem at the same angle. The moss pillars are fat on the loud side and flat on the other. Colour matters only when a piece tears loose and floats up into the brown dusk near the surface, where it turns out to be the dark red of liver.

Near the falls the growth is continuous and thick, a shaking forest where stems rub and squeak. Farther off it breaks into groves with wide plains of grey glacial silt between them, bare and smooth. The groves persist because the reeds hum. Current through their hollow joints draws a note from each stem, a grove of them makes a chord, and the chord feeds the grove. A stand that silts up at the base goes quiet and thins within a few seasons. One that catches a good current thickens until it chokes its own flow, then it too quietens and dies back from the middle, leaving a ring.

The cross-valley ridge blocks the roar. Behind it lies a sound-shadow half a kilometre long where almost nothing grows, the equivalent of a north slope. The silt there is deepest and softest, and the hum of the nearest grove arrives only faintly, bent over the ridgetop.

On a bench inside that shadow, twelve metres down, are seven rings of footing stones with a hearth slab in each. There is charcoal under the silt, kept whole by the cold. There is a saddle quern, a pile of split cobbles and an axe-head of green stone, ground on one face and abandoned. Somebody chose the quiet side to live on. The terrace does not say whether the water stood lower then, or whether nothing about them required it to.

The rest of what they built is out in the loud water, and it only makes sense there. Scattered down the valley are drystone walls, chest-high, each laid in a crescent with its hollow side toward the falls. They have no gates and enclose nothing. Each gathers the roar and throws it to a point a few paces in front, and at that point stands a thicket denser than anything else at that distance, a plot of reeds and dishes packed stem to stem. Some crescents have slumped. Where a wall has lost its curve the plot in front is sparse, with a few old leaning stems and a lot of felt.

Then there are the frames. They are tripods of pine poles lashed with plaited reed-fibre, their feet weighted with stones in baskets. From each apex hangs a slab of clinkstone, the grey rock of the headwall that rings when struck. Beside each slab a cobble hangs on a shorter cord. When the current shifts, the cobble swings against the slab and gives a clear, hard note that carries a long way. Around each working frame is a round garden ten or fifteen metres across, lush to its edge, with bare silt beyond. Perhaps thirty frames still stand. In the cold, with nothing to gnaw the cord, the lashings have lasted far longer than they should.

Nothing gnaws because nothing is here to gnaw. There are no fish in the lake and no snails on the stems. Nothing burrows in the silt or settles on the surface or swims in the water. The plants are never grazed. Their dead fronds lie where they fall and compress slowly into a brown felt that carpets the grove floors a metre deep. The silt plains keep every mark. Where a frame tipped over long ago and its slab dragged downslope, the furrow is still sharp-edged. Every sound in the basin is made by water, stone, ice or stem.

The moss adds one more. The pillars give off gas in slow beads that climb, merge and reach the surface as strings of bubbles. On still days the bubbles tick against the surface, and in winter they tick against the underside of the ice, where they freeze in as stacked white discs.

The place runs on two clocks. The short one is daily, in the thaw months. Meltwater from the snowfields peaks late in the afternoon, the falls swell, and by evening the roar has doubled. The dish-leaves tilt a few degrees, the hum of the groves rises in pitch with the quickened current, and the clinkstones knock more often. By dawn the river has shrunk and the valley has slackened again.

The long clock is the freeze. In early winter the river dwindles to a thread and then to an icicle the height of the headwall, and the roar stops. For some days the basin is as quiet as it ever gets, with only the bubbles and a few stray notes from the stones. Then the lid of ice thickens and begins to work. Cracks run the length of the lake with a sound like a struck cable, a high ping dropping into a boom, and they come from overhead and from every side at once. The plants answer. Over the dark months each stem straightens and turns its new growth upward toward the singing ice. In spring the falls break loose and the stems bend up-valley again. Every reed in the lake carries a zigzag as a result, one kink for each year, and the oldest have more than two hundred.

Sometimes the headwall sheds a slab in the thaw. It hits the water with a concussion that every grove in the valley feels, and a cloud of rock-flour rolls down the old river channel and settles for weeks. The following summer a line of new shoots comes up along the path the boom took. They are pale and thin, and they lean toward a sound that isn't there anymore.
