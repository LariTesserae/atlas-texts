The plateau stands four and a half kilometres above everything around it, and it is new. Its edges have not had time to round. The escarpment faces are clean fracture, glassy black where the basalt chilled fast, and the scree at their feet is thin because very little has fallen yet. Across the top it runs farther than a storm can travel in a week. It is still coming up. In some districts you can measure the rise against your own shin over an afternoon.

The heat comes from underneath. The rock is skin temperature nearly everywhere and much hotter along the seams, so the air is thin and hot at once: you breathe as if on a mountain and sweat as if in a river delta. The sun has little to do with it. Above the plateau hangs the lid, a layer of vapor and mineral dust that the ground exhales and that never clears, brown shading to violet, turning in convective cells each wide enough to cover a small country. Around noon some daylight gets through as a tarnished copper dusk. The rest of the light is local: dull orange down in the seams, a blue-green fuzz of corona along ridge crests and on the tips of horns, and sheet lightning inside the lid that shows the whole country for a quarter second at a time.

There are no rivers. The lid sweats. In the cooler part of each cycle a film forms on every surface, beads on the metal veins and runs along them, and collects in the dished tops of the basalt columns, a few litres each, thousands of small round pools standing at different heights. Now and then a cell overhead collapses and it rains for ten minutes, fat warm drops, steam standing off the seams. Within the hour all of it is back in the lid.

The ground works. Nothing here was built; there is no wall, no tool, no cut mark. But the basalt jointed into hexagonal columns that never locked, and they move, whole fields of them rising and sinking in ranks, one metre or thirty, on strokes that take anywhere from a breath to a day. Fumaroles vent in sequence along lines kilometres long, like a firing order. Native copper and iron run through the rock in parallel bands as thick as a torso, straight for a horizon at a time, and they carry current. They are warm to the touch, the hair on your arm lifts toward them, and where two bands pass close the air between them glows. Fault faces close on each other and open again, and whatever was between them (ash, moss, mud, any animal that was slow) comes out as a slab, flat and banded, matching the slab before it. The slabs stack. Along certain rifts metal weeps out in ribbons and cools in coils. The plateau produces by the million tonnes, for no one, and each stack is shouldered outward by the stack behind it.

Plants take hold wherever the film collects and the strokes are slow enough. They are black-green and broad-leaved for the dimness. Cushion mosses pack the gaps between columns, and a liverwort follows the copper bands in strips so exact they look ruled. On the steadier ground between piston fields, tree ferns with furred trunks and fronds the colour of wet slate stand close enough for their crowns to interlock. None of it is old. A grove gets a few seasons before its ground is taken for something else, so everything grows fast and seeds constantly, and the air at knee height is thick with spores. They are part of what the lid is made of.

The animals are everywhere. Barrel-chested grazers with wide split hooves crop the column tops and ride them, a single herd spread across thirty different heights, stepping across without looking when two columns pass level. Thumb-sized moths cluster along the live veins in such numbers that the copper looks furred. Small-toothed gliders launch from risen columns to feed on them. Lizards bask flank to flank at the vent mouths and shuffle along in order as the vents fire down the line. A long, low hunter works the piston fields alone. Everything keeps moving, because every place expires.

Almost none of this can be heard. A noise holds for about the length of a body and then it is gone, without fading, as though taken. A column field the size of a city cycles, and from its edge you hear the drip from the nearest pool and your own swallowing. Collapses arrive as a push in the chest and a wall of dust. The lightning has no thunder. The animals have given up on voices: the grazers stamp, and the herd reads it through the rock with their feet; the gliders flash pale underwings; the hunter signals nothing. When something loud does happen, a slab cracking or an animal screaming, the glow between the nearest bands brightens for a moment. Shout, and the hair on your arms lifts.

Things here come in runs, and the slabs are only the plainest case. A rockfall comes off a ridge, eleven boulders, one splitting on a ledge. An hour's walk east the same eleven come off the same ridge with the same split, and again an hour past that. A grazer with a torn left ear and a pale scar across the rump stands in a herd of grazers with torn left ears and pale scars across the rump, each one chewing at a slightly different moment. The ten-minute rain falls, then falls again one valley over, drop for drop. Whatever happens on the plateau happens as a batch.

The animals live by this. A hunter that misses does not chase. It trots east to the next iteration and sets up where it now knows the grazer will break left. The grazers seem to know which of them are later copies, and keep those on the outside of the herd.

Runs drift. Each repeat is off by a little and the error adds up. By the twentieth rockfall the boulder doesn't split. By the fortieth it lands before it falls, the dust rising first to meet it. Late-run grazers have joints that bend in directions that don't help. A fern grove far down its series has fronds with no thickness, visible from one side only. These get scrapped. The ground under a failed run softens, a seam opens along it, and the whole stretch, rock and ferns and animals still chewing, is drawn down into the orange. The columns close over the top. Next cycle something else comes up there.

Walk a series the other way, west toward where it starts, and it tightens. The scar on the grazer grows more exact and the light on wet rock gets harder, until the things in front of you hold more detail than your eyes can take and looking at them aches like looking at a welding arc. No first one has ever been reached. The grazers will not walk that direction, and the moths that do come back white.

At the rim the lid hangs down over the escarpment in skirts, and the lowlands cannot be seen. A stack of slabs reaches the edge. The outermost one tilts and goes over into the brown, and no sound comes back. Twenty paces along the rim, the same slab tilts.
