The station has no name yet. A stencil at the foot of the scaffold stair says C-14 STATION BOX, and the pump crew who come down on Thursdays just call it the Box. It lies thirty metres under a hot city, a single vault two hundred metres long and about twenty across. The crown is an arch of grey sprayed concrete pinned to the rock with bolts, each bolt capped by a square steel plate. Below each plate a rust stain runs down the curve, the oldest of them only as long as a forearm.

Very little here is finished. One platform edge has been cast, a clean concrete lip running the length of the vault. On the other side there is only blinding concrete and bundles of starter bars, their ends capped with faded orange plastic mushrooms. The trackbed is a bare trough with no track. At the east end both tunnel mouths are open and black. At the west end one of them stops after forty metres at a face of raw rock, still scored with the half-barrels of drill holes and marked with a date and a chainage number in spray paint. Two inclined shafts rise toward a concourse that was never dug. They were meant for escalators and hold only the scaffold stair and a sagging yellow ventilation duct.

The vault is mostly empty air. There is a site cabin with one window, a pallet of cement bags long since set solid in the damp, and a drilling rig parked with its booms folded, the hydraulic rams furred with dust. A string of caged bulbs runs along the north wall, and perhaps one in three still works. Two sodium floodlights at the sump throw an orange pool that fades within thirty paces. Past that the vault is brown dusk, then only shapes.

Groundwater comes in at every construction joint. It beads on the concrete and trickles along the mesh, and it hangs on every steel surface as condensation that never dries. The trackbed holds a long shallow pond, milky with cement fines, ankle-deep at the west end and knee-deep at the sump. On the crown the seepage has started the vault's first formations. These are white straws of lime leached out of the concrete itself, thin as drinking straws and a finger long, each with a drop at its tip. They grow about a centimetre a year, and they are the only thing in the Box that keeps to a schedule.

The heat comes from the animals. The rock is warm and the transformer and pumps add a little, but the crown is carpeted with free-tailed bats, tens of thousands of them packed between the bolt plates and hooked into the mesh. Their bodies heat the still air under the arch until it is hotter than the tropical night above. At platform level it is like standing in a mouth. At the top of the scaffold stair, near the roost, the air is hotter again and reeks of ammonia, and sweat will not evaporate.

They are never quiet. A dry, incessant chittering fills the vault like static and rises to a shriek when a lamp flickers. Underneath it the machinery keeps going. Three submersible pumps run on float switches, each cutting in with a cough and a rising whine, and the discharge pipe knocks against its brackets. The axial fan at the shaft foot drones. The transformer hums at a pitch felt in the teeth. The crew wear ear defenders and talk by hand signal.

Under the roost the cast platform is buried in guano. It lies in long dark drifts, knee-high against the wall, and the surface moves. Cockroaches work it in such numbers that the drift seems to simmer, and with them are hide beetles, mites like blown dust, and pale crickets with antennae twice their body length. Long-legged centipedes hunt along the wall above. Each working bulb has geckos stationed around it, fat and translucent, picking off moths that have followed the airflow down the shaft. Rats use the cable trays as a road. Toads sit in the trackbed shallows, and the pond's edge is black with tadpoles. Where the vent shaft narrows a rat snake hangs from the ladder cage most evenings, striking into the passing stream of bats.

Plants are scarce, and they grow only where there is light. Each living lamp has a green halo on the concrete around it, algae first and then a skin of moss. At the two brightest there are a few small ferns rooted where a drip falls. A small colony of fruit bats roosts apart near the shaft, and under it fig seeds sprout from the dung, white and leggy, leaning toward the nearest bulb and collapsing within a week. Far up the shaft, real fig roots have come down from the surface along the ladder. They are as thick as cables now and still twelve metres short of the floor.

At dusk the roost empties. It takes forty minutes, a roaring column of bats funnelling up the shaft past the snake. When they are gone the pumps sound suddenly enormous. Over the next hours the air under the crown cools by two degrees and the condensation thickens. The roaches go on working the drifts. Before dawn the bats return and the vault warms again, and another drop falls from each straw.
