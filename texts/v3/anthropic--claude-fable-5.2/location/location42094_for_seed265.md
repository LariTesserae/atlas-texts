There is no light here. This is different from night. The lamps bolted over the pump-house doors are switched on, and a palm held near one finds the glass too hot to touch and hears the filament ticking inside it, but nothing comes off them except heat. A struck match stings the fingers, smells of sulfur, and shows nothing. Something passes overhead that warms the plain for some hours and then stops, and later returns. It may well be a sun.

So the place is known by the feet first. The ground is basalt, and new. It still has its glassy rind, and the lip of every crack and burst blister is sharp enough to open a boot. Nothing has been rounded. There are no pebbles and no sand, only a grey paste in the lowest hollows. The rock is warm the way a wall is warm behind a stove, and warmer downward: a hand pushed into a fissure comes out hot and damp.

Water comes out of it everywhere and nowhere fast. It beads along the fractures, joins, and crosses the rock as a film about a finger-joint deep, because the plain is too young to have cut beds for it. It tastes of salt and iron. It moves so slowly that its direction can only be learned by standing still until the warmer thread finds an ankle.

The plain is wide. A shout comes back from nothing. The wind arrives whole from one side, warm and wet, and has clearly travelled a long way without meeting anything taller than a person. The sea is out there, and above. It is a faint, even hush heard from slightly overhead, at the same distance in every direction wherever one stands. The ears register the depth as pressure.

The works are few and far apart: one-room houses of riveted plate on brick footings, each with a single-cylinder engine, joined by a narrow rail and a cable strung on short poles. Each engine makes one stroke in about the time it takes to count to three hundred. There is a long intake, a pause, and a thud that reaches the feet through the rock before the air brings it. Between strokes the plain is nearly silent.

The discharge pipes do not lead to any outfall. Each runs fifty paces and ends in a cast-iron spreader, a row of slots that lays the water back across the rock. The engines lift warm water from below and spread it thin. They are not draining the plain. They are keeping it wet.

The reason is found on the few rises that stand clear of the film and have dried. On dry rock the footing takes a moment to arrive. The edges there are sharper and hotter, like something cast minutes ago. A little farther in there is the temperature of ground without the firmness, and a boot goes down into warmth that has not yet settled into stone. An arm held out over such a patch stops feeling like one's own. The plain keeps one shape only where something is touching it, and the film touches everything.

The animals do the same work, or live off it. Flat crabs the size of a palm, soft-shelled and pale by assumption, cover the wet rock in a single layer, leg over leg, with no gap a finger fits into. The whole sheet shifts perhaps a body-length in an hour, toward warmer water. They do not scatter from a walker. They lift, let the boot down, and close over it. Thin eel-like things thread the film between them and slide across the instep.

Above them the air is full of moths, so many that it has a grain, a constant soft collision against the face and the backs of the hands. Their wings make the only continuous sound, like cloth drawn over cloth in another room. Now and then there is a space in the air with no moths in it, the size of a cart, warm on its near side and moving slowly. The crabs go flat as it passes. It has never touched anyone.

Counted by hand, the animals do not come out right. A palm pressed flat finds four crab backs in the room of one. They are not stacked. They share the place, and each has its own twitch. Press again and there are two. A moth settles on the wrist with the weight of several.

Little grows. A slick felt that smells of eggs covers the oldest seeps. In the paste hollows stands a stiff, jointed plant, knee-high and brittle, salty when bitten, each one alone. On the pump-house walls a vine with broad thin leaves has turned every leaf toward the lamp and pressed it to the glass. The nearest are scorched crisp. It keeps putting out new ones.

Inside a house the gauge glasses have been broken out and the needles bent forward to be read by thumb. The slide bars are freshly greased and the oil can is full. A coat hangs on a hook, damp, warm on the inside, with moths in the pockets.

The rail does not measure the same both ways. From the second house to the third it is eleven hundred sleepers, and coming back it is about four hundred. The hand trolley is at whichever house one reaches. The doors carry raised numerals: a 2, a 3, a 6, then a 3 again with the same dent in its plate and its engine at a different point in the stroke.

When the warmth comes overhead the moths rise and the face is clear for a while. The crabs drift toward the spreaders and the felt fizzes faintly. When it goes, everything settles back down.

Past the last house the rail continues onto rock that still crackles as it cools. The sleepers there are old, tarred, and worn hollow where feet have crossed them. The crabs are already on it, a thin first layer, holding it in place.
