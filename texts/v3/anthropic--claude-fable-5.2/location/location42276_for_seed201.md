The bowl sits just under the ridge crest, a scoop taken out of the mountain's shoulder, perhaps three hundred paces across. Three sides are wall. The fourth is a lip of ice-polished rock, and past the lip there is nothing for a very long way down, only the tops of clouds most mornings.

The ice left recently. A grey apron of it still clings to the foot of the headwall, shrunk back into the shade. The rock it uncovered has had no time to soften. Edges are sharp enough to cut, and boulders sit exactly where they were set down, some balanced on three pebbles. Every scratch on the floor runs the same direction, toward the lip. There is no soil worth the name, only grit and rock flour, pale as ash.

The light is the first thing that hurts. The sky overhead is a dark, hard blue and the sun comes through it almost unfiltered. The new rock is pale, and the ice and the leftover snow patches throw light back from below and from the sides. Anything standing on the floor has two or three faint shadows, or none. Orange lichen looks painted on.

To see the vegetation you have to walk the bowl from the ice outward, which is also walking from youngest ground to oldest:

- **Near the apron:** bare grit, then a green-black film of algae where the grit stays wet, then moss in tufts the size of a thumbnail.
- **Halfway across:** cushion plants, tight domes as hard as bread crust, starred with pink and white flowers smaller than lentils.
- **Near the lip:** the cushions have merged into a continuous ankle-deep pelt, threaded with willow that grows flat along the ground. Its trunks are as thick as a finger and its catkins stand straight up like candle stubs. Sedge fills the seams.

Kneeling, the place is lush. Standing, it is nearly bare, because nothing rises above the shin. The whole volume of the bowl is air.

There is no stream. Night freezes everything solid. By mid-morning the apron begins to sweat, and by afternoon threads of water run over the slabs, braiding, sinking into gravel and surfacing again. One hollow near the middle fills hand-deep by evening, skins over in the dark, and has mostly drained away by the next noon. The other water comes as cloud. Most afternoons it climbs the outer face and pours inward over the lip, and for an hour the bowl is white and close and every hair on every cushion carries a bead. Then it tears open and the light comes back.

The same updraft brings the food. Aphids, winged ants, moths and small beetles are lifted from valleys far below and dropped numb on the snow patches. Nearly everything here lives off that fallout:

- Dark spiders patrol the snow margins on foot.
- Dun finches with white in the wing work the patches in loose lines, heads down.
- Springtails pepper the melt hollows like spilled soot, shifting when a shadow crosses.
- Heavy, furred flies do the pollinating, and bask in flowers that turn through the day to keep facing the sun.
- Short-eared, tailless hay-cutters run between the cushions and the boulder field with their mouths full of stems, stacking them to dry under overhangs.
- A stoat, already whitening at the flanks, hunts the boulder field one gap at a time.
- Wild goats come over the eastern arête at dawn, in file, to lick a pale mineral streak on the wall, and are gone before the sun reaches it.
- Red-legged crows hang on the wind at the lip.
- Higher up, a bone-eating vulture turns wide circles.

For all that movement there is almost no sound. The thin air carries poorly, but the walls matter more. Frost works at them every night and leaves slabs perched on nothing much, and a sharp noise can be enough to bring one down. The animals behave as if they know it. The hay-cutters' kin lower on the mountain whistle at every passing shadow. Here they stand upright and flick their ears, or stamp once. The finches open and shut their wing patches. An alarm crosses the bowl as a ripple of white, and the stoat is often the last to notice.

A few sounds remain:

- Meltwater ticks, and the grit creaks as it thaws.
- Wind moves over the lip like breath across the mouth of a jar.
- Now and then the headwall cracks and something slithers down after.
- Sometimes there is a flat clap when the vulture drops a bone on the slabs to split it, and everything holds still for a few breaths.

People come here, though not many and not for long. In the lee of the largest boulder is a knee-high curve of stacked slabs, enough to keep the wind off two sleepers. Its floor is bedded with willow twigs and shed goat hair. Beside it is a blackened hearth ring. The fuel is dung and willow root, so the fires are small.

Nearby a slab-lined pit with a lid stone holds a hide bundle, a handful of chert flakes, a horn cup, a bone awl and a coil of sinew cord. Along the lip runs a line of upright stones, each about the size of a crouching person and set a few strides apart, leading toward a notch in the rock. The goats will not pass between them. They veer along the line instead, and the notch is where someone waits.

In the hay-cutter runs there are deadfall traps, each a flat stone propped on a carved stick. The trigger sticks are wrapped in hide, and each slab is set to fall onto a pad of moss so that it lands soft. Several have sprung with no one here to reset them.

Where the route comes up over the lip there is a cairn, with a strip of dyed hide tied to it that the sun has bleached from red to pink. Pecked into the cairn's top stone, and again into the boulder above the shelter, is a ring with a bar across it.

Evening comes all at once. The sun drops behind the western arête and the floor goes blue while the sky stays bright for another hour. The water threads stop mid-run and glaze over. The flowers close, the flies crawl down into the cushions, and the finches pack themselves into cracks. Rime starts to grow on the windward side of every stone. The headwall begins to tick as it cools. Some time after dark the first rock lets go.
