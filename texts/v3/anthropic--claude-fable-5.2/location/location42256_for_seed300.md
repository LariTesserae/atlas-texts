**The Slag Dock**

The ground arrives at the dock without any slope. It is made ground, a century of furnace waste tipped onto estuary mud, and it has had long enough to settle into what it will be. It is level, black-grey and hard-skinned, with pools standing in every shallow because the water table sits a hand's depth below the surface. The pools all tremble slightly, though no wind is strong enough to explain it.

The dock is cut into this ground. It is about the size of a church nave, sixty paces by twenty, and its walls are cast slag block, blue-black and glassy, pocked with bubbles that froze when the blocks were poured. Every edge has been rounded by weather and the joints have opened and closed and found their peace. The gritstone copings carry rope grooves worn deep enough to lay a forearm in. None of this is still decaying. It finished wearing some time ago and now only persists.

The water has not finished anything. The sea enters through a breach at the seaward corner, where one gate leaf lies flat in the mud and the other is gone. It also enters from below, through old cooling culverts and charging tunnels that nobody has mapped since the works closed. So the tide meets itself in the basin. Smooth domes of water rise from underneath and spread and are folded under by other domes. Seams open on the surface, travel a few yards, and heal. Eddies wander the length of the dock, taking a raft of foam with them and letting it go somewhere else. The water is brown with silt, and it turns rust-orange in long streaks where the iron seep comes through the east wall. No pattern on it lasts more than a few breaths.

Almost all of this is silent. The water is deep and heavy and slides rather than breaks, and the mud and the porous slag swallow what little noise it makes. You can stand at the coping over a basin that is visibly convulsing and hear only a drip from the wall, and sometimes a faint tick from the power line that crosses overhead in the damp.

The dock also keeps its own time inside the tide's. Every few minutes the whole surface lifts a hand's width and then sinks back, a slow breathing that belongs to the basin and not to the sea. At the breach there is often a visible step where the level inside and the level outside disagree. Because of this the walls carry a band of many fine wet lines stacked close together, with no single tidemark among them.

At low water the floor shows for less than an hour. It is fine grey mud, gleaming, and it is crowded with firebricks, lengths of chain, a wagon wheelset, bottle glass, clinker, and a ladder with three rungs. They lie nearly touching one another, and they never lie in the same places twice. One ebb leaves the bricks drifted into the north corner like a dealt hand, and the next has them strung down the centreline with the glass where the bricks were. The channels in the mud are redrawn each time as well, braided on one tide and a single deep curl on the next. A footprint would be gone before the water even returned, because the mud itself creeps.

The rule of the place is easy to read. Whatever lies below the coping is rearranged continuously, and whatever stands on or above it stays for generations. The quay still has its rails, set flush and running straight to the edge, where they stop. A crane pedestal keeps its ring of sheared bolts. A cast-iron penstock stands with its gear rack raised, each tooth softened by rust to the shape of a knuckle. On a galvanised pole there is a grey steel cabinet, still powered, with one small green lamp. It hums too low to hear, but a palm laid on the door can feel it. It is measuring something, presumably the water, and it must produce a very strange record.

Not much grows here. Bladderwrack hangs on the lower courses and a green algal skin covers the slag where the walls weep, which they do for hours after every ebb, orange water threading out of the joints. There is orange lichen on the copings, and a few spikes of glasswort on the one mud shelf that holds its shape behind the fallen gate. A single dry buddleia stalk stands in the crane base.

There is less animal life still. Crabs live under the bricks and move house when the bricks do. On the flood, grey shapes that might be mullet nose along the wall and are turned aside by the boils. Some days a heron stands on the gate leaf, facing the breach and watching the step in the water. It does not stay long, since nothing in the basin holds still long enough to be fished.

The light is plain overcast daylight, even and without shadow. The air is cool and smells of iron and salt. Mist lifts off the surface in small rags that are pulled sideways and taken apart as the water under them turns over.
