The stair comes down through forty metres of basalt, a tight iron spiral, and the sound reaches you before the heat does. It is a chord of three low notes, with a fourth that comes and goes, and it is steady enough that after the first minute it stops being heard and becomes a pressure against the sternum and the back of the teeth. Then the heat arrives, thick and wet and somewhere past what a body wants. The stair lets out onto a gantry above the hall.

The hall is about the size of a church nave. Half of it was cut and half was found. The near end has squared walls, drill scars, and a floor of riveted iron plates. The far end is the original cavern, a blunt throat of rock where steam comes up out of the earth and was once put to work. The pipes that took it are still there: a dozen iron mains as wide as barrels, running along the walls and up into the ceiling. They are where the noise comes from. Their valves rusted open long ago, and their joints have corroded into lips and slots, so the steam passing through plays them. Nobody is left to shut it off, and there is nothing left to shut it off with.

Light comes from two places. A ventilation shaft high in the roof drops a grey column the width of a table, which shifts a little across the floor through the day. And along the gauge board on the north wall, five or six bulbs still burn a weak amber behind wire cages, fed by a small dynamo that turns somewhere out of sight. Everything else is brown dark, with shapes in it.

Most of the shapes are plants, which stand shoulder-high and close together. They are fern-like but heavier than ferns, with fronds as thick as a tongue, such a dark red they look black, and warm to the touch even where the air isn't. They grow up through the seams in the floor plates, over the lagging of the dead boilers, out of the open doors of a row of lockers. They pack in so tightly that their fronds overlap like roof slates. Between them the floor is bare iron, polished and dry.

From the gantry you can see that the bare parts are not paths. The plants grow in bands, long curving ribbons that sweep across the floor, branch, close into loops, and meet the walls at clean angles. The bare lanes between them are just as regular. It is the figure sand makes on a plate when the plate is bowed. The floor is shaking with the chord, and the plants root only along the still lines, where the iron doesn't move. A seedling that starts anywhere else is shaken loose before it can hold. Walk down among them and you can feel it through your boots: numb buzzing in the open lanes, then one step into the ferns and the floor goes quiet.

There is no animal here of any kind. Every frond is whole, with no holes, no mined edges, no webs strung in the pipe brackets, nothing moving in the leaf litter. What falls lies where it lands and goes slowly to a black felt. The plants manage without. Their spores come off in the vibration, a fine rust-coloured smoke that rises in the convection over the vents and settles everywhere, in the air you breathe, on the gauge glasses, on the enamel sign by the stair that still reads, in part, EAR PROTECTION AT ALL.

Water here is mostly a rumour. The steam is dry by the time it sings, and the rock drinks what it can. But the ceiling collects the breath of the place, bead by bead, and a few times a day it reaches some limit and lets go. Ten seconds of warm rain falls straight down through the dim, hissing where it hits the pipe casings and running off the fronds in strings. Then it stops, and the iron is dry again within the hour. The only standing water is in the old sump at the low corner, a black square the size of a door. Its surface never ripples or settles. It holds a fixed lattice of small peaks, like hammered metal, each one standing in place.

Everything here has finished becoming what it is. The rust has gone as deep as it is going and has sealed itself under a hard purple skin. The brass on the gauge board has crusted over white, with every needle resting on its pin. A wheeled tool cart sits where it was left, a fern growing through the tray, the wrench beside it fused to the steel.

The one thing that changes is the pitch. The pipes are still thinning, by a hair's width in a decade, and as they thin the chord sinks. As it sinks the still lines move, and the garden moves with them. You can find the evidence at the edges of the bands: on one side young fronds are pushing out onto iron that has newly quieted, and on the other a margin of dry dead stems still stands where the floor began to shake again. Farther out there are older lines, faint ridges of black felt curving across the bare lanes, marking where the figure stood a century ago, and before that.

And there is the fourth note, the one that comes and goes. When it sounds, for a breath or two, the fronds nearest the cavern throat stiffen and tilt all together, the way a field tilts under wind. Along their undersides are rows of hard pods, sealed, the size of thumbnails. They have not opened in the time the lamps have been burning. They are waiting for an interval the pipes haven't reached yet, and the pipes are slowly sinking toward it.
