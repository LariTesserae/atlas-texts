Near the top of the mountain, where the sky has gone a hard dark blue and a breath holds about half of what the body expects, a lava flow three or four winters old lies across the ice. Nothing has softened it. Its crust is glass, and the glass still has all its edges; a glove dragged across it comes away in threads. Strands of spun glass, gold and finer than hair, sit in the cracks where the wind left them the week the flow stopped moving.

In the lee of one buckled slab there is a blister, a gas bubble that set in the rock and later lost one side. The opening is two hand-spans wide. Behind it is about as much room as there is under a kitchen table. At the back, low down, a slit in the floor breathes steam.

That is the whole place. For three vertical kilometers in every direction there is only ice, rock, and wind.

Inside, it is hot and soaked. The walls are black-brown glass, bronzed where the steam has worked on them, hung with small teeth where the roof dripped while still molten. Over the glass lie crusts: sulfur needles of a clean yellow that were not there yesterday, a white rind of silica, a rust stain, a blue-green bloom beside the seep. Over the crusts lies an orange and grey slick of bacterial film, with white threads of something like fungus running through the sulfur. There is nothing green, and nothing with a stem. Water beads on the roof, sheets down the left wall, and gathers on the floor in a pool a hand deep that simmers without pause, since at this height water boils long before it is as hot as boiling ought to be.

The light is ordinary. Daylight comes in through the opening and the steam spreads it evenly, grey-white, like a room with a north window. Nothing glows, and everything can be seen plainly.

Every surface is moving. The film is grazed by colorless lice the size of a pinhead, so many that the wet seems to have a grain that shifts. The pool is skinned with pale, dry-backed rafters packed flank to flank on the trembling water, and when the vent exhales the entire raft slides one body-length toward the light and then back. Wingless flies with long legs walk the roof between hanging drops. Where the seep leaves the wall, red worms as fine as thread stand in the hot flow in tufts and sway. They are the nearest thing here to grass, and they are meat. Flat pale hunters work through all of it, and their legs do not hold still under counting. There are sometimes eight, and sometimes whatever number the animal needs for the gap it is crossing.

Almost none of this makes a sound. The air is too thin to carry much, and the vent's breath is a long push felt on the face more than heard. Thousands of feet are crossing wet glass in silence. Now and then the rock ticks as it cools. The drops ought to add a steady plink, and they don't.

The drops are the reason. A bead of condensation swells on a glass tooth, lengthens, and reaches the point where it should let go. Sometimes it falls. More often it puts down legs and walks away along the roof. There is no other way these animals could have arrived, since the rock is younger than their generations would need and the ice around it is too wide to cross. They condense. At this heat and this pressure, in this one hollow, water has liquid, vapor, ice, and a fourth state that is an animal.

The rest follows from that. There are no eggs anywhere and no carcasses. A hunter that catches a fly does not chew it; the two touch, and after a moment there is one hunter, slightly larger, and a damp place on the wall. A louse that stops moving flattens into a bead and runs down the wall.

Close to the vent, where the steam is fastest, the bodies are unfinished. A set of legs carries a clear drop where the abdomen will be, and a drop has one dark eye and nothing else yet. In the thickest steam a single rafter can be seen in two places, not having settled on either. These half-made things cast complete shadows, as if the shadow was finished first. Toward the opening the animals grow definite, with edges, joints, and bristles. An arm's length covers a whole life.

Counting does not work. A palm-sized patch of wall gives a number. Counted again it gives a larger number, though nothing has walked in. The animals are not shrinking and they are not too quick. The patch simply has more wall in it the second time. The blister has the volume of a cupboard and the surface of a hillside, and the fauna have filled the hillside.

At the lip, the heat drops from scalding to frost across the width of a hand. Animals that wander past that line stop and whiten. By dusk the rim of the opening is furred with rime in the shapes of lice, flies, and a few hunters with their legs raised. Overnight the cold pushes inward and the fringe thickens. The vent slows. The pool stops simmering, and some time after dark the basin holds no pool. A single long soft animal lies there instead, the only large thing in the place, about the length of a forearm and exactly the shape of the basin. By morning the pool is back.

One kind of animal crosses the frost line and returns. They are grey, thumb-sized, and soft, with forelimbs that end in something close to fingers. Flakes of glass spall off the walls with each cold night, and these animals pick through them, test them against the rock, and discard most. A good flake is carried. They use it to score the sulfur crust and drink what wells up. At first light, before the sun reaches the opening, they go out onto the lip and chip the rime figures free one at a time, then drag them back into the warm.

Some of the figures thaw and walk off. Some thaw into plain water, which the grey ones drink. Nothing about a figure beforehand shows which it will be, and the grey ones handle both kinds identically. Any figure they miss is settled by the sun when it arrives: it either runs down the black glass as a wet streak or it steps back inside.

The loudest sound the place makes is a glass edge clicking on ice, a few dozen times each morning.
