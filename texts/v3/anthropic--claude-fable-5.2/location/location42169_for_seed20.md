Just under the summit of a mountain that is younger than most trees, there is a notch in the rock, and in the notch a bowl about forty paces across. The bowl is full of warm water. The water does not stop at the rim. It keeps going up, a column of lake standing above its own basin with nothing holding its sides, and on the downslope edge, where the rim is broken, it stands as a sheer face. From inside you can look out through that face at fresh scoria falling away into cloud.

It is loud, and the loudness comes before anything else about the place. The floor is still being made: lava bulges into black pillows, skins over, splits with a crack like a slammed gate, and steam collapses on itself a hundred times a second. In water the sound has no direction. It arrives through the jaw, the sternum, the fluid of the eyes. After a while it becomes clear that the roar is not happening in the water. The roar is the water. Where the noise thins, the lake thins with it, and the standing face on the open side shivers in and out by an arm's length as the sound swells and slackens.

There are also silences. They are the size of a crouched body, sometimes smaller, and they drift, and inside them it is dry. A silence slides across the floor and the glass grit beneath it lies still and pale, in air, until it passes. Bubbles here are only small silences. They don't rise. They move away from the loudest place, the central vent, in every direction: along the floor, up the bowl's sides, out through the wall of the lake. Beyond it they go on through open air as round soundless beads, visible only because the rain bends around them.

The light is the grey-green of day seen through wet canvas, and it never seems to get earlier or later. Red seams open in the floor and close, and they give off more heat than glow. Near the standing face there is a paler band where the outside's overcast leaks in. The water is blood-warm, with hot threads you swim through like wires and a cooler skin at the edges where the mountain air leans on it.

Nothing here keeps its shape. The ridge you steadied yourself on has been swallowed, and a new one, razor-edged and glassy, stands a little to the left. Currents shear against each other without pattern. The whole floor rises and settles like a chest. Everything is sharp, because nothing has had time to wear down.

The stones are the exception. There are five cairns, set far apart, and a knee-high curve of wall that encloses nothing. They are stacked by hand, with no cut or mark on any of them, and every stone is rounded and river-smooth. No river has ever run on this mountain, and the stones are older than the rock they sit on. When the floor heaves they ride it like floats and do not topple. Two of them are stacked wrong way up, the largest stone on top, and stand anyway. In the wall there is a gap the width of two cupped hands, where something was once set down and is not there now.

Very little grows here:

- **Vent mats:** rust-coloured threads of slime at the vent mouths, torn away and regrown within the hour.
- **Cairn fuzz:** a dark green growth on the cairn stones, only on the side that faces the wall.
- **The frond:** a single frond, long as a forearm, rooted in the wall's gap and streaming in whichever direction the noise is going.

Almost nothing lives here either. You might wait through three remakings of the floor and see one animal: a pale flat creature, hand-sized and eyeless, pressed to the lee of a cairn with its whole underside open toward the roar. Sometimes a silence sits beside it, and it isn't clear whether the creature has followed the silence or left it behind. Once in a long while something much larger crosses overhead, high in the part of the lake that stands above the rim. The dimness dims further, and the echoes of its passing reach you before it does.

Echoes generally come first here. You hear the crack come back off the bowl's sides, and then the pillow splits.

Every so often the roar falters. It doesn't stop, but it catches like a breath, and the whole lake drops a hand's breadth at once, everywhere. The standing face sags. For half a second the tops of the cairns are in air, dripping, and in that gap you can hear them. They hold one low note, all five the same, and the wall holds it too. It is the note the rest of the noise is built around.

Then the sound closes over them and the water is back. The floor cracks open in a new place, and a bead of quiet slips out through the side of the lake and falls away into the cloud.
