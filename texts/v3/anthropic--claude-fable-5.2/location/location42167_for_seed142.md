The plateau is what remains of a mountain range after everything that could leave it has left. It stands about four thousand meters up and is nearly level. Its only relief is a handful of low swells, none taller than a house and kilometers apart, the last roots of peaks. The surface is desert pavement, small stones fitted edge to edge and coated in a black mineral varnish that is smooth and slightly greasy to the touch. The stones are warm. The old granite underneath gives off its own slow heat, so the ground sits near the temperature of a living body and the thin air above it is only a little cooler. It is a dry warmth with nothing in it to smell but stone and, near the works, hot iron.

There is no light to speak of. The sky is black and carries no stars. The town has lamps, small filament bulbs in wire cages on every gable and along the rail lines, and they are all lit, but the air is too thin and clean to scatter anything and the varnished ground gives nothing back. Each lamp is a dull orange dot with no halo. A hand held just beneath one shows faintly, and a step away there is only the dot. Seen from one of the swells, the town is a wide, loose scatter of such dots that outlines nothing.

The works covers most of the plateau, though thinly. Each building stands over its own borehole and the boreholes were sunk far apart, so a generating hall of riveted plate is followed by twenty minutes of open pavement, then a kiln house, then a winding tower, then a row of long sheds. Narrow-gauge track and overhead wire join them. The engines run on the small difference between the warmth of the rock and the lesser warmth of the air. They are low-pressure engines, enormous for the work they do, with flywheels two storeys tall that take several seconds to come round. From outside a hall the turning is felt through the soles before it is heard. Closer, there is a ratchet tick, a soft exhalation at the end of each stroke, and one thin steady note from the wires. Between buildings there is nothing to hear, and the note from the wires reaches the ear before the next building's wall reaches the hand.

Nothing leaves the works. The dynamos feed the lamps and the circulation pumps in the boreholes, the pumps bring up the heat that drives the engines, and the engines drive the dynamos. An aerial ropeway runs the whole length of the town with buckets hung every thirty meters, each one loaded with gravel. The buckets are never tipped. The gravel has ridden the circuit so long that the slight shiver at each pylon has rounded every piece, and they now lie in the buckets like eggs. At its far end the ropeway passes the last pylon and carries on out over the scarp, where the plateau ends in a drop of unknown depth. The buckets go out over it one at a time, round the return wheel on its cantilevered gantry, and come back.

The town is as worn as the land. Rivet heads are flush with their plates. The iron steps into the generating hall are dished in the middle, and the handrail thins to the width of a finger at the height where a hand would rest. The doorways are the right height for people and the switch handles are set where an arm would find them. Nobody comes through the doors and nobody touches the switches. The rail-heads are polished bright, though no wagon has run on them.

Something does walk the rails. The animals are about the size of a hare, pale, eyeless and long in the leg, and there are very few of them. Hours can pass on a stretch of track between one and the next. They follow the warm conduits from building to building with a quick, even patter that is audible a long way off in that quiet. They feed on the oxide bloom that forms on the copper bus bars, rasping it off, and the contacts stay clean because of it. The bloom forms no faster than they eat it, and their numbers stay the same.

The plateau's only water is at the back of the generating hall, where the engine exhaust meets a steel plate cooled by the outside air. A film of moisture stands on the plate, enough to wet a fingertip, and it never gathers into a drop. The animals come to it singly, and across its middle is a shallow hollow the width of a tongue, worn into the steel.
