The town has no streets. It is one mass of drystone cells built wall to wall on the flat ground behind the storm beach, roofed in turf over whalebone and driftwood, and so old that the roofs have merged into a single lumpy pasture where small dark sheep graze. People move through it by low passages under the turf. From above, it looks like a field with smoke coming out of it.

There is one place near the middle where the roofs don't meet. It is a court about nine paces by six, sunk a man's height below the turf line, open to the sky.

The walls are limed with burnt shell and relimed every year, so the white is thick and soft-edged, rounded over the stones. The low northern sun strikes the upper half of the north wall and comes off it onto everything else. The court is brighter than the open ground above it. People coming out of the passages stop at the threshold with a hand over their eyes.

It is cold. The air has the still, mineral cold of a cellar, and breath hangs in it. The lower third of the south wall never gets sun and carries a band of hoarfrost with a straight upper edge. A row of antler pegs marks that edge. The pegs are grey and cracked with age, and the frost still meets them exactly.

Everything below the lime is green. Moss covers the floor in cushions deep enough to take a foot to the ankle, and the paths across it are only slightly flatter moss. Hart's-tongue fern grows from every joint in the stonework. Polypody runs along the ledges. An ivy with a trunk as thick as a thigh has gone up the east wall and over the lip, and the sheep keep it cropped level with the turf. Dwarf willow, crowberry and scurvy grass fill the corners. Orange lichen has taken the lintels and grey-green lichen the quern stones stacked by the door. No stone is bare unless it was limed this year.

The only water here is what the air gives up. At the brightest hour the sunlit wall sweats, and the drops find a groove pecked into the stone long ago and follow it down to a basin hollowed from a single boulder. The basin holds a few handfuls. It has a skin of ice with one hole in it the size of a thumb. Nobody keeps the hole open, and it does not close.

The court is full of animals:

- **Wrens** work the wall joints all day, a dozen or more, going in and out of the ferns.
- **Rock doves** sit in the niches the builders left for them, each niche occupied.
- **Voles** have runs under the moss, visible as faint moving ridges.
- **Snails** winter in clusters in the stone joints, sealed, in packed rows.
- **Spiders** have webs across every gap, each strand furred with frost.
- **Bats** hang in the passage mouths, folded up and cold to the touch.
- **A hooded crow** stands on the roof edge among the sheep, looking down.

None of them makes a sound. The wrens do not scold. The doves' wings open and close without the clap. This is the unusual thing about the court, and the people of the town treat it as ordinary. The moss explains part of it, and the thick lime, and the turf above. It doesn't explain a dropped pot, which lands with the sound of something set down in snow. Speech goes about an arm's length and then is gone. The sea is two hundred paces away and cannot be heard.

So nobody talks here. People come in hide shoes to fill a cup at the basin, to scrape a skin in the good light, or to sit on the bench of flat stones along the north wall with their eyes closed and their faces lit. They trade places by touch on the shoulder. A child learning to knap flint is brought here because the work can be watched without being heard.

It is hard to say what has changed in the court, because nothing appears to. The frost stays at the pegs. The moss never rises over the doorsills. The basin doesn't overflow or run dry. The lime is renewed, but only back to the same white. The oldest people in the town say the ivy was that thick when they were small, and that the crow was there.

In one joint of the east wall, behind a fern, someone long ago pressed a limpet shell into the wet lime, hollow side out. It holds a single drop of water, a little green at the edges, with a wren's footprint in the moss beside it.
