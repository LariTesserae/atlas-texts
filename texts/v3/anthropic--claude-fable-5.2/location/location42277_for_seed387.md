Under the turbine hall of the Harbour Road power station, below the cable basement and the old ash tunnels, there is a room that has been full of water since 1931. The drawings call it Suction Well No. 4. It is nine metres long, five wide and six deep, and there is no air in it anywhere. Warm water returning from the condensers enters through a culvert in the east wall. Cooler water from the river screens enters through the north. Three pumps hang their intakes down through the ceiling like upturned bells and lift the mixture into the heating mains that run under forty streets.

The room is mostly noise. Each pump has its own note, a blade-drone you would feel in your teeth before you heard it. Under that runs the mains hum of the motors overhead, carried down the shafts. Over it is the cavitation, where vapour bubbles are born on the impeller edges and crushed again by the thousand each second, a crackle like gravel frying. Sound in water has no direction. It comes through the walls, the floor and the ribcage at once, and the room is small enough that every wave comes back off the concrete before it has finished leaving. When a valve closes somewhere under the city, the hammer returns down the main and lands as a single blow that makes the ladder ring.

There is almost nothing in here. There is the cast-iron ladder bolted to the west wall, which nobody has climbed in ninety years. There is a valve spindle in a corner and the three bell-mouths. The rest is water, the colour of weak tea, thirty-four degrees, never still.

The light comes from one place. A bronze-framed glass port, thick as a fist, looks in from the dry gallery, and the gallery lamp pushes a brown cone a metre or two into the well before the water swallows it. In that cone you can see the weather of the room. The warm and cool streams meet on a shimmering seam that wanders from wall to wall. Bubble clouds bloom milky and vanish. And under whichever pump is working hardest stands a thin white cord of vapour, a vortex running from the floor straight up into the pump's mouth, whipping like a rope in the wind. It breaks, re-forms half a metre away, and breaks again.

Nothing is steady for more than a minute. The city's demand decides it. At six in the morning the third pump cuts in, and every current in the well rearranges itself. The seam swings south, the vapour cord jumps from one bell to another, and grit that had settled in a corner lifts and goes round. At midnight two pumps drop out and the water slumps into a slow, heavy turning before something upstream changes and it thrashes again.

For all that, the room itself is finished. The water did its work on it decades ago. The cement skin is gone from the walls, leaving the aggregate standing proud and polished like a cobbled street set on end. Beneath each bell-mouth the floor is worn into a shallow dish. The ladder's rungs are thinned on their upstream sides to dull blades. Only in the two dead corners does anything accumulate: knuckles of rust, and an orange fleece of iron bacteria that feeds on the rungs and grows no thicker than a thumb before a surge strips it away.

One eel lives here. It came through the river screens as a thread of a thing and now occupies the stub of a blanked-off pipe on the north wall, the only still water in the room. It is pale and thick, and it goes weeks without coming into the lamp's cone. Sometimes a stickleback arrives stunned through the culvert, circles twice, and is taken by the eel or the pumps.

There is also a spanner. A fitter dropped it down the shaft the year the well was flooded, and the pumps have never been able to lift it. It walks the floor in a circuit, along the south wall, across the dishes, back up the north side. Ninety years of this have worn it round, jaws and all, into a smooth grey lozenge of steel. Every few hours it knocks against the foot of the ladder, a small sound that the room's noise covers completely.
