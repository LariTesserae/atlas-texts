Everything that could be taken from this land has already been taken. What remains is the basement, the roots of mountains that stood here before there were animals to climb them, now planed down to a plain so nearly level that rain doesn't know which way to go.

The rock surfaces as pavements, broad grey shields of granite and banded gneiss rising no more than a body's height over half a kilometre. They are less like hills than slow swellings. Their skins peel in sheets a hand thick, and the sheets lie where they loosened, tilted slightly and ringing when a lizard runs across them. Between the pavements the ground is coarse quartz sand, pale as salt, with drifts of ironstone gravel the colour of dried blood. The lowest places are pans of fine grey clay, where water stands shin-deep and warm for months.

There is water everywhere, and none of it is going anywhere. It lies in sheets over the sand, clear enough to show every grain. It fills the rock pools, flat-floored basins with undercut rims that are dissolved a millimetre deeper each century. It weeps from the seams where the peeling slabs meet the rock beneath, and those seeps run for weeks after the last rain and paint long streaks down the stone. The streaks are black when wet and rust-grey when dry, made by a film of living crust that is the oldest tenant here. A fallen leaf on any of this water stays where it fell. In the wettest years the pans link into chains and something like a current forms, too slow to see, toward a coast that is days away and barely lower.

The air is warm and close and smells of wet stone and algae. Mornings begin with steam lifting off the pavements. By midday the light is plain and full, and the sky has a high white haze. In the afternoon, clouds build into towers along the horizon, where two or three residual hills sit blue and small as the last evidence of relief. Most days it rains somewhere in sight, in a grey column that walks across the plain. Thunder arrives long after, flattened by distance.

Plants keep to the margins. On bare rock there are mats of a wiry sedge that looks burnt and dead until rain touches it, then goes green within a day. Sundews grow along the seep lines, red and sticky and no bigger than coins. On the sand the tussock grasses stand apart from one another, each with its own skirt of bare ground. Every long while there is a single low tree, thick-leaved and wind-shaped, with nothing near it. The space between things is the main feature of the place.

Animals fill that space with noise. Each rock pool is its own small sea. Fairy shrimp hatch from eggs that waited in the dust and row upside down through the warm water. Clam shrimp cross the bottom, and tadpoles hang in black clouds along the rims. Dragonflies patrol each basin and clatter when they meet. Stilts and small plovers work the edges of the pans, piping, always moving a little ahead of their own reflections. Skinks bask on the peeled slabs and termites raise red spires on the sand. These spires are the only structures here, hard as brick, each leaning slightly toward the noon sun. A kite circles overhead. Narrow trails are pressed into the sand between one pan and the next by something that walks them at night.

The cicadas own the day. Each lone tree carries hundreds, and each tree's drone reaches the next across the open flat, so the sound seems to come from the light itself. At dusk the frogs take over. They start with a few clicks from one pan and an answer from another, and then the whole plain joins in, bleating, rattling, and knocking like struck wood. The chorus is dense enough to feel in the chest. No slope turns it back and no forest soaks it up, so it carries over the standing water for miles.

Under all the noise almost nothing changes. The pools deepen by a grain at a time, and another sheet of rock loosens over ten thousand years. The land has reached the shape it will keep.
