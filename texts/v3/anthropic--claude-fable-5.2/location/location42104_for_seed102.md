The chamber is a bubble in basalt about nine paces long and five wide. Its roof is low enough at the edges to make a person crouch and high enough at the center to stand under with a hand raised. The rock is young. Its skin is still black glass, ropy where it sagged and bristling overhead with drips that hardened mid-fall. Those are the only drops that have ever hung here. Nothing seeps or beads, and the air is as dry as the inside of a gourd, warm as a palm held near coals.

At the low end, a seam in the floor lets out gas, and the gas burns. The flame is a hand tall, blue with an orange tip, and it does not flicker. It gives the only light, a brownish dusk that reaches the walls without quite showing them.

The gas arrives through a slit, and the slit sings. It is one note, low and reedy, loud enough to feel in the teeth and the breastbone. The chamber takes that note and stands it up. The sound does not fill the room evenly. It hangs in fixed sheets and knots, and there are places where the air shakes hard and places, a step away, where it barely moves at all.

Everything alive has sorted itself by that pattern. Where the air shakes, the floor is swept bare, clean black glass without a grain of dust on it. Where the air is still, the dust has gathered in curving lines and rings: glass grit, moth scale, frass. On the dust grows the garden. It is thick, but only in those bands. There are cushions of rust-colored felt deep enough to lose a fist in, and jointed stalks coated in wax, the color of bone. Dark straps like tongue-leather overlap in heaps. From the ceiling, along the same still lines mirrored above, hang beards of dry fiber that almost touch the stalks below.

None of it is green and none of it is moist. A broken stalk would bleed a slow amber oil that smells of pitch and orange rind. The edges of each band are as sharp as a cut, with lush growth on one side and bare glass a finger-width away.

The animals keep to the same lines. Ash-colored moths hold station in the still sheets of air, strung in rows as if hung on threads. Beetles with antennae longer than their bodies walk the bands end to end and turn around. A pale lizard without ear openings lies along a strap-leaf with its throat against it, listening through its jaw. The crickets have files on their wings and rub them, though nothing could hear it. The neighbors feel it through the stalk.

Anything that must cross open glass goes flat and fast. A moth that drifts out of its sheet gets shoved sideways, tumbles, and finds the next still place.

Someone knew this room. A crawlway at floor level is closed with a fitted slab, chinked with clay that still holds fingerprints. In the largest quiet ring, the cushion-growth is pressed into a hollow the length of a curled body. A ledge on the wall shows fresh shell-shaped scars where flakes were struck off, and the flakes themselves, small black blades, are laid in a row along the edge of one garden band to mark where it ended. They mark it still, and the growth has neither crossed them nor drawn back.

On the wall above, there is a tally scratched in ochre. It runs to forty-odd strokes, each identical, then stops.

Here the flame does not gutter. For every wax stalk that drops a joint, one adds a joint. For every moth that falls from its row, one climbs out of the felt beneath and takes the place. The note has never wavered.

High in the far wall are three holes the width of a thumb. Two are stopped with clay plugs, thumbprints baked into them by the long warmth. The third is open. Pull a plug and the note would shift, the still lines would move, and every garden would be standing in the wrong place. Nobody has pulled one.

Below the holes, on the bare glass, lies a bone flute with a single hole, cut to the pitch of the room. Blown here, it could not be heard.
