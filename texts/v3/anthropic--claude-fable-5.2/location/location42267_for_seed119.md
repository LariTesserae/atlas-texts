The building stands on the fen like an overturned bowl. It is a single corbelled dome of unmortared stone, forty paces across at the foot and as tall as a mature ash. Nothing under it is ground. It sits on a platform of oak piles driven into peat, capped with brushwood, then cobble, then a hand's depth of blue clay trodden flat. Thumbprints are still in the clay where it shows at the edges. Even the fen around it was dug: the pools are old peat cuttings, their corners softened but still square, with baulks left between them as paths. A causeway of split trunks leads out to the dome, three layers deep where each generation of timber sank and was laid over.

There is no door. A stone-lined channel runs under the wall, knee-deep and shoulder-wide. You wade in bent over, in cold water, and then the roof rises away and you are standing inside a volume of air that contains nothing.

No hearth, no bench, no grave, no post. The floor is fitted slate, dished very slightly toward the center, where a round pool sits under the only opening, an oculus about the size of a cartwheel at the crown. The light that comes down is grey and thin. It picks out the pool, a ring of wet floor, and a little of the lower wall, and the rest stays the color of the inside of a mussel shell. Each course of stone oversails the one below by a palm's width, hundreds of courses, so the wall leans inward over you in steps too fine to count. The stones were never dressed, only chosen. Here and there a pecked hollow shows where a hammerstone corrected one. In one joint, high up, the tine of an antler pick is still wedged where it broke.

The dome is always wet. Fen air comes in through the channel and meets stone that never warms, so the whole inner surface sweats. Drops gather on the lip of every course and fall to the course below, and the wall is never still. It creeps and glints all day. Where the same drops have fallen for long enough, there are lime-white streaks and a shallow groove in the slate, a ring around the room exactly below the steepest part of the vault. The water table sits just under the floor and comes up through the joints after rain, when the slate carries a film a finger deep and the whole floor mirrors the oculus.

Not much grows. Liverwort lies in flat green scales where the light ring touches the wall. A dark algal skin on the stones at the waterline has been rasped into looping tracks by snails. One hart's-tongue fern is rooted in a joint directly below the oculus, on the side where rain blows in. That is all.

The animals are many and all at the edges. Martins have taken the upper vault, and their mud cups fill the angles of the corbelling in thousands, row over row. Over the years they have done what the builders did not and mortared it. The joints of the upper third are sealed with fen mud and spit, and the dome is tighter now than when it was finished. Below them, where the light gives out, bats hang in the deeper gaps, packed like dark fruit. Frogs sit along the drip groove, evenly spaced, each in its own worn dip. Newts hang in the pool. Eels come up the channel at night and lie along the foot of the wall with their heads upstream. Above the water midges rise in a fine smoke. Everything here eats them, or eats what eats them.

The noise is enormous. The vault throws every sound back, and it throws it back late: a full breath passes between a call and its return. The frogs have settled into this. One arc of the ring calls, the echo answers, the next arc calls into the answer, and the chorus goes round the room like a wheel, never quite closing. The martins chatter over it without pause. The drip adds a steady ticking from all sides. When wind crosses the oculus, the building sounds like a jug blown across its mouth, one note so low it comes through the breastbone more than the ears. The note climbs as the floor floods and the air space shrinks. When it reaches a certain pitch the frogs stop all at once, and the silence comes back off the vault a breath later.

At dusk the martins funnel in through the oculus and spiral down the wall to their cups, while the bats peel off and spiral up. The two streams turn in opposite directions and both keep to the stone. Nothing crosses the middle. The center of the room, the column of air between oculus and pool, stays empty. Every echo in the dome converges there, and a creature that flies by listening will not enter it. The martins don't cross it either, though they see perfectly well. Rain falls straight down it into the pool, drumming loudly in a room that is already loud. A heron sometimes stands in the mouth of the channel, lit from behind, and comes no further.

If you wade to the center yourself and stand in the pool, the sound closes over you from every direction at once, wheel and chatter and ticking and the low note under them. Your own breathing comes back to you a moment after you've finished it.
