The sound comes first, and it is less like a noise than a condition of the room, the way temperature is. It is one note a little below G, with a rasp laid over it and a second, higher tone riding on top like a wire. It does not rise or fall, and it has no rhythm that an ear can find, though a hand laid on any surface would feel one. It is loud enough that a wrench dropped on the floor would land in silence.

The room that holds it is small, perhaps eleven metres by eight, and sits on the top deck of some far larger works that it shows nothing of. A steel main as wide as a doorway enters through the east wall, passes through the machine, and leaves through the west wall. Everything else is there to serve that passage. The floor is chequer plate on beams. The walls and ceiling are cream insulated panel with taped joints. Six light fittings in wire cages give a flat white light like an office at noon, with hardly any shadow. There is one door, which has a wheel instead of a handle, and no window. The height is stamped on the nameplates, RATED 4400 M, and it shows in the motor's cooling fan, whose blades are broad and steeply pitched to get a grip on thin air.

The machine is a blower, a ribbed grey casing the size of a small car on a skid. It takes dried air from one side of the pipe and pushes it into the other. The air has been through desiccant somewhere upstream and is drier than anything outdoors. Enough of it seeps from the flanges that the room is the same. So although the thermometer by the door reads thirty below, there is no frost, no rime on the bolts, and no breath-cloud history on the panels. The cold has nothing to make itself visible with. It shows only in the cable sheathing, gone stiff as horn, and in the grease at the bearing nipples, which has set like candle wax.

Everything is new. The cut edges of the floor plate still carry their burr. The welds on the skid keep their heat colours, straw then violet then blue, unweathered. The gauges wear their protective film with a bubble in one corner. There is chalk on the walls from the people who set the room out: an arrow, a datum line, the letter N, a column of figures added up and underlined. A pair of ear defenders hangs on a hook by the door, still sealed in its bag. The yellow paint on the handrail has not been scuffed anywhere.

The floor never stops vibrating, and it has sorted its own dust. The grinding grit and swarf-powder left from construction have been shaken off every part of the plate that moves and gathered along the lines that do not. These lines form a figure, with tight loops and a kind of lattice near the skid, and wider, slower curves out toward the walls. Between them the steel is swept bare. The figure is perfectly still, because it is the shape of the note, drawn on the floor.

The lichen grows on those lines and nowhere else. It is a grey-green crust, brittle as paint flake, following the dust in threads a finger wide, so that from the door the floor looks like a pale diagram inked onto dark metal. At the feet of the four anti-vibration mounts, where the lines knot, there are cushions of something denser, moss-like and hard as pumice, each about the size of a fist. Along the cable tray on the north wall runs a single creeping stem with leaves no bigger than rivet heads. They vibrate so fast that their edges cannot be seen, and each one looks like a small green blur with a firm centre.

None of it appears to grow. At some point someone traced the largest moss cushion in chalk, a rough ring with a date beside it that is too smudged to read. The moss still fills the ring exactly and has not crossed it. The oil in the sight glass stands on its line. The thermometer needle is steady.

Only the hour meter on the control panel moves. Its last white digit creeps upward behind the film, counting hours that nothing else in the room has recorded.
