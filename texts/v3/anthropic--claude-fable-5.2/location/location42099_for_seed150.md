High on the mountain's shoulder, under a headwall whose fracture faces are still pale and unlichened, there is a shelf about four hundred paces long and half as wide. The headwall dropped it not long ago, as mountains count time. It is a spill of angular blocks, some the size of a hut and some the size of a hall, their edges still sharp enough to cut. No stone shows on the shelf itself. Every block has been taken.

A cushion plant has taken them. It grows as a mass of rosettes packed so tightly that the surface is hard, and a knuckle rapped against it gets a dull wooden answer. It has climbed each block and closed over the top, so each is now a dome or a ridge or a long-backed loaf, grey-green going bronze where the cold bites hardest. The domes stand shoulder to shoulder and wall to wall. From the scree above, the shelf looks like a town of low, round-roofed houses built without gaps and with no preference for any direction, cut through by narrow streets.

The streets are the first odd thing. They are all nearly one width, a little broader than a body, and they run downhill in a branching pattern that joins and joins again. They were not cut. At night the cold drains off the headwall and flows down between the domes like a second, invisible water, and any rosette that tries to root on a lane floor is killed by it before spring. The lanes belong to moss instead, a black-green pile ankle-deep and soaked through. Water moves in it without ever showing as a stream. Step on it and the print fills, shines a moment, and slowly rises back.

The town has storeys. At the bottom is a cellar of raw rock and the plant's own dead, since each cushion stands on a brown, fibrous core of its earlier selves, peat it made and now roots in. Above that are the living walls. On them grow crust lichens in rings of mustard and ash, and a dwarf willow no taller than a finger, which runs its woody threads flat along the dome surfaces like wiring and lifts a single catkin where two threads cross. Where an old dome has died at the centre, the roof has sunk and opened. The hollow inside holds small ferns, a liverwort like wet green leather, and still air a few degrees warmer than outside. Nothing on the shelf roots in mineral ground. Everything stands on something else alive or lately alive, and seedlings start only on the backs of their elders.

The plant is too thick for the age of the scar. The break above looks a few lifetimes old at most, and cushions of this kind should need far longer to put on an arm's depth, yet these are deeper than a body is tall. The shelf offers no explanation. The domes are firm, they are warm when a hand is pressed in past the surface, and they smell faintly of resin.

Cloud sits here most days. It does not rain so much as arrive: the air comes up the valley already full, touches the rosette tips, and leaves a bead on each. By mid-morning every dome is silvered. The beads join, run, and drop off the eaves into the lanes, so the town makes its own slow drizzle under a sky that never quite lets go.

The light is cloud-light, grey with green in it, and noon is only a lighter dusk. Down in the lanes, between walls higher than a head, it dims further. The brightest things are the water beads, and at dawn the frost.

Frost keeps the shelf's clock. Each night the lane moss freezes, and needle ice grows under it and lifts the whole carpet a finger's width. Each late morning the moss is set down again. The domes rime over white and then shed it from the top down. They let go sooner than bare stone would, because of the warmth they hold. In the thaw hours the place ticks with small releases of ice, a dry crackling under the drip. By afternoon the lanes are running, silently, toward the low end of the shelf, where they gather into one channel and go over the lip as a rope of water no thicker than a wrist. By midnight that rope has frozen into a fluted column. By the next noon it has fallen off and started again.

Near the middle the domes stand back from a level pan perhaps twenty paces across, the nearest thing the town has to a square. It is not empty. A lawn of pale bog moss floats on black water there, skinned with ice at the edges, and it trembles outward in rings when anything touches it.

Sound does not carry. The cushions swallow it, and wind passes over the roofs with a long hush and never comes down into the streets. Otherwise there is only the dripping, the ticking, and the far thin hiss of the fall.

Animals are few. A small grey-brown bird nests in the hollow domes and walks the lanes rather than flying them. It is seen perhaps once in a day, and its two-note call seems too loud for the place. Springtails gather on the frost like spilled soot. For about an hour around the warmest part of the day the roofs open flowers the size of pinheads, yellow-green and nearly the colour of the leaves. A few sluggish flies come to them, and then the flowers close. Sometimes there are droppings in a lane, round and dry, from something that grazes the willow at night and has not been seen.

The town is still being built. At the upper edge lies a block that came down within the last few winters, grey-white and raw, its corners unblunted. It already has a green hem. Moss has come up from the lane floor to the height of a hand, lichen has started its first coin-sized rings, and on the sheltered side sit three rosettes, each smaller than a fingernail. There is another scar on the headwall above, paler still, with a crack beneath it that holds ice every night.
