The salt pan gives no warning. It is flat and white in every direction, cracked into plates the size of tabletops, and the light comes up off it as hard as it comes down. What gives the place away is a tremor in the soles of the feet, then a pressure in the chest that swells and fades several times a minute. Then the ground stops at a lip, and the sound arrives all at once.

The sinkhole is perhaps two hundred paces across and thirty deep, and a city has been fitted into it the way a wasp nest fits into a skull. Its buildings are jars. They are coil-built, fired clay vessels the height of two or three people, round-bellied and narrow-necked, set shoulder to shoulder on terraces that step down the walls of the pit. There are hundreds of them. Every one stands open to the sky, and every one is singing.

The wind that slides across the pan drops over the rim and draws across the mouths, and each jar gives back the single note its hollow was shaped for. The big-bellied ones at the bottom drone. The slender ones along the upper tiers howl. Between them are so many middle voices that the ear stops sorting them and receives the whole as a solid thing, like standing inside a struck bell that is never allowed to finish ringing. Beneath it all the pit itself sounds, one vast mouth blown across by the whole sky. Its note is too low to hear, and it is the pressure in the chest.

Nothing here is wet, and nothing appears to have been. The builders cut channels along every terrace, smooth-bottomed and sloped, and joined them with fitted clay pipes. They made a wide basin on the floor of the pit where a fountain should stand. None of it carries a waterline or a mineral stain. The channels carry wind, and the pipes pass it from jar to jar so the upper tiers feed the lower. The basin holds a drift of seed husks, turning slowly.

Everything was made by hand. The coils still show under the wind-polish, and so do thumbprints, rows of them pressed in to bond one coil to the next. The ladders between terraces are poles of a pale, bone-dense wood lashed with fiber cord gone hard as horn. There are scrapers of chipped stone on a ledge by the kilns, and the kilns are jars too, blackened inside, the only dark things in the city. Where a terrace has slumped, the fill shows older jars beneath, crushed and packed with shards, and older ones under those. The city has been built on its own broken pieces many times over.

The jars are empty. They hold no bedding, no hearthstone, no bowl or bead. The mouths are no wider than a head, and no one could have climbed in or out. Yet the thumbprints are on the inside walls too.

The green begins a third of the way down and thickens until the lowest terraces are buried in it. It is a dry lushness, built of wax and oil: leathery leaves silvered with bloom, stems as thick as a wrist and swollen with resin, fist-shaped rosettes seated in the necks of jars like stoppers that didn't quite fit. Grey beards hang from the pipes, hung with papery bracts that rattle unheard. Amber beads stand along every cut and scar, and in the heat they sag into threads that harden between one stem and the next, humming faintly when touched. The air smells of turpentine and hot candle. At noon, with the sun straight down the shaft and the white rim throwing more light after it, the leaves glare like hammered tin and there is no shade anywhere but inside the jars.

The vegetation follows a pattern that takes a while to see. Down the main street, the ramp that spirals from rim to basin, it grows in bands. A stripe of thick growth is followed by four paces of bare, swept clay, and then another stripe, at the same interval all the way down. In the small plazas it makes rings and crossed arcs. The sound does this. The air in the streets stands in fixed waves, and where it shakes hardest it scours the ground clean. Where it holds still, dust settles, and with the dust come seeds, and the seeds root in it. Step out of a green band into a bare one and the noise becomes a hand against the sternum. Step back and it eases, slightly. The gardens are a map of the quiet.

The map moves. A vine reaching into a jar's neck narrows it, and the note sinks. A rosette that finally plugs a mouth kills that voice altogether, and somewhere down the street a band of stillness slides half a pace sideways, leaving its plants in the roar to be stripped while new dust gathers where they are not. Meanwhile the salt grit in the wind grinds every lip wider and every note higher. Growth pulls the pitch down and wear pushes it up, and between the two the city's chord has drifted over the years within limits it has not left. The oldest jars at the bottom have lips flared smooth as river stone, and their voices have gone breathy. The vines are thickest there.

The day has its own motion. In the morning the jars are cool and the chord sits low and almost sweet. By noon the clay is hot to the palm, the air inside has thinned, and the whole city has gone sharp and seems to climb the walls. Pods split in the loudest hours, when the air can carry their seed to the still lines.

Animals are few. One is a pale lizard, long-toed, with smooth skin where its ear openings should be. It lies along the dust ridges, exactly on them, nose to tail down the centerline of the quiet, sometimes six in a row a body-length apart. They speak with their throats, which unfold in a disc of orange and close again. High above, now and then, a bird crosses the pan, reaches the air over the pit, and veers.

At dusk the pan cools faster than the pit, and the wind falters before it turns. For a few minutes the jars lose their breath one by one, high voices first, until only the bottom drones are left, and then they stop too. The silence is so complete that the resin can be heard ticking as it sets. The lizards rise, all at once, and run to new ridges. Then air begins to climb out of the pit instead of falling in. A single small jar near the rim catches it, a little flat, and the others join it.
