The ground is new basalt, and it is still warm. It came up out of the crust recently enough that nothing has dulled it. The ropes and folds of the old flows keep their glassy skin, the fracture edges would cut a hand, and the collapsed lava tubes still have clean lips. The plain runs for thousands of kilometres at almost no height above the black, shallow sea it eventually slides under, and across that whole distance the relief is never more than a few metres of pressure ridge, blister, or crack. No wind has reached the rock and no stream has run across it. It was covered before any weathering could start.

What covers it is one plant, or one kind of plant repeated so exactly that the difference doesn't matter. At the bottom is a felt of root, two fingers deep, pressed into every pore and fracture of the basalt. It is damp and warm, with the temperature of a hand held under an arm. Out of the felt rise the trunks. They are straight and unbranched, as thick as a thigh, and they stand on a hexagonal grid about four metres to a side. The grid does not bend for the terrain. Where a ridge intervenes, a trunk stands on the ridge; where a tube has fallen in, a trunk rises from the floor of the trench, longer than its neighbours by exactly the depth of the hole. Their bark is smooth and slightly waxy, like the jacket of a heavy cable. At fixed heights each trunk has a swollen collar, and from the collars horizontal runners cross to the six neighbouring trunks, joining them with a small hard knuckle at each end. There are three tiers of these, at knee height, head height, and twice head height.

Above the third tier is the roof. It is made of plates: stiff, leathery, as broad as a table, and overlapped like shingles in layers many metres thick. They are leaves in origin, but there is nothing for them to do as leaves, because no light reaches them. This face of the world is turned permanently away from its star, and the sky above the roof is as black as the air beneath it. The plates are fins. They carry the forest's heat up and give it away to the cold. The top of the roof is dead level from one coast to the other, a flat dark surface the size of a continent.

The whole forest is an engine, and it runs on the difference between the warm rock and the cold sky. The root felt takes heat from the basalt. Water, warmed there, rises through channels in the trunks. At the roof it spreads through the plates, cools, and comes back down by a second set of channels, and the circuit never stops. In each collar there are valves, flaps of stiff tissue that open and shut to keep the columns moving. They make a tick too soft to hear from an arm's length away. There are billions of them, and their ticking, with the movement of fluid in the trunks, adds up to a single low tone at the bottom edge of hearing. It is the only sound in the place. It is felt more in a palm laid on the bark than in the ear, and there is no ear here to hear it. No animal lives here, down to the smallest grazing thing in the felt.

The forest is also electrified. The tissue against the rock and the tissue in the cold plates differ in some chemistry of their sap, and a current runs between them, up the trunks and out along the runners. The knuckles where runners meet trunks are warmer than the bark around them. Very rarely, and no more than once in a square kilometre in an hour, a junction arcs. A blue-white point lights a few centimetres of wet bark and a bead of water hanging from a runner, and then it is gone. That is all the light there is. The smell near such a junction is faintly of hot metal, and everywhere else it is of sap and wet fibre.

The forest's only product is itself. Under every plate in the roof a replacement is already formed, folded flat. When the old plate reaches the end of its service, the new one stiffens and pushes it loose. The old plate slides down through the layers over many days, lands on the felt, and is drawn into it. The same is true of runners, of valves, and of the trunks themselves, which are renewed from the inside outward in sleeves. Every part is the same size as every other part of its kind. A runner from the western edge of the plain would fit between two trunks on the eastern edge, and its knuckles would seat. The rate of replacement equals the rate of failure everywhere, and so the litter on the felt is never deeper or shallower than a single layer of softening plates. Every trunk has the same girth. Nothing is young or old, because the forest is all at one stage, and that stage does not advance.

Water is everywhere in small amounts. It hangs in beads from the undersides of the runners. It films the trunks. It stands in the hollows of the basalt, in pans a hand deep, perfectly still, at the temperature of the rock. The air under the roof is saturated and does not move, and it has settled into layers. A band of fog lies between the first and second tiers of runners, with clearer air below and above it, and the band stays at that height across the whole plain.

Above the roof, the heat and vapour the plates give off have built a cloud deck. It is a lid the same shape as the continent, and it never thins or drifts. From it falls a fine, warm drizzle, the same at every hour, landing on the top plates without any sound and running down between the shingles into the channels. This is the only weather. It began when the roof closed and has been the same ever since. The forest makes the cloud, the cloud returns the water, and the amount in circulation neither rises nor falls.

At the coast the rock goes under the sea, and the forest keeps going. The grid continues out over the shallows. The trunks stand in warm black water to the first collar, then to the second. Further out, where the bottom falls away, the felt lifts off the rock and floats as a raft, and the trunks hang their roots down into the water. The roof stays level. From above, if anything could see, there would be no line where the land ends.

Because nothing here scrapes, flows, freezes, or blows, the basalt under the felt is exactly as it was on the day it cooled enough to be gripped. The edges are still sharp. The glass skin on the flow-ropes is still intact beneath the roots, and the roots hold it without breaking it. The heat in the rock goes on rising into the felt as evenly as it always has, and the valves go on ticking in the dark.
