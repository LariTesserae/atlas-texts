You hear the place before anything else: knocking. Wooden mallets on brick, stone on stone, copper chisels ringing short and bright. Under it all runs a slower pounding, where crews tamp wet clay into floors with the flat ends of long bones. The sound has nowhere to go. The basin is lidded, so every blow comes back from above a half-beat late, and the town knocks against its own echo all day. Nobody here uses the word day.

The basin is low and shallow, a mile and a half across and sunk below the surrounding plain, and it is built to the brim. Nothing here is ground in the old sense. The floors are brick laid on brick, bedded in grey lime. If you pry one up, there is another course beneath it, darker and denser, and beneath that another. The clay for new brick comes from the Grindings on the eastern side, where old courses are dug out, pounded to grit in stone troughs, wetted, and pressed again into moulds. The town is made of the town. No one has reached anything underneath that was not laid by hand. The deepest bricks brought up carry thumbprints on their lower faces, as though whoever set them had been working upward from below.

The lid came about by accumulation. Walls rose and were bridged, the bridges were floored, the floors were walled, and now slab lies over slab eight or nine storeys deep, with the lanes at the bottom barely a shoulder wide. In places two walls lean so close that a child's flat hand is the gauge between them. There is no sky. There are the kiln mouths, forty-odd dull orange holes along the Firing Rows, each lighting a few yards of wet brick. There are tallow lamps in clay saucers, smoking and yellow, which show a face and a pair of hands and little else. Everything beyond is black, cool, and smells of lime, hot fat, and wet stone.

So the work is done by ear. A mason knocks a wall before she trusts it. A sound course gives a tight, high note, a void behind it gives a hollow one, and a wall going out of true buzzes. Lanes are known by their echo. The narrow ones clap back at once, and the long ones hand your footsteps on ahead of you. Each crew keeps a caller, who strikes a slate bar in a fixed pattern so the haulers can find their way back with their loads. At any moment thirty or forty of these patterns cross each other in the dark. People born here can pick one out of the din and walk straight to it.

The weather is homemade. When the Rows fire, the heat climbs through the storeys and meets the underside of the top slabs, which are always cold. Within the hour the ceiling sweats. The sweat gathers and runs along the joints, then lets go, first as scattered drops that tick on the lamp saucers and then as slow, heavy rain smelling faintly of soot. It falls through every gap in every floor, so on the lowest lanes it arrives in threads and curtains, never the same place twice. Gutters of split, fired tile carry it to the cisterns, round brick wells whose notes deepen as they fill. By the time the kilns cool the rain has thinned to a drip, the air goes clammy and still, and a grey bloom creeps over any mortar less than a season old. The bloom is the only growth here, a film closer to stain than moss. Crews scrape it off with bone blades and it is back by the next firing.

The mortar never quite sets. The damp keeps it cheese-soft for years, and the town treats this as a courtesy the material extends. Walls are not final. A crew with levers and wetted wedges can walk a wall a hand's width in a shift, and they do. They widen one lane and close another, and they shift a stair that came out wrong. Whole quarters are still only floors, with their walls chalked but not raised. Others are walls with no doors cut. You enter by ladder from above and hear your own breath come back from four sides. No one alive has seen the plan, and it is not drawn anywhere. It is kept as a long count, hundreds of beats of knocks and rests, which the oldest callers carry and teach by striking it on a pupil's forearm. To build is to continue the count. Arguments about where a wall belongs are settled by two callers knocking it out side by side until one of them falters.

Animals are few. Pale newts live in the cisterns, finger-long and eyeless, and are left alone because their presence means the water is good. A dun moth breeds in the tallow stores. It circles the lamps and now and then drops into one with a spit of flame. Sometimes, usually in the still hours after rain, something walks on the lid. Its tread is heavy and unhurried and belongs to one animal, crossing above from west to east. The callers stop and the mallets stop, and for a few minutes the only sounds are dripping and that slow weight overhead, until it passes beyond the rim. Then someone strikes a slate and the count resumes where it left off.

There are a few rules, and children know them before they know their letters, which here are knocks. Never seal a room without leaving one brick dry-laid, so the room can be asked whether it is sound. Never fire all the Rows at once, or the rain comes hard enough to drown the lamps. Never dig in the Grindings below the course where the thumbprints change sides. And when you set a brick, press your own thumb into its upper face before the next goes on top. The mark will never be seen, but it tells whoever digs it up which way was up when it was laid.
