The basin lies at a little over six thousand metres, a shallow dish in the summit of an old basalt massif, perhaps a day's walk from rim to rim. Whatever shaped it finished long ago. The tors along the edge have weathered into stacked loaves, the scree has stopped moving and been grown over, and the floor is paved in the wide hexagons that columnar basalt shows from the top. Each is six or seven metres across, with its joints sunk into narrow channels.

It is dark. The sky is black and crowded with stars that do not flicker, because there is too little air above them to disturb the light. If a day ever comes here, the plants show no sign of expecting it.

Every hexagon is full. The main cover is a cushion growth, black-green and dense as shorn fleece, knee-deep at the centre of each cell and thinning toward the joints. Stiff needles stand out of it, each ending in a point hard enough to go through a glove. Between the cushions grow tongue-leaved rosettes and a creeping rope with bark the colour of old pennies, and lichen crusts the tors in orange and verdigris. Nothing grows higher than the knee. Above that the basin is empty, only thin cold air and distance.

The growth feeds on voltage. The massif stands so far into the sky that it shorts the gap between the cloud floor and the upper air, and current leaks down through anything pointed. The needles take it. The roots follow seams of copper and magnetite in the joints, and where a seam gives out the plants have made their own. They extrude hair-fine wire of native copper root by root, kilometres of it under the mat, and it shows bright wherever a hoof has scuffed it bare.

Nothing here was built, and it runs like a works all the same. The cells come on in rotation. An active cell swells, its cushion rising a hand's width over several minutes as sap is forced through it. It warms to a few degrees above freezing and steams. Then it subsides and a neighbour begins. At any moment forty or fifty thin columns of vapour stand over the basin, straight up in the still air, and none stays in one place for long. Along the cell margins the mat sheds its waste in black, glassy grains of iron clinker the size of lentils, heaped ankle-high at the oldest junctions. The air smells of ozone, hot metal and wet peat.

When the charge is high the needle tips carry a violet corona, so faint it is easiest to see by looking slightly to one side. It marks out the working cells and nothing else. The only other light comes from below. The cloud floor lies two thousand metres under the rim, and storms move through it and light the horizon from underneath in silent sheets. When a storm sits beneath the western rim, the western cells run hard. They cycle in half the usual time, their steam thickens, and the corona brightens enough to show the shape of a tor.

Most of the time the air is freeze-dried. Water arrives when the cloud floor heaves up and spills over the rim in a fog-tide that crosses the cells at walking pace. The needles comb it, and rime grows on them in feathers that point into the flow. When a rimed cell next comes on shift the ice melts and runs to the joints. For an hour the channels carry a finger's depth of black water, and then they film over. At a few triple junctions the water collects in tarns no bigger than a room, warm at the bottom where the roots are and skinned with ice on top.

The animals are numerous and far apart. The grazers are low and barrel-bodied, woolled to the ground and about the size of a sheep, with teeth stained green. Each walks a route of its own along the cell edges, a closed loop several kilometres long and worn into the mat. It reaches each cell as that cell comes warm, crops the swollen cushion and moves on. Its fleece carries charge to cells that no seam connects. Where the wool brushes the needles there is a tick and a blue thread of spark, and the plants lay down a sweet frost along the route in payment. The loops never cross. Two grazers can live their whole lives four hundred metres apart and come no closer.

The one crowded thing in the basin is the pale springtails, which drift like spilled meal at the foot of every steam column. Grey ground-birds the size of a fist follow the shift on foot to eat them. They never call, because the air is too thin to carry a voice any useful distance. They raise a crest instead, and the tips of it spark.

The basin is very nearly silent. There is the tick of fleece on needle, the faint creak of a cell swelling, and a hiss where meltwater reaches a hot root. Breath is louder than any of these.

The hunter lies in a joint along the seam. It is long, pale and flat, and as cold as the rock until the cell beside it comes on. It takes grazers at the junctions where their routes bend, and it does not chase them. Afterward the dead animal's loop goes unwalked. The cells along it miss their charge, drop out of the rotation and brown at the edges within a few shifts. Then a neighbouring grazer's track begins to widen, pressed into the mat one cell at a time, until the gap is taken up.

Near the middle of the basin lies a mass of meteoric iron the size of a grazer, pitted and half sunk. The growth around it is the deepest anywhere. Copper wire has been run to it from six directions, and the needles on the nearby cushions all lean toward it. Its upper surface is polished where generations of fleece have rubbed past, because every loop within a kilometre has bent to touch it.

The basin treats any new conductor this way. Something upright that stood still on the mat would find the corona gathering at its shoulders and the cell under it swelling out of turn. The nearest grazer, three hundred metres off, would stop and hold its head in that direction for a while before going back to its route. The wire would reach the newcomer in about a season.
