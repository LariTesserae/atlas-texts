The basin is about three kilometres across and sits barely above the sea. It is floored with rock younger than a person. A lava lake ponded here over wet ground, then drained, and what it left is the pillars. There are tens of thousands of them, hollow chimneys of black basalt where steam once bored upward through the melt. Each is as wide as a torso and they stand an arm's length apart, sometimes less. They all stop at the same height, a little above head level, where the lake's surface used to be. Walking among them means walking under a vanished floor.

Nothing has softened yet. The rock still wears its glass skin, bottle-dark and iridescent where it chilled fastest, and it cuts skin. There is no soil and no sand, only angular grit that crunches like broken crockery, and ropy folds of lava between the pillars that still look poured. A drip-shelf rings every pillar at knee height and another at the shoulder, marking where the lake paused on its way down.

Fog lies on the basin most days, close and cold enough to show breath. It condenses on the glass and runs. Every surface weeps, and the water gathers in the folds, films the grit, and trickles down the inside of the pipes. Each pipe holds a column of water at its own level, with a column of air above it.

That air is why the place is loud. Wind across the open tops sounds every pipe at once, each at the pitch its air column allows. Together they make less a chord than a mass, a standing roar with notes inside it, like an organ with every key held down. It is felt in the sternum before the ear sorts it out. The centre of the basin is wettest, so its pipes are nearly full and shrill. The drier rim sounds low, close to a groan. Crossing from rim to centre is a climb through pitch without any climb in the ground.

The basin also sounds without wind. The rock is porous down to the sea, and the tide moves under it. As the water table rises, it pushes air up through every pipe and the field exhales in one long, even tone lasting hours. As it falls, the pipes draw air back down with a breathier, rougher sound. In the slack between there is only the dripping, and that is not quiet either. Every drop falls into a resonant tube, and there are millions of them, a wide irregular ticking like a hall full of clocks.

Light is poor. Noon looks like late dusk, and the black rock gives almost nothing back. What brightness there is comes from water: grey discs of sky in the brimming pipe tops, and a dull shine on wet glass. In the standing pools there is also an orange bloom of iron-feeding film with an oil-rainbow sheen on its surface, the nearest thing to growth the basin has. It wrinkles when touched and takes days to settle flat again.

A collapsed lava tube crosses the field as a trench, roofed in places and open in others. A stream runs along it with a noise far out of proportion to its size, because each roofed stretch works as a throat. On the seaward side the flow ends in a low, raw cliff. Swell drives into the cracks there and the blowholes answer with slow concussions, a bass under everything else. Near the centre, a few pipes still give off faintly warm air from rock that has not finished cooling. Their notes sit slightly flat against their neighbours, and the fog thins in a small halo above them.

Someone has been here, using nothing but hands. A line of clinker cairns, each three or four stones high, leads in from the rim. Where it ends, some forty pipes in a rough ring have been stopped with fitted stones and packed with grey clay that still holds the press of fingers. Inside the ring the roar drops away to a murmur. It is a room made only of subtraction. Its floor has been cleared of grit. One pillar has been broken off at knee height and capped with a flat slab, and beside it is a small heap of limpet shells, rain-washed and unscattered.

There are other plugs far out in the field. They are single pipes, widely spaced and nowhere near any shelter. Followed, they turn out to share a note. Someone has gone through the basin removing one pitch from it, pipe by pipe, and has not finished.

Animals are few. Pale amphipods the length of a fingernail paring turn slowly in the pipe water. At dusk a small number of dark seabirds, perhaps a dozen pairs across the whole basin, come in low under the fog and drop into the dry pipes along the rim, where they nest at the bottom. Their calls cannot be heard here. But a pipe with a bird in it sounds muffled and a shade off pitch, so on a steady wind a careful listener can find each bird as a small soft flaw in the low register.

Change is slow. The collective pitch creeps upward through weeks of rain and sinks again in the drier season. Grit sifts into the pipes a few grains at a time, and each year a few of them lose their voice. The glass skin is dulling, though not yet enough to notice.
