The plateau is one plant.

It covers the tableland from rim to rim, a distance that takes a walker most of a year. It is a mat of interlaced stems as thick as a forearm, closed on top by a leathery, grey-green skin that gives slightly underfoot, like the flank of a large animal. Those who cross it call it the fell. Nobody has found where it started. It has overgrown itself for so long that it has its own topography: long swells where old growth piled up, shallow basins where the understory rotted and settled, and ridgelines following fractures that healed before anyone kept count. None of it is rock. The rock is somewhere below, three to ten metres down, worn flat ages ago and bone dry. The fell keeps all its water for itself.

There is no open water anywhere. At dawn the skin sweats a film of cold beads that the sun burns off within the hour. In the afternoon the plant's own breath, risen and cooled, falls back as short, heavy rain that never forms a puddle. The skin drinks it as it lands. The damp lives inside the mat, in the dark thatch between the living surface and the stone. That layer is a continent-wide crawlspace of dead stems, pale roots, and swollen bladders the size of gourds, each holding a few mouthfuls of sweetish water. Here and there a bone spile sticks out of the skin, hammered in by someone and plugged with wax. Some still drip when unstoppered. Most have been grown over to the neck.

The heat is high-country heat. The air is thin and the sun close, and the skin is warm enough by mid-morning to feel through sandals. The heat starts the noise.

Billions of rasps live in the upper thatch. They are thumb-long, armoured, and blind, and each one draws a ridged leg across a hollow in its own shell. They begin when the skin reaches a certain warmth, so the sound arrives each morning from the east as a front. It is audible first as a hiss on the horizon, then as a pressure, and then it is overhead and underfoot and inside the chest. By noon it is a continuous white roar with a grinding undertone, loud enough to make the teeth ache. Speech is impossible, and the people here do not attempt it by day. They talk with their hands in a broad, slow sign language meant to be read at a distance, and they wear plugs of rasp-wax and fibre in their ears from childhood.

The noise is the rasps' protection, since nothing can pick one voice out of that many. But a rasp stops when it cools, and the fell makes its own clouds. By early afternoon the plant's exhaled moisture has stacked into flat-bottomed towers. Their shadows slide across the plateau at walking pace, and under each one the sound drops out. These silent patches can be a kilometre across or twenty. From a swell you can watch them coming, because they are marked from above. Columns of shadow-kites, thousands of long-winged birds, wheel along each leading edge and drop into the quiet to listen. In the silence a rasp shifting in the thatch is audible, and so is a burrower's claws. The kites stab through the skin with beaks like awls. Behind them come the lean, long-eared runners that hunt nothing but the cooled and the stunned. Then the shadow passes, the skin warms, and the roar closes over the patch again.

The grazers avoid the shadows and move toward the masts.

The masts are the only tall things on the plateau. They are the fell's fruiting bodies, single ribbed columns forty or sixty metres high with a crown of pods at the top, and each stands a day's walk or more from the next. Between them there is only skin and sky. Around each mast, though, the animals gather in numbers that are hard to take in. The skin-grazers are heavy, barrel-bodied beasts with rasping plates in place of teeth, and they move in herds that take a morning to pass. They scrape the fell down to its pink underlayer in swathes that heal within the week. Because the mat is hollow underneath, their hooves drum on it, and a herd on the move is felt through the feet long before it is seen. Mites ride the grazers, small birds eat the mites, and pale flies in drifting veils thick enough to dim the sun follow all of them. When a mast's pods split, which happens in the worst heat with a crack that cuts through even the rasps, everything within a day's travel converges on the fallen seed. For a few hours the ground there is solid animal. Afterward the ground lies empty again as far as the eye reaches.

The people are few and leave little, because the fell swallows things. A tool set down on the skin is rimmed with new growth in a month and gone in a season. So the lasting marks are cuts. A blade drawn through the skin leaves a scar that heals darker, almost black, and stays for generations. The plateau is crossed by scar-roads, thin dark lines running straight from mast to mast, recut by whoever passes with a flake of chert or a hammered copper edge. At intervals along them there are rings, tallies, and hand-shapes whose meanings are mostly lost. The only buildings are the quiet-houses. These are low domes of dead mast-wood packed with thatch and sealed in wax, set on stilts so the fell cannot take them, with room for four or five people to sit. Inside, the roar falls to a hum, and people can speak aloud to each other for a little while. The floors are worn smooth. Some hold a clay lamp, a coil of cord with knots in it, and a child's ear-plugs left on a ledge.

At dusk the skin cools from the east and the rasps go quiet in the same order they began. The night that follows is not silent. The whole mat contracts as it loses heat, with stems drawing against stems across the width of the plateau. It makes long groans, ticks, and the occasional deep report, like a ship's timbers. Under that, faintly, comes the drumming of a herd that has not stopped walking, and under the skin the sound of things in the damp thatch coming up to drink.
