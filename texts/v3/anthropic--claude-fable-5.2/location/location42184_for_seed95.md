The plateau stands a long way above the forest country, and nothing on top of it is higher than a standing person. Whatever peaks it once had were taken down before there were animals to see them. What remains is a table of pale rock several days' walk across, worn into low whalebacks and shallow pans. The relief is so slight that rain has no downhill to find and sits where it falls. The rock is warm underfoot at every hour, because it takes in sun all day and gives the heat back through the night.

Each evening the lowland air climbs the cliffs and turns to cloud at the rim. The cloud moves in across the surface, knee-deep and then overhead, and by morning everything is beaded with water. By noon it is dry again except in the pans. That is nearly all the weather there is. Real rain comes a few times a year and changes little.

Almost nothing grows on the rock itself. There is a grey-orange lichen film as thin as paint, and something tight and dark cushions the joints. Here and there a shrub the height of a forearm leans away from the rim, its leaves like leather coins.

The surface looks cobbled, and the cobbles are animals. From rim to rim the plateau is covered in shell-backed grazers, low and domed, packed so close that their shells touch. The smallest are the size of a fist and the oldest are as wide as a washbasin. They are the color of the rock. Each has four blunt legs and a rasping plate where a mouth would be, and with it they scrape the lichen that the night's wetting regrows. An animal clears a patch the size of its own shadow in a day. There are many millions of them, and none appears to have a voice.

Because they touch, none can move unless a neighbor does, so what travels here is gaps. An animal leaves its place, the one behind shuffles into it, and the vacancy passes backward through the herd at about a body-length an hour. From the top of a whaleback several are visible at once, small dark patches of bare wet rock drifting across the pale field, all tending slowly outward toward the rim. Lichen comes back thickest where a gap has lingered. The grazers seem to hold gaps over poor ground, though holding is a strong word for something that takes a season.

Most of the green on the plateau rides on shells. Old grazers carry moss in the seams between their plates, and the largest carry a turf two fingers deep with a small fern in it. Slate-colored lizards no longer than a thumb live there, a dozen to a shell. They never touch the ground in their lives and cross from back to back where the domes meet. There are moths with wings reduced to tabs, and mites red as rust. Pale snails hang under the shell rims, sealed shut by day. In the pans each wetting hatches shrimp so thick that the water turns pink and looks granular. They live nine days and leave their eggs in the dust.

Over the pans stand tall grey birds, one to a pan, each motionless above its own pink water for hours. They do not call either. At the turn of the year each bird brings a single pebble up from below the cliffs, since the plateau has no loose stone, and sets it on the lip of its pan. Old pans have curbs shin-high, with the pebbles fitted close. Some curbs ring pans that have silted up and gone dry. No bird stands in those, and grazers have filled them to the brim.

The other built things are the walls. A grazer near its end works toward the nearest one, which can take years given how gaps move. It climbs onto the last shell there and stops, and mineral from the fog cements it in place. The walls are crescents open toward the rim, waist-high at most, and some are a hundred paces long. They grow by a shell at a time, over a span that makes the pebble curbs look recent. Cloud condenses on them and runs down, and along the foot of each is a seam of true moss a hand wide, the wettest ground outside the pans. Every hollow shell in a wall holds a cupful of water, and nearly every cup holds one small frog that never calls.

At the rim the updraft is constant. Broad dark gliders hang in it in stacked layers, hundreds deep, and feed without flapping on insects lifted out of the forest. Their shadows slide over the grazers, which do not flinch. In the cracks just back from the edge, where the fog is heaviest, the grazers' eggs lie in rows like wet gravel. This is where the gaps end. A vacancy reaches the rim after decades of drifting, and something the size of a thumbnail walks into it.

So the whole herd is moving inward, from the cracks to the walls, at the pace of a lifetime that may run to centuries. The only sound of it is a tick when shell knocks shell as a gap passes, then another tick farther off. Beneath that is the rasping, which is too soft to hear from any single animal and sums to a faint dry hiss over the whole plateau. In the mornings the walls drip. The wind has nothing to whistle through, because every edge here has been rounded.

The walls get older toward the middle. A day in from the rim they are sharp-plated and grey. Farther in, the lower courses have slumped into lumps the color of the ground. At the center the crescents are only swellings, hardly distinguishable from the whalebacks they stand among. The whalebacks there are also crescents, and they also open toward the rim. Where a gap has scraped one clean of lichen, the bare rock shows a faint pattern of six-sided plates.
