The caldera floor sits at five thousand meters, and it is hot. The sun has little to do with it, though the sun is fierce enough in air this thin. The heat comes up through the ground. The basin is about eleven kilometers across, ringed by a broken wall of dark andesite, and the old magma under it never fully cooled. Snow lies on the outer flanks of the rim year-round, but none has ever reached the floor. What falls toward the floor turns to rain, and on some days the rain evaporates in grey streaks before it lands.

The floor is crowded with sinter towers, thousands of them, built up grain by grain around fumaroles. They range from knee height to the height of a house, pale and knobbed like stacked knuckles, each with a hole at the top breathing a thread of steam. Between them stand older, dead towers, rounded off and stained rust and green, and between those are collapsed hornitos, ash pans, shallow scalloped pools, and crusts of yellow sulfur. Nothing here is sharp, because the field has been wearing down and building up at the same rate for a long time. There is hardly a flat place to stand that isn't the edge of something else, and every path a walker takes was already decided by the towers.

Every tower is occupied, in rings sorted by distance from the heat. The inner ring, where the rock would blister a hand, is a wet orange-and-olive film of microbial mat. One step out, where the film dries to a rubbery skin, brine flies graze it by the tens of thousands, so many that the surface looks like it is slowly boiling. Past the flies are small grey spiders that build no webs. Past the spiders are flat, soot-colored lizards, each pressed to its own patch of warm stone and facing inward. Past the lizards the ground is merely warm, and there are the birds.

The birds are plump and dun, about the size of a partridge, and they do not sit on their eggs. They bury them in the ash at the depth where the earth holds the right temperature, and they spend their days tending the pits. They push the bill in, hold still, and then either scratch ash on or kick it away. Each active tower has a ring of pits around it, and each pit has a bird. When a vent strengthens or fails, the whole ring shifts outward or inward over a few days.

The only plants grow where the rings of neighboring towers fail to meet. There are cushions of a hard, resinous green thing tight as a fist, a few tufts of wiry grass, and a red moss that grows only on the downwind side of vents, where the steam condenses and drips. The cushions are rare enough that each has a resident. Usually it is a long-tailed rodent that sits upright beside it in the mornings with its eyes closed.

For all this crowding, the place is nearly silent. The thin air doesn't carry sound far. The fumaroles make a faint sustained hiss, like a kettle two rooms away, and the flies make a dry rustle when a shadow passes over them. The birds don't call. The lizards make no sound. The loudest regular event is a pale fox trotting the lanes between towers, checking pits, and even that is only a small scuffle and a puff of ash. Overhead, three or four large raptors turn all day in the column of warm air the basin sends up, never needing to flap.

A line of nine wellheads runs across the eastern third, squat steel trees of valves and flanges, their red paint weathered to the pink of old brick. From them a lagged pipe runs on trestles, as thick as a barrel, weaving where the towers force it to and then climbing straight to a notch in the rim and over. Near the notch is a corrugated shed with a turbine in it. The turbine is running. It is felt more than heard, as a steady thrum in the boot soles within a few hundred meters. A single lamp burns over the door in daylight. Inside, a clipboard hangs on a nail, and the last entry is faded past reading.

The pipe has been taken over by animals. Rodents have torn out the lagging in long stretches for nesting, and the bare steel is too hot to touch, so along those stretches the rings repeat in a line: a stripe of flies on the trestle footings, spiders on the cross-braces, and lizards on the ground below in two neat rows like a guard of honor. The birds have dug pits along it for kilometers. Where a flange leaks, a small sinter tower has started, wrist-high and already tenanted.

Elsewhere there is less. An ore-cart track with its rails cemented into the sulfur crust leads to a roofless stone hut. Farther on, a concrete pad carries four rusted bolts and nothing else.

The basin makes its own weather on a schedule. At dawn the steam stands straight up from every tower, thousands of white threads catching the first sun. By midmorning the threads have merged into a haze, and by early afternoon the haze has become a flat-bottomed cloud that sits on the rim like a lid. The light goes soft and shadowless. The flies settle. At around four it rains, warm and brief, with heavy drops that smell faintly of matches. The rain fills the scalloped pools and runs down the towers in sheets, and for an hour the whole field shines.

Then everything comes to the pools at once. Each pool is scalding at the center and drinkable at the margin, and the animals know exactly where the line is. Birds, rodents, and lizards drink shoulder to shoulder, and the fox drinks a little apart. By dusk the water has sunk back into the ground. The cloud thins, the rim reappears, and the stars come out very close and very many. The ground stays as warm as it was at noon, and the birds go back to testing their pits in the dark.
