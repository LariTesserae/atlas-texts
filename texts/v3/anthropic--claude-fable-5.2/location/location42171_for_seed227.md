Before there is anything to see, there is the ground knocking. Out on the low heath above the shore, through the long dark of the cold months, the turf carries a thud every ten or twelve breaths, faint as a pulse felt through bedding. Follow it seaward and the heather thins to woodrush, then to a lip of bare rock with a gap in it about the length of a boat and hardly wider than a person lying down. The noise comes out of that gap.

On the landward side, footholds have been pecked into the bedded stone, each one a shallow cup worn glossy by heels. The top three glaze with ice in winter, but lower down the air rising from below is sea-air and stays just above freezing. A rope of twisted heather-stem hangs from a peg of whale rib driven into a crack. Beside the peg is a niche the size of two fists, holding wads of raw wool rolled in tallow. These go in the ears. Nobody descends without them twice.

Below is a single room. It is twelve paces long and six wide, with a roof three people high where the slot lets in the sky and lower everywhere else. The rock is thin-bedded flagstone, grey-brown, tilted a few degrees so that every wall is a stack of narrow shelves. The collapse that opened the roof happened long ago, and the sea has since carried off everything that fell. All the edges are rounded and nothing is fresh.

At the seaward end the floor dips to a fissure an arm's length wide, its lips smooth as the mouth of a jug. This is the throat. Under it a tunnel runs out beneath the cliff to open water, and no one has seen it. The sea does not come up through the throat. It sends its air.

A swell enters the tunnel and seals it. The trapped air has one way out, so it comes up through the throat, across the room and out of the roof. First the room inhales, a long hiss that pulls at sleeves and hair and draws lamp flames flat toward the floor. Then comes the blow. It is felt more than heard: a shove in the chest, a jolt in the teeth, the lungs emptied a little without consent. After it the exhalation tears upward through the slot with a sound like a sail splitting. Under all of this runs the grinding of cobbles being rolled in the tunnel below. It rises through the rock into the feet and it never stops. At the lowest ebbs of the year the tunnel fails to seal, the concussions cease, and there is only the grinding, which is still too loud to shout over.

The room holds almost nothing. The air moving in and out has seen to that over a very long time. Along the line from throat to slot the floor is naked rock, polished and faintly dished, with no pebble, shell fragment or blown leaf on it. Nothing loose survives there. Whatever is set down in the middle of the room will not be there the next day. So the middle is empty air and noise, and everything else that exists in the place sits at the edges, in the lee, rooted or wedged or tied.

At the edges it is green, though in this light the green is close to black. Outside the scoured path the shelves are furred with moss a hand deep, and the moss is overgrown with liverworts in wet overlapping scales. Hart's-tongue ferns hang their straps from every joint in the rock. Deeper in, where the sky is a grey seam seen sideways, there are filmy ferns with fronds one cell thick, translucent as wet skin, which grow only where the air never dries and the sun never arrives. It never does arrive. For a few hours around midday in winter the slot turns from black to pewter, and that is the light these plants live on. Up at the rim, in the real weather, there are roseroot and scurvy-grass and cushions of thrift gone brown with frost. The boundary between the two gardens is sharp, drawn where the spray-mist stops and the freezing starts.

Water comes here mostly as breath. Each exhalation of the throat carries salt fog, which beads on the fern tips and the moss. At each concussion every bead in the room lets go at once, a single brief rain from walls to floor, and then the beading starts again. There is one source of fresh water: a seep from the peat above, brown as tea, that threads down the landward wall into a basin in a fallen slab. The basin was a natural hollow that someone deepened by pecking, and the tool marks are still there under the algae. Its surface is never still. Between blows it holds a fine standing pattern of ripples in rings and crossed lines, the grinding of the tunnel made visible. At the blow the pattern shatters, and within a few seconds it has re-formed.

The human things are few and all in the lee. In the landward recess, out of the air's path, stands a cell of drystone no taller than a crouching person, roofed with three flags and built with gaps left between its courses on purpose. Poles of driftwood are wedged across the inside. Split fish and strips of mutton hang from them in season, because the room's breathing passes through the gaps and dries them slowly in salt cold. No fly will come down into this dark, and the frost that would spoil the meat up top doesn't reach it. There is also a stone lamp, a cobble with a thumbed hollow, black with old fish oil. On the wall beside the cell are rows of short scratches in groups, made by different hands at different depths, the oldest of them nearly closed over by lichen. They count something, perhaps fish or winters. There is nothing else. Everything that enters is carried in tied to the body and carried out the same way.

No one speaks here, because there is no point. A hand on the shoulder means "look," two taps mean "up," and a flat palm means "wait for the blow." Work is done in the inhalations and braced through the rest.

Animals are scarce. On the ceiling of the recess, herald moths sit out the winter with their wings folded like dead leaves, and their eyes give back two red points when the lamp is lifted. Cave spiders hang nearby, long-legged and glossy, each guarding an egg sac suspended on a thread like a pale drop. The sacs swing with every breath of the room and have never been seen to fall. A wren works the moss shelves for springtails. Sometimes it stops and tilts its head back and its throat shudders, and nothing whatsoever can be heard. Now and then a smear of fish scale and small bones appears on a ledge near the throat, which means an otter has come up from below by some route of its own, though no one has seen it do so.

The place changes too slowly to watch. A slab drops from the slot perhaps once in a lifetime and is gone by the next spring tide. The moss advances a finger's width and the blast holds it there. The throat widens by the thickness of a hair. As the room grows, its note deepens, and the blow that the oldest scratches on the wall were made to is a shade higher than the one that shakes the lamp now. No one has lived long enough to hear the difference.

Climbing out, the wool comes from the ears at the rim. For an hour afterward the heath sounds thin, the wind in the heather like something heard through a wall, and the knock in the turf underfoot still marks the time.
