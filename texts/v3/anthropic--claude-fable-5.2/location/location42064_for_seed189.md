Below the forest there is no soil. As far down as anyone has dug, the ground is one plant, rhizome packed against rhizome and tuber against tuber, pale and dense as raw yam. Leaf litter lies over it like a skin, and the trees, if they are separate trees, stand out of it like hairs. About a body-length under the surface, the flesh has pulled apart and left a hollow.

The hollow is new. Its walls are tear-faces, stringy where the tissue stretched before it gave, wet-white going to cream where the air has started on it. Splinters of harder strand stand out sharp enough to open a palm, and nothing has callused. Clear sap beads on the walls, and milky sap in the deeper rips. The room smells of cut turnip, sweet and faintly sour, and it is warm like the inside of a mouth. It is perhaps four paces from wall to wall. A person could stand in it but could not stretch.

It is lit by ordinary, slightly gold daylight, falling at a slant as if through a high opening, though the ceiling is closed flesh. The light is what the leaves are getting. It comes down through the plant the way water does and arrives with its shadows still in it. The floor is dappled with the moving shade of twigs and leaves, swaying in a wind that doesn't reach here. Sometimes the shadow of a bird crosses.

The days are short. The light swings from the left wall to the right in about the time it takes to get hungry, goes amber, and goes out. A brief warm dark follows, and then it starts again on the left. The days above do not keep this pace. Perhaps the plant hands them down in pieces, or hands down every leaf's day separately, one after another.

The walls roar. Sap is moving in bulk behind the tear-faces, like a river behind a door, and its pitch climbs as the light climbs. Over it come the cracks of tissue still parting somewhere close, each one like a green branch broken over a knee, loud enough to feel in the teeth. The canopy's noise comes down with the canopy's light, so wind in leaves plays at full volume in air that does not stir.

Rain is sometimes heard, hammering, while the light stays dry and bright. At other times the ceiling sweats and lets down a thin real rain in total silence, and the hiss turns up later, with nothing falling. The floor is never more than tacky. The plant drinks back what it drops.

The drips show what is wrong with the middle. A drop that lets go near a wall lands at once. A drop that lets go over the centre falls and keeps falling, slowly enough to count to twenty or thirty before the small tick of its landing. The hollow holds more distance than its walls enclose. At the edges it is a room, and toward the centre the floor goes on.

Sound behaves the same way. A crack from the far wall is heard twice, immediately through the floor and much later through the air, thinned as if it had crossed a valley. Now and then the faint one arrives first. Nothing here has settled. The hollow is green in the timber sense, unseasoned, and its distances are still warping as they dry.

The plant is trying to close the wound. White root tips nose out of the walls all around, furred with fine hairs, growing fast enough to watch. Near the walls they do well, and weave a fringe a forearm deep. The ones that set out for the centre lengthen and thin without getting any nearer. By dark they are threads, and they sag. By the next light they lie on the floor like spilled string, all pointing inward, and the fringe is no thicker than before. So the room stays almost empty. The packed mass of the plant presses in on every side, and in here there is only air, light, and noise.

The walls move with the light. At the overhead moment, when the plant drinks hardest, the flesh draws in on itself. The hollow widens by a hand, the tears gape, and the sap runs. In the dark it swells back, the splinters cross one another like laced fingers, and the roar drops to a mutter. A tan skin forms on the tear-faces overnight. At first light it splits and the wood underneath is white again. By the look of the wood, the hollow opened three days ago.

There is a stick on the floor near the wall. It is a digging stick as long as a leg, one end charred hard and scraped to a point, and the flat facets left by the stone that scraped it still show. Bark-fibre cord is wrapped at the grip, mostly rotted to dust. The stick is grey, checked along the grain, and very dry, far older than three days. The plant has not grown onto it. It casts its shadow correctly.

Once in each short day, when the light stands at a certain slant, another shadow joins the leaf-shadows on the floor. It is a person kneeling, arms working, driving the shadow of a stick down again and again. The sound of the digging arrives at some other hour, usually in the dark, as a thud and the wet chop of a point going into flesh overhead. The ceiling doesn't mark. The shadow has not yet broken through, and the stick is already here.

One animal lives in the hollow. It is a nymph of the cicada kind, thumb-sized and amber-pale, with blind domes for eyes and forelegs shaped like small digging tools. It lived with its beak in the plant until the wall it was drinking from pulled away. Since then it has been walking. It set out from the edge toward the other side, and it is somewhere in the middle now. From the wall it is a speck that could be a bead of sap. It is the one silent thing in the room.

Every few lights it stops, drives its beak into the floor, and drinks. The roar does not change. Then it goes on walking, with the fallen root-threads pointing after it. Above it a drop lets go of the ceiling and begins to fall.
