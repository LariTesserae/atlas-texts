The plateau is younger than most of the people who live on it. It stands about three thousand metres above the old lowlands and runs farther in every direction than weather can cross in a day, a tableland of dark grey-green stone laid down in courses, each a few metres thick and each ending in a step so sharp it could have been cut this morning. Nothing has broken yet. There is no scree at the foot of the steps, no gravel, no sand, no soil that wasn't carried here. The surface keeps the texture it was made with, a fine combed ripple like the skin on cooled wax, and in low sun the whole plain shows its grain.

Rain and snowmelt fall on it and have nowhere to go, because no valleys have been cut. Water lies in sheets a finger deep and a hundred kilometres wide, and drifts with the wind, so that a district is dry stone one day and a mirror the next. At night it grows a skin of ice. By mid-morning the ice has gone to plates, then to slush, and the sky is lying on the ground again. Where the water has stood longest, an algae film has turned it the colour of weak tea.

The town is spread across this as if it had been sown. Perhaps forty thousand people live in an area that could hold several million, in buildings that stand a kilometre or more apart. Some are slender and very tall, some long and low, and all of them were drawn up out of the stone they stand on and are the same grey-green. They are faintly warm to the hand. There are no roads between them. People cross in closed vessels that travel a little above the water without disturbing it, or they walk, in boots, with the nearest building small on the horizon and the next one smaller.

The edge of the work is visible from anywhere in town on a clear day. It shows as a pale line to the north where the newest course is going down. The setters hang over it, hill-sized, casting ordinary shadows, with no visible parts in motion. Beneath them the stone simply continues a little farther than it did yesterday. It makes no noise and gives off no heat, only a low mist where cold new rock meets damp air. Nobody in town can fully explain it, including some of the people who operate it.

It is a quiet place. The machinery is silent. With no pebbles for the water to run over, it makes no sound, and without grass or wires the wind is mostly felt and seldom heard. At dawn the ice ticks. Almost everything else is animals.

They came before anyone invited them. The sheetwater hatches small crustaceans by the billion within days of a thaw, from eggs that arrived on birds' feet, and in the short summer midges rise off it in columns that lean with the breeze. Waders work the moving shoreline in lines many kilometres long. Geese come in hundreds of thousands, and when a flock lifts, it is the loudest thing that happens here. Falcons hunt from the tall buildings and from the setters themselves. Foxes den in the warm gaps under foundations, and voles came up in a fodder shipment and stayed. In the dry weeks, long-legged grazers walk up from the escarpment to lick salts off fresh stone and walk back down hungry.

There is little for any of them to eat that isn't in the water. Lichen grows in orange and grey coins, each the size of a thumbnail and a decade from being larger. Moss keeps to the lee of buildings, where warmth leaks out. The cushion plants were seeded in a grid, and the grid has begun to go ragged at the corners, which the botanists count as their first real success.

With no twigs or reeds to build from, the birds nest on bare rock inside rings of shed feathers and shrimp casings. The town leaves out bundles of cut straw in autumn, and they are gone by spring. Geese need grit for their gizzards, and at first they flew two days to the lowlands for it. Now they carry it up and lose it here, and the first natural sediment on the plateau is a scatter of foreign gravel around the roosts.

The plateau makes its own weather. Thin sun on that much shallow water raises cloud by early afternoon, the cloud drops sleet by evening, and the sleet is tomorrow's mirror. The town can turn the worst storms aside, and beyond that it leaves the sky alone.

One rule is taught to every child: no one digs a drain. The water is to find its own way off, however long that takes. Surveyors walk the steps after every thaw looking for the first hairline that carries a current. Four years ago they found one on the southern lip, a groove as long as a forearm with a few grains of loosened stone at its lower end. It has a ring of markers around it now, and people travel out there to see it.
