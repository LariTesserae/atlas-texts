Under the salt flat there is a chamber the size of a valley, and it is as bright as the flat above it. The roof is a crust of salt and selenite a few metres thick, arched from wall to wall without a pillar anywhere. Sun comes through it the way it comes through a paper shade, whitened and without direction, landing on everything equally. Nothing here casts a shadow. The light has never been seen to dim.

There is no soil. The floor is a mat of living stems, moss over liverwort over the wiry rhizomes of ferns, layered to a depth nothing has measured. It is springy underfoot and warm as the flank of an animal. The heat comes from both sides, down through the bright roof and up through the mat. The air sits somewhere above body temperature and does not move.

Out of the mat rise the croziers. They are fern fronds still wound in the bud, furred with rust-coloured scales, and they come in every size. Some are as small as fists and some as large as cartwheels. Toward the middle of the chamber, where the floor dips, the coils stand twenty metres up on stalks as thick as trunks. They grow so close that crossing means shouldering between them, fur against fur. Between them stand horsetail shoots, pale and jointed, with their tips still sheathed. The moss carries capsules with the caps still on. Nothing is in flower or in seed. There is no brown leaf, no litter and no rot. With the light passing through every surface, the green is nearly yellow, the colour of a leaf held up to a window.

From the roof hang pale stems as thin as cord, thousands of them. They reach down toward the damp and stop an arm's length above the tallest coils.

Rock shows only where it is newest. Ribs of black basalt break the mat in a dozen places across the valley. They are glassy, with the ropy skin of the flow still on them, and their edges are sharp enough to cut. No grit or sand lies at their feet, and every fracture is clean. The stone is hot to the hand and it ticks, the way stone ticks while cooling, though it stays as hot as it was. Along the ribs are vents, finger-wide, that hiss a little steam straight upward. The mat stops short of the hot rock in a clean line, and its edge there is dry and slightly crisp.

The steam is the only weather. It rises and beads on the underside of the selenite, then falls back as single drops: one here, and a long while later one far off. There is no stream and no pool. Each coiled crown holds perhaps a cupful in its centre, and the mat takes the rest and stays as damp as a wrung cloth.

The drops are also most of the sound. They land on the fur of the croziers with a soft tap. Behind that are the hiss of the vents and the ticking of the basalt. The mat swallows footsteps completely. From the top of any rib the far wall is visible eight kilometres away, a pale band where salt meets black stone, slightly hazed. The whole green floor lies between, coiled and packed and motionless.

A few things here were made, all of them by hand:

- **The ring:** On the largest rib, cobbles are stacked without mortar into a ring knee-high and wide enough to sit in.
- **The path:** Fourteen flat stones are laid in a line across the mat between two ribs. They have not sunk and are not overgrown, and the moss stops a finger's width from each.
- **The arch:** Near the centre, two young croziers are bent toward each other and tied with a third stem in a plain overhand knot. The knot is still green and no tighter than when it was tied.
- **The handprints:** On a tongue of lava by the eastern wall, six small handprints were pressed into the crust while it was soft. They head toward the wall.

The animals are few. At some vents a pale gecko rests on the rim, its skin thin enough to show the dark of its organs, and now and then it licks a drop from the stone. On the hanging stems there are snails the size of rice grains, clear as the selenite. It is possible to walk a whole day and see neither.

Everything in the chamber has begun and nothing has gone further. The coils stay wound as tightly as they were. When a drop falls, another is already hanging where it formed. The sixth handprint is shallower than the other five, as if the crust had been firming under the hand, and past it the moss begins.
