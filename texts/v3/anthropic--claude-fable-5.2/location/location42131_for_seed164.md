The plateau stands four and a half kilometres above whatever lies under it, and it is wide enough that its far side sits beneath a different part of the sky. The rock is basalt, and new. Lava flows lie where they stopped, their surfaces still ropy and glass-skinned, black with a blue cast where the wet catches the light. Where the flows cracked in cooling they made columns, and the scarps show them in section: six-sided, the edges still sharp enough to open a hand, with no apron of rubble at the foot. Nothing has fallen off yet. The pressure ridges are heaps of tilted slabs whose broken edges would still fit together if pushed.

There are no rivers, and no beds where rivers have been. There are no gullies, no rills, no fans of gravel. Yet the whole tableland is wet. A cloud deck hangs flat-bottomed about three hundred metres up, as level as a ceiling. The air beneath it stays at the dew point, a degree or two above freezing, and every surface carries a skin of beads. The scarps weep in sheets. The hollows in the lava hold clear water a finger deep that neither fills nor drains. Water is always arriving, and it is never allowed to gather and run.

The moss catches most of it. It lies over the basalt in cushions a metre thick, wine-red on the crowns and yellow-green in the dips, and it gives underfoot like a soaked mattress, then slowly rises back. Rosette plants stand out of the moss on trunks sleeved in their own dead leaves. They are taller than a door, and the living leaves at the top are silver, felted, and cupped to hold water. Some carry flower spikes as thick as a thigh, with every floret open. Nothing visits them. There are no flies or beetles, no birds, and no tracks pressed in the moss. The spikes stand open in the cold with their pollen unshed. Tussock grass grows in the drier seams between flows, and pale green liverworts are pasted flat on the lava glass.

Iron takes the water the moss misses. Cast gutters run along the foot of every scarp, half-pipes bolted end to end and furred orange with lichen. They fall on a gradient too gentle to see toward the towns.

The towns are sixty kilometres apart in every direction, and they are the same town. Each has four streets of brick tenements with slate roofs, a square, and a tram loop. At the centre are the works: a boiler hall, an engine hall, a dynamo hall, and six stacks in a row. The boreholes go down to where the young rock is still hot, and steam comes up them for nothing. The engines use it to do one job. They take in the water the gutters bring, flash it, and drive it up the stacks as white vapour, which climbs three hundred metres and merges with the underside of the cloud. The cloud lets it down again as mist. The loop is closed, so no water crosses the ground with enough force to move a grain of rock, and the rock stays as sharp as the day it set.

Everything in the towns is running. The flywheels turn behind tall windows, and belts slap on their pulleys. Arc lamps burn along the streets in full daylight, each with a violet point at its centre and a buzz of its own. The tram goes round its loop with its seats empty and its bell ringing at every stop, and the pole throws a blue spark where the overhead wire crosses. Grass grows between the rails to exactly the height the axle allows. The enamel signs at the stops are white and blank. The tenement doors are shut, the windows are clean, and moss climbs the brick on the weather side to the second-storey sills and stops there. Around each works the waste heat makes a ring of deeper green. Ferns grow under the dripping steam traps, and the moss there is almost emerald. On the windward railings there is a rind of rime two fingers thick that never thickens or thins.

The noise is enormous. Close to the engine hall it comes through the ground, a pounding of twelve strokes to the minute felt in the knees. Above that there are the roar of the stacks, the scream of a relief valve that has been lifting for as long as there has been pressure, and the drone of the dynamos, pitched somewhere near a low B. There is no pause in it and no shift change. Sound cannot escape upward here. The cold layer under the cloud works as a duct, with the deck for a lid and wet rock for a floor, and the sound travels along it for tens of kilometres without thinning much. Moss swallows the high frequencies and the low ones pass through.

In the open country between towns the noise becomes a chord. Any one town is far off, a line of six white plumes on the edge of sight, but three or four can be heard together from different quarters, their engine strokes a fraction apart. The strokes drift into step and out again, so the plateau throbs slowly, with a swell about every eleven seconds that can be watched as a shiver crossing the standing water in the lava hollows. The rosettes stand in this with their cupped leaves full. There is no wind to speak of. The air is thin and cold and tastes of wet iron and hot oil even here, a day's walk from any chimney.

The light is ordinary, the grey-white of an overcast morning, bright enough to read by and too diffuse for shadows. Through the cloud the sun shows as a pale coin about two-thirds of the way up the southern sky. The coin stays in that position.

The plateau ends at the rim without foothills. The moss runs to within a metre of the lip, and the last gutter runs with it, then turns through a cast elbow and heads back inland with its water. Below the lip the cliff is columnar basalt for the whole drop, pale with dust and completely dry, and no waterfall comes off the plateau anywhere along its length. On the last flat rock an arc lamp stands lit on an iron post. The sound of the engines goes out past it over the edge.
