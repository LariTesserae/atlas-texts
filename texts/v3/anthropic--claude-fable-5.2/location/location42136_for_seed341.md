Every street in the lower town tilts toward the same place, and the water goes with the streets. Where they meet there is a paved yard sunk four steps below the last doorsills, perhaps ninety paces across. Brick sheds wall it on three sides, and on the fourth stands the blind back of a boiler house. The town calls it the Sump. All this ground was estuary once, and the Sump is where the estuary is still being argued with.

The floor is blue engineering brick laid in a shallow dish, its joints worn hollow. It is never dry. A film of water a finger deep crosses it constantly toward the grated channels, warm as a bath left standing.

Most of the yard is this open, shining floor. The things that stand on it stand far apart: two beam engines in tall, narrow houses at opposite corners, a round chimney banded with iron, and a travelling crane on rails. Along the east wall runs a row of settling tanks with their lips level to the ground. Between them the wet brick reflects what little light there is.

There is not much. Exhaust steam and the vapour off the tanks rise to about chimney height, meet the cooler air above the roofs, and stay there as a grey-brown lid. Noon under it is the colour of weak tea. Electric lamps in wire cages burn all day on iron brackets, each in a halo of its own damp, each lighting a cartwheel of brick. The heat is boiler heat on top of delta heat, and the walls sweat a rust-coloured liquor from their mortar.

The engines set the terms for everything else. Each one makes a long indrawn suck and then a drop, a blow that comes up through the brick into the shins. They run slightly out of time, so the beat drifts. For a few minutes it is a stumbling four-count, then the two blows close on each other and land together, and every sheet-iron roof in the yard jumps on its nails. Under the beat a dynamo whines in the north shed and a slack belt slaps its pulley. A relief valve on the boiler house lets go without warning in a white shriek. Below the gratings the culverts roar, and after a few minutes that roar seems louder than anything above ground. Speech does not carry here. The oil cups on the engines are always full and a coat hangs on a hook inside the east engine-house door, but the yard is most often crossed by no one.

What grows here grows in thick clumps with bare floor between them:

- **Taro:** It stands along the tank margins with leaves like tabletops, each one tilted and running.
- **Water hyacinth:** It rafts the tanks from wall to wall, and its mauve spikes are the brightest things in the yard.
- **Banana:** A row of it leans against the boiler-house wall where the bricks are too hot to touch, the leaves shredded to ribbons by the valve.
- **Ferns:** They fill every gutter and hang from the downpipes in beards.
- **The fig:** It has its roots through the lowest iron band of the chimney and has started on the second. Another fig splits the end tank and a third lifts the threshold of the east shed. Under the brick all three are one tree.
- **Creeper:** It has taken the crane, which has not travelled in a season and is laced to its rails.

The growth is fast enough to watch. A tendril will cross a hand's width of rail between one rain and the next.

Nothing eats any of it. The hyacinth flowers open and stand open and nothing visits them. Figs drop and lie whole on the brick until they ferment, then run off to the channels as brown wine. No skin of larvae twitches on the tanks, nothing circles the lamps or the chimney, and nothing scrabbles in the culverts. In this heat and wet the place should seethe. Whatever lives in the upper streets stops at the fourth step. Every sound in the yard is iron, steam or water, and it takes a while to notice. The plants get on without help. They spread by runner and offset, and by broken pieces the flow carries off and leaves somewhere to root.

About every forty minutes the lid grows too heavy. It darkens first, so the lamps sharpen and the floor goes black, and then it rains. The rain falls in the yard only, straight down without wind. It is warm and tastes faintly of iron and cylinder oil. On the sheet roofs it makes a new kind of loud, a hiss over the beat. The film on the floor thickens to a visible current, the channels bellow, and the taro leaves duck and spill. It lasts six or seven minutes. Then it stops as if a cock had been turned, the boiler-house roof smokes, the floor smokes, and the lid begins to build again from below.

The town holds that the Sump has only ever pumped the one water. The engines lift it to the outfall main, the boilers burn a share of it into the air to do so, and it comes back.

On the boiler-house wall is an enamel gauge board with a float-driven pointer. The pointer rests between the four and the five, where someone has painted a red line. The line has been repainted so often that it stands off the enamel like a scar. The pointer does not leave it. When one engine is stopped for repacking, the other keeps its pace and the level still holds. By morning the green in the idle corner has put on a foot, and every leaf-tip hangs a bead of water, lets it fall, and hangs another. The taro weeps like this at every dawn in any case. On those mornings the whole corner does, and the drip is steady enough to hear in the gap between the engine strokes. It is hard to say which part of the yard is the machine.

Night changes little. The lid goes from tea to black. The fire-door glow reddens the banana leaves and the lower courses of the chimney, and the lamps keep their haloes. At six the shift whistle blows off the boiler-house roof, a long column of steam going straight up into the ceiling, and the rain comes early.
