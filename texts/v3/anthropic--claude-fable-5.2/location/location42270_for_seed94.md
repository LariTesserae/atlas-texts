**The tableland**

The tableland is as large as a small country, and it sits high enough that clouds arrive at ground level. They don't pass overhead. They come over the western rim each morning as a grey front and cross the plateau at walking pace. The front is combed by everything it touches, and it thins as it goes. By the time it reaches the eastern rim, days of walking away, it has given up most of what it carried. Above it the sky is plain and bright. Under it the light is even and shadowless, good for seeing detail and poor for judging distance.

There is no bare ground. The oldest rock shows only at the rims and in a few low whalebacks, worn round and furred over with lichen. Everything else is plant on top of plant. Cushion mosses are packed tight enough to stand on, sedge grows through the cushions, liverworts fill the spaces between the sedge, and a film of algae covers the liverworts. Under the living layer is peat, many meters of it, laid down at about the thickness of a fingernail each year. It is cold all the way down, and it holds water the way a full sponge does, with no room for more.

**The works**

From any rise the mire shows a pattern. Low ridges of moss run along the contours, hundreds of them, each damming a narrow black pool behind it, stepping down the faint slope like terraces. Nobody made them. Slow water and slow growth sort themselves into ridges and pools over thousands of years. The effect is still of a works: intake at the western rim, storage in the peat, metering through the ridges, discharge at the east. Water moves through it at a set rate, wet season or dry, and the rate hasn't changed in a very long time.

On the ridges stand the rosette plants. They are trunked and taller than a person, each wrapped in a thick sleeve of its own dead leaves. They grow in lines because the ridges are lines. They make heat. At dawn, when frost is still on the pools, the crown of each one is a few degrees warmer than the air and gives off a thin steam, so that for an hour the plateau looks like ranks of small stacks venting. Flies shelter in the warm crowns overnight and carry pollen out with them in the morning. There are almost no bees here. Flies, midges, and a small bird that goes rigid and cold each night and restarts with the sun do the work.

Where groundwater seeps out of the peat, it runs orange. Iron bacteria coat the seeps in rust-colored floc and leave a film on the still pools that looks exactly like spilled oil, with the same rainbow sheen, until it's touched and breaks into angular plates. Under the seeps, hard pans of bog iron are forming, ore laid down grain by grain. The air near them smells faintly of a machine shop.

**Sound and charge**

It is very quiet. The moss takes in sound the way it takes in water. Rain falls into it without a patter. Streams run inside the peat more than on it, and a channel is a slot of dark water sliding between overhanging banks, giving off only an occasional gulp. The hundred waterfalls at the eastern rim throw their noise downward and outward into the lowlands, and none of it comes back up.

What can be heard is close and small: drip from leaf tips, the tearing of sedge in a grazer's teeth, wings. Before the afternoon storms the fog carries a charge, and the pointed tips of the rosette leaves begin to tick, a dry and intermittent sound like a cooling engine. Sometimes there is a dim violet fringe on the tallest crowns. The animals move off the ridges when the ticking starts.

**Animals**

There are many animals, and nearly all of them are quiet. Heavy-coated grazers with wide, splayed hooves move in loose herds of several hundred along the ridge lines. They never crop one place twice in a season, and the pattern of their movement is so regular that their paths have become part of the terracing. Voles tunnel in the cushions in enormous numbers, and from above the moss shows faint seams where the runs are. Long-legged birds stand in the pools. A pale, low-flying raptor quarters the ridges without a wingbeat, and a short-eared fox the color of dead sedge follows the herds for the calves and the afterbirth.

The frogs don't call, because the fog would swallow it. They sit on the ridge tops and raise one white-soled hind foot, slowly, and lower it. In the breeding weeks a whole terrace will be flickering with these small signals in complete silence.

**The shift**

The day runs on a schedule. The first hours are frost and steam. Cloud comes in mid-morning, and the animals that work in clear air change places with the ones that work in fog: the raptor goes down, the wading birds spread out, the midges rise in columns. Afternoon brings the charge, the ticking, and a short hard rain. Evening is a clear hour of long light during which everything feeds at once. Then the cold comes back, the small birds lock up for the night, and the rosettes close their crowns over the flies.

It all leaves at the eastern rim, where the water goes over in white threads that are cold, iron-tinted, and exactly as plentiful as yesterday. Far below, out of sight and out of hearing, there are valleys where things run on that water. From up here nothing shows of them. The plateau has no gate or gauge and no mark of survey. It is in continuous production, and no one runs it.
