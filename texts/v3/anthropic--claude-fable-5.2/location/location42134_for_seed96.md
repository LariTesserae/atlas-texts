The whole shoulder of the mountain has been cut into pans. From the saddle down to where the slope breaks off into cloud there is no ground left that is only ground. Every surface is a basin, a rim, a channel, or a stair between them. The pans are shallow, roughly the span of two outstretched arms, and fitted against each other with no waste, their shared walls a hand's width thick. There are thousands of them across the valley head, climbing both flanks. Each is full almost to the lip, so the mountainside looks plated in bright water.

The walls are pale grey stone, dressed by pecking. The marks are still crisp, small crescent bruises where a hammerstone landed, overlapping in rows. Nothing has rounded yet, and the rims are sharp enough to scrape a palm. The pan floors are lined with yellow-grey clay smoothed by hand. In the newer ones the hands are still visible as long parallel finger-drags, and in a corner there is sometimes a full print pressed deep, as if someone leaned there to stand up. Along some walls the tools sit in rows: hammerstones graded by size, flint wedges, flat stone paddles for tamping clay. They are wet and clean, and nothing has been dropped or left lying crooked.

The water comes out of the mountain itself, at the top of the works, where a cut face twice a person's height runs the width of the valley. The rock there weeps along every seam, not in streams but in a steady sheen that gathers at the foot of the face into the first channel. The water is cold and tastes bitter and faintly metallic, and it leaves white on the lips as it dries. From the channel it enters the top row of pans through notches, each notch plugged with a fitted stone that lets through a thread no thicker than a reed. That is how water moves everywhere here, pan to pan, by threads. Nothing pours. It takes many days for water from the face to reach the middle terraces, and by then it has changed.

In the top rows it is clear over the yellow clay. Lower down it turns milky, then a pale opaque blue. Lower still a crust forms at the edges and grows inward like ice, though it is not ice. It is white and granular, raised in small ridges where the wind has pushed the surface. In the lowest terraces the pans are solid white with a finger of brine on top, and in the very last row before the drop they are dry. The works are sized so that nothing reaches the bottom wall. The water only leaves by going upward.

It does go up. In the morning the whole valley head lies under a low mist that hangs at knee height over the pans and beads on every rim. The stone is always damp, and the stairs, which are narrow and pecked rough for grip, are dark with it. By mid-morning the sun clears the eastern ridge and the mist is gone within minutes. Then the light is hard to bear. It comes from the thin, close sky and again from below, off every pan, so that shadows are short and blue and the undersides of things are lit. The white terraces at the bottom are too bright to look at directly. The air stays cool through all of it, and a hand held over the brine feels the cold coming off.

It is quiet. The saddle blocks most of the wind. What sound there is comes from the threads of water falling a finger's height from notch to pan, thousands of them, too soft singly to hear and together a faint steady hiss. At midday, as the crust warms, the lower terraces make a dry tick now and then as a plate of white splits.

Nothing grows on any of it. The rims carry no green film, the damp joints no dark stain, and nothing skates on the water or drifts in it. The clay is clean. The brine apparently does not allow life. Its absence gives the place a scrubbed look, as if it had been finished yesterday. At the edges that is nearly true, because the outermost pans on both flanks are still bare of crust and their clay is still dark with its first wetting. Beyond them are a few courses of walls with no floors yet, and beyond those the hillside has been stripped and pecked level, waiting to be built on.

The cut face at the top is the same. It is a working face, and the pans are built from what comes off it. Each season it retreats a little into the mountain and a new row is laid in the space it leaves, so the works climb as slowly as the crust thickens. The mountain is being turned, a hand's width at a time, into a vessel for its own water.

Each rim carries a cluster of small pecked dots on its downhill side. They are not a sequence, since neighboring pans may bear four and nineteen. Whatever they count, it is not position. Some of the oldest pans, deep in white, have rims dotted from corner to corner, and the newest marks are still pale and sharp.
