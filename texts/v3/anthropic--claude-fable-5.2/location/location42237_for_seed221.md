The room is inside the crest of a dam that was never finished, a little above three thousand metres, and the only way in is a steel hatch and nine rungs down. The hatch doesn't seal. Grain snow blows through the gap and melts on the top rung, which is always wet in the same place.

The sound reaches you before your boots reach the floor. A single Pelton set fills the middle of the chamber: turbine casing, generator, and a belt-driven exciter on a bracket. It has run at one speed for as long as there has been a room around it. The jet striking the buckets makes a ripping white roar. Under that, the generator holds a fifty-cycle hum that the walls have taken up. The chamber is thirteen metres and a bit from end to end, which fits the hum exactly four times, so the noise stands in the room in slabs. Walking its length, you pass through four bands where the pressure lifts off your eardrums and the roar thins. Between them it comes up through your boot soles into your molars. Speech is impossible anywhere.

Everything here was made, and none of it was completed. The east wall still wears its plywood shuttering, propped with steel and marked in carpenter's pencil with dimensions for a pour that was never ordered. Starter bars stand out of the floor in two rows, waiting for a partition. Scaffold tubes cross the room at head height and again at knee height, so close to the pipe runs and cable trays that you turn sideways to get through. The cables end in taped coils. The turbine casing is in red-oxide primer. The concrete of the west wall is dark and sweating, set but never cured, and it smells of lime as though the mixer left this morning. On a sill lies a spirit level with its bubble centred.

Water is on every surface. The penstock flange weeps a fine spray that hangs in the air as cold fog. The pour joints seep. The ceiling condenses and drips. The floor drain was never cut, so the floor holds four centimetres of water, and the surplus leaves through an empty conduit sleeve whose lip sits at that height. The air is a degree or two above freezing, kept there by the generator's waste heat, and ice forms only as a rim on the hatch frame. A tin mug left under a drip is full to the brim and overflows at the rate it fills.

The hum shows on the water as a fixed crosshatch of ripples that tremble without travelling. Springtails raft on it by the million, a grey scum like cement dust. They have gathered along the lines where the surface holds still, so the pattern of the sound is drawn on the floor in living grey, a lattice with clear black water in each cell.

Eleven bulkhead lamps in wire cages run off the generator, which powers nothing else. They burn under-voltage, their filaments brown-orange, and they light about an arm's length each. Around each one the concrete has a green halo of algae fading to nothing at the edge of the glow. One lamp near the penstock has a moss cushion the size of a thumb and a single flat liverwort. There is a thin green line at the water's edge along the wall. That is all that grows.

The animals have sorted themselves by the noise. Black alpine salamanders lie in the four quiet bands, packed flank to flank and layered two and three deep in the shallow water. They form four glossy stripes across the floor, each the width of a doorway, with bare flooded concrete between. Step over one stripe and the next is three paces on. They do not move aside.

The loud slabs belong to the midges. They hang in upright sheets from the water to the formwork, each sheet as thin as a curtain and as fixed in position. Passing through one feels like passing through cold gauze. Under the lamps they show as a brown shimmer.

Choughs hold the steel. One sits on the cut end of every starter bar, and more line every scaffold tube, shoulder to shoulder, yellow bills all facing the machine. They call constantly, a high rolling whistle pitched well above the turbine, and together they put a second, shriller ceiling on the noise. Their nests are wedged in the cable trays among the taped coils, built of tie wire and rag, and under them the cable sheathing is white with droppings. The ammonia cuts through the lime and the ozone from the exciter brushes. Along the top edge of the shuttering, where the plywood meets the soffit, bats hang in a continuous seam of fur.

The rule of the place is that everything in it has reached the stage just before and stays there. The concrete is set and never hardens. The wall is tied, shuttered and unpoured. Each nest holds eggs that are warm when a bird shifts off them, and there are no chicks and no shells. The salamanders are heavy-bodied and gravid. The midges hold their mating columns and never drop to the water. No animal lies dead on the floor or under the roosts. The room's contents come out the same at every counting, as steady as the water level and the pitch of the hum.

You can pass through without disturbing any of it. A chough shuffles a claw's width along a tube to let a shoulder by, then shuffles back. A salamander nudged by a boot settles into the gap it left. The midge sheets close behind you. The exciter belt has a splice in it that ticks against the guard once per revolution, too fast to count, and the tick can only be felt, with a hand on the bracket.

At the far end, past the fourth stripe, the conduit sleeve passes through a metre and a half of dam. It gives a view the size of a coin, cloud-grey and without depth, and the overflow runs out along its floor in a film as thick as a fingernail. Wind comes in over the water in the sleeve and carries a few flakes with it. They land on the nearest salamanders and are gone at once.
