The ground is a lava flow that hasn't yet learned to be ground. It lies in a saddle between two spatter cones, high enough that the clouds stay below it as a flat white floor. Nothing has dulled it yet. The clinker is stacked like smashed crockery, every edge still an edge, and the surface carries the blue-bronze sheen that fresh basalt keeps for its first few centuries. Threads of volcanic glass lie in the hollows in pale gold drifts, fine as hair. There is no soil, and no water has ever run here. The air is dry enough to tighten the skin of your knuckles, and it is warm. The sun at this height is clear and ordinary, so the warmth isn't coming from it. It comes from below, where the rock is still giving up its heat along a single long seam.

Along that seam, and only there, is the town.

It is perhaps sixty paces end to end and twenty across, and it stops at its edges the way a rug stops. Inside the boundary stand some four hundred towers of grey board, most between knee and shoulder height, with a few taller than a person. They are close enough together that you would have to turn sideways and place each foot with care to pass between them. The makers are pale insects the length of a thumb joint. They chew ash, glass-thread and plant fiber together with a resin of their own and lay the result in thin courses. The board is the color of wasp paper and slightly warm to the touch. Every tower is a chimney, set over a crack in the seam and built to catch the rock's breath, which is hot and carries the faintest trace of vapor. That trace is all the water there is, and the town is the apparatus that keeps it.

The roofs are green. Each tower ends in a cupped crown packed with fat rosettes whose leaves are waxed so heavily they look lacquered. A vine with coin-shaped leaves spills over the rims and crosses from one tower to the next until the upper story of the town is a single continuous canopy. Under it is a second layer of grey felted moss, and under that are roots that run down the inside of the chimney walls to hang in the rising warmth. Against the bare black flow the town looks thickly overgrown, though the plants spend nothing they can avoid spending. They flower at night, small and dull red, and leaves that drop are carried back inside before morning.

The streets follow the cracks. The main one runs straight along the seam, with crooked alleys branching off wherever the rock has split sideways. There are bridges of board at the second and third levels, and galleries, and ramps worn smooth. Traffic is constant and unhurried. The makers go in files along the walls, carrying pellets of ash or beads of the amber syrup that the rosettes sweat at their leaf bases. Flat grey beetles drag out the dead. Mites move over the moss like slow dust.

Ash-skinned geckos press themselves to the warmest walls at the base of the towers, so still that you see the first one only after you have seen the tenth. Small ground finches the color of clinker nest in the old quarters and line their cups with gold glass-hair. They have no song. They signal by lifting one wing to show a white patch underneath, and you see these brief pale flashes passing from roof to roof. Spiders string the upper alleys, and their webs fill with fine ash and hang like grey flannel.

Almost none of this makes a sound. The paper walls and the felt take in whatever noise there is, and the thin air carries very little of it. The moths that come at dusk have fringed wings and arrive like falling lint. With an ear against a tower you can hear a dry, faint rustle of chewing. At evening the flow beyond the boundary sometimes gives a single tick as it cools. Otherwise you hear your own breathing, and after a while that seems rude.

Water here is passed along and never released. The seam breathes it out, the roots take it, and the leaves make syrup. The makers drink the syrup, the geckos eat the makers, and the finches eat the moths and the mites. Every dropping is collected within the hour and walled into a roof garden. Nothing drips, nothing pools, and no dew forms. A stain of damp on a wall would be a kind of wound, and the makers would gather on it and seal it with resin until it was dry again.

The town is also moving, though far too slowly to watch. At the eastern end the seam has gone cold. The towers there stand open and tilting, with their gardens shrunk to grey husks. The finches use them, and the board is starting to fray back into ash. At the western end the rock is still almost too hot to touch, and there the towers are low and new. The very last of them is only a ring of grey paper a finger high around a hairline crack. One rosette no bigger than a lentil has been set in it.
