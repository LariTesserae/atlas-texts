The sea sits on top of a tableland four thousand metres up, and it is as wide as a small continent and only about sixty metres deep. It has no shore of rock or sand, because the basin that holds it is wood. The floor is a single plaited mat of root, pale as ash timber, with its grain running in long sweeps toward the nearest trunk the way floorboards run toward a wall. The rim is a lip of buttress, grey and rounded, alive under its weathered skin. The water lies inside one plant like sap in a cistern.

The water is cool and clear. It is six degrees at the surface and six degrees on the floor, six at the rim and six a thousand kilometres in. There is no thermocline, no cold pocket, and no warmer shallow, since the whole volume is kept turning over so that no part of it can become different from any other part.

The trunks do the turning. They stand several kilometres apart, each one thirty metres thick, rising from the floor straight through the surface and on up into the air as hollow stacks. Between them there is only water, and from the foot of one trunk the next is a faint vertical shadow, with the one beyond that out of sight. Daylight comes down between the surface pads in broad even shafts and lights the floor to an ordinary, slightly green afternoon.

Each trunk wears a girdle of intake slits at mid-depth, lipped with leathery flaps the size of barn doors. Every eleven seconds the trunk takes a stroke. A thud arrives through the chest before the ears, then comes a long rush as water is drawn in and driven up the bore, then the flaps clap shut together. The stroke has always been eleven seconds. But no two trunks keep the same beat, and water carries sound a long way, so the thousands of strokes arrive smeared together as a continuous grinding roar, like a mill heard from inside the wheel-pit. The beat of the nearest trunk sits on top of it. There is also a steady fizz of oxygen leaving the bark in threads, and nearer the trunks a dry crackle, because the plant is electrified. Black rhizomes as thick as a torso run across the floor from trunk to trunk, slick and faintly humming, and the water around them prickles on the skin.

Above the surface the stacks stand forty metres into the thin air and vent vapour. Each plume leans the same way at the same angle in a wind that never shifts, and together they feed a flat, bright overcast that covers the whole sea at one height. Fine rain falls from it everywhere, at one rate. The stacks sound like organ pipes with the stop held down, a low chord of three notes, and the rain hisses under it.

The pads float between the stacks. They are leaves the size of fields, ribbed underneath like the hulls of boats and set far apart on open water, and they catch the rain and drink the light. When the light behind the cloud goes down, the pads come on. They glow from both faces, a plain white with a touch of green, and they bring the sea back to exactly its daytime brightness. From below, the hour cannot be told. The production line under the bark, whatever it makes of light and water, runs without shifts.

No one planted any of this or cut a channel or set a valve. There is not a made thing anywhere on the plateau. The plant has grown itself into a pumphouse, a power grid, a cooling works and a lamp, and all of that effort goes into keeping the sea the same.

So nothing accumulates. The floor has no silt. A dead fish does not reach the bottom, because the return flow has it within the hour and carries it along the grain to an intake, where it goes up the bore and is rendered. A moulted feather goes the same way. The grooves the return flows have worn into the root-mat are shallow and polished, like the hollows in old stair treads, and they have stopped deepening, since the wood replaces itself cell by cell at exactly the rate it wears. The plant does not flower or set seed. It finished growing a long time ago and has no saplings, no dead limbs and nothing young.

The animals live at the trunks, and the water between is nearly empty. Grey fish the length of a hand hold station in front of the intakes by the tens of thousands, noses to the flow, taking what the current delivers and darting sideways half a second before each stroke. They spawn all year, and every size of them is always present in the same proportion. Flat snails with rasping feet work the valve lips and keep the seals clean. They are the only things the flaps never catch. Pale shrimp walk the floor channels in single file. Long brown eels lie along the rhizomes the way cattle lie along a warm wall. They carry a little of the current in themselves and give off a tick of light when touched.

On the pads there are birds, heavy-bodied divers with small wings, nesting in the lee of the stacks in every stage at once, with eggs, chicks and fledglings side by side. They do not call. A voice is useless in this noise, so they signal with a white patch under the wing, opened and shut. The fish cannot be startled by sound either. The thud that would scatter any other shoal passes through them like a pulse through a wrist.

At the rim the buttress wood rises a few metres clear of the water and runs off in both directions past seeing. Beyond it the plateau ends. The plant's outer roots go over the edge and down the escarpment like hawsers, and between them the sea lets go of its surplus, the exact difference between the rain that fell and the vapour that rose. The surplus leaves as a thin unbroken curtain that turns to mist long before it reaches anything. Far below, through gaps in the lower cloud, there is country with storms crossing it and snow coming and going on its hills. None of that weather climbs to the plateau.

The water tastes faintly sweet and resinous, with an edge of tin. A hand held flat against a trunk feels the bore fill and empty, fill and empty, and the bark neither warms nor cools under the palm. The stroke comes every eleven seconds.
