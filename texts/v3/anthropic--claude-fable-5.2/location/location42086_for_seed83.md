The way in is a cast-iron lid in a street, then forty rungs down a shaft of glazed brick. By the tenth rung the air coming up is warmer than breath. By the thirtieth the rungs are warm too, and the daylight from the open lid has shrunk to a coin overhead that lights nothing.

At the bottom is a hall. It is a brick barrel vault about thirty paces long, ten wide, and as tall as a parish church, though it is never seen whole. There is no floor in the ordinary sense and no rock anywhere. There is iron grating in three tiers, and through, above, and between the tiers runs the pipework that fills the hall from end to end. The steam mains are riveted iron, a metre through, wrapped in plaster lagging and canvas and bound with wire. Around them run lesser lines in copper and lead, horseshoe expansion loops, flanges ringed with bolts, drip legs, and valve wheels at every height from ankle to well overhead. The gaps are narrow enough that a person goes sideways, one shoulder leading, and learns the route by hand.

It is hot. On the top tier, under the crown of the vault, the air is baked dry and tastes of scorched dust and hot iron, and a bare railing there will take the skin off a palm. In the absence of light the heat does some of the work of sight. The big mains can be felt on the face from an arm's length away, as large warm presences to the left and right, and a gap between them reads as a slightly cooler stripe. People who worked here walked by their cheeks.

Lower down the hall is damp, and the reason is one pipe. A black-painted main runs the length of the bottom tier, unlagged and cold, and nobody has left a record of what it carries. Every bit of moisture in the hall finds it. It sweats continuously, the beads gathering along its underside and dropping through the grating into the sump below. The sump is a brick trough of blood-warm water, tea-brown with rust, its surface skinned over with a mineral film that wrinkles when a drop lands. That trough, the dew on the black main, and a few weeping flanges are all the water there is. A metre above them, the canvas is as dry as paper.

The hall has been here long enough to finish most of its decaying. Lime leached from the vault mortar hangs in hollow straws as thin as pencil leads, some as long as a forearm, each with a drop at its tip. Where joints once leaked, scale has grown over the leak and closed it in cauliflower crusts, white around the iron, green around the copper, orange where the two meet. The colours are there, unwitnessed. The lagging has slumped on the undersides of the mains like old skin and stopped slumping. Rust has lifted in leaves, and the leaves that were going to fall have fallen and lie in drifts on the lowest grating, soft underfoot. What still leaks seems to be needed. Nothing here seems to be still in the process of breaking.

There is one light. At the gauge board, midway along the middle tier, a bulkhead lamp in a wire cage burns a dim amber filament behind thick glass, and it lights a patch about the size of a tabletop. A few of the gauge dials beside it were painted with luminous numerals that still give off a green so faint it vanishes when looked at directly. These, and the lamp, are the hall's whole supply.

Around the lamp, and nowhere else, things grow. A maidenhair fern is rooted in the damp scale on the nearest flange, with black wire stems and fronds no bigger than a thumbnail, all turned toward the glass. Past the fern is a cushion of moss on the brick, past the moss a flat liverwort, and past that a green film of algae that thins to a stain and stops. The vegetation draws an exact map of how far the lamp reaches, a green circle two arm-spans across, and past its edge there is only brick. Elsewhere only white fungal threads grow, in the rotted canvas and in one forgotten plank, and they need no light.

Animals are few. Pale crickets with antennae twice their body length live in the lagging, and an hour might pass before one is felt crossing a hand. Springtails dot the film on the sump. A gecko, or a succession of geckos, keeps to the lamp cage and takes whatever the light draws, which is not much. It is nearly translucent, and its toes leave small clean prints in the dust on the gauge glass.

The hall is close to silent. The metal ticks irregularly as it takes up or gives back heat. The drops from the black main fall at intervals long enough to lose count between. Something hums below hearing, more a feeling in the grating through the boot soles than a sound. About every forty minutes a steam trap on the lowest tier opens with a clack, lets out one short wet exhalation, and shuts. That is the loudest event there is, and afterward the quiet seems thicker for a while.

Everything else moves too slowly to catch. The mains rest on iron rollers, and as the load above shifts with the seasons they lengthen and shorten by about a hand's width. Each roller has polished a bright strip on its track, the only clean metal in the place. The straws grow perhaps a finger-joint in a year. The valve wheels creep too. Each cycle of warming and cooling walks a spindle a hair's breadth one way and not quite all the way back, and over decades the wheels have turned themselves, unattended, into settings no engineer chose. The pressures agree with them. The needles on the gauge board stand where they have stood for years, each resting against a faint line it has worn into its own dial.
