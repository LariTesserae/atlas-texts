The roof came down recently, and the walls still shed rock most days. What used to be a cavern is now a basin four kilometres across, sunk two hundred metres below a plain that gives no sign of it until the edge. Three spans of the old ceiling survive as arches, each wide enough to shade a village. Everywhere else the sky is open, and daylight falls straight in and bounces off pale rock, so the floor is as bright as any field at noon. Steam softens the light, and for most of the morning nothing casts a hard shadow.

The floor is unfinished. Hot water comes up through it in hundreds of places, carrying dissolved stone, and lays that stone down again the moment it cools. Rims of white and rust-orange travertine rise around every seep, a finger's width in a week. Dams build, pond, overtop and are abandoned when the vent that fed them chokes on its own deposit and breaks out thirty paces away. Between the terraces lie flats of grey mud that domes, bursts and resettles, and stretches of dry, hot crust that rings underfoot because nothing has yet filled in beneath it. No stream has cut a channel here and no stone is rounded. Everything has a fresh edge or a wet skin.

The basin has no river and no lake. The water comes as films, knee-deep pools, seeps down the walls, and a warm rain the place makes for itself. Steam climbs to the rim, meets the cooler air of the plain, and falls back in fat, lukewarm drops that smell faintly of struck flint and eggs. The air stays at about the temperature of breath, and the ground is warmer than that.

Nothing rooted lives here and nothing green stands up. The closest thing to plant life is a set of slicks, ochre, black-green and a liverish pink, that coat the wet rock wherever the water is the right heat. They eat the minerals and the light, and everything else in the basin eats them or eats their eaters.

The animals are too many to count. Flies lie along every pool margin in a band so thick it looks like a second shoreline, a dark felt that parts around a foot and closes behind it. Their grubs pack the shallows. Pale, many-legged things the size of a thumb, soft-shelled and blind, graze the slicks in herds that move across the terraces like poured grain. In the warmer pools smaller swimmers swarm so densely that the water is red and seems to granulate. Flat grey crawlers that hunt the flies plate the walls. They overlap like roof tiles and flinch all together when a shadow crosses.

The air above is just as crowded. Sickle-winged birds nest in the mud of the arch undersides in their tens of thousands and work the fly-bands in shifts, skimming so low their breasts are stained ochre. Under the largest arch, where the old dark survives, something leathery roosts in a mass that drips. At dusk it comes out as a column that takes most of an hour to pass. Long-legged waders stalk the red pools. Things with jaws wait in the mud.

The noise never stops. Under it all is the hiss of vents and the slow, fat percussion of the mud. Over that lies the fly-drone, which is steady enough to stop hearing until a pool goes scalding and a whole shoreline lifts at once with a sound like tearing cloth. The grazers click. The birds shriek in the arches, and the arches return it doubled. Several times a day a slab leaves the wall with a crack and a long, rattling settle, and for a breath afterward every animal in the basin is silent. Then it all starts again.

Nothing stays where it was. A pool that was grazing-warm at dawn is boiling by noon, and its swimmers are cooked to a pink scum that the birds fight over. A terrace drains through a new crack, and ten thousand pale grazers are left on drying stone, and the wall-crawlers come down in a sheet. A fissure has been creeping across the eastern floor at a walking pace of perhaps a stride a month, breathing heat, and the whole pattern of pools is slowly reorganising around it. The swarms follow the heat, the birds follow the swarms, and the guano falls where the birds are thickest. The guano feeds the slicks, and the slicks decide where the stone will grow next.

The animals are building the place as much as the water is. The grazers moult constantly, and their cast shells drift against the rims and are cemented in overnight. The dams here are half mineral and half husk. A piece broken off shows them layered like pages, along with fly cases, bird bones and whole wall-crawlers caught mid-stride. They are fossils a season old, in rock still warm from being made.

People come down, though not many, and they do not stay. A stair of stacked slabs descends the western talus, and it is relaid after every fall. On the floor there are a few windbreaks of piled sinter, roofed with hide stretched over long bones because there is no wood within three days' walk. They have shallow fired-clay pans for drying out the bitter white salt, bone rakes with close-set teeth for skimming fly grubs off the shallows, and skin bags to carry the grubs up, dried and rattling. They mark safe crust with cairns, and beside each cairn they leave a row of pebbles, adding one each visit. A cairn with many pebbles is not to be trusted, because the ground has had time to change beneath it.

Whatever they leave behind, the basin keeps. A rake forgotten at a pool's edge is furred white in ten days and part of the rim in forty. The oldest windbreak has no door any more. A terrace has grown through it, and a clay pan stands glazed into the floor, brim-full of red water and swimmers.

Along the foot of the northern wall there is a line of handprints, pressed into soft sinter by people who came before the ones who come now. The oldest are nearly filled, shallow as thumbprints in wax. The newest still holds a little warm water, with flies around its edge.
