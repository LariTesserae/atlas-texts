The ground here came out of the earth recently and nothing has worked on it since. It is a plain of basalt a few miles across, lying barely above the tide between a long black slope and the sea, and its surface still shows how it moved: ropes, coils, and lobes that slumped over one another and stopped. The glassy skin is intact, blue-black with an oily iridescence where the light catches it. There is no sand, no gravel, no soil. Edges that formed sharp are still sharp, and hide sandals last about a month.

None of that is what a person notices first. The first thing is the noise, which arrives well before the town is visible through the steam and does not let up at any hour.

There are three layers to it. The lowest is the big vent at the inland edge of the plain, a throat in the rock that blows steam at one pitch, a flat tearing roar felt in the sternum. Over that come the blowholes along the shore, where each swell is driven into the lava tubes and thumps out through the roof, irregular and heavy. Over everything are the terns. Hundreds of thousands of them nest on the plain and the town, a body's width apart, every one screaming at its neighbors, its mate, and the frigatebirds that hang overhead waiting to rob them. The sound has no gaps in it. At arm's length a shout is only a moving mouth.

So nobody shouts. The people of the town speak with their hands, a full and rapid language of fingers, wrists, and chin, and they get someone's attention by touching a shoulder. Across a lane they signal by knocking stone on stone, which carries through the walls as a tremor more than a sound. Every child has a hand-name, given first. The spoken name is given later and used only indoors, mouth against ear.

The steam rises from the vent field and from hairline cracks all over the plain, flattens against the cooler air above, and stays there as a lid. Under it the light is grey-brown and shadowless. At noon the sun is a pale smear, and at night the lamps are stone bowls of bird oil with a sinew wick that give a small yellow flame and a rancid smell. The air is as warm as breath and the rock is warmer. People sleep directly on their floors.

Everything is wet. The steam condenses on any surface cooler than itself, so walls weep, lintels drip, and hair is never dry. The water collects in the natural hollows of the flow, which the town has sealed with plaster and roofed with slabs to keep the droppings out. It tastes of sulfur and iron. At the rims of the open pools there are orange and dull green mats of slime, slick as fat, and these are the only growing things on the plain other than the kelp offshore. Seawater also sits close under the surface. At high tide it rises in the street cracks, and warm brackish water stands ankle-deep in the lowest lanes until the ebb.

The town fills the middle of the plain and houses several thousand people in a space that would suit a few hundred. The houses are slabs pried from the flow with bone wedges and hammerstones, stacked without mortar and leaning on each other, with newer rooms built on the roofs of older ones three and four deep. The lanes are shoulder-wide. Rafters are whale ribs and door-hangings are sealskin. Every outer wall is plastered white with a paste of burnt shell and guano, partly to seal the gaps and partly to give the dim light something to reflect from, so the town shows from a distance as a pale lump on the black.

Every roof is built stepped on purpose, ledge above ledge, because the birds are the harvest. Terns nest on the ledges, the sills, and the tops of the cistern covers, and boobies stand in the lanes and have to be walked around. The whole town smells of ammonia and fish.

The birds are only the most numerous of the animals:

- **Crabs:** Red land crabs the size of a hand work through the streets in the thousands, eating broken eggs, dead chicks, and anything dropped. They are the only street-cleaning there is.
- **Skinks:** They lie flat on the warmest stones near the cracks.
- **Flies:** They sit in a haze over the guano heaps.
- **Petrels:** They nest in the tube mouths and collapsed skylights and come in after dark, churring from underground, so the night has its own shift of noise under the terns.
- **Seals:** They haul out on the one smooth tongue of lava that serves as a boat ramp, barking, and are tolerated until one is needed.

Tools are stone, bone, and skin. Along the flow's margin, where the lava met the sea and chilled fast, there is a seam of good black glass, and the knappers sit there all day striking blades. In that din their work is felt in the wrist and never heard. Cord is sinew or twisted kelp. Boats are hide stretched over rib frames. Driftwood is rare enough that a straight piece is kept in the family. Nobody needs fire for cooking: every household has a claim on a small fumarole, and eggs and fish are steamed over it in gut bags.

There is no ground to bury anyone in. The dead are carried to a particular skylight on the seaward side and lowered into the tube, where the tide and the crabs take them.

The strangest thing about the plain is that it holds still. The rock is new, but nothing here ages it: there is no frost, no river, and no root to open a crack. The only wear is a slight dulling of the glass along the most-used lanes. The vent does not surge or fail, and the cloud does not lift. The warmth is the same at every hour, and the latitude has no seasons. The terns breed on a cycle of their own that ignores the year, colony overlapping colony, so there are always eggs, always chicks, and always fledglings at once.

The people have a hand-sign for the tide and one for a person's age, but none for a time of year. Their histories do not run in sequence. They list who built on whose roof.
