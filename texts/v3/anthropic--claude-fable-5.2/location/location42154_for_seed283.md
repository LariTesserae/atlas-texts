**The Basin at Night, Late in the Dry Season**

The heat does not leave when the sun does. It moves into the ground and the trunks and comes back out of them all night, so that at two in the morning the air under the canopy is still the temperature of skin, and bark is warm to the palm. There is no moon. A lid of haze has sat over the lowland for weeks and takes the stars. Under the trees the dark is complete, and the place has to be known by smell, by touch, and by the small sounds that carry in still air.

The basin is an old floodplain, a day's walk across, with a low ridge on either side that the river finished cutting long ago. Everything here has had time to settle into its shape. The terraces are rounded off, the oxbows silted in and grown over, and the levees have worn down to long swells you notice only in your calves. The soil is a fine red-brown alluvium, hard as fired clay in this season and cracked into plates. Over it lies a hand's depth of fallen leaves, because the forest here drops half its canopy when the rains stop. The other half holds, and it is enough: figs, thick-leaved evergreens, and lianas as thick as a thigh, looped from crown to crown. Beneath them is a thorny understory grown so close that the only ways through are the ones bodies have worn.

Water is here, but not much, and it is spoken for. The river is a wide bed of sand with a chain of pools in it, each one smaller this week than last. Before dawn, dew forms on the leaf litter and is gone an hour later. The rest is held inside things: in swollen trunks, in tubers, in the mud-sealed chambers where frogs wait out the months without a sound.

The animals are everywhere. The litter is in constant motion, and that is the basin's main sound, a dry, fine ticking with no source and no gap in it, the sound of termites by the million cutting dead leaf under cover of the dark. Stand still and it seems to come from the whole floor at once. Ants run every stem, and a hand set on a liana comes away crawling. Columns of them cross the animal paths in ropes several bodies thick, carrying leaf fragments and seeds and each other down to chambers far below the hardpan. There the air is cool and wet, and fungus is tended in the dark on a scale no one has counted.

Nothing in this basin was made by hands. It has no post, no cut stump, no track wider than a pig. The only large-scale work is done by the insects, and it runs in shifts without stopping. Whole trees are taken apart in a season. The year's leaf fall is taken underground and turned into soil.

Above the floor the air is just as full. Moths bump the face and the lips. Bats come through the gaps in such numbers that you feel them more than hear them, a puff of displaced air at the cheek and then another, and their voices are pitched above anything an ear can use. At a fruiting fig the larger bats are audible: a leathery shuffling, a dropped fruit, a brief squabble that ends quickly. Geckos sit on every trunk. A nightjar calls somewhere out on the sand, a few notes, then leaves it. The cicadas gave up at dusk. It is too dry for frogs to call. For a place this crowded, the basin is very quiet. The quiet is made of ten thousand sounds, each too small to matter alone.

The pools are where the crowding is hardest to believe. Each is the leftover of a river's worth of life. Fish lie packed back to back in water as warm as blood and thick with their own stirring, and at the margins crocodiles lie side by side like stacked timber, not hunting, barely breathing, waiting for the season to turn. Now and then one shifts and the whole pool slops once and stills.

The paths converge here. Deer come down in the dark and drink with their feet set carefully. Pigs come after them, and something heavier after the pigs. They all use the same worn chutes through the bank, and arrive at intervals, as if by arrangement. The mud at the edge is trodden to a paste and smells of musk, algae, and old fish.

Only two things come in from outside. Along the eastern rim, the underside of the haze has a faint, steady, dull orange stain, where a gas flare burns beyond the ridge. It is too weak to see by. From the sandbars it is enough to tell where the sky stops, and the moths at the top of the canopy drift toward it. And once each night, well after midnight, a freight train crosses the far plain. It reaches the basin as a long low pressure in the chest that goes on for minutes and then fades. The ticking in the leaves does not pause for it.

Change here is slow and runs one way until it reverses. The pools lose a finger's width a night. The cracks in the clay widen. The leaf litter thins from below as it is carried down. Somewhere past the ridge, weeks off, the air is thickening toward the first storm, and when that comes the sand will be river again, the frogs will dig out, and for a few nights the basin will be loud. Until then it stays as it is: hot, dark, and occupied on every surface.
