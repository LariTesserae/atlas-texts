You hear it before you see it. On the flat ground there is only a low brick parapet enclosing a rectangle forty paces long and twenty-five wide, and out of that rectangle comes a roar of sparrows, mallets, querns, pigeons and goat bells, and under it all a hum that seems to have no source. Then you reach the edge and look down eight body-lengths into the yard.

The stair runs down the long north wall. Every tread has been worn to a scoop, the scoop patched level with tile, and the tile worn to a scoop again. The handhold is a groove in the brick at hip height, polished dark by palms.

The walls are made of pots. Between limewashed brick piers, jars are laid on their sides in courses, mouths outward and bedded in mud mortar, thousands of them from floor to rim. Near the bottom they are big-bellied storage jars, their lips rubbed away by shoulders, baskets and goat flanks until the mouths are only smooth holes in a smooth wall. Higher up the jars get smaller and redder, and the rims are still sharp. The top course is this year's.

Every mouth belongs to something. Sparrows have most of the middle courses, and their noise never stops; it only thickens and thins. Pigeons take the larger jars on the south wall, where people scrape out the dung with a wooden paddle each new moon and carry it to the kiln in baskets. Mason bees have sealed a scatter of mouths with mud plugs, pale against the dark clay. Geckos sit in the rims like stoppers. Bats hang packed in the upper jars of the north wall, which the sun never touches, and the brick beneath them is streaked brown. In the lowest course, where the wall stays damp, toads sit in the jar bellies for months with their eyes shut. A rat snake as long as a man works the east wall from mouth to mouth, and no one stops it. The sparrows mark its progress with a moving patch of alarm.

The floor is potsherds set on edge in herringbone and packed tight. It clinks underfoot. Under it lies an older sherd floor, and under that another. Whoever digs here, for a post or a drain or a grave for a dog, brings up only floor. No one in the yard has reached earth, and no one expects to. The rooms of the lowest tier have doorways at chest height, and you enter them bent over. Lower still, the lintels of older doorways lie flush with the paving and serve as thresholds for nothing. The yard is filling with its own breakage at about a finger's width a generation, and each year a new course of jars goes onto the rim to keep the depth. The whole place is travelling upward, very slowly, through itself.

People live behind the jar walls in three tiers of rooms, reached by palm-trunk galleries lashed with rope that has to be replaced every few years. Reed mats are slung across the galleries, and the light comes through them in stripes. The middle of the yard is open to the sky. For about two hours around noon the sun stands straight down the shaft and the floor is too bright to look at. The rest of the day the light is bounced off limewash and comes from no particular direction.

At the east end stands the kiln, a brick beehive twice a man's height and black around its stoke-hole. It is fed thorn, chaff and dung cake. It fires every six or seven days, and for a night and a day that end of the yard is hard to stand in. The brick takes in the heat and gives it back for days, and nights at the bottom are never cool.

In the southwest corner, where the floor dips, is the cistern, a stepped tank plastered with lime and gone green below the waterline. Half-tile gutters run down every pier to feed it. Beside it are three shallow settling pans in which clay brought down from above in baskets is slaked and stirred with a paddle. The slip is grey and skins over in the heat. This corner and the sweating jars are the only places with water, and what grows in the yard grows here. A fig is rooted in the cistern's lip, and its trunk has taken the shape of the brickwork. Capers hang out of mortar joints on the west wall. A gourd vine has been trained along one gallery rail, and a row of cracked jars holds onions. The north wall carries a band of moss as wide as a hand along the seep line.

The noise is made of many things. Clay is wedged by foot on a stone slab and then beaten with wooden mallets. Four or five querns are always turning. The pots are built by hand, coil on coil, on a turntable pushed round with the toes, and paddled thin against a stone anvil with a quick patter. The goats on the lowest tier complain, and children run on the sherd floor. Over all of it the wind crosses the open top and sounds the jars. Each mouth hums when air moves past it, the small upper jars high and thin, the old big ones lower down so deep that you feel them in the chest more than you hear them. Everyone in the yard can tell the wind's direction by the chord. A particular sour note from the northwest means dust is coming, and the women take the drying pots in off the floor.

No one shouts, because it would do no good. Talk is done at arm's length with the mouth near the other person's ear, or with the hands. To call across the yard, each household has a jar hung by its door and a stick. Each jar has its own pitch, and a child knows its mother's before it can walk. A cracked jar goes dull and is taken down the same day.

At dawn the insides of the wall jars are beaded with condensation, and the lowest mouths drip. The pigeons leave all together, and the clap of their wings bounces off the four walls. The morning belongs to mallets and querns. At noon the geckos come out and lie flat in the glare, and the sparrows go nearly quiet. In the afternoon a kestrel usually comes. It has learned the yard, and it drops past the rim with its wings shut. For one breath every bird in the place is silent, and then the alarm goes up louder than before. Sometimes the kestrel rises again with something in its foot. At dusk swifts scream round the shaft in a tight ring, and when they leave, the bats pour out of the north wall like smoke. Lamps are lit, wicks floating in fat in sherd dishes, and moths come down to them. The geckos gather round each flame and eat.

Rain comes a few times a year. The mats drum and the gutters run brown and then clear. The cistern climbs a step. The toads in the bottom course wake and come out onto the wet floor by the hundred, and for a night and a day they are louder than everything else in the yard combined. Nobody strikes a call-jar until they are done.

By the time the mortar on a new rim course is dry enough to touch, a sparrow is already carrying straw into one of the jars.
