The valley sits at around two thousand metres, in the belt where the cloud lives, and every part of it has been laid by hand.

**Walls and soil.** The slopes are dry-stone retaining walls, each a metre or two high, stepped in their thousands from the river to the ridge roads. The oldest have stood a hundred and fifty years. Their stones are rounded at the edges and black with algae, and the walls belly outward a little and hold. The soil behind them was carried up in baskets and has been manured, mulched and turned for so long that it is as much a product as the leaf. Bedrock shows nowhere. Where it might, someone has built a drain across it or a shed against it.

**Water.** It does not rain here so much as condense. Walls weep and roofs bead and the air deposits itself on sleeves. All of that water is given a route.

- Each terrace has a back drain, which empties into a stepped stone chute.
- The chutes join lined channels, and the channels feed timber and concrete flumes that run along the contour to the head tanks.
- From the tanks, riveted steel penstocks drop straight down the fall line to the powerhouses on the valley floor. They are mossed along their upper sides.
- The river itself runs between masonry training walls, with a weir every hundred metres.

Men with hooked poles lift and drop sluice boards all day, shifting flow from one channel to another as the squalls move the load around the valley.

**The green.** The tea is pruned waist-high into one continuous table that follows every contour, so the slopes look upholstered. Silver oak stands through it in ranks for shade, lopped each year. Lemongrass is planted along the wall lips to bind them.

Then there is what nobody planted. Moss grows a hand thick on the north faces, ferns hang from the weep-holes, and liverwort covers the penstock saddles. A green film coats the corrugated roofs and climbs the ropeway trestles as far as the grease. The wild growth lives on the built things because there is nothing else to live on.

**Buildings.** The factories are four and five storeys of iron frame and painted sheet. They are set into the slope so that the top floor opens onto one road and the bottom floor onto another. Their upper storeys are all louvres, behind which the leaf lies withering on racks. Between them are wedged the long workers' lines with shared verandas, the workshops, the firewood stacks under tin, the co-operative store, and a small temple with a wet flag.

Roof meets wall meets drain meets bush with no slack anywhere. The paths are the wall tops, forty centimetres wide, and the stairs are the edges of the chutes.

**Motion.** None of it is still. The cloud comes up from the plains through the valley mouth in torn pieces, first at walking pace and then at a run. Visibility goes from three kilometres to ten metres and back in the time it takes to climb one flight. The rain comes in pulses, a dozen starts and stops in an hour, and the valley answers each one.

- Louvres along the factory faces tilt open and shut, a storey at a time, following the hygrometer.
- Tarpaulins are dragged over the leaf-weighing sheds and dragged off again.
- Six aerial ropeways converge on the factories and never stop. Sacks of green leaf swing in, firewood and briquettes go up, and chests of made tea go out over the pass, the buckets vanishing into the grey one after another.
- Hundreds of pluckers in plastic capes cross the table in slow diagonal lines.
- Somewhere a gang is always rebuilding a length of wall that slumped in the night, passing stones hand to hand.

When the cloud opens, the opposite slope shows the same thing mirrored, a whole hillside seething at close work.

**Sound.** It is almost silent. The cloud takes the sound, and the moss takes what the cloud leaves. The rollers, dryers and sorting machines turn behind thick walls on electric motors, and they come through as a hum in the boot soles on the factory road. The ropeway makes a tick at each trestle as a grip passes over the sheaves, then a patter of drops shaken from the cable. Plucking is a small tearing, two leaves and a bud, multiplied until it sounds like light rain. The estate lorries crawl in low gear and do not use their horns. Twice a day a shift siren sounds, and in the cloud it seems to come from the whole valley at once. Then the dripping resumes.

**Smell.** The smells carry further than the sounds. Withering leaf is green and faintly like cut apples. The fermenting floors give off something coppery and malty. The firing rooms smell of toast, and under that are woodsmoke and wet iron.

**Animals.** There are few. The table is too closed and too well tended to shelter much.

- A whistling thrush works one of the flumes at dawn.
- Dogs sleep under the dryer vents, where the warm air spills out.
- There are leeches in the back drains.
- A fringe of old forest is kept along the ridgeline because it holds the cloud and feeds the head tanks. Now and then a barking deer comes down from it at night and is caught in lorry lights, standing on a wall top.

**Light.** In the wet months the sun is never seen as a disc. Day is an even grey-green glow that casts no shadow, darker under the silver oaks. Bulbs in wire cages burn in the factories from morning, so the lofts show as ruled yellow lines through the louvres. By half past five the far slope is a constellation of factory tiers, veranda lamps and the powerhouse yard at the bottom, doubled in the tailrace.

**Rules.** Everyone knows them.

1. Water goes where it is sent, and no drain is left blocked overnight.
2. Leaf reaches the loft within a few hours of leaving the bush.
3. On the wall tops, anyone unloaded steps down into the tea to let a load pass.
4. A wall that starts to bulge gets a stake with a rag on it, and is taken down and relaid before the next heavy fall.
5. The ropeway stops only for a man on the line.

The valley has kept its shape for a century and a half only because so little in it is left alone for an hour. Late at night the louvres close storey by storey as the cloud leans in against the lofts. The cable goes on ticking over the trestles, carrying empty buckets down into the dark.
