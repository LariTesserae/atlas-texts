From below it looks like a small cloud that has snagged on the mountain's shoulder and failed to blow away. The wind up here is constant and cold. It comes across the lava field with nothing to slow it, since the flow is only a few decades old and still black, glassy and sharp enough to open a boot sole. The cloud sits in that wind without leaning, and its edges are too clean.

Closer, it is not a cloud. It is water sliding down a surface that isn't there. A dome perhaps a hundred paces long and twelve metres high at its crown is drawn in rivulets that braid, part and rejoin on their way down the empty air. Where they reach the ground they feed a ring of moss so green against the basalt that it looks painted on. Through the running water the interior is a blur of darker green and pale light.

Stepping through takes one stride and soaks you. The wind stops in the middle of its own noise. Inside is the heat of a lowland swamp at noon, heavy and saturated, hotter than blood.

The heat is the first rule of the place, and it has no source. The rock is exactly as hot as the air, and the pools are exactly as hot as the rock. Put a hand into standing water and there is no change of temperature at all, only weight and a slight resistance. Nothing steams, because nothing is warmer than anything else. The only cold thing is the boundary, and it is from the boundary that all the water comes. The inner air gives up its moisture there and it runs down the inside of the dome, across the rock in thin sheets, and into the cracks. From there it evaporates back into the air and returns to the boundary.

The ground is young and has not been softened. The ropy lava folds still have their knife creases, and blisters in the flow have collapsed into bowls with rims like broken bottles. Soil exists only where plants have made it, so the growth is abrupt and scattered. A crack is packed solid with ferns, then there are three metres of bare wet rock. A bowl is filled with broad leaves that are red underneath, each as wide as a serving dish, and then there is bare rock again. A climbing plant with nothing to climb has spread flat across a slab. Near the back stands a single tree fern, two metres tall, its trunk furred and dripping. None of these belong at this altitude. Nobody has recorded how they arrived.

There are five objects inside. Each is waist-high and shaped like a river cobble stood on end, pale grey. They are the only dry things in the place. Water will not sit on them, and the moss stops a finger's width short of their bases in a neat margin. Touching one is difficult to describe afterward. It feels neither warm nor cool, neither rough nor smooth, and the hand reports almost nothing. Their shadows are wrong. Whatever the hour and wherever the sun stands behind the running veil, all five shadows lie in the same direction, uphill and a little north.

That is the direction the whole place is travelling.

It moves a few paces a year. No one has seen the stones move. They leave no drag marks, but the dome is always centred on them, and each season it stands a little higher on the slope. So the garden has a front and a back. At the leading edge the rock has only just come inside, and it carries a film of algae and the first flat liverworts. In the middle, ferns and red-backed leaves grow thick over the first black soil. At the rear grows whatever has been inside longest, the tallest and oldest plants. The tree fern stood at the centre once. It is now eleven paces from the veil.

When the veil passes over a plant, the plant dies in one night. Downslope a long wake records this, a strip of thin dark soil the width of the dome, bristling with frost-blackened stalks. Farther down the stalks have fallen, and tough alpine grass has taken root in soil it could not have made for itself in a thousand years. Three grey stones lie in the wake as well, far apart. They are cracked and dull, wet with ordinary rain and cold as any rock. Evidently there were once eight.

Someone has been keeping track. Along the wake there are small stacks of three flat lava plates, each marking where the rear edge stood on some particular visit. There are fourteen of them, unevenly spaced. Inside, near the middle, there is a bench of piled clinker with a flat slab on top, placed where a person could sit and watch the front. Moss has covered half of it. The bench is also drifting toward the back.

It is quiet. The veil lets no wind through, and the constant dripping settles into a soft background that the ear soon stops hearing. Now and then the new rock ticks as it settles. A clap of the hands comes back from the dome late and pitched slightly lower than it left.

The animals are few. A frog the colour of wet basalt, small enough to sit on a thumbnail, gives a single dry note at dusk, and sometimes another answers. They lay in the bowls nearest the front, where the water will stay inside longest. Gnats hang over the pools, and the frogs eat them. In the morning a large black bird of the cold slopes walks in through the veil at the rear, shaking water off itself and plainly disliking the heat. It picks along the margin for frogs that settled too far back in the night and now lie stiff on the frost a hand's breadth outside. It eats, drinks once, and walks out again.

At night the outer air drops far below freezing and the veil thickens until it is opaque. The water comes down the inside so heavily that it leaves the surface and falls as slow, heavy rain, as hot as the air it falls through. By midday the flow thins to threads, and the mountain shows through in pieces: the black flow, the summit ridge a few hundred metres above, and the white beyond it. The five shadows point toward that ridge.

At the very front a liverwort no bigger than a coin has fixed itself to an edge of glass that could still cut skin. It was not there last month.
