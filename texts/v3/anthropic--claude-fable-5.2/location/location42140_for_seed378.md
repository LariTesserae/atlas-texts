The plateau sits high enough that water boils early, and it boils everywhere. It rises through the white ground in seeps, vents, and slow domed springs, spreads across the flats in a film one finger deep, and stands in the air as steam that never fully clears. The sun comes through thin atmosphere with very little to stop it. It hits the sinter, the salt crust, and the standing water, and comes back up from below, so everything here is lit from two directions and shadows are faint and short. The heat works the same way. It comes down from the sky and up through the boot soles. The nights do not cool much, because the ground gives back what it has held.

Nothing grows here. There is no soil, only silica laid down in scallops and terraces over a very long time, rounded now, the old rims worn soft where water has run over them for centuries. The colour comes from microbial film in the runoff channels, rust, ochre, and a green so dark it reads as black. It lies in bands, each one following a temperature. The bands are the nearest thing to a field.

The town is spread thin across this. A condenser hall stands by itself on stilts, then nothing but a quarter mile of bright water, then a row of pan-houses with their shutters propped, then a pump shed, then a long gap. The ground will not bear weight everywhere and the springs do not stay put, so nobody builds close. Pipe trunks connect it all, lagged in cloth and plastered white, running on trestles at head height, dripping steadily from every joint. Plank walkways run beneath them. The oldest pipes no longer need trestles. The sinter has grown up and over them, and they pass through low white ridges like bones through a healed limb.

The works draw brine from the hot ground, run it through evaporating pans, and take off salts in grey and white crusts that are raked, bagged, and sent down the escarpment on a cable tramway. Turbines turn on low-pressure steam. There is electric light, though by day it would be invisible.

It is quiet in a way that seems wrong for a place with engines. The vents are flared wide so they breathe rather than whistle. The tram runs on wet cable over greased sheaves. The air is thin, the steam is thick, and between them sound travels a few yards and stops. A dropped wrench makes a noise like one dropped in another room. People here gave up on bells long ago and signal with painted shutters on the hall roofs, which open and close in sequences. They talk with their hands while working and keep their voices low at other times, out of habit more than rule. What can be heard is dripping, the occasional soft collapse of a bubble in a mud pot, and the turbines, which register through the planks more than through the ear.

The animals are quiet too, and there are a great many of them. Flies live on the microbial film in such numbers that the edges of the channels look like dark felt, and the felt shifts when a shadow passes. Small red shrimp fill the cooler pans until the water turns the colour of brick. Pale wading birds stand in the shallows by the thousand, legs stained orange to the knee by iron, heads down, sieving. Thin lizards the colour of plaster lie along the pipe lagging in rows, all facing the same way. Something larger comes up over the plateau rim at dusk to lick the salt crust, long-necked and narrow, in groups of five or six. They don't call or squabble. The birds lift off without a cry and their wings make a sound like cloth being shaken out some distance off.

Each creature keeps to its own degree of heat. The flies hold one line and the shrimp another. The birds stand where the water is exactly as warm as they like it and no warmer, so they form long curved ranks that trace the temperature of the ground like contours on a chart. The lizards mark which pipes are running hot. The workers read all of this without thinking about it. When a rank of birds has bent inward overnight, a spring is failing. A stretch of channel bare of flies means something new is coming up underneath, and the walkway there gets moved before the week is out. The town has shifted this way, plank by plank, for as long as it has existed, and its plan follows the animals.

In the afternoon the steam rises far enough to meet cold air and comes back down as rain. It is warm and fine, and it falls in a ring a mile or so out from the hottest ground, where it can be seen from the hall roofs as a bright curtain with the sun in it. The birds on that side walk a few yards inward and wait. The pans dimple. The lagging darkens and then steams itself dry.

Toward evening the light goes from white to brass. The long-necked animals appear along the rim. The shutters on the condenser hall open and close, and are answered from the pan-houses across the flats. The lamps come on, each one a soft globe in the steam, far apart. Between them the ranks of birds stand in the warm water and the ground goes on dripping.
