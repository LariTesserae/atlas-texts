The tableland stands about three thousand meters above a lower country that is the same clear brown in every direction. It runs some forty kilometers across, nearly flat, and ends on every side in cliffs of fresh columnar rock. No talus lies at the foot of the cliffs, because nothing has fallen yet. Other tablelands stand at the horizon at the same height, their tops the same black. The air between holds no haze, so they look closer than they are.

The rock is new and has stayed new. It lies in ropy flows and in broad pavements of jointed columns, dark and glassy, every edge as sharp as when it set. There is no soil and hardly any dust. What grit exists has never been rounded. It has never rained here. The sky is a deep even blue that has not held a cloud, and the sun crosses it in hard, ordinary daylight that throws clean shadows off every ridge of the flow.

It is warm, though not from the sun. The warmth comes up through the rock, steady, about the temperature of a hand, and the thin air sits over it without moving. With no cold side and no hot side there is nothing for a wind to run between. Through the joints the plateau breathes out a dry, faintly sweet gas, too slowly to hear.

Across the black pavements run the seams. They are native metal, mostly copper with some pale nickel-iron, bright where the rock split around them as it cooled. They lie in long straight runs and meet at angles, a hand's width to a meter across, flush with the surface like something poured into a mold. A weak current runs in them, always. It rises from the depth of the rock and goes nowhere in particular. The current and the gas are what the vegetation lives on.

The groves stand where seams cross, and the crossings are kilometers apart. From any one grove the next is a dark green smudge low on the black, and between them is nothing at all: bare rock, light, a bright line of metal. Inside a grove, though, the growth is thick, close and heavy, head-high and higher. The stems are jointed and the leaves are broad, glossy and bronze-green, as thick as a tongue. They hold no water. Break one and it bleeds a clear slow oil that smells of resin and warm machinery and, faintly, of struck coins. The roots are flat ribbons. They do not dig. They lie along the seams and plate themselves on, a green skin over the copper.

The plants refine. The oil in them carries metal up from the seam, a little at a time, drawn by the current, and lays it down in the tissue. Every leaf has a foil underside, thin as breath on glass. The young stems are green, the older ones are green over a bright sleeve, and the oldest are tube, with nothing living left in them, standing in the middle of the grove in a gray-rose thicket of pipe and lace. Nothing rots, because nothing is here to rot it. No beetle, no fly and no worm has ever touched a leaf, and there are no birds over the plateau. What dies becomes metal and stays where it stood. A grove puts out new green at its rim exactly as fast as its heart goes to metal, so the living part is always the same size, a ring a few meters deep around a slowly thickening core.

The leaves do not turn to the sun. In each grove every blade lies along the same line, the line of the field over the crossing, the way filings lie. At the next grove the line is different. In the still air they never stir from it. The sun moves over them and they glare in sequence, one grove and then the next, as the angle comes right.

There are no flowers, since there is nothing to visit them. A grove spreads by a single runner, a green wire no thicker than a stalk of grass, which follows a seam across the bare rock toward the next crossing. Some runners are eleven or twelve kilometers long. Seen from the height of the oldest pipes, the country is a black plain ruled with bright lines, some of them edged in green, with dense dark nodes where the lines meet.

It is almost silent. Close to a grove there is a hum, low and even, at the edge of hearing, the same note at every crossing. At long intervals there is a single dry tick as a foil parts from a leaf that has finished. Away from the groves, on the open pavement, there is nothing to hear.

The plateau is being made. The cores widen by about the thickness of a leaf in a lifetime of leaves, and the seams under the roots thin by the same amount. At that rate the black will be laced over in a span that has no useful name. Meanwhile each day is the same day. The warmth comes up, the gas seeps, the current runs, and the green rings hold their width around the metal. The edges of the rock stay sharp.
