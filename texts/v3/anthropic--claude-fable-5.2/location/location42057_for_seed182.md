You come down by the hoist, and the heat reaches the cage before the bottom does. The air is thick and wet and smells of warm iron and leaf. Then the shaft opens at its foot into the one room the town has: a gas blister in a basalt flow not yet two centuries cold, ninety paces across and perhaps forty high at the crown. The surface people call it the Kettle. The three hundred or so who live here just say *under*.

**The rock**

The rock is young and shows it. Nothing has had time to round. The walls are black volcanic glass broken in shell-shaped scoops, and every scoop has an edge that will open a palm. So the town is padded. Wherever stone comes within reach of a body, someone has wrapped it in felt, cork or tarred rope: stair-sides, lane corners, the low lintels where galleries duck under a rib of ceiling. The padding has gone green. Moss takes the felt first, then small ferns take the moss, so the dangerous places are marked by the softest growth.

The rock is also still warm. Press a hand flat to a bare wall and it is like the flank of a kiln the morning after firing. That warmth is why the town exists. Bores run into the walls in every direction, water goes in and comes back as steam, and the steam goes to the engine.

**The town**

There is no open ground. The chamber is filled floor to crown with riveted frame and dark brick, seven and eight storeys of workshops, sleeping rooms and pump lofts, braced against the ceiling with screw jacks in the high corners. Lanes are a shoulder wide. Above them run galleries, and above the galleries run pipes: bore lines lagged in cloth, condensate returns, and the fat steam main with its slow elbows. You can cross the whole town at the fourth level without touching a floor, stepping from grating to grating between hanging troughs of beans.

The only gap is the engine well at the centre. There a single compound engine works a beam the length of a barge, eleven strokes a minute, mounted on cork blocks as thick as mattresses. It makes a sound like a long breath taken and let go, and you feel it in the handrails more than you hear it. The dynamo beside it hums one low note. A cable as thick as a thigh climbs the shaft and carries nearly everything the town makes up to somewhere with streets and weather. The town keeps a sliver of the power for itself and spends it on small amber filament lamps, perhaps thirty of them, hung where lanes cross. The light is the colour of weak tea and never changes, except at shift-change, when the lamps are dipped twice in place of a bell.

**The quiet**

There is no bell because nothing here is struck. Volcanic glass under strain does not take a blow well. A hammer on a wall bracket can send a plate the size of a door sliding off the roof a street away. So everything is screwed, wedged, pressed or tied. The bores are cut by slow rotary bits that turn for months. Tools carry lanyards. Children are taught to set things down. A dropped spanner goes in the log with the date and the name.

Speech has sunk to match. People talk at the level of the engine's breath. With the felt, the moss and the wet leaves taking the edge off every surface, the town is very quiet for a place so full. Mostly you hear dripping.

**Water and growth**

No spring feeds the place. One rationed pipe of cold water comes down the shaft. The rest is the town's own breath and steam, condensed on anything cooler than the air. The ceiling sweats into zinc gutters. The cold pipe sweats most of all, and along its whole route through the town it is sleeved in dripping growth as thick as an arm: liverwort, maidenhair, and a creeping plant with coin-sized leaves that nobody planted. They call the pipe the Brook, and people detour to walk beside it.

Everything green lives on those terms: the heat is constant, the lamplight is thin and the water comes in beads. Leaves here run dark, bottle-green to nearly purple-black, broad and held flat toward the nearest lamp. Roots go the other way, toward warmth. Lift a squash trough and its roots have left it to lie along the nearest steam line, tracing the joints. Where iron stays hot and bare, a rust-orange lichen grows that is found on nothing else.

At the lip of the engine well stands a fig, brought down as a cutting in someone's coat. It has wrapped the exhaust stack so thoroughly that the fitters now route around it. With no season to tell it what to do, each limb keeps its own calendar. One is bare, one is in new leaf, and one is heavy with pale fruit that ripens without ever colouring.

**The animals**

There are few. Each lamp has its gecko, a translucent, wide-toed one, and never two, though no one has seen how the matter is decided. The geckos wait for small white moths that breed in the Brook's moss. A bat once came down the shaft, circled the well for a day and went back up. People still mention it.

**The windows**

The rock is still settling into its own cooling. A few times a year, with a sound like a plate cracking in another room, a fresh face lets go somewhere on the wall. What it leaves is a black, flawless mirror, and in it the town sees itself a second time: amber lamps, galleries, the slow beam, all a little dimmer and deeper than the real ones. These are called windows. People bring stools and sit before a new one, looking out at the only view there is. Within a year the damp films it, moss finds the film, and the window closes.

**The cooling**

Once a month the engine is stopped for packing and oil. The updraft in the shaft falters, the air stills, and the ceiling lets go what it has been holding. For ten minutes a warm rain falls indoors on roofs that have no other reason to be roofs. People stand out on the gratings in it without speaking.

That is the fast cycle. The slow one is in the walls. The rock gives up about a degree every ten years. Along the east side the first bores have already gone tepid, and their pump houses are shuttered and given over to ferns, which have filled them to the rafters. The drills keep turning slowly outward toward the heat, and the town leans after them a room at a time. Its cold centre goes to the plants. Somebody has worked out the number of years remaining and keeps it chalked small and without comment on the engine-house door.
