The ground stands half a metre above the water and has stood there for as long as anyone has kept records, though the lowland underneath is sinking. The weight of the city presses the old floodplain down a hand's width each year, the city adds a hand's width to itself each year, and at the surface the two motions cancel. Below the lowest pavement lie earlier pavements, a thousand years of drowned streets fused into something close to chert. Nobody digs. The water table is right there, warm as a mouth, and it comes up through every joint.

So everything weeps. Walls carry a skin of moisture that never quite runs and never dries. The building stuff is a pale fired mineral, buff when dry and slate-dark when wet, and since it is wet nearly everywhere, the city is the colour of a river stone, with lighter patches on the upper faces where noon bakes them. The gutters are stained orange by films that eat iron. In the seams of warm conduit a grey felt creeps a few centimetres and dies back whenever the surface is remade. That is all the growth there is. Nothing has leaves, and shade means the shade of a wall.

The noise is how the city is built. Canals the colour of thin milk carry mineral feed down every street, and where driven sound meets that slurry, it sets. A wall going up sounds like a saw being drawn through glass. The shriek lasts forty minutes, and at the end of it there is a wall. Spans hold because a tone is kept running in them. Take the tone away and the span would sag over a few hours like wet clay. The city therefore never stops sounding, and the sum of it is a roar felt first in the sternum and the back teeth. Under everything runs a bass that newcomers mistake for their own pulse, with a broad middle band like surf above it, and the sintering cries rising and stopping on every side.

Nothing stays put. Districts thicken toward wherever people are and thin out behind them. A market at a canal junction in the morning stands a storey higher by evening, because the junction has been lifted under it and the stalls rode up without closing. An address is a bearing and a date.

Yet the churn has habits. The remaking travels in fronts along paths worn over centuries, the way a river keeps to its meanders. Old residents know their quarter will come round again in eleven years or so, with the arcades roughly where they were. A picture of any street is out of date within the month, while a picture of the whole would serve for five hundred years.

There is no room in it. Every gap between structures holds conduit, and every gap between conduits holds a dwelling, a stall, a nest or a trickle. People are shoulder to shoulder on the walkways and do not speak aloud outdoors. They talk with their hands and with strips of light sewn into their sleeves. For private matters, one presses a palm flat between the other's shoulder blades and hums, so the words arrive through bone. The markets shout by sight alone, with colour cards, mirrors, and flags snapped open and shut.

The animals have made the same accommodations:

- **Fish:** They hold station in the feed canals in evenly spaced rows, like beads on a wire, each one sitting where the pulses cancel.
- **Crabs:** White crabs graze fresh-set walls for unbound mineral. Their shells grow so thick that they sink like stones when knocked loose.
- **Gulls:** They have gone nearly mute and signal each other with the white patches under their wings.
- **Swifts:** They nest in the still points of the great spans, where the air is pressed from every side and moves nowhere.
- **Dogs:** Deaf for forty generations, they read the ground through their feet.
- **Moths:** They hang in brown clouds over every heat vent.

All of them know the rule. A still point is quiet but held, while a dead spot is quiet because the tone has failed. Where a tone fails, the wall sweats harder, its surface turns glossy and its edges start to round. The birds lift first, in a white flash that travels up the street like a seam opening. Then the dogs go, and then the people, who move away without hurry. The neighbouring tones lean in to cover the gap, and the chord of the whole block can be felt changing. Usually that is enough. Sometimes a frontage slumps into its canal and the water turns thick and white. The fish scatter, and within the hour they have lined up again along the new shape of the bank.

The city makes its own weather. Heat rises off the driven lowland in a dome a continent wide. The sky is an ordinary bright haze all morning and closes over by early afternoon. Then it rains, warm and heavy, for about an hour, a little later in each longitude. The pitch of everything drops a quarter tone to push through the wet air. After the rain the city steams.

The sound has fronts and tides as well. The work follows the sun, and on the eastern districts morning can be heard arriving as a swell in the roar an hour before any light. Thunder goes mostly unnoticed.

The light is plain. The days are white with soft shadows, and at night there are warm lamps and the flicker of sleeves in conversation. Nothing glows that shouldn't.

At the coast the city does not stop. The shelf goes on under water, and for two hundred kilometres out the sea is milky and hums. Inland the ground finally rises and the tone thins. The last buildings there are squat and thick, made the slow way, with weight. People who go that far say their ears ring for a week from the lack of sound.
