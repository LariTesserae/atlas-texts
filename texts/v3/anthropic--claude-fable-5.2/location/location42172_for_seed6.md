The drum sits at the top of everything, a round concrete hall thirty meters across on the last platform of a stack of works that steps down into cloud. Below it there are catchment roofs, terraces of sheet metal with gutters at every seam. No ground shows anywhere, only decking and cable trays, with downpipes thick as trunks dropping to the next level. The air is thin and wet and holds the day's warmth. Cloud moves through the railings most hours, and the roofs take it out of the air and send it up the risers to the drum.

The drum is full of water, floor to roof glass, with no surface inside it. It holds nine meters of water at the temperature of a bath just run, and the outer wall is warm to the hand. The concrete is new. The grain of the formwork boards is still printed in it, and the tie-rod holes are plugged with a mortar that hasn't yet weathered to match. The corners are sharp enough to scrape a sleeve. On the stainless band that holds the gallery windows, the welds keep their rainbow heat-tint. One panel still wears its blue protective film, and a corner of it has lifted on the wet side and leans a few degrees in the current.

Above the glass roof a frame of lamps makes up whatever the sky withholds. They brighten under cloud and at dusk and hold all night, so that inside it is always about ten in the morning. The plants in the drum have never had a night. From the terraces below, after dark, the drum is a lit green lantern with a hole of clear air above it where its warmth has burned off the cloud.

Through the gallery windows there is no open water to look into. The floor is a grid of steel mesh baskets on forty-centimeter centers, and every one is filled. A low mat covers the baskets themselves. Above that a fine-leaved cultivar stands in plumes that interlock with their neighbors, and through the plumes rise the ribbons, flat green straps as wide as a belt that go up past the lamp-light's best reach and keep going. A fourth kind climbs. It has taken the nutrient headers and the sensor stalks, and it is on the second rung of the service ladder and has not yet found the third.

Between the stems run the supply lines, the return lines, and the conduits with their stenciled arrows still crisp. Leaf lies against pipe and against other leaf, and a hand pushed in anywhere would touch six things. Every stem carries a stamped aluminum tag at its collar with a drawing number and a revision letter. The plants are parts, and there is a plate by the door that lists them.

Nothing has ever eaten any of it. The drum has no snails or grazers, and nothing leaves a track in the faint green haze beginning on the inside of the glass. The haze is perfectly even, like a coat of spray. Every leaf margin is whole. Nothing has died here yet either. There is no litter in the baskets and no brown anywhere. A rack of spare baskets stands on the gallery in its factory wrap, and the procedure for replacing a failed plant sits in a sealed binder box, unopened.

The plants make gas all the time. It comes off the leaves in threads of bubbles as fine as sewing cotton, thousands of them, each rising straight because nothing in the water disturbs them. The roof is a shallow glass cone, and the gas gathers at its apex and spreads outward as a silver disk. Seen from below it is a mirror at the edges and a window directly overhead. Over about nine hours the mirror widens until it reaches a float. Then a valve at the top of the center column opens, and for twenty minutes the disk shrinks back with a thin hiss. That is the loudest sound the place makes. The rule is that nothing leaves upward but gas. Everything else goes down the hollow center column into the penstock, and from there down the mountain of roofs to wherever the water is wanted.

The arm sees to that. It is a truss mounted on the center column, reaching to the wall at the six-meter line and carrying a sickle bar along its leading edge and a row of intake slots behind. It turns once in forty days. At the wall its tip covers about a hand's width an hour. It can't be seen moving. With a palm on the window, though, the drive can be felt: one click through the glass as each tooth of the ring gear seats, then a minute and a half of nothing.

The arm does not stop. Below its line the thicket is permanent, and above its line everything is regrowth. Just behind the bar is the only clear water in the drum, a slot a meter wide where the ribbons end in flat, fresh cuts all at one height. A day behind that they have put on a few fingers of new leaf. Ten days behind they stand a meter over the line, and at thirty days they stand two.

So the upper water is a ramp. It climbs the whole way round the circle from stubble to full height, and it ends at a cliff where the tallest ribbons, just ahead of the bar, press their tips flat against the roof glass and bend along it. At any bearing in the room the date can be read from the height of the leaves. The drum is a clock, and its single hand is always arriving at the tallest growth.

The sounds are few. A transformer somewhere in the platform hums, more in the feet than in the ears. The tooth clicks. Outside, the gutters run with a sound like a tap left slightly open three rooms away. No bird stands on the railing and no insects come to the lamps. The cloud arrives and departs without wind enough to whistle in the truss.

On the wall beside the door, at the bearing where the arm was first set going, someone has made two ticks in yellow paint marker, one for each completed turn. The paint is still glossy. The arm is a little past halfway through its third. Ahead of it, for the first time, a few of the ribbons have grown long enough to reach through the mirror when it is at its widest. Their tips lie in the gas for an hour or two before the valve opens and the water comes back up over them.
