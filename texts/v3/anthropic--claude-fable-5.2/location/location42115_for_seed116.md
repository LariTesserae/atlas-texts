Every four seconds the water is struck from below.

The blow comes up the rising main, a pipe as wide as a thigh that enters through the floor of the tank. It is the last of a stroke made by an engine so far down the tower that its own noise never arrives, only its push. The whole body of water jumps a finger's width, the iron walls ring one low note, and a column of white water stands up out of the inlet and falls apart. It is white because of the height. The air over the tank is a third as thick as the air at the tower's foot, and the gas the water carried comes out of it all at once as a fine fizz. The fizz rises, hisses against the lid, and clears before the next stroke.

This is the header tank, a riveted drum six paces across and a tall man deep, full to within a hand of its lid. It was once lined with white enamel, which survives only in the upper corners, crazed like old teeth. The flow has had the rest. The rivet heads are worn flush and show only as faint circles. The floor is dished into a shallow bowl, and the inlet's lip has been drawn out into a bright trumpet flare, the only clean metal in the room. The baffle plates that once stood across the tank to calm the water have been holed until the holes joined, and what remains is iron lace.

The wearing has stopped, though. A pale scale, hard as pottery, covers every surface, and the water lays it down as fast as it takes it away. The tank has reached the shape the stroke wants, and it is not getting any thinner.

The lid is glass, bottle-thick panes in an iron frame, greened on the inside. Above it the sun is fierce enough to blister paint. By the time it has come through the glass, the hand of wet air, and the film on the surface, it is the ordinary light of a greenhouse in the afternoon. It lies in shaking nets across the floor, and every four seconds the nets scatter.

The warmth comes from the steam return, a copper coil that makes three turns around the lower wall on its way back down to the boilers. Near it the water is as warm as blood, and at the centre it is a little cooler. The outer face of the iron stands in freezing wind, heard from inside as a steady drone under everything else.

Things grow thickly wherever there is something to hold. The coil is buried in moss, dark cushions as deep as a forearm, beaded with bubbles of their own making. The ladder has eleven rungs, and each carries ribbon weed longer than the tank is deep. The blades bend at the surface and lie along it, all pointing at the overflow. The lace baffles are furred with something fine and red-brown, and the float chain is a rope of green.

Between these gardens there is nothing. Most of the tank is warm, clear, open water with light shaking in it. The growth keeps to the edges of a bare room.

The animals keep time with the engine. Bronze fish the length of a thumb hold a ring around the inlet, facing inward. Each stroke throws them outward, and they swim back to snap at the flakes the main has shed on its long climb. Glass-clear shrimp work the scale in their thousands, and between strokes their picking is audible as a crackle like fat in a pan. Rust-shelled snails keep to the walls.

One eel lives in the overflow pipe with its head out, as thick as the pipe allows. Midges hatch through the surface into the air gap and dance under the glass. At the vent slot beneath the lid's eave, grey birds that nest in the tower's lattice wedge their heads in and pick the midges off the panes. Their beaks are loud against the glass.

The float is a copper ball the size of a head, worn thin and pinholed long ago. It is half full now and hangs in mid-water on its arm, neither risen nor sunk. The valve it governs therefore neither opens nor closes, and it chatters, a brass rattle on every stroke that is the loudest thing in the tank. Shrimp go in and out through the pinholes and breed inside. When the brood is heavy the ball rides lower and the valve admits more; when the brood disperses, it admits less. The level of the water is set by them.

A little more always comes in than is taken. The surplus slides over a weir into the overflow, past the eel, in a thread that never breaks.

The tower leans in the wind and recovers, slowly, and the water lags behind it. The surface climbs one wall and drops down the other, and the ribbon weed swings like a row of pendulums.

A few times a day something far below opens. What it is cannot be known from up here. The level falls a forearm in a minute with a roar down the outlet. The weed strains toward the strainer, the fish go into the moss, and the eel comes all the way out and hunts in the confusion. Then the valve's chatter rises to a single shriek and the tank refills.

At night the lid goes cold while the coil stays warm, and the water turns over. It rises in the middle and slides down the walls in a cool sheet. Fog fills the air gap, and condensation falls from the glass until morning as rain an inch tall.

From out on the lattice the tank is a brown drum no bigger than a shed, with cloud lying far beneath it. A green stripe runs down the rust where the overflow falls before the wind tears it off. None of the noise gets out. A hand laid on the iron feels a tick every four seconds.
