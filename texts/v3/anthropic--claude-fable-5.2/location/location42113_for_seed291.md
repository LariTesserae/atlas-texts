The basin is about forty kilometres across, rimmed by low hills of old dark rock that no longer move. Everything inside the rim is still arriving. The floor comes up from below in slow cold welts, a grey-green mineral paste, stiff as potter's clay and a few degrees above freezing. It domes, ridges, relaxes, and domes somewhere else. Nothing gets higher than a few storeys before it slumps back. The whole lowland behaves like dough being proved in a cold room, and a city of perhaps three hundred thousand sits on it, wall to wall.

People call the new ground *tender*. At four days old it cuts with a wire. At a month, left alone, it is still soft enough to take a bootprint. Vibration is what hardens it, so a dropped girder leaves a hard grey scab a metre wide, and a shout laid against a fresh bank leaves a brittle crust shaped roughly like the shout. The city therefore works in near silence, by necessity and by statute. Trams run on felt-seated rails. Gear trains turn in oil baths. The pressing halls, long sheds where the tender is wire-cut and squeezed into pipe, block, tile, and insulator blanks, are lined in moss a hand deep, and the foremen talk with flags by day and shuttered lamps by night. What you hear standing on a gantry over the whole packed valley is transformer hum, the hiss of hydraulics, and wings.

The hydraulics are the power. Wherever a dome is expected, crews plant rams, steel pistons the size of grain silos seated on older set ground, and the rising tender pushes them. The pressure goes to turbines, the turbines feed a forest of pylons, and the cables sag and tighten daily as their footings drift. Line crews are the largest trade in the city. After them come the shimmers, who jack buildings level, then the rail-layers, who relay about a tenth of the network every week. On a Monday, a street might run flat between a tile works and a row of tenements. By Thursday the tile works is four metres up and tilted toward the east, a ramp of fresh planking has replaced the pavement, and nobody on it looks surprised. Tens of thousands of people are moving at any moment, carrying, bracing, re-stringing, and unbolting, and the sound of it is like a library's.

When something needs to stay put, it is rung. Setting crews bring a tuned bronze bell on a sprung cart, sink a coupling rod into the ground, and strike one note at a steady interval, for hours and sometimes days. The tender hardens outward from the rod in a pale ring that can be watched advancing. Kept ground, as it is called afterward, is as hard as basalt and bluish. The oldest districts are islands of it, over-rung in places and finely cracked, with the soft floor heaving around them. A setting bell is the loudest thing most residents ever hear. It carries across the basin under the flat white sky, and people on unrelated streets stop and wait to see whether the ground under them has noticed.

Water never arrives in quantity. There is no river, and rain is occasional and thin. Fresh tender sweats as it meets the air, and fog comes off the rim hills most mornings and rimes every cable and railing. The city drinks condensate, collected from kilometres of chilled pipe hung with gutters and from the cooling stacks, whose vapour stands in straight white columns in the still air. Pools gather in the swales between domes and are gone in a day when the swale becomes a crest.

The tender is rich, and plants take it fast. Moss covers a new surface within two days, a vivid, almost yellow green against the grey. Sedge and scurvy grass follow within a fortnight, then dwarf willow and creeping birch by the end of a season. The city encourages all of it because it deadens sound. Roofs are felted in moss. Factory yards are knee-deep in willow scrub that gets lifted, split down the middle, and carried apart on two different slopes. Kale and cold cabbage grow in terraces against the warm lee walls of the transformer sheds, and the terraces are rebuilt as often as the rails.

Hares live everywhere in the yards, big pale ones, and they sit on ground that is about to rise. Nobody knows what they feel. The survey office keeps hare counts by district and plants its rams accordingly. Crows work the pressing-hall tips for the blind white beetles that bore the tender. They have largely stopped calling, or the ones that call have not done well here. Voles tunnel the moss roofs. Foxes travel the cable trenches. Geese cross high in autumn, calling the whole way, and people look up at them with something like alarm.

The pressing halls reject a fair amount of what they cut, because the tender does not always come up blank. It arrives with forms already in it, such as flutes, ribs, sockets, and blind arches a metre deep, all of them smooth and unfinished. Increasingly, the forms resemble what stands above them. Under the old tram depot it surfaced with two faint parallel ridges, spaced to gauge. Beneath a tenement block in the north quarter it came up with a row of shallow rectangular recesses at the pitch of the windows overhead. Some districts have rung these shapes hard and moved into them. Others cut them out the day they appear and send them to the presses. The city has no settled policy, and the ground keeps coming up.
