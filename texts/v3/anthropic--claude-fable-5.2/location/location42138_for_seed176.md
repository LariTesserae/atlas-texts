The shelf sits just under six thousand meters, a notch broken out of the mountain's shoulder perhaps ninety paces long and forty deep. By every rule of altitude it should be ice and bare stone. It is green instead, green to the edges.

The rock is young. The whole summit is a lava dome that finished rising within the memory of a few generations, and the shelf is where a slab of it sheared away and fell. The back wall still shows the break: grey-black andesite in columns with edges sharp enough to open a glove, and no talk yet of rounding. Seams of sulfur yellow run through it, and rust-orange where iron has met the wet. The floor is clinker and fractured plates, though you would not know it without digging. Everything horizontal is under a hand's depth of moss, and everything vertical is weeping.

The water comes out of the wall warm. It doesn't run so much as sweat, a continuous film that gathers into threads, then into runnels, then into three shallow pools the temperature of blood. The pools spill over the lip and are gone, torn to mist within a few meters by the cold beyond. The warmth comes from the fumaroles at the base of the wall, a dozen cracks that breathe steam, and from one that is not a crack at all.

That one is a wellhead. Someone drilled here. A steel casing stands out of the moss, flanged and bolted, with a valve tree painted a red that has mostly gone to scale. A lagged pipe runs fifteen paces from it to a corrugated shed the size of a single room, where a turbine turns. A second pipe goes to a silencer stack, a squat drum packed with broken rock, which vents the surplus steam. From the shed a cable as thick as a wrist goes out over the lip and down the mountain on pylons, the first of which is visible below, small and iced white, standing in the other climate.

The stack is why the place is loud. It roars without pulse or variation, a broad white noise that fills the notch the way water fills a basin, and under it the turbine holds one high note. The fumaroles hiss, and drips fall in the thousands from every leaf and ledge. After an hour the sound stops registering as sound and seems part of the air, like the humidity. It has not changed pitch in years. A hand laid on the shed wall feels the mountain's pressure translated into a steady tremble.

The plants arrange themselves by heat. At the vents nothing grows but mats of orange, olive, and a green nearly black, slick as wet leather. A stride out, liverworts and moss begin. Then come ferns, at first fist-sized and then chest-high, their fronds beaded. In the middle band, where the ground is merely warm, the shelf becomes a thicket. Twisted shrubs with waxy leaves and trunks no thicker than an arm stand so close their crowns have fused into one canopy at about twice a person's height. They are furred in moss and hung with small pale orchids. Broad-leaved herbs fill what gaps there are. There is no bare ground and almost no bare air.

The growth does not lean toward the sun, which is strong up here, a hard and ordinary daylight softened only by the shelf's own steam. It leans toward the wall, each trunk tilted a few degrees inward toward the warmth.

The pipe wears a sleeve of moss along its lagging, with a clean bare ring at each flange where the metal runs too hot. The shed roof carries a sod of ferns. On its door, under lichen, there is a stenciled number and a maintenance tally in grease pencil, two marks a year going back a long way.

The green ends in a line. At the lip and at both flanks it runs from knee-deep fern through a hand's width of browned moss to rock with rime on it. There is a ceiling too. On most afternoons snow falls toward the shelf and never arrives. About twenty meters up it turns to rain, and you can see the height where it happens, a faint flat seam in the air, white above and grey below. The steam rising from the stack meets it there, condenses, and comes back down. The shelf waters itself.

Not much lives here that moves. Midges hang over the pools. A small dun bird, perhaps thirty of them in all, works the canopy and never crosses the line. In the pools there is a frog the size of a thumb joint, and it has given up calling against the roar. It sits on a stone and slowly extends one hind leg, spreading a foot whose webbing is bright white, then draws it back. Across the pool, another answers the same way.

Change here is slow. The vents migrate a finger's width a year along the fracture, and the bands of orange, moss, and fern migrate with them. At one end of the shelf dead shrubs stand grey in new frost. At the other, moss is taking rock that was bare a decade ago. The shed has been shimmed level three times. Below the lip the cloud deck lies flat and far down, the cable runs into it, and somewhere under that, lights are on.
