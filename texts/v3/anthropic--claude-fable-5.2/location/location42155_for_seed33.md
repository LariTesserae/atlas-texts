The plateau is a saddle of grey-green schist slung between two summits, about twelve kilometres long, so high that the sky is indigo at midday and a few stars stay visible near the zenith. Below the rim there is only brown haze, level as a floor. Nothing has ever fallen here. There is no snow, though there should be, and no ice in the joints of the rock. The stone is as dry as fired clay, and the air is only cool, like a cellar, when at this height it ought to kill.

A city covers the saddle from rim to rim. Its walls are dry-laid slab, some waist-high and some four storeys, roofed with corbelled stone. There is no mortar, timber, or metal, and no stone carries a tool mark. Each slab is the shape wind and its own cleavage gave it, fitted to its neighbours as found, and the fit is exact. There are streets, stairs, squares, and long raised channels like aqueducts that have carried only dust. The cisterns hold dust too, its surface flat and fine as poured cream.

It has the plan of a dense town, drawn out until every building stands alone. Four hundred paces of bare paving separate one house from the next, and a tower stands by itself in a square the size of a field. The paving is continuous, the kerbs are true, the corners are square, and almost nothing stands on them.

The city's edge can be walked in a day, but crossing it takes far longer. The streets inside are longer than the perimeter allows. A wall counted from the north has forty courses and from the south has fifty-one. The number of houses along a street depends on which end the count starts from.

The cause shows first in the shadows. Each building casts its own, at its own angle. One house lays a long morning shadow westward while its neighbour across the street has a short noon shadow tucked under its wall, and a third has none, because for it the sun is down. There is one sun, small and white, and each house takes its light from where that sun stands at the house's own hour. The buildings are close together in space and far apart in time, and the bare paving between them is the hours laid out flat. That is why the light is dim: one day is spread thin across all of its hours at once. The coolness is the same sum, noon and midnight averaged over the whole saddle.

The windows keep their hours as well. Looking through a house often shows the street beyond with the dust lying differently, or the facing wall three courses shorter.

Nobody builds here. A loose slab left unwatched on the open paving is later found seated in a wall, and the only evidence is a single dry click, like a tooth set down on a plate, that reaches no further than a few arm's lengths. On their windward faces the same walls are coming apart grain by grain. The two rates match, so each house slowly walks east, a body-length in a very long while, and leaves a paler rectangle on the paving where it used to stand. The rectangles trail west behind every building in overlapping rows. At the western rim the oldest ones are cut off mid-outline by the drop, as if the houses had walked in from the air. At the foot of the eastern scarp, far down, there are fans of scree in which some stones still lie fitted together.

The wind is constant and makes no sound. It can be felt on the face and seen in the dust, but it does not whistle in the doorways or hum along the wall-tops. The dust moves like a tide. Toward each house's evening it runs down the streets ankle-deep and smooth as liquid, filling the channels and cisterns. Toward its morning it drains back uphill. Because the hours differ from house to house, the tide is always coming in somewhere and going out somewhere else, and where two streets meet the currents slide past each other without mixing. Static gathers on the high walls and crawls along the copings in blue threads. It discharges without thunder.

The only growth is a crust in the lee joints, black with rust-orange margins, thin as a stain. It may be mineral. It thickens only inside the long shadows, the early-morning and late-afternoon ones, and it advances along a wall as that wall's hour advances, then flakes away when noon reaches it.

A grazer feeds on the crust. It is grey, long-legged, and about the size of a hare, with a rasping mouth, and it is always alone and usually far down a street. It walks the wall-tops, and when it steps from one house's hour into the next its own shadow swings round beneath it. It leaves behind small faceted pellets, hard as gravel, which in time are also found seated in the walls.

Something hunts it. A house that had three doorways will have four, and the fourth is darker than the dim interior behind it should make it. A grazer comes along the foot of the wall. Later the house has three doorways again, and there is no sound and no mark in the dust.

Toward the middle of the saddle the shadows shorten and begin to agree with one another. The gaps between buildings grow longer instead of shorter. There is a square in there, visible from any rooftop, where a single low wall stands with no shadow at all. The click comes from that direction more often than from any other.
