The plateau stands high enough that water boils long before it is hot, and a pot of tubers takes a morning to soften. The lava that covers it is younger than the oldest stories told on it. The mountain that made it lies to the north, a shield so broad and gently sloped that it looks like a thickening of the horizon. It has laid flow over flow across the plain, the latest some forty generations back. That one is still glassy in places: ropes and folds of black basalt with their creases sharp, blisters with their roofs intact, fields of clinker that will open a hand. Nothing has had time to round off. Where one flow stopped on top of another the scarp is square-edged, and loose rock lies where it broke.

The city is built of this rock and spread across it for a distance a loaded animal needs most of a month to walk. It has no outer wall and no center. From any pressure ridge, roofs run to the horizon in every direction but north. They are low corbelled domes of stacked basalt, each with a smoke hole. The domes are joined into compounds that share walls, and the compounds into quarters. Between quarters lie bands of unbuilt lava and tussock where the herds move.

No timber grows within a season's travel, so nothing is spanned with wood. Doorways are topped with single six-sided columns prised whole from the cliffs at the flow margins. Roofs close by stepping each ring of stones inward over the last. Walls are dry-laid and double-faced, with rubble cores as thick as an arm's reach. The stone is full of gas bubbles, light to lift and slow to pass heat.

Age shows as color. New work is black. Within a generation a gray-green crust takes the weather side, and within five there are orange rosettes the size of a palm. The oldest quarters lie along the southern flow front, where the springs come out, and their walls are so furred with lichen they look like a different stone. Disputes over who built a wall are settled by measuring its largest rosette.

Rain hardly falls. On summer afternoons the black ground heats under the sun and the air above it rises. By mid-afternoon flat-bottomed cloud stands over the lava and nowhere else on the plateau. It drops graupel for a quarter of an hour, small dry pellets that rattle on the domes and drift into the lee of walls. Half of it melts into the stone and the dry air takes the rest by dusk.

Winter is clear and much colder, and the real supply is underground. The flows are threaded with tubes, drained channels wide enough to walk a herd through. Cold air sinks into them through collapsed skylights and stays. Seepage freezes onto their floors layer by layer, and the ice is blue, banded, and deeper than anyone has cut. Every quarter sits over a tube, so the plan of the city follows the tube system as it branches downhill.

The ice is cut with basalt adzes and bone wedges and carried up in net bags. Its level is checked against marks pecked into the tube wall. Cutting below the year's mark is the one offence for which a household can be put out of its quarter.

Frost is the other source. Over clay-lined basins stand loose cairns of dark stone, shoulder high and open to the air. Frost forms in their hollows overnight and drips into the basins at sunrise. On the eastern edge of the city the cairns stand in ranks thousands long, because the frost is heavier there. The breath of the herds and the steam of every cooking pot drift east in the night under a lid of cold air and settle on the first cold stone they meet. Frost belongs to whoever owns the stone it forms on.

Animals outnumber people perhaps twenty to one, before birds are counted. The herd beast is long-necked and heavy-wooled, with a cleft lip and two toes set on a leathery pad. There is a tall breed that carries and a small one that is shorn. Each compound is a ring of domes around a sunken yard, and at night the herd is folded into the yard flank to flank. Nursing mothers and the year's young are stalled under the sleeping platforms. Their warmth rises through gaps in the floor slabs.

The walls are tenanted too. Niches left on the sun side hold rock doves, several hundred pairs to a compound. They are kept for squab and for their dung, which is scraped out twice a year and makes the tuber beds bear. The rubble cores house fist-sized, tailless grass-cutters with round ears. They dry their cuttings in small stacks along the wall tops. In late autumn children take a third of each stack for the nursing stalls. They leave the rest, because a wall whose cutters starve has no stacks the next year.

Fat, squeaking rodents run loose under the kitchen benches. They are fed on peelings and eaten at feasts. Red-billed crows walk the roofs in pairs, turning over dung cakes for grubs. Small finches nest in the smoke-hole rims, where the stone stays warm. The dogs are lean and thick-coated. They sleep on the domes and rarely bark.

By late morning the black rock is throwing up warm air, and vultures ride it over the city in hundreds. The broad pale kind eats flesh. A narrower, rust-breasted kind carries bones aloft and drops them on slabs to break them. The ground cannot be dug and there is nothing to burn a body with, so the dead go to platforms on the bare lava at the northern edge. They do not last a day there.

Wild cousins of the herd beast, fawn-colored and slighter, cross the open bands in single file at dawn. Foxes follow the lambing. In summer, meltwater stands in collapsed pits, and geese come down to it. Small flies hatch over the water and the finches work through them.

The rock has no soil of its own. Tussock grass roots in cracks where dust has lodged. Hard green cushion plants sit on bare stone like upturned bowls and grow a nail's width in a year. Some are older than the quarters around them. They are resinous and burn hot, and burning one inside the city is forbidden.

Everything planted grows in made earth. Ground scoria, ash, dove dung and yard sweepings are carried in baskets and built up behind knee-high walls. An old quarter has soil forearm-deep, and a new one has a hand's depth. The beds hold bitter tubers and a small-seeded grain. The tubers are spread on the roofs to freeze, trodden barefoot at midday to press the water out, and frozen again. In the end they weigh almost nothing and keep for ten years.

Fuel is dung, slapped into cakes and dried on south walls. The city smells of its smoke, and of lanolin and cold stone.

The tools are stone and bone. Obsidian comes down from a dome on the mountain's flank and is struck into blades that outcut anything else here. There are basalt hammerstones, bone awls and needles, spindles with stone whorls, and looms staked to the yard floor. Pots are fired low in dung and sweat a little when filled. Native copper, found in nuggets and pounded cold into cloak pins, is the only metal. Nobody has made a wheel, and the ground would break one. Herds are tallied with dyed tassels in the ear, and debts with knots.

It is a quiet place for its size. A hundred thousand padded feet make less noise on stone than one hoof. People wear felt and the walls are thick. The air is thin enough that nobody spends breath on shouting, and across the roofs they talk with their arms.

The herd keeps up a closed-mouthed, nasal hum among itself. Doves murmur in the walls and the grass-cutters whistle once when a shadow crosses them. A knapper ticks at a core. A loom beater thumps. On winter nights the stone itself cracks, where water has frozen in a bubble and sprung a flake loose. Morning finds fresh black chips at the foot of the walls, and they are swept into the beds. That is how the rock has started to wear. Deep in the tubes the ice sometimes shifts with a long low note that carries up through the skylights.

At dawn the smoke of the cooking fires pools with the breath of the yards under the cold. It makes a gray-brown layer, flat on top, reaching as far as the roofs do. From a pressure ridge one stands above it in clear light, with only the highest cairns showing. By mid-morning the sun has burned it away. The light then is plain and hard. The lava is warm to the hand on its sun side and frost still lies in the shade. Cloud builds in the afternoon.

Toward evening the herds come in along the open bands. Each compound's animals peel off undriven at their own lane. The main lanes are the only smooth surfaces in the city, ropy lava rubbed to a dull shine by pads.

After the graupel season three-quarters of the herds leave for the rim grass beyond the lava, twenty days out. The city goes half empty and quieter still. They return when the pit water skins with ice.

Year on year the city creeps north, up the tubes and onto newer rock. It adds a few compounds each season. The clinker is cleared by hand and stacked into walls where it lay. In the southern tubes the ice stands a little below the marks the grandparents pecked. On cold mornings a single thread of steam rises from a vent on the mountain's flank, and no one alive has seen it do more than that.

At the northern edge the newest domes are still entirely black. Doves have been carried up in baskets and are already in the niches.
