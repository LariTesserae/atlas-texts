The valley is wide and shallow, some twelve kilometres from rim to rim, and the rim is barely a rim. Its hills are worn to long swells, with rock showing only where water has licked it bare. Everything slopes gently toward a fen at the centre and a gap in the south-west where the water leaves. The floor is peat over grey silt. Sedge tussocks and quaking sphagnum lawns cover the open ground, with bog myrtle on the hummocks and alder and grey willow carr in the wetter flats. Water lies within a hand's depth of the surface everywhere, and the air is so near saturation that bark, stone and leaf are always beaded.

Among the grains of the silt are others, the same size and the same grey, that were made. Nothing else here was. They cannot be picked out by eye. A handful of the silt simply weighs a little differently each time it is hefted, and water poured onto it hesitates before sinking. Whatever the grains were once set to do, what they do now is keep.

They keep the water first. A ribbon of stream a metre wide crosses a sedge meadow at chest height with nothing under it. It has the shape of a bed, with pebble-ripple visible on its underside, and it runs. The valley floor has been lowering for a very long time, and each channel the ground abandoned was not abandoned by its water, or not by all of it. The lower levels run nearly full. Higher ones are thinner and slower. The highest, forty metres up, is a thread of faint brightness across the cloud like a snail's track, the first river from when the floor stood there. From the rim the levels read like contour lines drawn in air, the valley's whole subsidence shown in section.

Plants have taken this as given. Watercress and brooklime grow in mats on the aerial streams. Willows root in water two metres off the ground, trailing roots down to the peat until each tree stands on a braid of both. Where a level is thinning, its trees are being lowered a hand's width a century, set down slowly with their moss intact. Eels use the hanging channels and are seen from below, pale-bellied, passing overhead.

The sound is kept too, and the valley is loud. On the north side a waterfall wore itself back into a riffle long ago, but its roar still stands in the air where the lip used to be. It is a column of noise at full strength, with an edge that can be walked through. Rain is audible always, though real rain falls perhaps one day in three. Old thunder has worn smooth into a ground tone that never stops. Frog choruses rise from meadows with no frogs in them. A voice has to be raised here. A real sound can be told from a kept one only because it has a beginning.

Light is held in the mist and let out slowly. Noon and midnight differ by little, and the valley sits in a brown-green dusk without shadows. The exceptions are the pools. Some reflect the present cloud and others reflect skies of other days. A few hold a hard blue, and these are the brightest things in the valley. Alders bend down toward them, and marsh marigolds on their banks open facing the water.

Temperature has been averaged the same way. There is no frost and no warm afternoon, only a steady coolness that is the mean of every season. An alder carries bud, catkin, green leaf and bare twig at once.

Animals mostly turn back at the rim. A falls roaring from empty air and ground whose depth cannot be judged are too much to parse. A few have stayed besides the eels. There are herons that have learned to stand under a hanging stream and strike upward through its underside. A snipe drums over the fen perhaps once a season, and that sound will still be there, thinner, in a hundred years.

Real weather still comes through. Fronts arrive from the west and their rain falls through the levels. Each one catches it, swells and spills to the next, so the valley floods from the top down, the fen rising last. At the gap the stacked rivers leave together. Past the last of the grey silt they lose their beds and drop, a ragged staircase of falling water onto ordinary country.

Other things have loosened with long keeping. The pools in a meadow cannot be counted to the same number twice, and it is the number that fails to settle, since the pools do not move. The middle of the valley holds more ground than its width allows, so crossings run long. On dead flat peat the legs sometimes labour up a grade, climbing a hill that is no longer there.

Not everything kept is old. In the fen there is a winding notch of clear air where rain does not land and the reeds lean away. No channel has ever run there. On every alder trunk in the valley, at one level height, the bark is bleached in a line, and under the rain-sound, very faint, there is surf. The grains keep the whole span, and the valley is low, and the sea has not come yet.

A heron stands in the sedge beneath that line, watching the underside of a stream.
