High on the north side of a dry massif, a few hundred meters below a summit nobody has had reason to name, a slab of pale gneiss has slipped forward and come to rest leaning against the face. Behind it is a gap wide enough for shoulders turned sideways. The air at this height holds almost nothing: no moisture to make frost, too little pressure to carry a smell. The scree is angular and clean. The last lichen gave up a thousand meters down. The only colour is rock varnish, a brown-black skin of manganese that thickens by a few atoms a year on the windward faces and is the nearest thing here to growth.

The gap opens into a hollow about four meters across and less than two high. It is small enough that one glance should take in all of it, but one glance doesn't.

The ridge has a constant wind, a dry hiss over the scree. At the gap it stops along a line so fine that a head can straddle it, one ear in the wind and one in stillness. Inside, sounds are made but arrive reduced. A boot scraping the floor is heard as a single grain shifting. Every few hours something in the hollow ticks, once, and it is impossible to say from where.

The mouth faces north and never receives the sun. Even so the interior is lit, dimly, by a grey-amber light that lies low across the floor. It comes from the back wall, where there is no opening, and throws shadows toward the entrance. It is the same at midnight as at noon. It looks like late afternoon, and like one particular afternoon. The working guess is that the hollow took in a single day's light long ago and has been spending it since, at a rate meant to last. Over decades it has dimmed by an amount instruments would argue about.

Counted clockwise from the entrance, the hollow has five faces. Counted anticlockwise, it has four. Neither count is a mistake. The fifth face is narrow, with a vein of white quartz running diagonally across it, and it exists in one order only. A hand can be laid on it. Go around the other way and its two neighbours meet at a plain edge with nothing between them.

It is three paces from the mouth to the back wall. The return is the same three paces and takes considerably longer. Nothing feels slow while it happens, but the shadows on the scree outside have moved further than three paces can account for.

A fine pale dust, feldspar ground to flour, covers the floor to the thickness of a fingernail. A second layer lies in the air along the east wall at chest height. It is a level band a hand wide, resting on nothing, with a small drift heaped at one end the way dust heaps on a shelf. A hand passed beneath it finds no surface. A finger drawn through it leaves a groove that takes a season to close.

Near the center of the floor is a pebble of dark hornblende the size of a knuckle. It is also near the back wall. Both have the same chip and the same hairline crack, because they are one pebble. If one is moved, the other moves about an hour later, in the mirrored direction.

The cold is far below freezing and perfectly even, floor to ceiling, hour to hour. It behaves less like weather than like a property of the room, the way grey is a property of the rock. Breath doesn't fog, since there is nothing for it to condense from. A bare hand stays warm far longer than it should and then is cold all at once. The hollow collects heat in single payments.

There are three made things.

- **The ring.** Set flush in the floor is a ring a forearm across, of a grey material that is not metal, glass or ceramic. There is no seam. The crystal grain of the gneiss runs up to its edge and stops. No dust lies inside it.

- **The filament.** Near the west wall something thinner than a hair runs from floor to ceiling. It can't be seen directly. It is known by the tiny clean circle where the dust parts around its base, and by the way the old light bends a fraction in passing it. A finger moved through it meets no resistance and comes out slightly to the left of where it went in.

- **The plate.** In the back wall at knee height is a dark plate the size of a palm, bearing a single line that is still being drawn. The line lengthens by about the width of a grain each year. So far it is a stroke with the beginning of a turn.

There are no tool marks, no fittings and no footprints older than a visitor's own. Either whoever placed these things cut no stone, or the hollow itself is the thing that was built and the three objects are only the parts that show.

The place has been like this for a long time and has worn into its condition. The edges where the walls meet are rounded, though no water has ever run here and the wind doesn't enter. The rounding is deepest where the room disagrees with itself most. The edges flanking the fifth wall are as smooth as river stones, while the entrance, where the rules are ordinary, is still sharp. Rock that has to hold two arrangements at once seems to give up a grain now and then, and the dust on the floor is what comes off.

The dust doesn't accumulate. It creeps toward the ring at a millimeter or so a year, and at the ring's edge it is gone. Wear and removal have come into balance. The layer was a fingernail thick long ago and will be a fingernail thick long from now.

Seen from directly above, the grey surface of the ring has depth. Inside it, small and exact, is a hollow with a floor of pale dust and low amber light coming from its back wall. A band of dust hangs along one side, and set in the floor is a ring the size of a lentil. The light down there is slightly brighter, because it is earlier there, with more of the afternoon left.

The tick that sounds every few hours is a grain going over the edge.

And in the hollow on the mountain, the hanging band along the east wall gains a grain now and then, though nothing in the room accounts for where it comes from.
