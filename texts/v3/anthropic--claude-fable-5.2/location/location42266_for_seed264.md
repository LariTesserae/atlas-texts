The basin lies a little below the level of the country around it, a shallow pan some twenty kilometres across. Its rim has worn down so far that from the middle it is only a darker line at the foot of the haze. What were once scarps are now long ramps of rubble that found their angle of rest and kept it. The floor is hardpan, iron-cemented gravel with stones shouldered together into a pavement and varnished brown-black. Pale aprons of sinter spread wherever warm water comes up.

The warmth comes from underneath. The ground is warm to the palm everywhere, warmer near the vents, and the air sits close to the temperature of breath. Over the basin hangs a thin lid of cloud made from its own steam. It is high and bright enough that the light beneath is ordinary daylight, a little softened, with shadows blurred at the edges. The lid does not thicken or break. What condenses from it comes down as a fine wetting, less than rain, enough to keep the pavement dark and to stand in the hollows of the stones.

The vents are far apart, a kilometre or more of open floor between one and the next. Each is a throat in the rock lipped with yellow and grey crust, and each works on a stroke: a held pause, a hard exhalation of steam, the clatter of water falling back. The interval never varies. Neighbouring vents fire in a fixed order, so the sound travels across the basin and comes back around, the way a firing sequence travels down a line of cylinders. Close to a vent the stroke is felt in the chest, and speech would have to be shouted. Out on the flats the strokes merge into a working drone, and under the drone there is a lower tone that seems to come from the ground itself and holds a single pitch.

That tone may belong to the current. Sulphide ore lies under the wet floor, its upper parts in rusted ground and its roots in brine, and a body of ore set like that behaves as a cell in a battery. The basin is charged, weakly, all the time. In certain pools a submerged hand prickles. Loose black grains of magnetite stand on the pavement in combed lines, every line brushed the same direction, and the damp never disturbs them.

Toward the middle the bedrock is cut by joints so straight and evenly spaced that the brine running in them moves as if in troughs. It is blue-green, warm, and tastes of coins. Where it crosses beds of iron-rich cobbles, copper comes out of solution and skins the stones in bright metal, so the pans are floored with what look like new castings, wet and salmon-pink. The iron inside each cobble dissolves as the copper arrives. In time a stone is all shell, collapses into grit, and washes on, and another cobble works loose from the bank into its place. The pans hold as many plated stones as they ever held.

Everything here keeps that kind of account. The sinter terraces grow at the lip and dissolve at the foot at the same rate, so their steps never advance. The pools carry one waterline each, a single crisp mark, with no banding above or below it because the level has never stood anywhere else. The steam going up equals the wet coming down. The air smells of struck flint and sulphur, and the smell does not strengthen or fade.

Nothing grows that would be called a plant. At the margins of the cooler pools there are films, orange and a dull grey-green, thin as a coat of paint and slick underfoot. Grazing them, rarely, is the basin's one visible animal, a flat crawler the size of a thumbnail with its back crusted in the same mineral as the stone it sits on. A long walk might turn up three or four. They rest with their bodies set along the lines the magnetite draws, all facing the same way. Now and then a bird crosses high under the cloud, travelling from one side of the rim to the other, and does not come down.

All the joints and channels end at the sump, the lowest ground in the basin. It is a lake of dense brine about a kilometre wide, the colour of strong tea, smoking faintly across its whole surface. It receives everything and gives it back to the lid as steam. Its shore is clean pavement with no strand lines and no drift. From there the far vents can be heard working through their order, one after another across the open floor, until the sequence returns to the nearest and begins again on the same beat.
