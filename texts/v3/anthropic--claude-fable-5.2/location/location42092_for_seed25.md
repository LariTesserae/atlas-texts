The pan lies low on the night side, a basin of old limestone a day's walk across, sunk just far enough below the surrounding country that cold air and noise both pool in it. No sun has ever stood over it. Along the windward rim there is a band of dull rust, the day leaking around the curve of the world, and that is the only fixed light. It gives the sky an edge and nothing more.

The stone finished weathering long ago. It lies in broad, rounded pavements split by fissures an arm deep, with every edge blunted and every surface dished into shallow basins. Rain does not fall here. Warm wind from the lit hemisphere cools as it slides down into the basin and gives up its water directly onto the rock, so the stone sweats. Runnels as thin as fingers feed the basins, the basins overflow into the fissures, and the fissures take the water down to somewhere that returns nothing. The air sits a few degrees above freezing and smells of wet chalk, ammonia and crushed shell.

Nothing here is green or ever was. Where plants would stand, animals stand instead. The fan-bearers are rooted by a muscular foot into every crack, each raising a stiff combed fan as tall as a person's chest into the wind. They strain it for what the day side sheds: spores, scales, dried scum, the dust of a living country they will never face. They cover the slabs shoulder to shoulder in their millions, a standing crop of meat. Every fan is a reed, and the wind plays all of them at once. The sound is a single enormous chord, felt in the teeth and the fluid of the eye before the ear accepts it as sound. When a fan clogs, its owner claps it shut to shed the sludge, a crack like a split board. There is never a moment when thousands are not clapping.

That is only the floor of the noise. Shelled walkers the size of fists pour between the fan-stalks in rivers, eating the shed sludge. They keep their spacing by clicking, and their paths are troughs worn ankle-deep into the limestone and polished like the inside of a bowl. The basins seethe with pale swimmers so thick that the water sounds like something frying. Fliers work the air above the fans in stacked layers, each layer screaming at its own pitch so as not to hear the others' echoes. They are so numerous that their wings add a second weather to the wind.

Some slabs lie thin over hollows and ring when struck. Heavy-limbed animals squat on them and drum, and each slab has one note. The oldest are worn into saddles where generations have beaten the same spot. Everything that moves here steers by those notes. The pan is mapped in pitch, and a creature knows where it is by which tones reach it from which side.

Silence is the one thing that carries information. When a hunter moves through the fan-fields, the fans fold ahead of it and the chord develops a hole, a furrow of quiet sliding across the dark. The walkers break from it like water from a keel. One long, low kind of hunter has learned this. It hums as it comes, a fair imitation of the fans it is flattening, and it arrives inside a patch of sound that seems whole.

The wind is not constant. Every so often the far weather shifts and it slackens. The fans fold in a wave from rim to rim, and the chord drops out to uncover everything beneath it: the clicking, the frying water, the gnawing, the drums. Without the cover it sounds louder. The stone dries in patches, the walkers mob the basins, and things that hid in the fissures come up to feed on the folded fans. Then a thin note starts at the windward edge and the field opens to meet it.

The dead are stripped where they fall. Their shells wash into the fissures and settle, and the pan lays down new stone from its own animals about as fast as the sweat dissolves the old. It has held this shape, at this volume, for longer than the paths have been worn.
