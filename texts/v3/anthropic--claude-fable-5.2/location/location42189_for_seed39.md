The basin sits at about 5,400 metres, and a good part of it is younger than a human lifetime. Twenty years ago this floor was under ice. Now it is three kilometres of unsorted rubble running from the glacier's snout down to a rock lip where the valley falls away. The boulders are the size of trucks, the gravel is sharp-edged, and the grey silt is as fine as flour. Nothing has been rounded and nothing has been sorted. The stones lie where the ice dropped them, some balanced on others at angles that will not last. There is no soil. A line runs along both valley walls, level as a bathtub ring. Above it the rock is mottled with old lichen in yellow-green and black. Below it the rock is clean and pale, scratched in parallel grooves by the ice.

Water is everywhere in small amounts. The glacier leaks from its whole front edge. The meltwater has not settled on a channel yet, so it runs in a dozen braided threads that shift from week to week, abandoning one bed and cutting another through the silt overnight. Between the threads the ground is saturated, and a boot goes in to the ankle and comes out grey. The rock walls weep from every joint. Ponds sit in the hollows, milky turquoise or the brown of tea, depending on whether the glacier still feeds them. The largest lies against the ice front itself, and every few days a slab of ice drops into it and turns over.

The glacier is dirty, and most of the grey on it is soot rather than rock. No machine has ever been in this basin. There is no road, no cairn, no cable, no wall of stacked stones. But the engines of the plains have reached it anyway. Their smoke rises, drifts for a thousand kilometres, and settles here as a fine dark film that drinks sunlight and speeds the melt. The ice surface is pocked with thumb-wide holes where dark grit has sunk in. On most days a brown band of haze lies below the lip of the valley, flat-topped like a liquid. Aircraft cross overhead on a fixed corridor, four or five an hour, drawing white lines that spread and merge with the high cloud. On clear nights the southern horizon carries a faint orange stain from cities far below it. The basin is the product of industry in the way a tailings pond is, made at a distance by people who have not seen it.

Plants are present but have to be looked for. A dark crust of algae and cyanobacteria films the wettest silt, the first living skin on the place. Moss lines the seeps in bright green threads. Farther from the ice, where the ground has had fifteen or twenty years, there are cushions: fist-sized domes of sandwort, a saxifrage pressed flat to its stone, a woolly thistle wrapped in its own hair like a wad of lint. A few tufts of sedge stand at the pond margins. Often there are fifty metres of bare rubble between one plant and the next.

The animals outnumber the plants many times over, which seems wrong until it is clear what feeds them. Every afternoon the valley wind climbs from the lowlands carrying aphids, flies, small moths, and ballooning spiderlings, and the cold drops them here. They land torpid or dead on the snow patches and the wet stones. The basin lives on this fallout. Springtails gather on old snow in such numbers that they look like spilled soot, until the patch flexes and moves. Small dark jumping spiders work the rubble, and wolf spiders run the pond edges. Midges hatch from the meltwater in the short warm part of the day and hang in loose columns over the shallows. Fairy shrimp row on their backs through the clear ponds. In the grit-holes on the glacier there are rotifers and water bears, a whole community in a cup of meltwater.

Birds walk the snow margins picking the day's delivery off the surface. There are snowfinches in loose flocks of forty or more, accentors, a redstart with a white cap that holds each seep as a territory, and horned larks. Choughs come through in tumbling gangs. A pair of ruddy shelducks has taken the largest clear pond and leads a string of ducklings across it. Snowcocks work uphill on foot through the boulders in the morning and glide back down at dusk.

Pikas have reached the older moraine and are building hay piles from the few cushions and sedges, clipping stems and laying them on flat stones to dry. A mountain weasel hunts them through the gaps between boulders, thin enough to follow wherever they go. Blue sheep come down the walls in files of twenty or thirty to lick the white mineral bloom that the seeps leave on the rock. They are nearly the colour of the scree, and a slope seems empty until part of it stands up. Fox scat, full of pika fur, marks prominent rocks. Once in a while a line of large round prints crosses a snow patch from one wall to the other without pausing, tail drag beside it. A lammergeier quarters the basin most days, low and unhurried.

For all this life, the basin feels empty. The animals are small or far off, the air is half as thick as at sea level, and there are long reaches of rubble where nothing moves. Everything alive is concentrated along the water and the snow edges.

The day runs on a schedule. Dawn is hard-frozen and silent, with the streams reduced to a trickle under shells of ice and the silt crusted stiff. Direct sun reaches the floor late and only for a few hours, because the walls are high. In that window the ice shells collapse, the threads of water swell and turn opaque, the midges rise, and the birds spread out. By early afternoon cloud comes up the valley from below, pours over the lip, and closes the basin under a low grey lid. The light goes flat and dim, with no shadows. Often it snows in soft pellets that bounce off the rocks and are gone within the hour. The streams peak near dusk, hours after the warmth that fed them, then fall back as the cold takes hold.

It is quiet, though seldom completely silent. Water runs as a constant low hiss from all directions. Stones click as the ground settles. The glacier cracks sharply, then says nothing for an hour. Now and then a section of moraine wall, standing steeper than loose debris can hold, lets go with a short rattle and sends a plume of dust across the floor. Snowfinches chitter, a pika gives its one-note call, and the shelducks honk. A jet's sound arrives long after the jet has gone, already fading.

In autumn there is one loud week. Cranes and bar-headed geese come up the valley in skeins, circling to gain height, and cross the col at the basin's head, calling the whole way. Some set down on the ponds for a night. Then they are gone, the ponds skin over, and the basin returns to water sounds and a little less ice than it had the year before.
