Near the top of a cinder cone young enough that its slopes still slide underfoot, there is a pit about eighty metres across, filled to the lip with water. It has no shore and no shallows. The walls drop straight down as columnar basalt, hexagonal pipes packed side by side, their edges still sharp enough to open a hand. Skins of black glass show where the lava chilled fast. Nothing has had time to round off. The scree at the rim is all corners.

The water is cold, about nine degrees, and tinted the blue-green of old bottle glass by dissolved copper. The copper is why nothing green lives here. There is no weed, no algal slick, no stain at the waterline. The only growth that isn't animal is a white bacterial felt on the vents, thin as breath on a mirror.

The air is thin at this height and the sun comes down hard. The water is so clear that the floor, forty metres down, is lit like a room with good windows. From the rim you can see everything at once, and everything is occupied.

You hear the pit before you see it. On the approach it arrives through the boots as a slam, deep and flat, like a press coming down, once every eleven seconds. This is water hammer. Gas and cool brine shift through the cracked columns under the floor, a slug of pressure meets a constriction, and the mountain knocks. The interval does not vary. At the rim the sound is in your sternum.

The surface registers it too. Instead of ripples it holds a fine, standing grid of dimples that tightens and loosens with the pitch of the noise below. Insects that fall onto it are shaken along the grid lines and gather at the still points in a neat array of dark dots. Nothing eats them. Nothing here takes food from above.

Everything in the pit came up from below, through the plumbing, and it has sorted itself into layers.

**The walls.** Every column face is covered edge to edge with lidded shells the size of a thumbnail, tens of thousands of them, each with a hinged plate. On the knock they open. A moment later they clap shut, and the clap travels down the wall as a visible band, a pale flicker sinking from the surface to the floor in about four seconds. The sound is a long tearing rattle, like a chain running out through a hawsehole. Then eleven seconds pass and the next knock comes.

**The middle water.** Here hangs the wheel, a ring twenty metres across made of blind eels with copper-coloured flanks, swimming nose to tail and three or four deep. There are perhaps two thousand of them. The ring turns once between one sunrise and the next. You cannot see it move, only that it has moved. The eels drum their swim bladders at a single shared pitch, and the result is a steady drone like a transformer behind a fence. A compass held at the rim does not point north. It follows the wheel, creeping round the dial over the course of the day.

**The floor.** A dozen sulfide chimneys stand here, from waist height to twice the height of a person, each leaking a lukewarm shimmer that never reaches the surface. They grow about a finger's width a year. Each carries a real voltage between the fluid in its throat and the lake outside, and the animals know it.

Pale segmented crawlers stand on the chimneys in lines that follow the field the way filings follow a magnet. At the vent mouth, one takes a grain of fresh mineral. It passes the grain to the next, mouth to mouth, and that one to the next, and each mouth leaves a coat on it. At the end of the line, where the voltage falls away, the last crawler lets go of a finished bead. It is brass-bright, about the size of a lentil, and matches every other bead on the floor.

The beads lie in drifts, in places a metre deep. Small clawed shrimp live in the drifts in enormous numbers, and they strike each bead to knock loose the bacterial film they feed on. Each strike is a crack. Together they make a continuous crackle, a riveting-shed noise that fills every gap between the knock, the rattle and the drone.

No one made any of this. There is no tool mark in the pit, no cairn on the rim, no path worn to it. It still runs like a works, with a stroke, a flywheel, a line and a product. No one is there to collect the product, and there is no off-shift.

For all the noise, almost nothing moves. A bead released at the top of a chimney falls straight down. The water does not circulate, and the temperature is the same at the lip as at the floor, except in the hand's breadth around each vent. The sound travels and the water stays put.

Change comes in long steps:

- **Chimneys:** when one grows too tall, its throat clogs. Over a season its line of crawlers thins and reassembles on a neighbour.
- **The wheel:** it drifts to centre on whichever vent is strongest. The move takes years.
- **The floor:** the beads pile up, raising it by about a bead's thickness in a decade.

The pit is slowly filling with what it makes. At some point far off, the brass shot will have pushed the water up over the rim. It will run down the cinder slope in a thin green thread, and the knock will sound nearer the surface than it does now.
