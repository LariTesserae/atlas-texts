Under the coastal plain, where the limestone stands only a few meters above the sea, there is a flooded chamber wide enough to hold a town. The roof is low, two or three body-lengths above the floor in most places and rising here and there into shallow domes. Water fills it wall to wall and floor to ceiling, with no pocket of air anywhere. Heat comes up through the floor from seeps that taste of iron and sulfur, and it holds the whole basin close to the temperature of blood.

It is dark. On the landward edge a single sinkhole opens to the forest, and at midday a column of grey-blue comes down through it and gives out after ten meters or so. Within that column a thin green film grows on the rock, and tree roots hang through the opening in long fringed curtains, far from their leaves. That is all the plant life the chamber has. Everything beyond is black, and the animals there have small eyes or none.

They have built a city. The oldest builders are worms, each no longer than a finger, which cement sand grains into tubes with a glue from their own bodies. One tube is slight, but there are millions, stacked and fused into towers taller than a person and as thick as a tree trunk. Each tower leans a few degrees seaward, into the direction the food comes from. They stand in ranks, spaced by the reach of the worms' feeding tentacles, so that the gaps between them are remarkably even. Bivalves have settled in stepped terraces along the bases, shell on shell. Pale sponges fill the spaces between towers and join them into walls. In the mud at the foot of the walls, shrimp dig galleries with mounded doorways, and small fish line their vertical wells with pebbles and shell fragments chosen one at a time and carried in the mouth.

The ceiling is built on too. The water is layered: rain seeping through the rock floats as a fresher lens on top, and the sea lies heavy and salt below. The boundary between them can be felt as a sudden change in how the body floats and a faint oiliness against the skin. Above it, amphipods spin tubes of silk and silt that hang from the roof by the thousand like soft pendants, and tube snails lay down crusts of calcite between them. In some districts the hanging city and the standing city come within a hand's width of each other across the boundary. Neither can cross it.

Twice a day the sea pushes in through passages on the seaward side, and twice a day it withdraws. The tide always takes the same lines through the chamber, and along those lines the floor is scoured to bare rock. These are the avenues, straight and smooth, with tower-fronts along both sides. Away from them the flow slackens into wide still places where silt gathers ankle-deep and brittle stars lie arm over arm.

When the tide turns inward, every tube opens. Feeding fans come out along the whole length of an avenue in a wave that travels at the speed of the water. For a few hours the city strains the sea, and what leaves on the ebb is cleaner than what arrived. At slack water the fans withdraw and the building starts. Sand is scarce here, so grains are pried from dead tubes and carried to living ones, and most of the city has been built many times from the same material.

There are hunters. White eels the length of an arm lie in abandoned towers with their heads at the openings and read the avenues through their skin. In the still plazas, flat fish wait under the silt. Remipedes, long and many-legged, swim on their backs just under the ceiling and pick amphipods from their tubes. The architecture answers them: entrances are narrow, the worms carry lids, the fishes' wells have a stone to pull across.

A touch on one tower makes it close, and the closing spreads outward to its neighbors and theirs, a shiver of withdrawal that crosses a whole district in a few seconds. A minute later the district reopens in the same order. An eel moving up an avenue can be followed by the dark, shut band that travels with it.

There is little to hear. Snapping shrimp crack singly, like a twig breaking in another room. Urchins rasp at the terrace edges. Occasionally a fish makes a short low croak from inside its well and is answered from somewhere across the plaza. At the turn of the tide, close to a tower, there is a faint dry hiss of fans sliding out of their tubes. Once in many years a slab lets go of the roof and the whole basin hears it.

After storms, the sinkhole delivers. Fresh water comes down brown with tannin, carrying leaves, drowned beetles and the occasional frog. The upper lens thickens and presses the boundary downward, and the tops of the tallest towers, caught in water too fresh for them, die back by a hand's length. Then the litter sinks, and for a week the plazas seethe with isopods and the eels are fat.

The city stands on itself. Beneath the living tubes are dead ones, and beneath those the tubes have collapsed and fused into a pale stone that still shows a honeycomb grain where it is broken. That foundation is several meters deep in the old quarters, and it settles under its own weight about as fast as the top grows. So the gap between the standing city and the hanging one stays at about a hand's width.
