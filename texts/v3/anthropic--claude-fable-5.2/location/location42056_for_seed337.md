From the plain there is almost nothing to see: short grass, a grey sky moving steadily east, and a low white bank that could be the wall of a sheepfold. Smoke gives the place away first, thirty or forty thin threads rising straight out of the ground. Closer, the bank turns out to be heaped chalk rubble, tipped there basket by basket and still loose enough to shift underfoot. You climb it and the town is below you.

All of it is cut. Nobody here has raised a wall. The chalk lies a hand's depth under the turf, soft enough when fresh to take an antler tine, and the people have gone down into it the way others go up with timber. The lanes are trenches twice a person's height. The courtyards are square pits, and off every courtyard the rooms run back into solid white. From the bank it looks like a honeycomb with the lid off, several hundred cells filling a shallow basin that takes a slow half hour to walk around.

The partitions between pits are an arm's length thick and no more. Their flat tops serve as a second set of streets, narrow causeways where children and basket-carriers cross the lanes on planks. Below, every face is in use. There are doors hung with hide, lamp niches, peg holes, bins, stairs cut into the corners, and benches left standing when the floor was lowered around them. Notched trunks lean wherever a stair would not fit. There is no spare wall, and space is gained only by digging deeper or by starting a new pit at the edge.

The surfaces are all recent. The antler pick leaves short curved gouges in rows, the ox shoulder-blade leaves wider scoops, and both are still legible on every wall, so that a left-handed digger's room can be told at a glance. Step edges are square. In the oldest quarter, where somebody's grandmother began the work, the walls have gone faintly grey-green and the treads are only starting to dish. In the east nothing has had time to happen. The chalk there is white enough to make the diggers squint when the cloud thins, and the floors hold every footprint in dust.

Two bands of black flint run through the town, one at knee height and one at the shoulder, level from lane to lane like courses of dark stone. Where the nodules come loose they go to the flint yard. Where they hold they are left as knobs, and coils of rope and wet cloaks hang from them. The town owns one copper adze, which is lent by the day and used for the door-frames.

Rain comes in passing showers and the chalk drinks it. The town turns the grey of wet ash, causeways first and then the walls, and over the next hour it pales again in patches. Gutters a thumb deep run along the lane floors to cisterns lined with puddled clay, since bare chalk holds nothing. In the mornings the lower walls sweat and the deep rooms smell of wet stone and tallow. On the north side a seam of marl weeps a steady line, and jars stand under it in a row with a fern above them. It is the only fern in the town.

At the lowest court a shaft goes straight down, with a forked post and a hide rope over it. It has been three winters in the digging. The chalk that comes up now is darker and cold to the hand, and it clings to the basket, but it is not yet water.

Little else grows. Moss has started in the gutters of the old quarter, and nettles have found the outer slope of the spoil bank. One household keeps onions in a trough of carried soil and moves it through the day to follow the light, which comes down evenly off the white walls and leaves only pale shadows.

For a place so full of people it has few animals. Banded snails gather on the damp faces. A pair of jackdaws has spent the season inspecting an unused niche without settling in it. Dogs are few and stay on the causeways.

Cold air settles in the lanes and stays there, and breath shows until midday. The rooms are the same temperature at noon and at midnight.

The town is quiet because the chalk swallows sound. A pick going in makes a noise like a knuckle on a loaf. Feet in hide shoes make none. Voices stay low out of habit, with a neighbour's bed an arm's length through the wall. The sharpest sound is the click of the knappers in the flint yard, which carries across the whole basin.

Under the click, if you wait, there is knocking. Before anyone deepens a room they rap on the wall and listen for the rap that comes back, and they judge the thickness by its tone.

The work goes on all day. Baskets go up the ramp full and come down empty, and the bank grows by a stride or so each season.

Past the last courtyard on the eastern edge, pegs and cord mark out six more squares on the unbroken grass. The turf has been lifted from one of them and stacked beside it. The exposed chalk is a hand deep and already scored with its first row of gouges, and a basket has been left standing in the middle.
