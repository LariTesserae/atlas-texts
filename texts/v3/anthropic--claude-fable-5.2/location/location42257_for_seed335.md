The flat country above gives no warning. It is alder carr a few metres above sea level: black standing water between tussocks, bare March branches, and sleet coming sideways out of a sky the colour of wet slate. The first sign is the sound, a low steady drone that seems to come from the ground and that the feet find before the ears do. Then the trees stop.

The hollow opened three winters ago, in one night. It is perhaps thirty paces across and half that deep, and its edges have not had time to soften. The turf at the rim overhangs like broken crust, and alder roots dangle from it into the air, dripping. Below the turf is a band of stony brown till. Below that the mudstone is broken along its own joints into clean blue-grey faces, with corners still sharp enough to cut and rust only starting to bleed along the cracks. Two alders rode the plug of ground down when it fell. They stand on a low island of rubble in the middle of the pool, tilted toward each other and still alive, with catkins out.

Water enters all the way round. Where the till meets the rock the carr leaks into the hollow through a ring of seeps: threads, sheets, and two real cascades that fall clear of the wall and hit the pool hard. Every wet line has gone green. Moss has taken the seep-paths first, then liverwort in flat overlapping scales, then hart's-tongue fern and mats of golden saxifrage on the ledges where grit has collected. Willow seedlings no longer than a finger have rooted in the till. Between the wet lines the rock is still bare, so the walls are striped with emerald and raw stone.

Most of the place is air. The life is a skin on the walls and a ring at the waterline, and the middle is a dim empty column with sleet falling through it onto black water. A jackdaw crossing from ledge to ledge spends two full seconds in nothing. The light comes from one disc of overcast sky. It reaches the bottom grey and thin, and for most of the day the fog thins it further.

The fog comes from the crack. At the foot of the east wall the mudstone has parted along a fissure a hand wide and taller than a person, and the fissure breathes. Somewhere far below and off to the side there are workings, with fans, pumps, and belts running rock toward a shaft miles from here. None of that is visible. There is no fence, no post, no painted mark; the hollow is something the industry did without knowing it. But the air the fans push finds its way up through the broken ground and out of the crack at one unchanging temperature. It is cool, though warmer than March, and it smells of stone dust and faintly of oil. Where it meets the cold it turns white and rolls across the pool.

The drone arrives with it. The hollow is shaped like a bottle and the fans blow across its neck, so the whole space hums at one pitch. In certain spots along the wall, an arm's length apart, the hum swells until it presses on the chest or nearly vanishes. Over the hum is the roar of the cascades. Over that, in this season, are the frogs, hundreds of them packed in the shallows on the rubble island and purring. The walls throw the sound back until it seems to come from the rock. The jackdaws on the ledges have to shout over all of it. About every forty minutes something heavy passes underneath and a long shudder comes up through the stone. Loose drips fall all at once, a few pebbles tick down from the till, and the frogs go silent for a breath before starting again.

The pool has a tide, though the sea is nowhere near. The pumps below run hardest at night, and by dawn the water has dropped by the height of a tall child. That leaves a band of black mud, bleached snail shells, and stranded diving beetles rowing in puddles. Through the day it climbs back. The animals have learned this faster than seems reasonable. A heron comes down through the fog at first light and works the falling edge, taking newts from the shrinking pockets. Wrens go over the fresh mud for whatever is stuck in it.

In the first spring the frogs spawned at the high line, and the spawn hung drying on the rock by morning. Now the clumps that survive are the ones laid in the raft of dead alder twigs that floats around the island and rises and falls with the water. Each year more frogs lay there.

At the lips of the crack the mild air keeps gnats flying in weather that should have killed them, and they hang there in a smoke that the wrens raid by day. At dusk the bats come out of the fissure itself. They roost down in the constant air, and they emerge in ones and twos, then by dozens. They circle up the empty column and scatter over the rim into the carr, while the jackdaws drop in past them to roost.

Twice a day, when something changes far below, the breath reverses. The fog stops spreading, hesitates, and slides backward across the water into the crack, taking the gnats with it. The hum drops a tone. For a few minutes the hollow is clear to the bottom, and the cold comes down the walls so fast that the highest seeps glaze over. Then the fans come back, the fog pours out again, and the ice lets go in small bright plates that slide down the moss into the pool.

The rim is still moving. Each thaw takes another bite of turf, and a third alder now leans out over the edge with half its roots in air. The next fall will give the island more rubble, the frogs more shallows, and the seeps new stone to turn green.
