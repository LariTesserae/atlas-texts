The saddle lies between two summits that are not finished. The stone here is too new to have a surface in the weathered sense. Every edge is the edge it broke with, grey-black and glassy on the fracture, and in places still warm to the palm. There is no scree, no soil, no gully, because nothing has come down yet. The slabs tilt up toward the two peaks and simply stop, at heights anyone can see are provisional.

The city was here first. It spreads across six kilometres of the saddle in pieces: a rolling mill with a roof of riveted plate, a coke battery, two gasholders, a pump house, the cable station, and brick terraces of housing stepping up the eastern slab. Between each lies a quarter-hour's walk of bare rock, crossed only by pipe-runs on iron trestles and the long sag of the cableway. Its buckets come up through the cloud deck full of coal and brick and go down empty. No one in the terraces has been to the bottom station. The arc lamps along the trestles are spaced so far apart that each one lights its own small island, and the dark between them belongs to the mountain.

It is always some version of dusk. The sky at noon is close to black, with a few stars that do not twinkle. The sun does not climb this high. It crosses somewhere below, over the cloud three thousand metres down, and the day arrives secondhand, a cold pearl light rising off the cloud tops. So shadows lie upward. The undersides of eaves and trestles and the bellies of the cable buckets are the bright parts, and the roofs are dark. The furnace mouths add their orange at ground level, and the faces of anyone walking between them are lit from the chin.

In the evening the cloud comes up through the works and leaves itself on the iron. This is the only water. It beads on rivets, runs down stanchions, and drips from the trestles in lines. Where it reaches the rock it has nowhere to go, because no channel has been cut in this stone and the water does not seem to know which way down is. It stands on forty-degree slabs in shallow domed sheets, trembling with the mill's vibration. Sometimes it creeps sideways or a little upslope before it is drunk or freezes. The tin gutters and canvas catch-sheets rigged along the housing are the only rivers the mountain has had.

Green follows the leaks. Under every pipe-run is a strip of moss a hand deep, exactly as wide as the drip line and ruled across the rock like a drawing. There are liverworts at the joints and ferns wherever a steam trap vents. On the lee side of the coke battery the slag is warm and the soot has made a sort of soil, and there dwarf willow, saxifrage cushions and a thicket of rhododendron with leathery, sooty leaves grow shoulder-high. It is lush in the manner of a greenhouse someone forgot to wall. Ten paces off the line there is nothing, not even lichen, because the rock is younger than lichen can use.

The animals are everywhere the green is, and in places it is not. Choughs nest by the thousand in the mill's roof trusses and pour out at each shift change. Pikas have tunnelled the pipe lagging and cure their hay of moss and willow on the warm flanges. Ibex walk the trestle tops in file, licking condensate and rust-salt from the rivet heads. Marmots have taken the void under the boiler house and whistle at the hammer. Pale moths drift against each arc lamp until they bank up at the post like snow, and bats work the drifts. Snowcock call from the gasholder rims.

Some of the animals stand on what is not there yet. Out past the end of the eastern slab, where the rock stops and the spur to the higher summit has not arrived, a dozen ibex are usually bedded in the open air at the height the spur will have. They chew, and their legs are folded on nothing. Pikas run along ledges that are due and vanish into holes that are due. The choughs give the unfinished cliffs a wide berth, banking around rock faces that are still only cold air.

The city is built the same way. The newest terrace hangs in a steel frame eleven metres above the slab, with its doorsteps opening on a drop and its chimneys smoking. Brass survey marks are fixed on posts at the level the ground is expected to reach. It reaches them, at about a doorstep a year. The older terraces were hung like this once and are now seated. Moss was already growing on the undersides of their thresholds when the rock came up to press it.

The rising makes a sound, a continuous grind beneath everything, low enough to be felt in the teeth and the water sheets. Now and then a slab parts somewhere with a crack like a rifle. Over that the mill is loud with the roll stands, the steam hammer's double beat and the shriek of the cable sheaves. The shift horn carries across the whole saddle in the thin air. Its echo comes back late, and it comes back wrong. It is deeper and longer, returned off the two summits at their finished heights, from faces a kilometre above anything that exists. The choughs ignore the horn. It is the echo they rise to, all at once and bright from underneath, and they circle the place where the peak will be.
