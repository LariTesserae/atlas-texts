**The Low Lift**

Four lanes run downhill into the yard and stop there. Each ends in an iron grating as wide as a cart, and under each grating the water of the town above arrives: roof water, gutter water, the seep of cellar walls, brown and warm from travelling under streets. This is the lowest ground in the district. Everything that falls on the slopes ends up here, and the yard exists to send it back.

It is a small place. The cobbles run sixty paces one way and perhaps forty the other, walled on every side by pump halls of blue engineering brick. Above those rise the backs of other buildings, warehouses and works stacked up the slope with their windows dark. Over the court someone long ago threw a roof of iron trusses and glass, and nearly all the glass is still there. Algae has taken the inside of every pane, so the daylight that gets through is the colour of the bottom of a pond, green-brown and shadowless, the same at noon as in late afternoon. The rest of the light belongs to the works. One sodium lamp still burns orange on its bracket inside a mane of fern. Through the hall doorways, rows of pilot lamps glow red and amber on the switchboards, small as berries.

The sound fills the court the way the water does. Each hall holds one ram pump, motor-driven, with a flywheel taller than its doorway turning twenty times a minute. Every stroke is a thud that comes up through the cobbles into the shins. Behind the thuds the transformers in their caged corner hold one flat note that never wavers. Valves clack shut. Water falls twenty feet into the sump with a roar that has no gaps in it. The glass overhead buzzes in its putty. None of this can leave, so it stacks, hall wall to hall wall, and the air in the middle of the yard feels thick with it.

The air is thick anyway. The motor casings and the transformer oil give off a steady warmth, and the water gives off the rest. Every iron surface sweats. Drips fall from the trusses at fixed points, always the same points, and each has drilled a cup in the cobble beneath it, a smooth bowl of clear water with a rim of moss. A shallow lens of water lies over the centre of the court, a hand deep. A dark line runs round the brickwork one finger above it. The water has never been seen at the line and the line has never moved.

Things grow from everything. Hart's-tongue fern hangs out of every mortar joint. Maidenhair has spread across the white tiles inside the halls, following the crazing in the glaze. Liverwort covers the lower brick in overlapping green scales. Horsetail stands in ranks in the gutters, and a willow rooted in the sump wall trails its whips in the outfall. A fig lies splayed across one gable, its roots following the old gas pipes down to the wet. The metal is layered too. Conduit is bolted over older pipe, which is bolted over older brackets, with porcelain insulators beside cast-iron cleats, and moss has packed the gaps between each generation of fittings until the walls look upholstered.

Nothing has eaten any of it. Every leaf is whole to its edge. There are no webs in the window frames and no flies over the water. No trails cross the tiles, and nothing has nested on the truss ledges, though the ledges are ideal for it. The cups and puddles hold water and nothing else. Fronds lie where they fall and go slowly to black felt under fungus and damp, so the margins of the yard are buried a forearm deep in leaf, each year's layer still legible. Bindweed on the transformer cage opens its white flowers and closes them unvisited. What spreads here spreads by spore, by runner, or by a twig snapped off and carried by water to the next crack.

The pumps have had a long time to arrange the growth. The chequer-plate floors in the halls tremble with each stroke, and moss grows on them only along the lines where the plate holds still. It makes curved, branching, symmetrical figures, a different one in each hall because each pump has its own beat. The water in the drip-cups stands in fixed ripples. The horsetails in the gutter nearest No. 3 all lean one way, away from its door. The wall ferns never stop shivering, each frond blurred at the edges, and they let their spores go in pulses.

In No. 2 a reduction shaft drives the rake that clears the intake screen. It turns once in several hours. A creeper that came in through a broken skylight was caught on that shaft years ago and has been winding onto it ever since. The shaft now carries a bale of stem as thick as a barrel, grey at the core and green at the outermost turn. It takes up the vine at the rate the vine grows. Between skylight and shaft the stem hangs in a slack curve that never tightens and never reaches the floor.

The brass on the engines is green wherever it stays still and bright wherever it moves. The oil cups are full. The nameplates have worn down to LOW LIFT, a number, and a few letters of a maker's name.

The four pumps do not run at quite the same speed. Their strokes drift apart and together over about forty minutes, mostly as a ragged gallop. Once in each cycle all four land together and the yard takes a single blow. The lens of water in the court jumps into a brief field of points and the roof gives one clear note. Every fern lets go at once, and a brown haze hangs in the green light for a minute before it settles on the water. Then the strokes begin to separate again.

A riveted main as wide as a doorway climbs the wall of the north hall and goes out through the roof toward the top of the town. The brick corners below it are worn round and the cobbles are dished. The iron has scaled as far as it is going to and has sealed over under moss. Put a hand on the main and the water can be felt going up inside it, warm, in surges of four, toward the roofs it fell from.
