The river goes underground at the bottom of a sinkhole whose walls are still raw from the collapse. The limestone is pale and sharp-edged, and broken fig roots hang over the lip. Below that point the land is all interior: a chamber large enough to hold a small town, with a ceiling lost in its own weather. Warm air rising off the floor meets the drip from the roof, and the two make a thin cloud that hangs at mid-height. The far wall is a rumor. Across the dark there is only a short string of electric bulbs, yellow and small, like a hamlet seen from the other side of a valley at night.

The sound comes first and never leaves. The river enters the chamber over a ledge and drops forty meters onto broken rock, and the cave returns the noise from every surface, so the roar has no direction. Other sounds sit on top of it. Swiftlets steer by a dry rattling click, thousands of them at once. Bats keep up a high chatter that is felt in the teeth more than heard. A generator hammers in a tin shed, and now and then the winch shrieks. No one who works here speaks inside. They use hand signs and lamp flashes, and for emergencies a steam whistle that is the only thing able to cut through the rest.

This is a young cave. The river broke into the void recently, as rock counts time, and has not yet chosen a channel. It braids across the floor between fallen blocks the size of houses, whose faces are still clean and whose corners are unrounded. There are no columns and no great draperies. The only calcite is a wet skin on the walls and a growth of soda straws on the ceiling, each a finger long with a drop at the tip. The mud is unsorted, clay and grit and cobble dumped together. Sometimes a slab lets go from the roof. Nobody hears it fall; they find it later, lying where it wasn't.

Everything is wet. The walls weep, the air condenses on skin and metal, and the bulbs wear halos. It is warm from the tropical rock, and warmer from the bodies overhead and the slow fermenting heat of what they drop.

Every surface is occupied. The roof above the cloud is covered with bats packed shoulder to shoulder, a dark fur lining that ripples when a lamp beam crosses it. Below them the swiftlets hold the walls, their nests cemented in rows as tight as scales, each rimmed with white. The floor is guano, meters deep in the hollows, and its surface moves. Cockroaches work it in such numbers that the ground seems to simmer. Pale crabs pick through it, and long-legged centipedes hunt on the rock above. Crickets with antennae three times their body length feel their way along the ledges. Racer snakes lie on the ledges near the flyways and take bats out of the air by touch. In the side pools, away from the falls, there are pink, eyeless fish, and eels that are not blind at all and eat them.

Almost nothing green grows here. Under the sinkhole, where a gray column of daylight reaches the floor for a few hours, there are ferns and a slick of liverwort, and seedlings that sprout from dropped fruit and die pale. The only other green is around the bulbs. Each lamp on the cable has grown a hand-sized halo of moss and algae on the rock beside it, a garden exactly as wide as its light.

The works are small against all this. There is a narrow-gauge track on sleepers that sink and have to be relaid, and a winch house at the foot of the incline up to the sinkhole. Iron skips carry guano out to be bagged for fertilizer. A riveted pipe taps the river above the falls and drives a turbine no bigger than a barrel, which feeds the bulbs when the diesel is down. Bamboo poles and rope ladders go up the walls for the nest harvest, which is taken only after the fledging, a rule older than the track. An earlier skip lies tipped beside the line, buried to its axles. It was abandoned thirty years ago, and its burial is the plainest measure of how fast the floor rises.

Change here is slow and mostly circular. At dusk the bats pour out through the sinkhole in a twisting rope that takes an hour to pass, while the swiftlets pour in beneath them, and at dawn the exchange reverses. In the rains the river swells and the braids merge. A line of white paint on the winch house shows the level at which everyone leaves. Between floods the mud settles a little more, the straws lengthen by the width of a hair, and the guano deepens by a thumb's breadth a year. Sometime in the season, without announcement, the river shifts a few meters toward the wall it will eventually follow.

The men come up the incline at shift's end with their ears ringing, into ordinary evening. The outside quiet seems to them a kind of thinness, as if the world above had less in it.
