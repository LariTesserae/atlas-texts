The basin was a mountain range once, and the proof is in the floor. Gneiss has been planed to within a hand's height of level for forty kilometres in every direction. Its folds are cut clean through and show as slow whorls of grey and rust-pink, the roots of peaks laid open like the rings of a stump. Nothing stands on it. The highest ground is a swell you would notice only because the water runs off it a little faster.

There is always water. It comes up through the joints more than it comes down from the sky, a brine two fingers deep that sheets the whole floor and never finds anywhere to go. It is colder than ice and stays liquid because it carries current. The ore bodies under the basin are a battery, sulphide at one pole and wet air at the other, and the film is the wire. Rime climbs any still thing left in it within minutes. That is the first rule here: whatever stops gets glazed, and whatever is glazed becomes floor.

So nothing stops. The basin is covered, plate to plate, with animals. They are low and broad as washtubs, the colour of wet slate, and they move in lanes that run parallel for kilometres, straighter than anything unmade should be. Nobody laid these lanes. There is no rail, no shed, no wire, no mark of a hand anywhere on the plain.

The lead lane scrapes. Its animals have tongues like rasps and take a plate's width of rock to grey slurry on each pass. Behind them a paler kind with comb-fringed bellies strains the slurry and passes pellets, iron shot and copper bead, so cold leaving the body that they smoke frost. Behind those comes a third kind, smaller and far more numerous, which swallows the pellets and smears them back down as film: a thin band of grey, then a thin band of pink. Nothing leaves. There is no pile and no product, only throughput.

The noise is a rolling mill's. A few million tongues work on stone, and pellets clatter like hail on sheet metal. Under both is a hum from the joints, where long blind eels lie nose to tail and discharge together at the pitch of a transformer. Every animal blows white breath from a row of pores along its flank, and some of the pores whistle. The wind crosses forty kilometres without meeting anything and adds its own flat roar. The air smells of wet coin and ozone.

The light is poor. A lid of cloud sits low enough to touch down at the rim, and the day is a brown-grey smear that travels around the edge without lifting. Most of what you see by is arcing, blue stitches where plate touches plate. That is constant, so the herd flickers like a failing tube out to the limit of sight.

Sleet comes through in bands. When one crosses, the strainers lift their fringes and take it in, and for a minute the lanes look furred. Fog condenses on the carapaces and runs off. When the pressure drops, the film rises a finger, the current strengthens, and every lane speeds up together.

Tall pale waders walk across the grain of the lanes on legs jointed higher than their backs. Their faces are hammers. They pick out the slowing animals, the ones with rime starting at the plate edges, then crack them, empty them, and leave the shells to ice over. A wader that stands too long loses a foot to the eels. Mites in the film give the water a grain like ground glass.

Look closely at what the smearers lay down. It is the gneiss itself, with the same bands and the same contorted folds. It comes out of the body already deformed, already a billion and a half years old. The scrapers take it up at exactly the rate it is laid. The basin's age is a level being held, like water in a tank with the tap and the drain both open. If the smearers ever gained on the scrapers, the floor would not get higher. It would get older. That is the second rule.

The third is that there are more animals than places for them. You can see it in the doubled outlines, two carapaces sharing one edge with a shimmer where they disagree. The excess is stored in the noise. When the din dips, in a lull of wind or a gap between sleet bands, animals arrive in the lanes from nowhere, shouldering, and the film slops over their backs. When the roar builds again they thin out to merely touching. A loud enough moment would show bare rock. There has never been one.

Twice in each grey day the hum drops a tone. After it, every animal is one lane over and facing the other way, with no interval in which any of them moved. The frost-smoke from their last pellets still hangs over where they were.

In places the scrapers have run ahead and worn the floor through its own thickness. What shows underneath is the basin again, seen from above, with the same lanes and the cloud lid lying below them, one shift behind. Pellets that drop through come up out of the film somewhere else, rising slowly and clicking against the undersides of the herd. The waders step around these places. Sometimes one stands at an edge, looking down at its own back, until its feet begin to whiten.

The rim never gets nearer. The herds filing out at the east margin are the ones filing in at the west, their plates a little more worn. The only relief anywhere on the plain is a scatter of low smooth humps, each the size of a washtub, each banded grey and pink all the way through.
