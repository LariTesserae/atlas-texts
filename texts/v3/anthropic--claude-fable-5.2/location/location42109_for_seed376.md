From the saddle at its head, the valley looks like a mill town on a freezing morning. Forty or fifty columns of white vapor stand up from the floor, lean together downwind, and flatten into a grey lid at about the height of the ridges. The eye goes looking for roofs under the plumes and finds none. There are no walls, no road, no cut stone. The floor is eight kilometers long between two worn shoulders of rock, covered in plant from side to side, and the plant is working.

The mat is several meters deep. The living top is made of cushions packed tight as upholstery, rosettes furred with silver wool, sheets of liverwort, and thick ribbed stalks. Below that is a brown felt of the recently dead, and below the felt is peat. It is black, greasy, and bedded in seams like coal. The deepest roots go down into it and feed. The valley burns its ancestors slowly and lays down new ones at the same rate, and that combustion is where the heat comes from.

The air is a few degrees under freezing. The stalks are hollow, thick as a thigh, taller than a person, and they grow in stands of several hundred. Each one breathes out air as warm and wet as a mouth's. A stand fires for a day or two, then goes grey and limp and folds into the mat, and the stand beside it comes up to replace it. The plumes hold their places on the valley floor though no stalk in them is older than a week.

Everything else moves too. Between the stands, cushions swell and slacken in rows, one after the next, each taking about ten seconds, pushing water uphill through the felt. The whole surface drifts downvalley, growing at its leading edges and dying at its trailing ones, a hand-width a minute in the fast lanes and slower along the margins. Where two lanes shear past each other, the stems twist, stretch, and part in a long wet seam that closes behind itself.

At mid-valley, where the buried floor steps down, the mat piles against its own slower front and rears up. It turns over in a green breaker ten meters high that takes most of an hour to fall. Its underside comes up into daylight, white roots and brown felt, and has greened by afternoon.

Faster than any of this, color travels. A flush of ox-blood red crosses a hectare of cushions in under a minute, and behind it a new stand begins to steam. Wool sleeves cling near those fronts. A fingertip put to a stalk gets a small bite.

No stream runs here. Cloud comes over the saddle most afternoons and the silver wool combs it out. The plume vapor refreezes downwind as rime, in white stripes of feathered crystal, and the next warm front erases them. Sumps open in the mat, round wells of tea-dark water an arm deep or much deeper. Some are skinned with ice and some are lukewarm, and none lasts longer than a week.

All of it happens without sound. Everything here is soft and wet, and the wind has nothing to rattle. Tearing, collapsing, and growing all happen into felt. The breaker falls and what arrives is a slight pressure in the ears. A voice carries a few meters. With an ear pressed to a cushion you can hear a faint seething of fluid, and that is the only noise the valley makes.

Animals are few. A small grey bird works the rime stripes for springtails and never calls. Once in a day's watching, perhaps, there is a hare asleep on a warm cushion, being carried downvalley. It wakes, lopes a hundred meters upstream, and sleeps again.

At the lower end the valley hangs above a deeper one. The mat pushes out over the lip in a thick lolling overhang and breaks off in slabs the size of barges, which drop into cloud. Nothing comes back up. A few meters of bedrock show there, grooved lengthwise and polished smooth as an old stair, already filmed with green.

The ridges are rounded and without scree. In the peat seams at the step, every layer curls the same way, so the breaker has been falling on that line since the ice left. Nothing living in the valley is older than a month, and the shape has not changed in ten thousand years.

Take a stone from the saddle and set it on the mat in the flat white light of noon. Before you have straightened up, it has a green rim and has moved the length of your boot toward the lip.
