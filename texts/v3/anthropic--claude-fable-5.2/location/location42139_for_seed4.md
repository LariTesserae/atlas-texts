At the edge of a lowland bog, a black spruce went half over in an autumn gale and caught in the branches of its neighbour. Its root plate lifted on one side like a lid raised a forearm's height. The moss carpet stretched over the gap instead of tearing, and held. Then snow sealed the lip. Underneath is a wedge-shaped room about as wide as a bed, low-ceilinged and without light, and there is a town in it.

The room is new, and everything in it shows that. The snapped roots end in pale splinters that have not greyed. Resin still comes out of them, slow in the cold, in beads. The peat wall on the hinge side keeps the exact moulds of the roots that were pulled from it, smooth channels with sharp rims that have not yet slumped. The ceiling is the underside of the tree: root, rootlet, and the brown bases of sphagnum, with more sphagnum above that and then snow. The floor is peat, which is moss that has been dead longer, and it slopes down to a pool where the water table shows through. There is no stone anywhere, and no mineral soil. Every material in the room is plant, living or dead, and a fair amount of it is undecided.

The air sits just under freezing and stays there. The pool skins over, breaks, and skins again. The walls weep. The tree is still alive on its unbroken side, and the roots that run to the trunk carry the outside cold down with them. Frost grows on those roots in soft beards that drop off under their own weight.

The people of the town stand about as tall as a spruce needle is long. They came in with the first snow. They build nothing on the floor, because the floor moves. Their houses are bulbs of woven moss stem, sealed with resin and hung from the sound roots. A root will bear only one house, and the roots are spaced as the tree spaced them, so the town is mostly air: forty-some houses hanging in the dark with long gaps between.

Across the gaps run cords, and the cords are the streets. They are twisted from rootlet fibre and stiffened with pitch, and you travel them hand over hand. Knots tell you where you are:

- **Junctions:** a double knot.
- **A house:** a splice of bark.
- **Lines down to the pool weir:** a frayed tassel.
- **Lines that pass near a spider's sheet:** a thorn tied crosswise.

There is a granary of spruce seed and dried crowberry packed into a hollowed root-end. There is a resin works where the beads are gathered and kneaded. There is a midden line that ends over the deepest part of the floor, where whatever is dropped is gone within the hour.

Fire is forbidden. Peat burns, pitch burns, and the snow lid would rot and come down. Work is done by touch, with chert flakes, thorn awls, and shears made from beetle jaws. No one in the town has seen the town. At midday, where the snow lies thinnest over the lip, one patch of black turns slightly less black. In the torn wood of the largest root, honey fungus gives off a green that can only be caught by looking beside it. That is all the light there is.

The animals outnumber everything. The floor's surface is springtails, a layer of them, grazing the fungal threads that grow across the peat overnight. When the temperature shifts by a fraction they move all at once, pouring upslope or down like slow grain. Under them the peat is stitched through with white pot-worms. Box mites, hard as seeds, walk the walls and the cords too. Travelling a street means climbing through them, lifting each hand over small armoured backs that do not hurry and do not move aside. The town nets springtails where the drifts funnel between two roots, and the nets are never empty.

Rove beetles work the midden. A stone centipede keeps a regular circuit of the east wall and is given the right of way. Money spiders hang their sheets across the open dark and stay active all winter. Wingless snow flies walk the ceiling upside down. In the big root, something soft-bodied is chewing, a rasp you feel through the wood before you hear it. Overhead, voles run galleries through the roof moss, and their passing knocks down frost and litter.

All of it is nearly silent. A million animals make less noise than the drip.

Set into the hinge wall, half in the peat, is a wood frog, frozen through. Its heart is stopped and its skin is glazed. To the town it is the size of a hall. Cords route around it and a bark splice marks it, and the springtails cross it like any other ground. It is the other reason for the fire law. On mild days the ice on it goes dull and wet, and people take the long way round.

The quiet is kept on purpose, because of the shrew. It comes through every few hours. It has to, since it starves if it stops. It hunts by whisker and by ear. First comes a pressure in the air, then a fast wet breathing, then the springtails going off beneath it, thousands of tails snapping at once with a sound like dry rain. Everyone on a cord stops and hangs still. It takes worms and beetles, and sometimes it takes a house.

The one deliberate sound is the clock. At the centre of the town a rootlet tip drips onto a stretched leaf, and a day is a fixed count of drips. In hard cold the drip slows and the days run long. When a thaw comes through the snow above, the drip quickens, the pool rises a finger's width, and the lowest cords are unknotted and rehung. On those days the springtail drifts climb the walls, the fungus puts on a visible length, and the days go by in half the time.

When wind takes the crown of the spruce far overhead, the trunk works against its neighbour and the whole lid shifts. The roots creak. Every house swings a little on its root, and the cords go slack and then tight again.
