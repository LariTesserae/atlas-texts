The plateau is too wide to have edges. Somewhere, days off in every direction, it falls away into cloud and lower country, but from the middle there is only the flat, and the flat is covered in walls.

They are dry-laid, of grey-green schist that the frost splits into slabs, and of pale quartzite cobbles from a riverbed that ran here before the land was lifted. Nothing is cut. Every stone was picked up and turned in the hands until it fit, and the walls show it: no straight courses, only a tight, patient fitting. They stand chest-high in some quarters and twice head-high in others, and the lanes between them are a shoulder's width across. The rooms are corbelled cells, each ring of slabs stepped a little inward until one flat stone closes the top. There are hundreds of thousands of them. From any slight rise the domes and wall-tops run out to the horizon like the grain of a thumbprint, and the horizon itself is made of the same stone.

The city is deeper than it looks. It has been built on its own rubble for so long that the ground of the lanes sits a few body-lengths above the bedrock. Old lanes were roofed over and became drains. The cisterns go down through three or four earlier cities, and their shafts are ringed with courses in different hands and different stone.

Water is in everything, but it does not run. The plateau has no slope to speak of, so rain soaks in, spreads sideways through the rubble, and comes out again as a sheen on the lower courses. The walls weep, and the lane floors carry a finger's depth of clear water that moves too slowly to follow by eye. The cisterns stand brim-full and dark. On a cold morning every stone is beaded with dew, and when the sun reaches it the whole city steams faintly for an hour.

The weather is the plateau's own. On most days four or five showers can be seen at once, grey columns walking slowly across the wall-tops with lit country between them. One arrives and wets a district for twenty minutes, then moves on and leaves the schist black and glaring. The wind that drives them is steady and cold, but it passes overhead. The walls are just tall and close enough to lift it, so the air in the lanes is still and a dropped feather falls straight.

That stillness is most of why the place is so quiet. Water in films makes no noise, and there is nothing for the wind to hiss through. What sound there is comes small and close:

- a drip into a cistern from far down
- a bare foot on wet stone, one lane over
- the click of a slab settling a hair's width after sunset, when the day's little warmth goes out of the wall

Very little grows here. Moss fills the joints on the shaded sides and holds water like a wick. Map lichen spreads in grey-green rings on the old faces, some of them wider than a doorway, and an orange lichen marks the few wall-tops where birds perch. Sedge stands around the cistern mouths, and cushion plants root in the sills of cells no one has entered for generations. A willow lives here too, though it never rises above ankle height. It creeps flat along the wall bases and puts out catkins the size of a fingernail.

Animals are fewer still, and a whole day can pass without seeing one. A small grey bird nests in the gaps of the wall hearting and flies low and silent down the lanes, turning corners without slowing. Pale hares sit in doorways and do not run until the last moment. Newts hang motionless in the cisterns. Sometimes there is one large hawk, very high, working the wind that the lanes never feel.

There are people too, thinly spread. A household may sit in a district of ten thousand cells, and they are easier to notice by their work than by sight. A stretch of wall shows pale where the stones have been turned and reset and the lichen has not yet come back. A joint is chinked with clay that still holds the print of four fingers. A lintel is worn glassy where shoulders have brushed it. Some lanes have been swept clear of fallen slabs, and beside them are others where no one has gone, where the walls have slumped into the low, rounded shape of something that has finished falling.

The city is not growing. Frost lifts a stone out and a hand puts it back, at about the same rate, and that balance is older than anyone's knowledge of when the building began. The rule seems to be simply that a fallen stone is replaced by whoever finds it, before dark, in the place it came from if that place can be found. So the walls are always being rebuilt, a little at a time, and never look new.

Toward evening the showers thin out and the light comes in low and yellow under the cloud. Every wet wall-top catches it, and for a few minutes the plateau is a net of bright lines. Then the stone gives up its damp to the cooling air. Fog forms in the lanes and rises to the level of the wall-tops but no higher, until the city is a white floor with dark domes standing out of it in their thousands.

Below the fog, where it is nearly dark, the water goes on moving through the rubble toward edges it will take years to reach. In one wall there is a stone set back in place that afternoon, its underside turned outward, dry and pale and not yet weathered.
