Under the ridge, at about twenty-eight hundred metres, the mountain has come apart along a seam. The hollow this leaves is a hundred and fifty paces long and half as wide, and its two walls match. A knob on the north face answers a socket on the south, shelf for shelf, like the halves of a mould drawn apart. The rock is black and close-grained, and so recently broken that its edges will open a palm. There is no scree at the foot of the walls, no grit, no soil. Nothing has ever fallen off anything here.

Moss grows on it regardless, rooted straight onto the clean fracture faces, a hand deep and soaked through. Out of the moss come cushions of saxifrage, lady's mantle with a bead of water held in every leaf, dwarf willow lying flat, sedge in tussocks. Each plant is in bud, in flower and in seed at once, on the same stem. Nothing stands higher than a knee. Above that the hollow is empty air up to the cloud, which lies across the top like a ceiling, flat on its underside, forty metres up. It has not moved.

Every face weeps. On the headwall the water runs as a film no thicker than a fingernail, and at the low end it gathers in a black tarn the size of a tennis court. Nothing spills over the lip. Instead, along the south wall, a stream the width of a wrist runs uphill from the tarn to the top of the headwall, steadily and without spray. The air is three or four degrees and smells of struck flint and wet iron. Breath shows.

The light comes from the cloud. It is grey-violet, about what is left twenty minutes after sunset, and it arrives from everywhere, so nothing has a shadow. It flickers too fast to see straight on, but the edge of the eye catches it.

The flicker keeps time with the hum, which can be heard well before the rim. It is one low note, a shade above G, and it is in the moss, the rock, and the back teeth. Under it a beat comes up from below the tarn once a second, heavy enough to feel in the ankles. Over it are the frogs. Two people standing together would have to raise their voices.

In that light, anything moving is caught at the same point of its motion every time, and stands still. The film on the headwall is plainly running, and a willow seed lying on it has not travelled. Under each overhang a drop hangs in place, falling. A hand put under one is struck hard and cold by the full weight of the water, which sheets off the wrist, and the drop still hangs there. Anywhere else this would be a trick of the lighting. Here there is no gap between how a thing looks and how it is. The hollow runs at full speed and goes nowhere, and both are simply the case.

Nobody built any of it. There is no wire, no wheel, no cut stone, no path to the rim. It is a works all the same.

The animals keep stations. Every six or seven metres across the floor, with open moss between, a pika sits at a tussock cutting sedge. It bites, the stem falls, and the stem is standing. The pikas breathe out together on the beat, a hundred and forty-four small puffs of exhaust. Each has the same nick in the left ear. They are not alike; they are one pika, run a gross of times, and a burr caught in the fur of one is in the fur of all.

There is a gross of everything:

- **Frogs:** They sit in the seeps of the headwall, clicking open and shut. With each click a blue point of light on the nearest willow catkin goes out and another comes on.
- **Snails:** They graze a rust-coloured stain high on the wall. Their shells are identical to the limit of what a fingernail can feel, and they are warm and faintly vibrating, like the housing of a small motor.
- **Choughs:** They are the only things in the upper air. They fly a long loop from tarn to headwall and back, evenly spaced and beating hard, and the loop does not advance. It is a belt between two pulleys that are not there, and the wind off it can be felt on the floor.
- **Midges:** They stand in columns over fixed points in the moss, straight up into the cloud, like stacks.
- **Trout:** They lie nose to tail in a ring in the tarn, turning. The stones of the bed are lodestone, and iron grit would stand on them in whiskers if there were any grit.

The ring of trout is what hums. The current climbs through the wet moss, and a damp hand laid flat feels it as a tingle, like a tongue on a battery. The frogs switch it, the cloud glows with it, and the stream is lifted by it back up the south wall.

The line runs downhill. The snails take the rust. A grey bird at each snail takes it, on the beat. At the lip of the tarn there is a flat stone, and on it one egg, grey and warm, the size of a thumb joint, tipping into the water. The fish have it. The egg is on the stone, tipping. The stain on the headwall is no thinner. The hollow's entire output is this one egg, consumed as fast as it is made.

The place has other rules:

- **Nothing wears.** Pika teeth, chough feathers and the edges of the rock all stay as they are.
- **No heat is lost.** The animals are warm and the air around them never gains a degree.
- **Sound has already happened.** A shout comes back off the walls slightly before it leaves the mouth.
- **Nothing can be taken out.** A pebble carried over the rim is not in the pocket on the far side. It is lying where it lay.
- **Nothing can be left behind.** The moss does not keep a footprint even for the length of the step.

A body that stays a while finds its pulse has gone to the beat and its stride has settled to six or seven metres between halts. By the lip of the tarn there is one tussock with no pika at it. The count still comes to a gross.

Somewhere on the rim a marmot is whistling, one long rising note, the kind that at any works would mean the shift was over. It has been sounding the whole time, and is the top edge of the hum. Nothing in the hollow looks up.
