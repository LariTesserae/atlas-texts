The plateau sits just above the cloud. It is a tongue of lava about six kilometres long and half as wide. It poured from a cinder cone at its upper end and stopped at a scarp, where it hangs in a frozen black lip over the white. The rock is younger than some of the steel that stands on it. Under the growth it is still clinker and glassy plates that would cut a hand, with ropes of pahoehoe whose wrinkles have not softened. Nothing has had time to round, and there is no soil in the usual sense. The plants live on a thin brown felt of their own dead, laid directly on the rock.

Almost none of that rock can be seen. Moss covers the flow a forearm deep, in cushions packed so tightly that their edges press up into ridges where they meet. Ferns stand out of the moss, and out of the ferns rise waist-high heaths with small waxy leaves, their stems sleeved in lichen. Finer moss grows on the lichen, and on that moss sit pinhead liverworts. Every surface carries another surface. Push a hand in anywhere and it passes through four or five living layers before it touches stone, and the stone is cold and slightly wet.

The wetness is hard to account for, because the plateau has no streams, no pools, and no sound of water. Rain is uncommon and vanishes at once into the voids of the flow. Water arrives here sideways. Each evening the cloud deck rises like a slow tide, comes over the lip of the scarp, and lies across the plateau for a few hours without moving. The leaves comb it. By morning the cloud has sunk back, and every tip and hair and wire holds a bead. By noon the beads are gone, drunk or burned off, and the surface of the moss is dry to the touch while its depths stay sodden. The whole green mass runs on this ration and wastes none of it.

The light is immense. The air is thin, the sun is high and hard, and the cloud below works as a second sky that throws brightness upward, so the undersides of leaves are lit and shadows come out pale, sometimes doubled. In the morning the dew turns the plateau into a field of small lenses and it is difficult to look at. Even deep in the heath there is no real shade, because the leaves are small and half-translucent and the light simply continues down through them, growing greener as it goes.

A line of lattice pylons runs the length of the flow, eleven of them, from the scarp to the foot of the cone. Their concrete footings were poured into rock that had barely finished cooling. At the upper end stands a low station building of concrete and corrugated steel with a fenced transformer yard. Beside it are three capped wellheads, and a lagged pipe as thick as a body goes on toward the cone and disappears into it. Near the scarp are rows of fog-mesh frames, a few of them torn, draining through gutters into a concrete cistern with a rusted hatch. There is one rail trolley on forty metres of track that connects nothing to nothing. That is all the building there is. No one is present, but the insulators are clean, and the conductors hang at their proper tension and carry current.

There is no sound on the plateau. The transformers are running, and a palm laid on the casing feels the steady mains tremor in the bones of the wrist, but nothing reaches the ear. The moss takes all of it, and so do the porous rock beneath and the damp, close-packed leaves. A dropped wrench lands without a noise. A voice seems to stop about an arm's length from the mouth. Wind is rare, and when it comes the heath bends without rustling, because the leaves are too soft and too wet. After an hour you start to hear your own blood, and after a day you no longer expect to hear anything else.

Under the power line the vegetation behaves differently. A ridge of taller, darker growth follows the conductors exactly from pylon to pylon. It lies in three parallel bands, one beneath each phase, with two narrow lanes of shorter moss between them. The ferns in the bands lean inward and upward. The pylons are being climbed, but only on the inside of the lattice, in a slow green column that has reached the first crossarm on the oldest towers and the knees of the newest. At the station the yard fence has become a hedge, but the transformers themselves are bare, and so is a neat margin of gravel about a metre wide around each one. Nothing maintains that margin. The growth stops there, as if at an agreed line.

Animals are few. Pale moths rest on the insulator sheds with their wings flat and do not fly when approached. There are small amber snails in the moss. Now and then a single dark bird rides the updraft at the scarp for a long time without calling, then drops back into the cloud.

Change here is slow. The moss gains a hand's width a year across the last bare tongues of clinker near the cone. A skylight in a collapsed lava tube fills gradually with tree ferns that reach for the hole. The green rises one lattice panel up a pylon in a decade. The cloud comes up each evening and goes down each morning, and the meters in the empty station keep turning.
