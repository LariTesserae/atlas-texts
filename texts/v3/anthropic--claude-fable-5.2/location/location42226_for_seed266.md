On the Hob, warmth goes up. Nobody in Linehead can tell you why. The lowlands, eleven days below by rack railway, lie under frozen cloud, and every stage of the climb takes a layer of clothing off you. At the top you stand in shirtsleeves on rock as warm as a loaf an hour out of the oven, at any hour of any season.

The Hob is a tableland the size of a small continent, and it is flat because it has been licked flat. Its surface is a pavement of close-fitted stones, wine-dark, carrying a varnish a hair thick. The varnish is the only thing here that grows, if growing is the word; it returns to a scraped stone in about three hundred years. No rain has ever been recorded. The weather is dry weather: a steady inward wind off the rim called the Draw, pressure seasons that last years, and in the still months a layer of pale dust that hangs at knee height across the whole plain. People seen at a distance appear to be wading.

The dust is meal, and the meal comes from the rasps. A rasp is the size of an upturned bathtub, plated and low, legless as far as anyone has established. Underneath is a belt of teeth that takes the varnish off the stone and a little of the stone with it. They travel in herds of several hundred, each animal a few lengths from the next, and cover about one body-length a day. Behind them the pavement is left pale. These bands are the trails, and they cross the Hob in long shallow curves, the oldest worn a hand's depth into the rock. From the roof of the rectifier house you can count nine herds on a clear day. The nearest is an hour's walk off and the farthest a grey smear on the horizon. Surveyors put the whole population near four million. Most of the plain is empty.

Linehead stands at the rim because its power comes from the difference between the warm rock under it and the cold below. Forty-three steel-jacketed lines hang over the edge into the cloud, carrying ammonia down warm and bringing it back cold, and the engines turn on that difference. They are large and slow, with flywheels three storeys high going round perhaps ten times a minute, and they make almost no noise. Current goes out by busbar on pylons to the potrooms, long sheds where meal dredged from the hollows is cooked down to a light grey metal in rows of pots. The sheds are older than the town's records. Their iron sheeting has been dust-blasted to a satin finish and every corner has gone round. They stand far apart, with bare pavement between them, joined by tramway and overhead bar.

The lines come back up over the rim furred with rime, and that rime is the town's water. Scrapers work the return gantries before sunrise with wooden blades and canvas chutes, and a good line gives a bucket a day. Water is issued by the cup and tastes of iron. Clothes, dishes, and people are scrubbed with sifted meal.

The first by-law says nothing is built across a trail. Where the town could not avoid one, the building stands on legs. Number Six potroom predates the by-law. When a herd arrived, the end walls were taken out and the pots shifted, and the herd took nineteen months to pass through. One rasp stopped under the crane rail and has not moved in sixty-one years. It still ticks each morning when the sun reaches its plates through the open wall, as the herds outside do, a sound like a tin roof warming. Somebody chalks the date on it once a year. The chalk lasts.

Above the potrooms the heated air rises in a column, and the tarps hang in it. They are flat and translucent, each the span of a bedsheet, thousands of them stacked through a mile of air and turning slowly. In the still months they come down to the knee-high dust and sieve it for meal-fleas, which are the size of rice grains and live on whatever the rasps leave in the meal. No tarp has been heard to make a sound. On the busbars, where the metal runs warm, grey lice the size of a thumbnail sit packed edge to edge by the million. Linesmen sweep them off the insulators with long brooms, and they walk back.

The Hob's only storm is static. The charge builds for weeks under a clear sky until the pylon tips glow blue and the tarps light along their edges, so the column over the town becomes a slow stack of burning paper that never burns. Everyone wears a grounding chain at the ankle, and you can hear a shift change by its dragging.

That is most of what you can hear. The thin air carries voices about twenty paces, so people use hand signs across the yards, and the tram carries a lamp where a bell would be. The rectifiers hold one note, which the town's children can hum on request.

The light is ordinary yard light, the plain light of any afternoon. Only straight overhead does the blue go deeper than it should. From the rim the cloud deck lies far below, white and hard-looking, visibly curved.

The down train leaves every ninth day with ingots. The crew put on their first coats at milepost forty and their second at ninety, and at the bottom the metal is unloaded with gloves. The up train brings the coats back folded in a crate, along with lamp oil, dredge teeth, and whatever water the lowland office has remembered to send. The water arrives frozen in its cans and is stood on the platform, on the warm stone, until it thaws.
