The mountain is not finished. It tops out well above the cloud deck, where the sky darkens toward the zenith and the horizon shows its curve. At the summit there is a city where ice should be, and in the middle of the city the rock is still coming up.

It comes as a spine, a single column of grey-violet stone some four hundred metres across. It is solid and hot, and it is pushed out of the mountain's throat at about the width of a hand each day. It doesn't glow or flow. Its flanks are grooved vertically where it scraped the vent, and polished in places to a dull shine. Nothing has weathered it, and nothing on the summit has had time to weather. Every edge is sharp enough to open a glove. There is no soil and no dust, only spall, glassy flakes that gather in the gutters and are swept up and weighed.

The city's work is to cut the spine down as fast as it rises. Wire saws are strung between gantries around its crown, long loops of braided cable drawn slowly through the stone with a trickle of water. The crown has stayed at the same height for as long as there have been records, so the quarry never deepens. Blocks go down the radial inclines on counterweighted cars, with gravity doing most of the hauling.

The stone keeps its heat. Blocks cut two hundred years ago are still as warm as a loaf, and the city is built of them. At this height the air ought to be bitter, but warmth presses up through the paving and comes off every wall. People sleep in hammocks slung across the alleys, where the draughts are. The thin air makes newcomers breathe twice for every step, and the heat makes them wish they hadn't.

Water arrives as cloud. A few times a week the deck lifts and washes over the summit, and it condenses on everything it touches. Iron sweats, walls darken, and the streets run. Every paving slab has a channel cut in it, and every channel leads to a cistern. Water boils early up here, so the puddles close to the spine shiver at a low simmer that would only redden a hand. When the cloud drops away, the city dries from the centre outward over the course of a day.

The new rock shoulders the old aside, and the whole summit creeps outward from the spine at about a pace a year. Nothing has foundations. Buildings stand on iron shoes and screw-jacks, and each morning the levellers go round with water-tubes and long wrenches, giving a quarter turn here and a shim there. The ring streets widen until a gap opens, and a new building is set into it.

The city can therefore be read by age. Near the centre the stone is pale and the corners are crisp. Farther out it is stained brown-black by centuries of condensate, and the buildings stand six and seven storeys with their eaves interlocking. Steam mains, cable trays and footbridges are layered between them until the alleys are roofed in pipework. From the saw gantries the whole place is visible, about three kilometres of it. It runs out through rings of engine houses, dressing yards and cistern courts to the tenements at the rim.

After about three centuries a building reaches the rim. It is emptied years ahead, and its fittings are carried inward. It leans for a while. Then, usually on a cloud day, it is gone. Below the edge the talus begins, the only broken ground on the mountain. Where the cloud parts you can make out courses of masonry in the scree, a window opening, an iron stair lying on its side. The slopes are made of old city, and the mountain is being built from the top.

The engines run on the difference between warm stone and thin air. They are large, slow beam engines that take one stroke for every several breaths. They turn the dynamos that feed the amber lamps and the saw drives. Sound doesn't carry well here, and a shout dies crossing a street, so signals are sent by shutter and lamp. What can be heard is the hiss of wire in the cut, the dripping, the tick of a jack being turned, and the long exhale of a cylinder. Once or twice a day there is a single dry crack from deep in the spine. Hands stop for it and people count, and then the work resumes.

There are few animals. A small grey bird lays its eggs in niches in the oldest walls and leaves them, because the stone does the brooding. You might see one bird in a day, riding the warm air over the rim.

The others come out of the rock. Now and then a saw opens a smooth-walled hollow in the fresh stone, and something is in it. It is pale and jointed, about the size of a hand. It walks out slowly toward the nearest water, and no one touches it. The sawyers stop the wire until it has cleared the cut. These creatures end up in the cisterns, resting on the bottom in the warm dark, and the cistern keepers count them at each cleaning. The count has never gone down.
