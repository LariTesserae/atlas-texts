The land lies low, a basin so wide that its rim is more rumor than landform, and cool air settles into it and stays. There is no ground in the old sense. The floor is board, a pale pressed fiber the color of oat husk, laid in panels that run to every horizon. The board is made from the crop that came before, which stood on board made from the crop before that. Nobody has ever cored down to anything else. At a seam you can see the layers, thin as pages, thousands deep, and the lowest ones visible are still board.

Everything standing on it is plant, and all of it is dry. The leaves are foil-thin, silver on top and chalk-white beneath, and they crackle faintly when they turn. The stems are jointed like cane and hollow. If you hold one to your ear you can hear the sap, which is a fine grey powder falling slowly upward through the joints. It moves the way sand moves in a glass, a grain at a time, and it carries charge the way other sap carries sugar. Water is treated here as a kind of contamination. There is a little of it, somewhere, and I will come to that.

The plant is the works. The trusses holding the canopy are stems that were trained square for so long that they now grow square. The conveyors are runners, flat ribbons of creeping vine that advance a hand's width every few minutes, carrying bales along routes worn glossy into the board. Tendrils with bright, coppery bark hang in catenaries between the uprights and hum at a pitch just under hearing. Seed-heads the size of cathedral windows turn on their stalks without any wind to turn them. They are the engines. Their turning drives the powder up the stems, the powder charges the leaves, and the leaves make the product.

The product is brightness. The sky over the basin is a flat, even white, and it has never contained a sun. The light comes off the undersides of the leaves and is dispatched upward in quantity. It is a hard, clean, shadowless light, the light of a room where precise work is done. Manufacturing it takes warmth out of the air, so the brightest aisles are the coldest, and in the high-output sectors your breath would show if there were enough moisture in you to show it.

Shadow is the waste product. It comes off the line in soft, dark sheets, and the runners carry it away to be baled and stacked. The stacks form long ricks of darkness, squared off and tagged, standing in open aisles under full glare, and nothing lights them. If you put your hand into one it comes back colder but no dimmer.

The basin has its own weather, all of it dry. Chaff fronts cross the canopy for days, pale gold banks of husk and scale moving at walking speed. They sift down through the leaves in a fine dry fall that never melts and is swept by the runners into the next generation of board. Charge builds along the fronts and discharges as a crawling lightning, slow violet filaments that walk from leaf to leaf across a hundred miles of canopy and are taken back into the stems. There is no thunder. The leaves feed on sound as they feed on charge, and a footstep here is absorbed before it finishes. What remains is small: the tick of a joint lengthening, the paper-turn of a leaf, the hiss of powder in the cane. If you speak, your voice reaches about as far as your arm.

This has been running a very long time, and the strangeness of it has settled the way a building settles.

The rows are numbered on enamel-smooth scars in the bark, and the numbers are correct, but they do not accumulate. If you walk the length of Row 40 and cross over, you are in Row 40, at the same seed-head, though perhaps at a different hour of its turning. Distance here is held as inventory, and only so much is issued. The far gantries stand at the same size however long you walk toward them, and then they are overhead, with no interval in which they grew.

Events follow the manifest and not the clock. A leaf lies on the board, brittle and spent, beneath the bud that has not yet opened to become it. Bales arrive at the ricks before the line that will produce them has been strung. The plant shows no distress at this. The runners route around the discrepancies in gentle, well-worn curves, and you can tell which paradoxes are oldest by how deep the groove is.

At intervals no one has charted, a shift changes. Every leaf across the whole basin turns over at once, white side up and silver side down, and makes less sound than a held breath. For that interval the light falls downward from the sky into the plant, and the ricks of shadow grow visibly thinner. Then the leaves turn back. The far side of the basin is always on the other shift, and the weather that reaches you from there is weather that has not happened yet. It usually arrives as chaff falling upward.

Animals exist, but barely. There is a moth the grey of the powder, about the size of a thumbnail, and you might see one in four days' walking. You may always be seeing the same one, because it has a notch in its left wing and so does every other. It carries the only water in the basin, a bead of it somewhere in its body. Where one finally drops, a black wet ring spreads on the board to a hand's width and stops. The plant walls the ring off with a blister of amber resin, hard as glass. These blisters are the only landmarks. They are scattered across the continent of board in the thousands, each one sealed over a single drop, and the runners part around every one of them.

Above them the seed-heads keep turning, the powder climbs, and the white sky is kept supplied.
