The tableland is what remains of a much higher country. Everything that could be carried off has been, and the cap of hard sandstone left behind stands far above the forest, nearly level, its edges rounded and its surface worn into shallow pans. Under the cap, near the middle, there is a hollow: a single chamber about sixty paces long and half that across, with a roof roughly as high as the chamber is wide. The way in is a slot in the plateau surface and a low passage that bends twice, so no daylight gets round the second bend, even at noon.

Inside, the dark is complete, and everything known about the place is known by hand, ear, nose and skin.

The air is warm, much warmer than the thin wind on the plateau, and close to the temperature of breath. It is wet without being flooded. The walls weep steadily, and the roof lets go a drop every few seconds somewhere in the chamber, never twice in the same place. The floor is fine sand, silted flat over a very long time, with water a finger's depth beneath it. A footprint fills slowly from below. In the lowest third of the chamber the water stands clear of the sand in a sheet about ankle deep, and small things move in it and bump against the shin.

The plants grow where no plants should. Ferns stand chest-high with fronds as soft as wet cloth, and the moss is deep enough to take a hand to the wrist. Broad leaves, cool and rubbery and bigger than a spread palm, hang from vines that come down the walls and trail across the sand. The growth covers the floor and climbs the walls, and to judge by what drops from it, it covers the roof as well. What colour any of it is, nobody can say. It smells green, of crushed stem and of rot.

It grows because of the posts. There are three, set in no pattern that pacing reveals. Each is waist-high and as thick as a thigh, tapering slightly, with a surface finer than any water-polished stone and no seam anywhere. Water will not stay on them, and a wet palm laid on one comes away dry. They are neither warm nor cool. They are exactly the temperature of the hand that touches them, whatever that is.

Something leaves them that leaves can use. It passes through a body without any sensation. The growth is thickest in a ring around each post, and every leaf within reach is turned flat toward it, as leaves elsewhere turn toward a window. Small flying insects orbit the posts in such numbers that walking up to one means walking through a soft, continuous tapping against the face.

The noise is the first thing met and the last thing left. Several kinds of frog call from the floor: one a clear knock like a struck bottle, one a rasp, one a rising whistle repeated without rest. Crickets call from the walls. The roof holds a colony of something winged that navigates by clicking, and the clicks come down in dry volleys as the animals leave and return through a route of their own. All of it is doubled by the stone. A shout takes most of a second to come back from the far wall.

The echo also shows how little is here. The life is a skin on the surfaces, a few hand-widths thick on the floor, walls and roof. Between the tops of the ferns and whatever hangs from the roof there is only warm air, crossed by the clickers and by falling drops. The posts, the only made things, stand twenty or thirty paces apart with nothing between them but sand and creeping vine. Walking from one to the next, there is a stretch in the middle where the hand finds nothing in any direction.

The chamber changes, but slowly. Over about forty days the growth around one post slackens and the growth around the next quickens, and the turn passes round all three. Leaves reorient over a week. The frogs drift toward whichever ring is thickest with insects, so the centre of the chorus moves a few paces a day, round and round the chamber. The posts make no sound doing this, or doing anything.

The rock is finished with its own changes. No fresh fall lies on the floor, and no edge anywhere is sharp enough to catch skin. The roof gives up a grain of sand now and then, and the grain lands in moss.

Going out, the chorus drops to a murmur after the first bend and is nearly gone after the second. On the surface the wind is cool and the bare stone is dry. A person standing over the middle of the chamber on a still night, with an ear to the rock, can just make out the bottle-knock frog.
