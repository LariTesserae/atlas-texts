The shelf sits two-thirds of the way up the inside of a caldera, a tilted bench of pale rock about four kilometres long, and the ground of it is hot. Nothing glows. The stone is simply too warm for water to stay in, so whatever seeps up through it leaves as vapour, and the vapour has nowhere to go. It rises to roughly the height of a six-storey building, meets the thin cold air of the altitude, and flattens into a grey ceiling with a strangely level underside. The city is built beneath that ceiling.

It is an iron city, laid out in a grid. The streets are gratings raised on piers a metre above the rock, so that the seep can run underneath in braided, steaming threads and feet never touch the ground itself. The blocks are enormous and almost empty. A pump house the size of a chapel stands alone in a lot the size of a parade square. A valve station, a row of cisterns and a gasometer in its guide-frame are each separated from the next by a few hundred metres of bare grating and warm fog. The engineers spaced everything for the heat, and they did not build walls, because walls kept it in. Most structures are roofs on columns, long sheds open on all sides, their trusses vanishing upward into the murk. There are pipe bridges overhead, tram rails underfoot, and gantries carrying nothing. It has every line of a city and almost none of the contents.

The street names are temperatures, taken from the ground at the time of survey. Sixty Street. The Eighties. Ninety-One Cross. The heat has wandered since, and Seventy now runs cooler than Fifty-Five, but the cast plates were never changed. Residents navigate by the numbers and distrust them.

It rains here only under roofs. Vapour drifts into the open sheds, finds the cool underside of the iron, beads there, and falls as a steady warm drizzle inside every hall, while the open street a few steps away is merely damp. Shelter works backward, and to get out of the rain you leave the building. The gutters run along the inside of the eaves, and the downpipes lead to cisterns that are always full and always overflowing gently back onto the rock. The city is a still that distils the same water over and over and keeps none of it.

Nothing green lives on the shelf, but things grow all the same. The water comes up loaded with silica and drops it wherever it cools, so every handrail is fat with pale sinter, every bolt-head is a white knuckle, and the stair treads have been built up and chipped back so many times that they are more mineral than metal. Alum whiskers stand on the pipe lagging like frost that refuses to melt. In the runoff channels there are skins of colour, flesh-pink where the water is hottest, then ochre, then a dull bottle-green at the cool margins. No one calls these skins alive, but they keep to their bands more reliably than the street signs keep to theirs. Deposit and dissolution came to terms here a long time ago. The crust thickens and flakes and thickens again, and the shapes of things stay about the same.

The light is poor. The day is a brownish lifting of the ceiling, and it has no sun in it. The lamps burn vent gas, a small blue-white mantle at each intersection, and each wears a halo twenty metres wide. Anything that passes a lamp throws its shadow into the fog as well as onto the ground, a long dark solid lying in the air. A person crossing Eighty-Four Street is preceded by a slanting corridor of darkness the size of a shed. This doesn't happen often. You can stand at a crossing for an hour and see one such shadow swing across a distant block and fold away.

The people live above. The stacks and standpipes go up through the ceiling, and at their tops, in clear cold air and actual sunlight, there are rooms bolted round the iron like collars. From the street they show only as patches where the grey is slightly paler than it ought to be. Rung-ladders lead up into the cloud. Someone comes down to turn a valve, trim a mantle, or read a gauge, and then goes back up. The air at street level is hot and thin together, which is a bad combination. Water boils before it is properly scalding, a climb of ten rungs leaves the chest working, and nobody does anything quickly.

There are birds, in the sense that there are eggs. They are chalk-coloured and slightly pointed, and they lie in twos on the valve housings and pipe flanges wherever the iron holds blood heat. Nothing sits on them, since the pipes do the brooding. The adults are grey and long-winged, and they are seen mostly as a movement at the top of a ladder, passing through the ceiling in one direction or the other. In the settling tanks something small rises at the hour the lamps are trimmed and leaves rings on the surface. No one has netted one.

It is quiet. The fog swallows distance, and what remains is close: drip from the eaves, the tick of iron taking up heat, the faint seethe under the gratings. The beam engine in the main pump house strokes once every forty seconds, and from most of the city it registers as a slight lean in the air more than as a sound. The gratings carry sound, though. A footstep three blocks off arrives through the soles as a thin ringing, without direction.

The place keeps a schedule of its own. At night the ceiling sinks to the third storey and the lamps become smears. By midday it has climbed back and the gasometers have risen with it, their great drums standing tall in the frames. By dusk they have sunk again, slow as a chest. The relief vents open in sequence from the caldera wall outward, each one standing a white column up through the grey, and the people above set their clocks by them. At the low edge of the shelf, where the grating ends at a rail and the rock drops away, the cloud pours over the lip in a wide slow fall and thins to nothing in the cold below.

A few times a season, real wind gets over the caldera wall and tears a hole in the ceiling. A shaft of sun stands in some street for a few minutes. The plates steam dry and the colours show, rust and sulphur-yellow and the hard white of the sinter. Along the pipes the eggs cast small, sharp shadows. Then the hole closes, and the drizzle starts again under the roofs.
