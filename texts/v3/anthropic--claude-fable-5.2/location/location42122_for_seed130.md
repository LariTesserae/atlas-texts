The ground is iron. Plate after plate of it, each the size of a threshing floor, is laid edge to edge across what was once an estuary. It was riveted down so long ago that the rivet heads have worn level with the surface and show only as rings of slightly paler grey. The seams between plates have rounded into shallow gutters. There is no soil here and no stone. Where the top plate has thinned to a lace of rust and then to nothing, there is another plate beneath it, older, with rivets set to a different spacing, and that one is worn too. Some plates once carried lettering, a foundry's name or a number. A palm can still find the shallow dips where the characters were, but they cannot be read.

Water lies over all of it, a finger deep and rarely more or less. It is cold and tea-brown and tastes of pennies. The plain sits below the level of the sea, and the water would like to be much deeper. It isn't, because of the engines.

The engines are heard long before they are seen, though heard is the wrong word for something that arrives through the boot soles. They are beam engines, forty or so across the whole basin, standing two and three kilometers apart. Each drives a pump that lifts water from the sumps beneath the plate. Every stroke is a concussion. No two engines keep the same time, so the strokes drift against each other, bunching into long rolling barrages and then spreading out into a steady hammering. The plate carries all of it, and the whole plain rings like a struck drumhead. The noise has no direction, and walking away from one engine only means walking toward another. It sits in the sternum and the back teeth. A shout at arm's length is just a mouth opening.

The sound draws on the water. Where the plate shakes hardest, the film of water cannot lie flat. It stands up in a tight stipple of points, and at the worst places it is thrown off the iron altogether into a knee-high haze of droplets, a fog made by vibration. Between these patches run lines where the plate hardly moves. There the water is perfectly still and black. The lines form curves and lattices hundreds of meters across, with long arcs that meet at right angles. They are the only quiet in the basin, and the quiet is relative. Everything that grows here grows along them.

The growth is mostly a dark felt of moss, with liverwort at the seams and a rust-colored algal skin on the water itself. In a few places thin rushes stand in a single row, like a line ruled across a page. A step to either side the iron is bare, shaken clean. Nothing crawls in the moss and nothing flies over it. If creatures with ears ever lived here, none remain.

Almost no light reaches the plain. The sky is a low lid of cloud that never opens. At what must be midday it lightens from black to the color of wet slate, and the plain gives back a dull sheen. The rest of the light is small and blue. Standpipes rise through the plate at wide intervals and burn off gas that comes up from below, each with a flame the size of a hand that leans in the wind. From any one of them, six or seven others are visible, strung out toward a horizon that can't otherwise be found. The still lines of water hold their reflections, and the shaken water smears them.

The gas comes from the drowned mud of the old estuary, still rotting under all the layers of iron. The engines burn it. Their boilers show as seams of orange in the dark, and their exhaust goes up in white columns that flatten against the cloud and become part of it. The cloud is made of that exhaust. When it rains, which is often, the rain is condensed steam, faintly oily, falling back onto the plate to be pumped again. The water the engines lift keeps the mud wet, and the wet mud feeds the engines. Nobody tends any of this.

The engine houses are gone. A course of brick stands ankle-high around each machine, the corners rounded like old molars. The machines themselves have survived by moving. Every bearing, pin and slide has lapped itself against its partner for so long that the fit is exact and nothing is left to wear. The beams are slimmer at the middle than they were cast, and their surfaces are as smooth as river cobbles. Around each engine is a ring of dry plate where the boiler's heat drives the water off. Just outside that ring, where warmth and wet meet, the moss is deepest, a green collar thirty paces wide that the engine's own shaking keeps from spreading further.

From each pump an iron trough runs away on trestles, carrying the lifted water toward the sea. These launders leak at every joint. Beneath each one hangs a continuous curtain of drips, and thin pale ferns grow down the trestle legs, pointing the way to the wall. The seawall has worn to a long low swell, easy to climb without noticing. Along its crest the sluices stand in a row, toothless frames with their gates ground to rounded tongues. On the far side lies the sea, flat and dark, pressing in.

There are other remnants. In one district, stanchion stumps stand in a grid, each worn to a knuckle, marking the footprint of something enormous that is gone. In another, rails lie sunk flush into the plate. They are the only bright things in the basin, and they run straight for a kilometer and stop.

When rain comes on hard, the sumps fill and the engines quicken, with no hand on any valve. The barrage tightens and the pitch of the plate climbs. As the pitch climbs, the still lines move. They slide a little outward and the lattice redraws itself. Moss that sat in quiet is now on singing iron, and over the following weeks it loosens and washes off. On the newly stilled strip beside it, a green stain begins. So the lines of growth walk across the plain, a hand's width in a wet season, back again in a drier one. It is the only migration here.

Twice a day the tide drops below the sluice sills. The launders, which have been brimming, empty all at once over the wall, and for a few minutes a new sound joins the others, forty cataracts falling onto the foreshore. Then the engines slow, the plate's note sinks, and the black lines drift back toward their old places.
