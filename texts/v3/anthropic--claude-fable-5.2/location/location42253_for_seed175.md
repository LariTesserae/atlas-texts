High on the shoulder of a tableland, where the sandstone has been worn so long it no longer has edges, there is a hollow about sixty paces across. Its rim is low and rounded, shaped like the inside of a cupped hand. Nothing here is being carved anymore. Whatever cut this bowl finished long ago, and the stone has settled into its form the way a river cobble does.

The floor is bare rock under a skin of water a hair deep. The water is warm, near the temperature of blood, and the stone beneath it is warm too, heated from below as well as by the sky. The air holds so much moisture that it beads on anything cooler than itself.

The plants grow in islands. Each is a mound of moss as high as a knee, crowned with a rosette of thick waxy leaves, a clutch of ferns, or a single small tree with a trunk no wider than a wrist. Each island is dense, green and dripping. Between them lie several paces of empty wet stone, and the gaps are nearly equal everywhere. Seeds that land in the open do not take. The islands keep their distance from one another, and the film of water between them reflects the pale sky, so that each mound seems to rest on a sheet of light.

There are no animals. No gnat hangs over the water and no web is strung between the fern fronds. No track crosses the moss, and no leaf carries a bore-hole or a chewed margin. The flowers open anyway, heavy and cream-colored, with a scent like warm bread. They stay open for whole seasons with nothing to visit them, and some of them set seed all the same.

Because nothing eats, nothing is cleared away. A leaf falls and lies where it lands. Over years the water leaches it from green to amber, then to a clear lace, then to veins alone. Beneath each tree is a stack of them, a hand deep, with this year's leaves on top and leaves like panes of glass at the bottom. Below those a fine pale net lies pressed onto the rock. Looking down through the stack is looking down through time.

The water makes no sound, because it never falls. It rises from seeps in the floor and spreads, and at the rim it climbs. The walls are darkest and wettest at the top. The film creeps up the stone against its own weight and leaves from the lip as mist, a faint ring standing above the hollow at midday. So the bowl drains upward, and there is no trickle, drip, or stream.

The quiet is deep enough that a visitor would hear their own pulse. Perhaps once in an afternoon a leaf lets go with a small click and lands flat on the water, as softly as a palm set on a table.

Someone was here once. On the east side a slab holds seven grinding cups, worn smooth by hands and a pestle. The pestle is a grey river cobble that does not belong to this rock, and it still rests in the fourth cup. On a ledge sits a chert flake with a keen edge, sealed to the stone by a rind of mineral. The largest tree carries a notch cut by a stone blade, long since healed under a lip of bark. Under the one overhang where the water does not climb there are five strokes of red ochre, each a finger wide. There is no hearth, no wall, and no path.

Light comes evenly through thin high cloud and casts no hard shadows. Night cools the hollow very little. The islands widen by a finger's breadth in a decade.

In the seventh cup a single rosette has rooted, the only plant that stands closer to its neighbors than the spacing allows. The pestle in the fourth cup has grown a collar of moss on the side that faces the seep.
