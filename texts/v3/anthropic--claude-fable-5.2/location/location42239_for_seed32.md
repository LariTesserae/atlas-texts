You hear the place before you see it. Across the salt pan, where the ground is flat and white and lower than any sea, there is a sound like a held breath given a voice. It is a wide, standing chord, loud enough at a hundred paces to be felt in the teeth. Then the pan simply stops at a straight edge, and below the edge is the room.

It is a sunken court, perhaps twelve strides by nine, cut down into the basin floor and lined with brick. The bricks were patted into shape by hand and still carry palm-prints, the heel of a thumb, here and there the drag of four fingers. Over them is limewash, laid on in smears and so often renewed, once, that it stands thick as rind. No one renews it now and it does not need it. It has weathered to the hard, chalky skin it will keep.

The court is full of jars. Coil-built and pit-fired, pale buff and ash-grey, they stand shoulder to shoulder from wall to wall, mouths up. The big ones are waist-high and bellied, and where their curves leave gaps, smaller ones have been set. In the gaps between those sit cups and thumb-pots, and what little space remains is chinked with shards on edge. There is no floor to see and nowhere to put a foot. How many tiers lie under the top one cannot be told. Looking down a crack shows only more clay, more rims, and a brightness that goes a long way down, because the lime throws the light into everything.

The rule of the packing is plain once seen. Every space is filled and every mouth is left open.

The wind crosses the pan from the same quarter at the same pace, cool and dry, and has done so for long enough that the jars have worn to it. The windward lips are rounded and matte, and the leeward ones still hold a little fire-gloss. Where two bellies touch they have ground each other to small flat facets, and they no longer shift. The wind passes over the open mouths and each one answers. The great jars give a note so low it is felt in the chest more than heard. The middling ones hoot. The cups whistle thin and high, and the walls hand everything back. Together it is a roar with a pitch, and a slow beating inside it where near-twins disagree. Speech at arm's length is lost in it. Walking the rim changes the chord, with more bass at the north corner and a bright reed-tone along the east wall, but standing still does not. It has no phrase, no swell, and no rest.

A few jars have cracked through and gone dumb. The sound has long since closed over them. They show only as slight thinnesses in the chord, the way a gap in a row of teeth shows in a word.

In those broken jars, where shards have trapped a spoonful of blown grit, the only rooted plant grows. It is a tight grey cushion no bigger than a fist, with leaves like scales, living on whatever trace of damp the cool air carries before dawn. There may be thirty in the whole court. On the lee shoulders of the largest jars are lichens, coin-sized rosettes of rust-orange and a green so pale it is nearly lime itself. They grow about as fast as the wind sands them back, so they are the size they were. Nothing else has taken hold. No web is strung across a mouth, no nest of chaff sits in a belly, and there are no droppings, husks, or tracks on the salt at the rim. Perhaps the noise forbids it. A fly would find no still air in the room.

The insides of the jars are clean. There is no tide-line, no stain of oil or grain, no seed in the bottom. Dust that falls in is lifted out again by the same breath that sounds them. They seem never to have held anything else, and it is hard, standing there, to think they were meant to.

The light comes from all sides. The sky is high and colourless, the pan flings it back, and the limed walls pour it down among the vessels so that shadows are only a paler buff under each shoulder. The air smells of cold fired clay and chalk, with salt at the back of the throat. When the day goes, the salt holds what light there is and the court turns pewter, still legible, every rim a ring. The chord does not drop a hair.

Nothing here is waiting for anything. The jars are full of what they were made for.
