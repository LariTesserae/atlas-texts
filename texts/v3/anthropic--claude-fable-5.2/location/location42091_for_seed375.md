The basin is a recent collapse. Its rim is broken rock that hasn't had time to round, with edges that would cut a palm and faces still the color of the stone's inside. The floor lies only a little below the surrounding plain, three times a person's height at most. It is about three hundred paces long and is filled, to an exact level, with a town.

Nothing built it. At the west end the floor is split by seams no wider than a hand, and out of them comes a cold, heavy vapor that pools in the basin the way water would if there were any. Where the vapor touches something solid it leaves mineral behind, dry, a grain at a time, and the mineral grows only in right angles. It goes up as walls a hand thick, dead straight, meeting each other square. They rise until they reach the surface of the pooled vapor, a little over twice a person's height, and there they cannot climb. They spread sideways into shelves and lintels, and where shelves meet, into roofs. From the rim the whole town is planed off at one height, flat as a table, with dark square openings where rooms haven't closed over.

The vapor moves slowly from the seams toward a notch in the eastern rim, where it spills out onto the plain. Where it flows, nothing deposits, and where it slows, walls come up. So the streets are the currents and the rooms are the eddies. The main way runs nearly straight west to east, wide enough for four abreast. Side lanes branch where the flow splits, and doorways stand wherever the air needed to get through. None of it holds. When a room roofs over, the current shifts, and a lane opens through what was wall a month ago. Elsewhere a doorway fills in from its edges a finger-width a night, until it is a slot, then a seam, then wall.

Fresh mineral is nearly white, with a metal sheen. Exposed, it tarnishes to straw within days, then rose, then a deep blue, then a flat grey, the last stage before it goes back into the air. Because the seams are in the west, the town is youngest there, and the main way passes through the colors in order. In the eastern quarter the grey walls thin until the next room shows through them. They open into holes with crisp edges, stand a while as free corners, and end as low stubs in powder. The powder is everywhere underfoot, flour-fine and ankle-deep in the east. It takes a print perfectly and gives no sound.

There are sixty or seventy rooms, and most hold nothing: a bare powder floor twenty paces across, four walls, and a ceiling or a square of sky. The sky is a high lid of dust the color of old paper, the sun a paler patch in it. What light reaches the floors has come off the walls first. The western rooms hold a weak yellow, the middle of town a pinkish dusk, and the east a blue that fades to no color at all.

Two things grow here. A rust-brown crust lives on blue walls and only blue walls, so it occupies a band perhaps thirty paces wide. The band travels west at the speed the walls age, dying off behind as its wall greys and creeping ahead as rose turns blue. On the roof stand pale jointed stalks, knee-high and leafless, a few to a room. Their bodies are up in the clear air where the light is better, and their roots hang through the roof slab into the vapor as grey threads. Every stalk stands on a wall that will dissolve. They let go a dust of spore into the upper air, and whatever lands to the west, on new roof, takes.

The animals live under one rule: the vapor deposits on anything. A stone left on the floor has a square collar by morning. A body that keeps still through one cold period wakes crusted, and through three it doesn't wake.

The grazers are flat, palm-sized, and many-legged, a few dozen in the whole town. They feed on the crust and never stop, walking walls and ceilings in slow loops even when full. Each carries a thin plate of tarnish on its back and sheds it whole every few days. The shed plates lie in the powder, straw or rose depending on how long the animal went between sheddings. In the pale western walls there is sometimes a dark flat shape a finger deep in the mineral, a grazer that stopped.

The hunter uses the rule. It is narrow, the length of a forearm. It folds itself into a corner within the blue band and lets the mineral take it. In a week it is a pilaster, square-edged and the right color. When a grazer crosses, the crust splits along one edge. Afterward a hollow cast stands in the corner, open down one side, with the animal's shape printed inside. There are far more casts than hunters, perhaps four or five alive at once. Each has to judge how long it can stay walled before the band moves on without it.

The weather is all in the vapor. In the cold period the seams breathe harder and the pool deepens to the notch. The overflow runs out across the plain as a flat stream, visible only as a wobble in whatever lies behind it, and the walls gain a finger. In the mild period the pool sinks a forearm's depth. The wall tops stand in ordinary air and begin to lose themselves, and the stalks' roots hang short of what feeds them.

Every so often a dry wind comes over the plain and drags across the top of the basin, pulling the vapor out in long rags. In one afternoon the whole grey east goes to nothing. While that wind blows, the dust in it charges, and every sharp upper edge in town carries a line of faint violet light. The light is steady and bright enough in the dimness to draw the entire plan in outline. When the wind drops, the seams refill the basin.

None of this makes a sound. The wall faces are stepped in terraces too fine to see, and a noise made in one of the rooms would go into them and not come back. Nothing drips or rattles. A wall that leaves just thins. Even the overflow at the notch pours out silently.

At the western edge, on bare floor between two seams, a line of white has started where there was no wall yesterday. It is a finger high and squared at the top. There is already a gap in it where the air goes through.
