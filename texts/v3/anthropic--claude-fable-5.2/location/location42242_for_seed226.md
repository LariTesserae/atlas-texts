The town stands on a shelf of new rock high on the mountain's flank, a bench some four hundred paces long and half as wide. Behind it is a headwall of columns, and in front the shelf ends at a clean edge with cloud below, most days. Nothing has rounded that edge yet. There is no scree at the foot of the headwall, no gully, no fan of gravel, no soil. The surface still carries the wrinkles it took when it cooled, ropy folds like the skin on a pot of porridge, and where the stone has broken it has broken sharp. It is a grey-green stone, fine-grained, and it rings when struck. A dropped chisel sounds like a bell.

The rock has stopped being hot but has not stopped arriving. It ticks. On a still morning you could put your palm flat on a paving slab and feel a small click travel through it, then another a long while later, as the bench settles into its own weight or is lifted a hair further out of the mountain. A line of chiselled notches up the headwall records this, one notch a year at the height of the same person's raised hand. The oldest notches are now out of reach.

There are no still mornings, though, because of the sound. The bench is hollow underneath, run through with tubes where the stone drained while its crust held. The wind goes in at the windward cliff and comes up through eleven throats in the floor of the shelf. Each throat sounds its own low note, and together they make a single chord, steady, so loud it sits in the jaw and the breastbone. It does not gust or break. Over days it drifts, a little sharper in dry air, flatter and thicker when cloud is passing through the tubes. No one here speaks outdoors. They gesture, or knock stone on stone, which carries.

Water comes as cloud more than rain. It climbs the mountain in the late morning, crosses the shelf at walking pace, and leaves everything beaded. The headwall weeps from every joint between its columns. The water runs down in thin sheets, gathers in a cut channel at the foot, and is led off through the town in drains a hand wide to a stepped cistern that is never more than half full. Lift any stone and it is dark underneath. There is no stream. The air is cool enough that breath shows at dawn but ice never forms.

The green got here before the weather did. Moss lies on the new rock in pillows and ropes a forearm deep, bright as a cut lime where the sun reaches it and nearly black in the lee of walls. Filmy ferns grow out of the moss, and larger ferns grow out of those. Along the headwall, where the drip is constant, tree ferns stand twice a person's height with lichen hanging off them in grey beards, and a straggling shrub puts out small red bells for no visible pollinator. In the open ground, tall woolly rosette plants stand apart from one another like posts. None of this is rooted in the stone. It sits on it. You can roll a mat of moss back like a rug and find the rock beneath clean and unpitted, its wrinkles intact. The plants live on cloud, on fine ash that sifts down from somewhere higher, and on one another's dead.

The town is all there in plan and almost absent in volume. Streets run straight from headwall to edge, paved in split slabs, crossed by narrower lanes at even intervals. There are something over two hundred house-plots, each walled in fitted block without mortar, the joints chinked with moss that has since taken hold and knitted them. Doorways have their jambs, and some have lintels. There is a plaza, a ramp, terraces stepping down toward the edge, and the cistern with its stairs. And there are no roofs. The walls stop at waist height, or shoulder, or at the fourth course, and above that is daylight. Each room is a square of moss floor open to the sky, holding nothing but air, the mist when it comes through, and the sound.

The custom is that a house is not roofed until its floor has gone a full year without ticking. A shallow stone bowl of water stands in the middle of each room, and someone walks the streets and looks at the surfaces. A shiver in the water means the count for that room starts again. No room has yet completed its year.

So the rooms stand open, and because they are open and differ slightly in size, each one takes the chord from the throats and keeps one part of it. Walking down a street, you pass through a slow scale. One room booms and the next holds something higher and narrower, while a third seems almost silent until you stand in its far corner. The builders know the plots by pitch. They have, here and there, taken a course off a wall or added one, and it is hard to say whether that was for the settling or for the note.

There are nine of them, or eleven. They live in the one roofed corner, a cluster of low huts under the headwall with turf laid over fern-trunk rafters and thin smoke coming out through the thatch. Their tools are hammerstones of a darker, tougher rock carried up from below, antler wedges, wooden levers and sledges, plaited fibre rope, and a few copper chisels gone soft and stubby with resharpening. Stone plumb bobs hang on cords at the street corners and are checked against scratched marks. The marks have had to be recut. Dressed blocks wait in stacks along the lanes, green on their north faces. Their work is the only other sound, hammer strokes that ring clear through the chord. When a block is struck, others cut from the same bed ring faintly with it across the shelf.

Almost nothing else lives here. A pair of dark birds works the updraft at the edge, tilting, never landing in the streets. If they call, it cannot be heard. Small pale snails cross the moss on the walls, and now and then a moth turns up drowned in one of the water bowls. No insects hum, and if any did, the hum would be lost.

By afternoon the cloud has gone through and the light is plain and high and even. It falls into two hundred open rooms, on the bowls and on moss steaming faintly. The stacks of block throw short shadows, and the rosette plants stand in the plaza where a crowd would be. A click passes through the paving. In a room on the third lane the water in the bowl trembles, and one of the builders, passing, stops, looks, and holds up a flat hand to another far down the street, meaning that the count for that room starts over.
