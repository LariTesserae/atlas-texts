The hall sits on the top deck of the tower, four hundred feet of riveted lattice above the engine houses. It is about the size of a small church: nine bays long, three wide, with iron ribs meeting in a ridge sixty feet overhead. Six bays are glazed. The seventh has its glazing bars and half its panes. The eighth and ninth are still open steel, with the sky standing in them. There is no rock up here and no soil that was ever part of a hillside. The deck is poured concrete on steel plate, and everything rooted in it is rooted in something manufactured: trays of crushed clinker, boiler ash, wool waste from a mill, coir felt wired to the columns in long green-black sleeves.

Nothing has had time to wear. The steel still carries blue mill scale under its red-lead primer, and the fitters' chalk is legible on every member: bay numbers, arrows, a tally of rivets, someone's initials. The concrete is pale and still gives off the faint lime smell of curing. Water has not yet learned the floor. It sheets across the deck in thin, undecided films, pools in places the drawings say are level, and finds the drains only by accident. There are no stains or channels, and no rust beyond a first orange blush at the bolt heads. The oldest bay has stood for two years and already feels like antiquity.

The heat comes up from below. Exhaust steam from the engines rises through a lagged header as thick as a barrel and branches along the hall into finned iron radiators. It was meant to condense there on metal. Leaf turned out to do it better, so the plants are on the drawings, drawn in the same ink as the pipework and given tolerances. Tree ferns stand in cast-iron tubs along the header. Pepper vines are trained up the columns on copper wire. Bromeliads sit in the gussets of the trusses, each holding a cupful of water. Filmy ferns and liverworts cover the felt so completely that the columns look upholstered. A fig has got its roots around a standpipe and nobody has had the heart, or the instruction, to cut it off.

The air is as warm as a bath and always in motion. Steam enters, cools on ten thousand leaf surfaces, and runs down. By mid-morning the underside of the glass is beaded. By noon the beads have joined and it rains inside, a soft, uneven rain that lasts twenty minutes and stops when the roof vents open on their chains. Then the panes clear, the light comes through ordinary and white, and the whole cycle starts again. It runs four or five times a day, and a little faster when the engines below are working hard.

Toward the unfinished end the climate changes within a few steps. In the seventh bay the big ferns give way to begonias and cress. In the eighth there is only moss, growing on steel that was hoisted last month. Where the warm air meets the mountain-cold air beyond the last rib, a seam of fog hangs upright like a curtain, leaning inward or outward with the pressure. On most mornings the true cloud is below the deck, a grey floor with the tower standing through it. Some afternoons it rises and comes in through the open bays, and for an hour the far end of the hall has no edge at all, just rope strung between stanchions where the handrail will go and whiteness beyond.

The growth is always slightly ahead of the building. Crates of glass wait in the eighth bay with ferns sprouting from their packing straw. New girders arrive at the hoist already filmed with green, though they were clean when they left the yard. The riveters say hot rivets hiss longer here than they should. The hall was designed for six bays. Leaf area kept outrunning it, so a seventh was added, then two more, and footings for a tenth are marked in chalk on a deck that is still only steel plate.

A travelling crane runs the length of the ridge on its own rails. Its girder has a garden, a fringe of ferns and hanging roots along the lower flange. When it moves, perhaps twice a day, that garden goes with it, and a line of drips travels down the hall underneath, crossing the trays one after another. The crane's passage is the only wind the glazed bays ever get. Fronds lift behind it and settle.

The hall is quiet. The engines are too far down to hear. They register as a steadiness in the soles of the feet and a fine trembling on the surface of standing water. What is audible is small: drips of several sizes landing on leaf, on iron, on water; the tick of the ribs as they warm; a relief valve on the header that sighs every eleven minutes; and now and then the creak of a new pane seating itself in soft putty.

Animals are few, and none were intended. A pale moth came up in the packing straw and has bred in the felt. Small amber snails live in the clinker trays. One kind of frog, thumb-sized, lives in the bromeliad cups. On most evenings only one of them calls, a single metallic note easily mistaken for something in the pipework. In the warm months a pair of swifts comes in through the open bays to take moths under the ridge and is gone again within a minute. You can stand an hour and see nothing alive that isn't a plant.

There are a few rules. The trays must not be moved, because the drip lines have been surveyed. The rope at the open end is the edge. The time can be read from the glass: clear, beaded, running, raining. At night the caged lamps along the header come on, the fog seam at the eighth bay glows a dull yellow, and the moss on the newest steel keeps advancing toward the chalk marks of the tenth bay.
