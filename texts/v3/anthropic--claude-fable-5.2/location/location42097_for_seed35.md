The chamber sits about forty metres under a desert floor, at the bottom of a single ventilation raise, and it is roughly the size of a church nave. It was blasted out of rock salt, and the blasting looks like it happened this morning. The walls are fracture faces, grey-white and banded with darker anhydrite, broken along cleavage into steps and shelves whose edges would open a palm. Half-barrels of drill holes run down the rock in parallel grooves, still clean. The rubble on the floor lies as it fell, with no settling and no rounding. Salt takes whatever moisture comes near it, so nothing has softened, and nothing will. The air is as warm as a kitchen with the oven on, and so dry that sweat leaves the skin before it is felt.

At the foot of the raise is the fan. It is a steel housing tall as a door, grey-painted, bolted to a concrete pad, with a cable as thick as a wrist running up the shaft into the dark. It pushes surface air down into the chamber, and it makes one note, low and full, somewhere beneath a man's speaking voice. The chamber happens to be the right length for that note. The salt rings with it. Put a hand to the wall and the wall is humming, and a tooth against a tooth hums too.

Because the note never changes, the sound has a shape, and the shape is visible. Fine salt dust has gathered on the floor in straight ribs, a pace apart, running wall to wall across the chamber. Between the ribs the floor is swept bare. The ribs mark where the air holds still. Walk the length of the room and the note swells and thins and swells with each stride. Stand on a rib and it is almost quiet, though the rock still shakes in the boot soles.

Everything alive here knows about the ribs.

The crickets are the most numerous. They are pale, nearly clear, with feelers three times the length of their bodies, and they cover the lower walls and the still lines of the floor so thickly that their antennae overlap into a kind of nap. They sing, all of them, on one pitch, and that pitch sits a hair under the fan's. The two notes together make a slow throb, a swell that comes about as often as a sleeper's breath. It is the only thing in the chamber that could be called a clock. It has not sped up or slowed.

In the drill grooves live the lizards. They are salt-white and blind, each the length of a finger, with one animal to each groove, pressed flat into the half-cylinder as if the hole had been bored for it. They bark. The sound is a hard double click, astonishingly loud, and it goes around the walls in order, groove to groove, like a count being passed along a line of men. It takes most of a minute to come back to where it started.

There is one lamp. It hangs in a wire cage from a bolt in the roof, and it gives a brown-orange light that reaches the nearest walls and gives up. Moths ride the airstream down the raise, grey desert moths, and they circle the lamp in a loose ball. The ball is always the same size. When one drops, another is already coming out of the duct.

Under the lamp, the fallen ones have built a cone of wings and scales and husks, knee high. It is the only soil in the place. Seeds come down the shaft too, and some land in the cone and sprout on what little wet a moth's body holds. They come up as white threads a knuckle tall, faintly yellow on the lamp side. They die at that height. There are perhaps two hundred standing at any moment, and the number does not vary, though they are never the same threads. Pale sun-spiders work the rim of the cone on stiff legs, taking crickets that come to feed. They step only on the bare floor between ribs, where the air shakes hardest, and the crickets seem unable to feel them coming.

A crust of lichen, orange going to grey, has taken the lamp cage and an oval of wall behind it. It lives on the trace of night damp the fan carries down. The oval's edge is as sharp as a paint line, and the lichen does not spread past it.

Inside the fan housing there are beetles, small, dark, and hard as seeds. They tick against the sheet steel in a steady rattle that sits on top of the big note. They walk in and out through the grille carrying grey pellets of moth wax, and they pack these into the bearing caps. The grease nipples are rusted shut. The bearings run cool.

Little else was left here. A length of steel ladder leans against the east wall, its rungs furred with crickets. A wooden chock lies on the floor, and a coil of blasting wire with yellow insulation gnawed off in neat rings. A hard hat sits upturned on a rubble block, full to the brim with shed cricket skins that weigh nothing. High on the north face someone has sprayed 7-E in red paint, with an arrow pointing at solid rock. The paint has run a little at the bottom of the 7. The drip still looks wet, though it is as dry as everything else here.

The moth cone gets no higher. Something carries it away from the base at the rate it arrives from above, and the edges of the salt stay as sharp as when they were cut. The fan holds its note and the crickets hold theirs just under it. The bark goes around the walls and comes back, and the throb rises, falls, and rises again in the warm dark.
