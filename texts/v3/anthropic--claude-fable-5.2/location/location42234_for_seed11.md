The first thing is the noise. Before the ground shows through the cloud there is a sound like a chord held on an organ the size of a county: low notes felt in the breastbone, a middle band of hooting and moaning, and over it a thin shriek like a wet finger on glass. It does not pause for breath.

Then the cloud tears and the plateau is there. It is the stump of a mountain range that was old before anything grew on land. Everything that could fall off it fell long ago. What remains is a table of pale pink quartzite some forty kilometres across, three thousand metres above the forest, worn into shallow swells and basins like a doorstep. A few tors still stand, rounded as knuckles and spaced an hour's walk apart. Between them nothing rises higher than a knee. Most of what is here is air, and the air is moving.

The wind comes up from the lowlands. It climbs the cliffs carrying the heat and breath of the jungle, so that at an altitude where there should be frost the gusts smell of leaf-rot and blossom. The rock stays warm to the palm, like bread an hour out of the oven.

The stone is riddled with holes. Every joint, pothole and slot has been worn into a pipe, and every pipe sounds. A hollow that resonates shakes the sand grains inside it, and the dancing grains scour it faster. Given enough time, every cavity drifts toward a note, and this place has had the time. Erosion finished the large work here ages ago and has since been tuning. Each wind direction plays its own chord. A southerly sits deep and steady, and a westerly brings the shriek to the front. When the wind swings, which it does a dozen times a day, the chord slides, and a body learns to brace at that slide before the gust arrives.

The green here is lavish, and almost none of it is rooted. Moss, sedge, sundews, thumb-sized orchids and rosettes of waxy red leaves are felted together into mats a hand thick, lying loose on the polished rock. The wind gets under an edge and rolls them up like carpet. They travel. They pile against the tors in green drifts ten metres high, sit a day, then peel away in sheets and go bowling off across the stone. From the top of a tor the plateau is a slow scramble of green pieces over pink, and the arrangement at noon is gone by mid-afternoon. Only the cracks hold permanent residents. These are thick-stemmed plants with leaves like leather tongues, and their flowers all open facing downwind.

No stream runs anywhere. Rain seldom falls downward here. Cloud goes through sideways at ankle height and combs itself out on every leaf. The mats are sponges, and a squeezed fistful runs brown water down the wrist. Where a mat parks in a basin it drains, and for an hour there is a finger-deep pool the colour of tea. Then the wind drinks it and the basin is dry stone again.

Near the centre are the sinkholes, round shafts with no visible bottom. Mats that roll into them do not come out. Sound that goes down them does come back, late and lower than it went in.

Animals are few and none of them make noise, since there would be no point. A black toad the size of a thumbnail cannot hop. It tucks its limbs and lets the wind roll it to wherever it is going. A narrow-winged bird nests in pipes that have silted up and gone mute, and it signals by tilting to flash a white patch under the wing. Something larger lives inside the rolled mats. A roll will sometimes bulge and move a little way against the wind, and once in a long while a wet brown flank shows at the open end.

People have been here, though not many, and possibly not this season. Certain flutes are stopped with clay and wadded grass, and the thumbprints are still in the clay. The plugging is deliberate. With the right pipes silenced, the notes on the lee side of a tor cancel one another, and a patch of near-quiet the size of a room appears. Walking into one feels like a stair missing underfoot. In those patches stand ring-walls of stacked slab, waist-high, with hearth-black on the inner stones. When the wind turns, the quiet slides off elsewhere and the shelter is left standing in the roar, useless until the wind comes back around.

Other marks:

- **Fog-nets:** knotted fibre cord strung between stakes of bone, dripping into pecked stone bowls.
- **Tethered gardens:** a single mat held down with a ring of weights and a braided tether. It strains at the tether like a tied animal.
- **Cairns:** a line of them, each capped with white quartz, leading toward the rim.
- **A mica disc:** found at one cairn, drilled and hung on a thong, for flashing signals the way the birds do.

There is no evidence that anyone has ever shouted here.

At the rim the table simply ends. Below is a floor of cloud, and the wind can be watched arriving. It heaves the cloud up the cliff face, throws it over the edge in rags, and sends the rags racing inland so that sunlight switches on and off across the stone. Out over the lowlands a thunderhead flickers. The lightning is plain to see, but the thunder never arrives. It is lost somewhere in the chord.

Then the chord slides, the shriek comes forward, and across forty kilometres every loose green thing lifts an edge and begins to move the other way.
