The floor of this basin was a sea until recently, as land reckons time. It lies a little below the level of the ocean that abandoned it: pale clay hardpan cracked into polygons, with seams of gypsum that flash when the sun is low and drifts of shell hash still sharp enough to cut. Nothing has carved it yet. There are no gullies, no fans, no channels, because rain reaches the ground perhaps once in several years, and when it does it spreads in a sheet a finger deep and is gone by noon. The basin is wide enough that crossing it on foot would take a season, and until the builders came it had no relief at all.

Now it has mounds. They stand every twenty paces in every direction: cones of cemented clay taller than a person and broader at the base than a room, fluted with buttresses and capped with blunt chimneys. They are harder than the ground they were taken from. The spacing is not planned, only enforced. Each termite colony forages outward until it meets its neighbors, and the borders settle into a rough honeycomb that repeats to the horizon and for hundreds of horizons beyond. From any mound top the view is the same in every direction, ranks of ochre towers thinning into haze.

The surface is the lesser half. Under the hardpan, galleries run from mound to mound, and narrow shafts drop thirty or forty meters to the old seabed's buried damp. Workers carry wet clay up grain by grain, and this is the only water that moves here. Inside a mound the air is dense and humid, held steady while the outside swings from cool dawn to dry afternoon heat. The colonies keep fungus on combs of chewed straw in chambers the size of a skull, thousands of chambers to a mound. These are the wettest gardens in the basin, and they never see light.

What the termites bring up, plants follow. Mound clay is richer than the pan, turned over and salted with waste, and nearly every mound wears something:

- a flat-crowned thorn tree rooted through the flank
- a knot of grey-leaved evergreen shrub
- a candelabra succulent
- a skirt of wiry bunchgrass around the foot

Between mounds the pan is bare, or carries a thin stubble of annual straw. So the green comes in dots, one per tower, like an orchard no one planted. Fallen leaves do not lie overnight. By morning each is sheathed in a thin crust of mud, and under the crust it is being carried away.

The towers are also lodgings. Monitor lizards break into the flanks to lay, and the workers seal the eggs in for them, incubated at a constant warmth. Small parrots and ground kingfishers dig nest holes high on the shaded sides. Mongoose bands hold dead mounds like tenements, a sentry upright on the chimney. Geckos take the cracks, snakes take the vents, and owls take whatever the snakes vacate. Dwarf antelope the color of the clay step between the towers, getting all their water from leaves. The whole economy runs on the builders. Aardvarks and pangolins open a mound a night and move on, and the breach is plastered by dawn. Raiding ants cross the pan in dark columns and return with soldiers in their jaws. Large-eared foxes walk with their heads tilted down, hunting by ear alone.

They can do so because the place is very quiet. There is no running water, and the mounds break the wind without giving it leaves enough to hiss through. The birds are sparing with their voices in the heat. The basin's own sounds are small:

- claws ticking on hardpan
- a seed pod splitting
- the dry click of a mound wall as the sun reaches it
- a faint papery rustle from inside a wall, heard only with an ear against it, where thousands of soldiers are knocking their heads on the tunnel roof in alarm

It is never still, though. At sunrise the east faces warm and the mounds begin to breathe, stale air rising through the chimneys while cool air is drawn in at the base. By midmorning dust devils stand up between the towers and wander the avenues. By afternoon the heat off the basin floor has built its own cloud, flat-bottomed cumulus spaced almost as evenly as the mounds below, trailing grey veils of rain that evaporate a kilometer above the ground. Sometimes dry lightning lights the stubble, and a low fire runs the corridors for a day, blackening the grass skirts and leaving the towers standing in ash. The trees resprout from the mound flanks. At dusk the foraging parties come up through pencil-wide holes and the foxes begin their rounds.

Once a year, on the single evening when the air turns heavy, every colony opens its walls at the same hour, and the winged ones pour out across the whole basin at once. For that night the quiet thins into a soft, dry whirring. Everything feeds: birds, lizards, mongooses, ants, and the foxes leaping. In the morning the pan is silvered with shed wings that shift in drifts with the first breeze, and the survivors have already paired and dug in.

Most dig in at the edge. Out there the honeycomb gives way to hardpan that is still blank, flat and salt-streaked, with nothing on it but polygon cracks. There the newest mounds stand a hand high, damp-dark at the tip, each with one grass seedling already rooted on its lee side. Behind them, in the oldest districts, dead towers are slumping into broad, low swells of real soil. These are the first slopes the basin has had, and they wait for the rain that will one day find them and begin cutting.
