The basin sits high, a shallow bowl of old granite perhaps twenty kilometres from rim to rim. Up here the sky is a darker blue than it is lower down, and the sun arrives without much air in the way. It should be cold. Instead the air is as warm as a low valley in late spring, and it stays warm after dark, as though the heat had been told to remain.

**The rock**

Everything is worn to its final shape. The rims are low rounded shoulders with no cliffs. Across the floor stand granite domes and tors, their boulders stacked and softened, the undersides hollowed into honeycomb by ages of salt and damp. Between them lies a plain of pale grit, quartz and feldspar crumbled from the domes, which gives the light back upward so that things are lit from beneath as well as above. Shadows are small and hard-edged. There are no fresh scars, no rockfalls, no new gullies. The drainage lines were decided long ago and the water, when it comes, follows them.

**The water**

On most afternoons cloud builds over the eastern rim, and on some of them it rains for twenty minutes. The domes shed it in sheets. It collects in weathering pans on the dome tops, flat-bottomed basins from a hand to a room across, and in a few sandy channels where it sinks and travels underground to a seep at the basin's low point. By the next noon the small pans are dry and rimmed with a pale crust. The large ones hold for weeks. They are thick with fairy shrimp that hatch within a day of filling, and with the tadpoles of a toad that spends the rest of its life sealed in the grit.

**The plants**

They keep their distance from one another. Bunchgrass grows in separate tufts, each with its own apron of bare ground. Tight cushion plants are hard as upholstery. Black lichen marks every route water takes down a dome. On the dome shoulders there are mats of a brown, dead-looking fern that turns green within an hour of rain and brown again by the following evening. A few twisted, small-leaved trees stand in clefts where the runoff concentrates. There are perhaps forty in the whole basin, each one known to every bird.

**The quiet**

Sound does not carry here. A stone dropped on rock makes a small, close noise that is gone a few paces away. Wind moves the grass and can be heard only by someone standing in it. Lightning over the rim arrives with a faint pressure in the chest and little else. Thin air accounts for part of this but not all of it. The quiet has an evenness that feels maintained, like the warmth.

**The animals**

The basin is full of animals, and they have arranged their lives around the silence.

- **The grazers.** Slender and fawn-coloured, they move across the grit in loose herds of thirty or fifty. They never bunch. Each keeps a clear line of sight to several others, and alarm passes through the herd as a raised white tail patch repeated from animal to animal, faster than a call would travel.
- **The tor colonies.** Rock-dwelling animals the size of rabbits post sentinels on the highest boulders. A sentinel signals by standing and dropping, standing and dropping.
- **The birds.** They do not sing. Small ground-feeding flocks have a patch under each wing as reflective as foil, and they speak in flashes that are visible for kilometres in this light, a glittering that runs along a dome flank and stops. Swifts work the air over the pans. Two or three large raptors are always up, turning very slowly.
- **The hunters.** A long-legged cat the colour of the grit hunts by day, since a stalk makes no sound here and what matters is not being seen. It moves only when the herd's heads are down. Most stalks fail. The herds watch, the cat waits, and the raptors watch both.
- **The small things.** Lizards bask on every warm surface. Beetles with white wax backs cross the plain at noon when nothing else will. After rain, pale moths rise from the cushion plants.

**The made things**

There are not many of them, and they are easy to miss.

The most common are the slabs. Each is a dull grey plane the size of a table and the thickness of a palm, resting level in the air a forearm's length above the ground. There are a few dozen across the basin, in no pattern anyone could walk. No dust settles on them. They have been here long enough to have a geology of their own: beneath each is a rectangle of clean pale rock that has never been rained on or grown lichen, and around that rectangle runs a shallow groove worn by the drip from the edges. The groove is centimetres deep in granite. The slabs are slightly warmer than the air at every hour. Lizards cover them at dawn. The grazers lie beneath them in the rain, and the cat knows it.

There is a line. It is a hair's width, perfectly straight, and it crosses the basin from rim to rim over domes and plain alike. Grit will not rest on it. It can be followed for hours as a faint clean thread, and the game trails cross it only at certain places, squarely, as if those were gates.

On the largest dome one pan is always full. It is circular where the others are irregular, and its water stays cool and clear with no crust at the rim, through the driest weeks. Every trail in the western half of the basin ends there eventually. Nothing lives in the water itself.

Near the seep stands the only thing that could be called a building, a low curved shell of the same grey, open along one side and big enough for a few people to sit in. It is roofed, yet the light inside is exactly as bright as the light outside, with no source and no shadows. A tor colony has denned in it for uncounted generations and worn a smooth dip in the threshold.

**The day**

Dawn brings a brief dew that beads on the cushion plants and is licked off by beetles and birds before the sun clears the rim. The herds graze through the morning. At noon the basin goes still and white, and only the wax-backed beetles and the raptors move. In the afternoon the cloud builds, its shadow crosses the plain, and sometimes rain falls, ticking very softly on the grit. Then the ferns go green, the pans shine, the toads come up, the moths lift, and for an hour the birds flash along every dome. Dusk is warm. So is the night. In the dark, when signals cannot be seen, the animals draw in around the slabs and the shell and lie close together, facing outward.

Nothing here appears to be running down, and nothing appears to be tended. The machines, if that is what they are, have weathered into the place as the tors have. Rain, herds, and the cat's routes all take them into account.
