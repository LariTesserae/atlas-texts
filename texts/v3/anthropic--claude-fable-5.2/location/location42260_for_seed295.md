**The Tableland at Night**

Sound comes first, because there is almost no light to compete with it. The plateau sits high enough that cloud passes across it rather than over it, and tonight the cloud has closed like a lid. Under it the air is warm and close, and the frogs are screaming.

There are thousands of them in the shallow pans between the mounds. Each pan holds a few centimetres of last week's rain and its own chorus, slightly out of time with the next. The effect is a wall of pulsing, metallic noise that seems to come up through the feet. Over it the katydids run a high, continuous sizzle from every grass stem and leaf. Over that, at intervals, tree hyraxes ratchet up from a creak to a full, tearing shriek, each answered from the next thicket and the next until the call has travelled the width of the tableland and come back. Standing here, you would have to shout into someone's ear.

The ground can be learned by walking. It rises, crests and falls, then rises again, every thirty or forty paces, as regular as a swell. These are mounds, and none of them is a hill. The oldest are broad domes, three or four metres high and twenty across, weathered smooth and slumped at the shoulders, their surfaces skinned with grass and moss. The younger ones stand on the backs of the old as hard, fluted spires, their clay still sharp-edged and rough as bark to the hand. They run in every direction to the rim of the plateau, where the land drops off into cooler, emptier air. There are tens of thousands of them across a tableland a day's walk wide.

No point on that walk touches ground that was not made. The red earth of the mounds was carried up from below, a mouthful at a time, and cemented with saliva. The paler soil of the pans is what the rains have washed off the mounds over centuries, fine as flour and already once swallowed. Beneath that the earth is tunnelled and backfilled to the depth of the water table. The plateau's original stone is somewhere far underneath, and nothing living here has contact with it. The contours, the drainage, the places where water stands and where it runs off are all the accumulated work of blind insects the length of a fingernail.

A hand laid flat on a spire feels a warmth that is not left over from the day. The mound is breathing. Stale air rises through the central chimney and seeps out through the porous skin, while cooler air sinks down the outer flutes. Deep inside, combs of chewed grass are furred with the white fungus the colony farms. With an ear pressed to the clay, the inside can be heard under the frogs as a dry, rapid ticking. It is the soldiers drumming their heads against the gallery walls, passing an alarm about the hand.

The mounds are where the trees are. Every old dome carries a thicket of figs, wild olives and thorny scramblers, with ferns in the crotches of the branches and lianas tying the crowns together. The built soil is richer and better drained than anything in the pans, so the plateau's forest stands as islands, one dark mass per mound, with tall, wet grass in the lanes between. Mist condenses in the leaves and drips all night. That drip, along with the brief rains that come every week or so, is most of the water this place gets. It is enough to keep everything damp to the touch and nothing flooded.

The thickets are as constructed as the ground. The outer branches hang heavy with weaver nests, hundreds to a tree, woven grass flasks packed so close they knock together when the wind moves. The birds are asleep inside, but never quite silent. A constant low chirring and shifting comes from the colony, and now and then the whole tree erupts when a snake or a genet tests a branch. Higher up are the black carton nests of ants, chewed wood-pulp balls the size of a torso, and the grey paper combs of wasps tucked under limbs. Certain trees are wrapped from crown to root in the dense, dirty silk of social spiders, a single tent housing thousands that sags with the night's dew. The termites have also built covered roads up every trunk, thin mud tubes that branch like veins, so that they can cross open bark without ever touching open air.

The mounds are hollowed from outside as well. Something large and long-clawed digs into their flanks to feed, and the holes it leaves never stay empty. Porcupines take them, then wild pigs, then mongooses in gangs of twenty, then swallows that plaster mud cups on the tunnel ceiling. Bats pour out of the oldest dead chimneys at dusk. They are overhead now in numbers. They are felt more than heard, a flicker of displaced air past the face, though their lowest clicks come through the din like fingernails tapping.

There is a little light. Fireflies drift over the pans in slow green dashes, enough to show the shine of water and a quick constellation of frog eyes. On one rotting log a patch of fungus glows so faintly that it vanishes when looked at directly. Once or twice an hour the cloud thins and a few stars show through, and for a moment the whole tableland is visible in silhouette, a field of round shapes and spires and bushy crowns repeating to the horizon like a city with no streets.

When rain comes, it comes without wind, a warm patter lasting twenty minutes. Then every mound opens. Workers cut slits in the clay and the winged ones pour out by the million, a soft, papery roar of wings that for a short while drowns even the frogs. Everything on the plateau comes to eat them. Frogs gorge until they cannot close their mouths. Nightjars sweep the lanes, bush babies snatch from the branches, and ants drag the fallen away by the wings. By morning the pans are surfaced with shed wings like a skin of mica, and the pale caps of the farmed fungus will be pushing up through the mound flanks, fruiting in open air for the only time in the year.

A spire grows about a finger's width in a wet season. A colony lives as long as its queen, perhaps twenty years. Then the mound stands vacant and softening until another founding pair digs into its base and the building resumes on the old foundation. The domes underfoot have been raised, abandoned, eroded and raised again more times than can be counted. Their spacing was settled long ago by slow wars between neighbours, and it has not changed since. Rain takes a little clay down into the pans every year, and every year the termites carry about the same amount back up.

Toward dawn the frogs will thin out and the weavers will take over, and the noise will be no less.
