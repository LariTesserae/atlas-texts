The land thins out here before it ends. For the last several kilometres the coast is a flat of grey-brown mud barely a metre above the spring tides, and the sea is only a line of slightly different grey. On this flat someone built a salt and soda works with its own town, ran it for about seventy years, and left. That was long enough ago that the leaving is no longer the main fact about the place.

It is the season before the rains. A lid of cloud has sat on the coast for weeks, and the sun is only a brighter patch in it. The light is pewter and casts no shadows. By midday it is forty-one degrees, and the air is too wet for sweat to cool anything. At night the sheet iron drops below the dew point and the whole works sweats. At dawn every eave drips water that tastes of rust and salt.

**The layout**

The ponds come first. They form a grid of rectangular pans divided by bunds of earth and stone, six kilometres along the shore and two deep. They step inward from the big outer condensers to the small crystallisers.

Behind them stand the works: pump houses along the intake channel, a wash plant, a boiler house with two brick chimneys, and bagging sheds. A narrow-gauge railway runs through it, its tipping wagons still on the rails, out to a jetty on the tidal creek.

Behind that is the town. It has eight streets of single-storey row houses, a clinic, a cinema with its front fallen in, and a water tower.

All of it was built for an industry that needed area, so everything is far apart. The walk from the bagging sheds to the nearest pump house takes twenty minutes on a causeway with brine on both sides. The streets are wide enough for trucks to turn in. The sheds are forty metres long and contain air.

**Materials**

The decay has finished its quick phase and settled:

- **Concrete:** it has spalled wherever the salt reached the rebar. The steel has swollen and burst its cover, leaving slabs lying beside their columns.
- **Brick:** it is fringed with white efflorescence to about head height, where the ground-brine stops climbing.
- **Sheet iron:** the corrugated cladding is lace at the bottom and sound at the top.
- **Timber:** the sluice boards are grey and hard as bone, pickled by the brine that was supposed to wear them out.

Whatever was going to fall has fallen. What still stands is under a crust of salt and scale and changes very little from year to year.

**What grows**

No plants grow here. The ground is saturated with salt, and the row of shade trees by the manager's bungalow is a row of grey stumps.

The only colour is microbial. The outer ponds are a murky olive, the middle ones orange, and the innermost the pink of raw meat, deepening to magenta. A leathery, black-green mat covers the floors of the shallower pans and peels up in sheets when they dry.

**The gates**

The tide gates still work. They are cast-iron flaps hung from top hinges. The spring tide pushes them open and the ebb drops them shut, with a single thud that carries across the flat.

So the works still makes salt. Brine moves inward pan by pan and thickens, and in the crystallisers a crust grows through the dry months to a hand's thickness. Nobody rakes it. The rains come and take it back, and this has gone on for decades.

**The animals**

They outnumber everything else here.

- **Brine shrimp:** the pond water is granular with them, a moving turbidity.
- **Brine flies:** they lie in a dark band along every waterline, a metre wide and kilometres long. If you walk toward the band it parts and closes behind you with a sound like a sheet being drawn off a bed.
- **Flamingos:** tens of thousands stand in the middle ponds, pink-grey in the grey light, heads down.
- **Other birds:** avocets and stilts work the shallows. Small sandpipers cover the mud in carpets that lift and resettle. Gulls run open-billed through the fly band.
- **Crabs:** on the creek mud by the jetty there is a fiddler crab to every hand-span, each male raising one pale claw. Mudskippers lie between them.

Every bird on the ponds is eating shrimp or flies.

The boiler house belongs to bats. Several hundred thousand free-tailed bats pack the roof trusses and the insides of both flues. The floor beneath them is knee-deep in guano, and the guano moves with beetles, roaches and moth larvae. The ammonia stings the eyes from the doorway. Between that floor and that ceiling are twenty metres of hot, dark, empty air.

That is the pattern everywhere. Everything alive is on a surface (a waterline, a truss, a wall, a skin of mud) and the volumes between are vacant.

In the town, pigeons roost in the rafters and geckos hold every wall. Rats live under the floors, and snakes follow the rats. Every doorway and window frame is hung with spider web so loaded with flies that it sags like grey rag. At night jackals come along the railway embankment for fallen bat pups and for the eggs of the stilts that nest on the bunds.

**Sound**

For all this, it is quiet. The mud and the wet air take sound, and most days there is no wind.

The flamingos make a low, continuous murmur, like a crowd in another building. The iron ticks as it warms. The gates thud twice a day. Close to, the crabs make a faint wet crackle.

**The day**

- **Dawn:** the eaves drip, and the birds feed hardest in the few cooler hours.
- **Midday:** the brine in the shallow pans is hotter than the air. The birds stand, the flies sit, and the crabs go down their burrows.
- **Afternoon:** the cloud thickens inland and thunder rolls. Sometimes rain falls for ten minutes, roaring on the sheet iron. It is the only loud thing that happens here, and afterwards steam rises off the causeways.
- **Dusk:** the bats leave. It takes forty minutes. They pour from the chimney tops and the broken roof-lights in a rope of smoke that bends inland toward fields and moths, with a soft rushing like rain that never lands. Falcons wait at the stacks and take them one at a time, unhurried.
- **Night:** there is no lamp anywhere in the town. The only light is a faint orange stain on the cloud base from a city far down the coast. The flamingos feed on in the dark, by touch.

**The rules**

- **Fresh water is the scarce thing, and it comes only from the sky.** Anything that holds rain (a tank top, a tyre rut, a bathtub in a row house) is wriggling with mosquito larvae within a week. Dragonflies arrive on the storm fronts to lay in the same water.
- **Salt decides who lives where.** Going inward, the number of kinds of animal falls and the number of individuals rises.
- **The tide runs the works, and the rain resets it.**
- **Everything large is here for something very small.**

At the innermost pans even that stops. The brine is too strong for shrimp, so no birds come. The bunds are bare, and the crust squeaks underfoot. The pink water lies perfectly flat under the grey sky, slowly making salt.
