The sea sits on top of a plateau, three thousand metres above the lowlands it never reaches as anything but mist. It has no shore in the usual sense. On three sides the water stands against scarps of black basalt so recently broken that the fracture faces still carry a glassy rind. On the fourth side there is no rock at all, only the lip, a straight edge four hundred kilometres long where the whole sea goes over.

That is where the sound comes from. The fall does not surge or pause. The inflow is cold upwelling through fissures in the floor, a steady pressure from somewhere deep in the massif, and it matches the outflow so exactly that the surface has not moved a finger's width in living record. So the roar holds one pitch at one weight, a low unbroken tone that the water carries across the entire basin and the basalt returns. Near the lip it is a physical thing: it shakes the vision, loosens the teeth, makes the surface stand up in a fine, fixed stipple like hammered tin. Six hundred kilometres away, against the back scarp, it is still too loud to speak over. No part of this sea has ever been quiet. In a few places the sound cancels itself.

The water is very clear. Nothing grows in it: no weed, no green haze, no drifting bloom. The plateau drowned before anything could root, and the cold has kept it that way. Daylight comes through a high, even overcast and goes down a long distance unobstructed, so the floor is visible from the surface in most places. It is a pavement of column-tops, hexagons a stride across, each one edged as sharply as when it cracked. Between them lie pillow lavas with their skins still wrinkled and sheets of obsidian. Nothing is rounded. The water has no grit in it to grind with, so it only passes over, a degree above freezing, and the edges stay edges. At the scarps a collar of ice forms, as wide as a hand, and it has never been wider.

Along the fissures where the water comes in there is a film, the nearest thing here to vegetation. It is a rust-and-sulphur skin of bacteria, thinner than paint, staining the vent margins orange and bone-white. Everything alive depends on it.

There is a good deal alive, though the basin is so large that it seems like nothing. One can cross water for a day and see only columns. Then comes a lane.

Because the source never varies, the echoes never vary, and the sea is ruled with standing lines. These are long corridors where the outgoing roar and its returns from the scarps meet out of step and erase each other. Each is a few metres wide and runs for hundreds of kilometres, straight as a surveyor's chain, in the same place as far back as anyone has measured. Inside a lane the pressure drops away. The surface goes glass-smooth in a stripe, and a swimmer can hear their own pulse. One arm's length sideways, the wall of noise is back.

The animals live in the lanes. Pale amphipods the length of a thumb hang there in columns from floor to surface, millions of them, all facing the same way and barely moving, a grey thread drawn across an empty sea. Slow, earless fish with skulls like flat stones and bodies that look translucent and unfinished graze the bacterial film where a lane happens to cross a fissure. These crossings are the only crowded places in the basin, a few square metres dense with bodies and then nothing again for forty kilometres. Something long and ribbon-shaped patrols the lane edges, taking amphipods that drift out and are stunned by the sound. Above the surface, white birds stand on the still stripes of water in single file, heads tucked, spaced as evenly as fence posts. They fly only along the line.

The builders found the lanes too. Their stations stand in them: riveted iron caissons on legs sunk between the columns, each with a gauge-house on top, set one every fifty kilometres down the longest null. The plate is cold-blackened but hardly rusted, since there is little in this water to rust it. Inside each house a float turns a pen against a paper drum, and the drum is driven by a small turbine in the legs that takes its power from the steady push of water toward the lip. The trace on the paper is a straight line. Rolls of straight line fill the lockers. Someone wound them and labelled them in careful hands, and at some point the labelling stops, and the pens go on drawing over their own ink.

A cable runs along the floor from station to station, armoured and as thick as a wrist, lying across the hexagons without a mark of wear. At the lip itself stands the one large structure, a turbine hall bolted into the basalt a little back from the edge. It is long and low, with cast-iron penstocks like organ pipes laid on their sides, and its rotors still turn at the rate the sea sets for them. It powers the lamps on the gauge-houses, which burn in daylight. It also powers a horn on the roof that sounds every hour and that nobody has ever heard. The only sign it is working is a brief second tremor in the handrail under the first.

Beyond the hall the water bends over in a single unbroken sheet and falls into its own cloud. The edge of the lip has not been notched. The cloud never clears and never thickens. The fall has stayed at one pitch for longer than the paper records, and the animals are still in their lanes.
