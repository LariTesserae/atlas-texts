**The Upper Works**

The Works stand above the cloud. From the outer parapet the cloud is a white floor far below, bright as a salt pan, and the ropeway cables slope down into it and disappear. Nobody on the upper tiers can say with confidence what the pile stands on. The oldest drawings show brick, and beneath that an older bond of brick, and beneath that the drawings stop.

The place is a horseshoe, perhaps three kilometres from tip to tip, built up in tiers around a central hollow the workers call the Basin. Every surface is made. The ground is engineering brick, glazed terracotta, riveted plate, wired glass and cast-iron grating with the pattern worn half away. The soil is made too: furnace ash, clinker fines, swept leaf and a century of bird droppings, raked into the terrace beds and the cracks. Where a foot has fallen for long enough the stair treads are dished like spoons. The brick corners are rounded. The iron stopped rusting long ago and settled into a hard brown skin that comes off on the hands as a faint smell and nothing else.

At this height the air should be thin and bitter, and one step outside the wall it is. But the furnaces are far down in the body of the pile, and their heat climbs through flues, steam mains and the brick itself, so the whole crown of the Works sits in a cap of warmth like a hand held over a lamp. Warmth follows the pipes here, and green follows the warmth. The outer wall has rime on its outward face most mornings and jasmine on its inward one. A pigeon crossing the parapet flies through forty degrees of difference in a wingbeat and does not seem to mind.

So the place is green, and thickly. Strangler figs have rooted in the mortar of the retaining walls and sent their grey roots down four and five tiers, gripping the buttresses like poured metal. Tree ferns stand in the old cooling galleries where the roof glass has gone milky. Bananas run in a line along the hot main on Tier Nine, and the line turns where the main turns. Old pipe lagging has grown a second lagging of moss a hand deep. Orchids sit on the pipe brackets, and tomato vines, escaped from some canteen garden generations back, sprawl over the gantry rails. The disused signal gantry on the east horn is nothing but bougainvillea now, a magenta arch thirty metres up. The masons work around all of this. Where a fig is prising a wall they leave it alone, build a relieving arch around the root, and date the brick. Some roots have three arches of three different brick colours.

Water is never plentiful and never quite absent. There is no river and no real rain, since the weather happens below. But every exhaust that leaves the warm cap hits the cold above it and turns white, and by mid-afternoon the day's venting has gathered into a low private ceiling over the Basin. Around the fourth whistle it comes back down as a fine warm drizzle that smells faintly of coal and lasts twenty minutes. The roofs are all pitched to catch it. Gutters lead to troughs and troughs to cisterns, and beneath the old aqueduct spans limescale has grown into stalactites as long as an arm, each with a patch of fern below it where the drip lands. By evening the brick is dry again, though the moss stays wet.

The light is hard and high. There is less air overhead to soften it, and it comes off the cloud floor from below as well, so the shadows under the viaducts are lit blue from underneath. The glass roofs of the rolling sheds flash like signal mirrors. The white glazed tile of the pump houses is difficult to look at before ten. Leaves are thick, waxy and very dark, and everything flowers in colours that can stand the glare.

It is loud in a steady, sorted way. Under everything is the beat of the big winding engines, slow enough to count, felt in the handrails as much as heard. Over that come the rattle of the ore conveyor, the cough of relief valves and the long wire-hum of the ropeway as crated castings ride down into the white. Over that again are the animals, and there are a great many:

- **Swifts** nest in every ventilation louvre and come out screaming in sheets when the shift whistle goes.
- **Choughs** work the canteen yards.
- **Kestrels** hang in the updraft over the main stack, hardly moving their wings, held up by furnace heat.
- **Bees** have taken over the dead junction boxes along the east horn. Box after box hums at a pitch slightly below the live ones, and the electricians tell them apart by ear.
- **Geckos** sit on the lamp housings, and long green lizards lie on the steam mains in rows, all facing the same way.
- **Goats**, belonging to nobody anyone can name, graze the sod roofs of the pattern shops and move between tiers by the fire stairs.
- **Frogs** start up in the cisterns at dusk and are louder by full dark than the engines.
- **Moths** come to the arc lamps in such numbers that the bats in the dead Number Three flue do not bother to hunt anywhere else.

Nothing here hurries. The engines have run at the same beat since they were set. The Works gain about a course of brick a year, laid on top, and the green rises with it a season behind. The figs are slower than the masons but more certain. On the lowest warm tier, where the first cold comes in and the ferns thin out, there is a wall so old that it is more root than brick, and it still bears its load. Someone has painted a date on it, and someone later has painted that out and written only: *holding*.
