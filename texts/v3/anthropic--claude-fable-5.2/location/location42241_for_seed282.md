Under the floor slab of an ice plant that is not yet finished, there is a room about the size of a rail wagon. It is reached by a hatch and a steel ladder whose lower rungs are still in primer. Nothing in it was found here. The floor is poured, the walls are poured, and the grain of the formwork boards is printed into them so clearly you can count knots. On the newest wall the boards are still in place, held by timber props wedged against the floor. The concrete is dark with its own water. It has not finished becoming stone.

The ceiling is low enough to touch and you would not want to. It is crowded with brine mains, black iron pipes as thick as a thigh, running in ranks with hand-widths between them. They drop through valves with spoked wheels and branch into flanges, some bolted up and some closed with blank plates. Half the pipes are wrapped in cork and tarred canvas. The laggers have not reached the other half, and those are white. Conduit threads between everything. To cross the room you turn sideways and duck twice.

One lamp in a wire cage gives amber light, and a green pilot glows on a panel by the ladder. The frost hands back more light than it should, so the bare pipes look faintly lit from inside while the lagged ones vanish.

The air sits just under freezing at head height and just over it at the ankles. Dampness comes down the hatch from the works above and the room takes it. On bare iron the frost grows as fur, then as needles a finger long, all leaning the same way in a draught too slight to feel.

Every few hours something overhead reverses, and for some minutes the mains run warm. The frost goes grey and slumps, then slides off in whole sleeves that land with the sound of wet sugar. For a quarter of an hour the room drips from every surface. Then it stills, and the white starts again at the flanges and creeps outward.

The meltwater runs to a sump in the corner. There is no pump, only a plinth with four bolts standing up from it, their threads showing the first orange bloom. So the sump fills and skins over, and the ice has built up in thin lenses, one per thaw. It is the only calendar in the room.

Other things are beginning. Where water seeps through the new slab it brings lime with it, and the ceiling has grown its first straws. They are hollow and thin as grass stems, a centimetre long, each with a drop at its tip. Under each is a white crust on the floor the size of a coin. The latest pour lies under hessian blankets, still giving off the small heat of its curing, and a low mist stands over it at shin height.

The hessian has sprouted. A dozen pale threads have come up from seed caught in the weave, white and leafless, all bent toward the lamp. On the wall around the lamp, the bulb keeps a hand's breadth of concrete above freezing, and a green film has taken hold there in a rough circle, with a sharp edge where the cold resumes. That is all that lives here. The angles of the conduit hold no webs, and the dust on the panel lies unmarked.

It is mostly quiet. The compressor above comes through the slab as a slow beat, felt in the ladder more than heard. Iron ticks as it contracts. Now and then there is a single knock in the main as the brine shifts its weight.

The fitters have chalked the walls and floor with outlines, arrows and bore sizes to show where the next runs will go, and the frost keeps to the chalk. At shoulder height a white band crosses the bare wall between two tick marks, exactly as wide as a four-inch line, where no pipe hangs. It is cold enough to take skin. A rectangle of rime sits on the pump plinth between the bolts, and the water beside it shivers in time with the compressor. On the blanked branches the gauges already read pressure.

In places the chalk has been rubbed out and redrawn a little to one side, to agree with where the white fell. A glove lies on a valve wheel. A tea can is frozen to the floor by its ring.

Here the cold is laid first and the iron follows. What the room lacks is already present as a temperature.

When the thaw comes, the phantom runs do not slump, because they have nothing to fall from. They fade from the edges inward and are the last to go. When the mains chill again they are the first to come back, before the first needle has formed on any real pipe.
