Above, the country is dry scrub on pale limestone, and nothing gives the chamber away except a fig tree far greener than its neighbours. Its roots go down through a hole no wider than a well-mouth. They hang the full height of the room below, about the height of the tree itself, before they touch ground.

The room is shaped like the inside of a bell, some forty paces across at the floor. The roof fell in long ago, and the rubble it left has had time to become a mound of black soil, rounded and settled, its stones cemented under flowstone the colour of old teeth. Nothing here is sharp. The walls have been smoothed by slow wet into folds and draperies, and where they aren't stone-smooth they are furred.

The heat comes from below. This is backwards for a cave, and the body notices before the mind does: the air gets warmer with every step down. The back wall is as warm as an animal's flank. A fissure there, low and lipped with yellow crust, breathes a thin steam that smells of struck flint and boiled egg. That breath is the room's only water. There is no stream and no pool. The steam climbs, meets the cooler stone of the ceiling, beads, and falls. Everything here is wetted a drop at a time, and the only standing water sits in rimmed basins in the flowstone, each about the size of two cupped hands.

Light comes through the hole already used, strained green by fig leaves. For less than an hour at midday a single shaft reaches the floor and crosses the mound like the hand of a clock. The rest of the day is a brown-green dusk that fades to black at the walls.

The plants arrange themselves by that light. On the crown of the mound, where the shaft passes, grow ferns with fronds as long as an arm and a fleshy, pale-leaved plant with red undersides. A few paces out there is moss, deep enough to lose a finger in. Beyond that grow liverworts, flat and wet as tongues, and then only a skin of algae on the rock. In the true dark by the walls the green gives out, and white fungal thread spreads over the guano in fans.

It is very quiet, and not because the room is empty. The moss, the root curtains, and the soft floor swallow sound. A dislodged stone lands as if on a mattress. The drips fall on moss and make no sound.

The animals keep the same silence:

- **Bats:** They roost in the dome by the thousand, a dark shifting pelt on the ceiling. All that comes down from them is a rustle like a page turned in another room.
- **Crickets:** They are pale and wingless and have no song. Their antennae are three times the length of their bodies, and they read the room by touch.
- **Frogs:** They sit at the rims of the basins and do not call. One will lift a hind leg, spread the white webbing of its foot, hold it, and lower it. Across the mound, another answers the same way.
- **Glow-worms:** Over the fissure, where the steam keeps the rock wettest, they hang their beaded threads and burn small and blue.

Every surface is occupied. There is a gecko every hand-span on the lit wall, each holding its small territory. Whip-spiders sit flat as pressed flowers in the dark. Snails with shells clear enough to show the dark coil of the body inside graze the hanging roots. Millipedes and beetles turn the guano over ceaselessly, so the floor seems to seethe slightly if watched. Small crabs the colour of candle wax keep to the basins. In the root-throat at the top, a snake lies looped, waiting for evening.

People were here once, and they had only their hands. The marks they left are few:

- **The hands:** On the wall the noon light touches there is a row of them, outlined in blown red ochre at shoulder height. Below it is a second row of smaller ones.
- **The hearth:** On the shoulder of the mound is a ring of stones, blackened on the inner faces. It holds moss and a little water now, and the frogs lay their eggs there.
- **The stack:** On a ledge sit flat stones laid three courses high, with gecko eggs glued in the gaps like small white teeth.
- **The way down:** On the thickest root the bark is polished in patches spaced an arm's reach apart. The polish has been half grown over but can still be seen.

The day runs on a schedule. At dawn the bats pour back in, and for an hour a dry, soft rain of droppings falls. The floor rises to meet it. At noon the shaft arrives. Gnats lift into it and turn gold, and the geckos gather along its edge and move with it. Through the afternoon the heat thickens and nothing hurries.

At dusk the ceiling comes apart and funnels upward through the roots. The snake strikes, and strikes again. Whatever falls is found by the beetles and gone by morning.

Then the rock above cools under the night sky and the ceiling begins to sweat in earnest. For a few hours it rains inside the room, warm and slow, with no sound. The basins fill, and the frogs' white feet rise and fall in the dark. The blue lights over the fissure brighten. A snail on the wall crosses the palm of one of the small red hands.
