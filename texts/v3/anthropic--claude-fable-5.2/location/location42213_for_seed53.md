The place is a notch between two horns of dark rock, a saddle so high that the sky overhead is closer to indigo than blue. It is small. You could walk it end to end in two hundred paces, and across in sixty, if walking across were possible. On either side the ground falls away into air and far brown distance. Nothing grows here, not even a crust of lichen on the lee side of a boulder. The rock is bare and varnished, the color of old iron, and everything that isn't rock is ice.

The ice fills the saddle like a congregation. It has been cut by sun into blades, thousands of them, shoulder-high and hip-high, all leaning the same way toward where noon stands. They are so close together that their edges nearly touch and the gaps between them are blue slots a boot wide. Each blade is thin enough at the crest to let light through. At midday the whole field glows from within, and the glare is so complete that the rock horns look like holes cut in the brightness. Shadows up here are hard-edged and nearly black. There is no soft light until the cloud comes.

The stones follow a rule that takes a while to see. The large ones, fallen from the horns, do not lie on the ice but stand above it. Each slab perches on a stalk of ice it has shaded from the sun while everything around it wasted down. There are dozens of these tables, tilted and crowded, some at knee height and some at chest height. The small stones do the opposite. A pebble or a pinch of dark grit warms and sinks, drilling its own narrow well straight down, and it stops when the sun can no longer reach it. They all stop at about the same depth, a forearm down. So the saddle has a second floor beneath the first, a level plane of small dark things, each at the bottom of its own tube of clear water under its own lid of ice.

People came here, though not many and not for a long time. On the rock rib at the eastern lip there is a windbreak of stacked slabs, curved like a cupped hand and waist-high, its chinks packed with smaller stones chosen to fit. Inside is a ring of blackened rocks with nothing left in it. Beside the ring lies one length of wood, carried up from somewhere days below, bleached silver and split along the grain, light as a bone. A coil of plaited fiber cord has frozen into the gravel. There are a few sherds of coarse red pot.

Out on the ice sits a platform of flat stones, laid carefully and squared by eye. It stands on a plinth of ice that no one built. The stones shaded it and the saddle sank around it, so now it is raised like an altar, though it may only have been a floor.

From the windbreak to the platform there was once a lane, hacked through the blades with stone tools. The ice has grown back, but the blades there are younger and shorter, so the path survives as a dip in the crowd's height, a line you can see but not walk. Along it, at the common depth, lie the things that were dropped:

- black glass flakes, struck from a core, their edges still sharp
- a hammered copper pin, gone green
- a drilled shell bead, which has no business at this altitude

Each one hangs in its own well, magnified slightly by the water and perfectly kept. Nothing here is lost. It is filed.

There are no animals. The wind sometimes delivers evidence of them from below. A moth lies spread on the snow, and there is a single barred feather. Both are sinking into small wells of their own.

The day runs to a strict order. At dawn everything is locked. The night has grown rime on the windward face of every blade and table, white feathers a finger long, all pointing into the wind that made them. Nothing makes a sound. The air is cold enough to hurt the teeth.

Then the sun clears the eastern horn and the rime goes without melting, straight into the air, and the blades begin to sweat. By midmorning there is a tick, then another, as drops fall into the slots. By noon the lids on the wells have thinned to nothing and the saddle is full of small open eyes of water. Threads of melt run between the blade roots toward the western lip, where they go over the edge and freeze to the cliff as a long grey tongue. Once or twice in a season a table stone outlives its stalk and slides off with a clack, the loudest thing that happens here, and begins a new pedestal where it lands.

In the afternoon cloud climbs the valleys and arrives all at once, and the glare turns to a shadowless white. It rarely snows properly. What falls is graupel, soft dry pellets that hiss on the blades and roll into the slots and collect there like grain. The wind moving through the edges of the ice doesn't whistle. It makes a wide breathy hush, like a held note with no pitch.

Then the cloud drops away, the light comes back low and gold through the blades, and the cold closes down. The wells skin over. The runnels stop mid-thread. For a quarter hour the ice creaks and pings as it tightens, and then that stops too, and the stars come out before the sky has finished darkening.

The saddle is not being worn away, and it is not building. The blades waste at the crest and are fed at the root. The tables rise and fall and rise. The wells keep their depth. Every day the saddle spends exactly what the night gave it.
