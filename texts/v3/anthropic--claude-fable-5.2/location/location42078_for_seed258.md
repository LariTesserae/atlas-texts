The plateau has no rock in it. What passes for ground is cane: stems as thick as a wrist, laid side by side and over-under, grown that way. The floor is woven tight enough to walk on and loose enough that water goes straight through. It gives a little underfoot, the way a dock does. Press a heel down and warm water rises around it, then drains when you lift.

The floor runs level for several kilometres in every direction, a tabletop the size of a valley with a town in it, except the town hasn't happened. Far apart across the floor, the weave lifts into shapes. There is a rectangle of wall, knee-high, four courses of stem coiled and stacked as neatly as basketry, with a gap in one side where a door would go. Three hundred paces on, a curve of footing closes into a ring. A flight of nine ledges climbs to nothing. A row of ribs, each thick as a thigh, rises from the floor and leans toward a matching row opposite, and the two rows stop a body-length short of meeting. Between these things there is only open weave, shingled with bronze-green leaves the size of thumbnails, all of it wet.

Everything here is made, and nothing made it but the plant. It is one plant, or at least there is nowhere to see one stop and another begin. There is no soil, and a dropped seed would wash through the floor. There are no stones either. The few round lumps that pass for boulders are galls, woody and hollow when struck.

Underneath is more of the same. The plateau stands on its own earlier floors, weave upon weave, pressed down, going brown and then black, rotting slowly at a depth no one could reach. The rot is what keeps the place warm at this height. Its heat comes up through the floor like breath through cloth, meets the cooler air, and turns to water. The fog is thin and bright, and the light through it is plain even daylight with no shadows. Water beads on every leaf-scale and runs down, and the whole plain drains through itself all the time.

That draining is the sound. It is a roar from underfoot, everywhere at once, like standing on the lid of a weir, and it never rises or falls. Over it come the ribs. Each one is hollow and open at the top, and the steady wind off the rim sounds them all in a low chord of five or six notes that never resolves. Near the ribs it is loud enough to feel in the teeth, and speech would have to be shouted.

It takes a long time to see that the walls are growing. The top course of each is pale as celery, soft, new. But the footing sinks into the settling floor exactly as fast as the top is laid, so the wall stands at four courses and has always stood at four courses. A wall here is a slow fountain, with material rising through a shape that stays put. The door gap has passed up through thousands of courses and is still the same width, too narrow for a shoulder and too tall for anything. The stair has always had nine ledges. The ribs have always leaned a body-length apart. Each rib ends in a closed bud the size of a fist, sheathed in wet scales, and no bud has ever opened.

Nothing left on the surface stays. A shed feather lies on the weave for a day, then sits a scale's depth under, then lies beneath a stem, and then it has joined the old floors on their way down.

At the rim the weave thickens into a braided lip, a selvage. Beyond it the outer face drops away in tiers of old floor, each darker than the one above, and water sheets from every layer into cloud. The bottom is not visible.

Animals are few. An hour's walk might turn up one glass-clear slug, long as a finger, on the pale course of a wall, drinking the film of water. And there is a small brown bird that roosts in the open mouths of the ribs. It has no call that carries over the roar. When it drops into a rib, that pipe goes silent, and the chord has a hole in it that can be heard across the whole plain.

From the top of the nine ledges the layout shows. The outlines square up with one another, and the strips of floor between them sit slightly lower and run slightly wetter, like lanes. It is the plan of a town, held at its first course. Somewhere to the east the bird comes out of its rib, and the missing note returns.
