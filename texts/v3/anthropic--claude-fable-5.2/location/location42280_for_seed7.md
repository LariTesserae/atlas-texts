The heat comes first, before the eyes have anything to work with. It rises through the floor and the soles of the feet, and the air lies against the skin like a damp cloth. It smells of wet iron, a little sulfur, and somewhere ahead the unmistakable sweetness of a greenhouse.

The chamber is a single length of lava tube, about seventy paces from end to end and tall enough to lose a thrown stone in. It is sealed at one end by a plug of its own frozen lava and at the other by a collapse so recent that the broken faces are still glassy black. Nothing here has been rounded. The floor is ropy basalt, pleated like cloth pushed across a table, and its ridges will open a palm. The ceiling hangs with thousands of finger-length lava drips that froze mid-fall and have been dulled by nothing since. Along both walls run level shelves marking where the molten river paused on its way down. The rock has not finished cooling, and it will be at that for several more lifetimes.

Someone drilled into it. A bored shaft comes down through the roof near the collapsed end, with a caged ladder and a hatch at the top that no longer admits anything. Below it stands the wellhead, a squat stack of flanges and handwheels in a red gone to brown. Two lagged pipes run from it down the length of the chamber on steel trestles. One is too hot to touch and the other is merely warm. A corrugated instrument hut the size of a wardrobe stands on the middle stretch of grating walkway, its gauge needles resting at angles they chose long ago. A chain hoist hangs from a bolt in the ceiling. Stencilled numbers run along the trestles, 1 through 11.

Clamped to the hot pipe is a generator no bigger than an oil drum. It has no moving parts to speak of, and its hum can be found only by laying a hand on the casing. It feeds a single cable, and the cable feeds the lamps. Eleven poles were set and three lamps still burn, caged amber bulbs, each lighting a rough circle of floor. Between them is the kind of dark that has never been otherwise.

Each lit circle holds a garden. The spores came down on boots, on crates, in the air of the shaft, and they found the three places where living was possible. Under lamp 4 the basalt has vanished beneath moss cushions deep enough to take a fist. Liverworts overlap up the trestle legs like scales, and maidenhair ferns stand waist-high on black wire stems, the fronds arranged in a dome that exactly matches the reach of the light. At the rim of the circle the growth turns pale and stretched and thin, and then it stops along a line that could be drawn with chalk. One step past that line the rock is bare, hot, and dry.

The water is as local as the light. The warm return pipe sweats wherever its lagging has split, and those few joints drip, slowly, perhaps once in a long breath. The drops fall into moss and make no sound. Under lamp 9 a hollow in the floor holds a basin of water the width of a dinner plate. It is skinned with green and warm as blood, and it is the largest body of water in the chamber. Elsewhere the moisture is only in the air. It beads on the cold steel of the ladder and nowhere else.

Nothing moves through any of this. There are no gnats over the basin, no pale crickets on the walls, no droppings, and no webs across the hut's doorframe. The ferns unroll and release their spores into still air, and the spores settle wherever the air lets them down, almost always on rock that will kill them. The quiet is nearly complete. Now and then metal ticks as a trestle shifts by a hair's breadth, and that one small sound is absorbed by the dark between the gardens before it reaches the far wall.

The changes here take years. White mineral crust creeps outward from the pipe joints at about a fingernail's thickness in a decade. Under the eight dead lamps lie the gardens that used to be, flat brown mats shrunk to felt, each still holding its perfect circle. A lamp fails, its garden blanches over a season and lies down, and the remaining gardens go on. No one is rationing the generator's output, and it will outlast the bulbs.

The exception is lamp 7. Its bulb is grey, and the glass has been clouded from inside for a long time. The garden beneath it is still green. It is not as tall as the others, and the ferns are of a darker, closer habit, but it is alive and holds its circle in total blackness. From its edge a thread of moss no wider than a bootlace has begun to follow the cable along the trestle, toward lamp 4 in one direction and toward the generator in the other. It has covered about three paces each way. At the pace things keep here, it will arrive.
