The lowland mills call it the Floor. It is a plateau of old hard rock that was planed flat, then lifted, and all of that happened so long ago that the lifting itself has worn smooth. There are no peaks. What were once tors are shoulder-high stubs, and the rivers finished cutting long ago and now seep in shallow braids. Crossing it on foot takes a season, and the far rim sits under a different sky.

The ground is sorted. Frost has spent millennia pushing the coarse stones outward and the fine soil inward, so the whole surface is tiled in rough polygons twenty paces across, with stone borders and low domed centers. A lichen crust covers the domes in grey, bone, and rust-black. Moss lies in the troughs, and a willow grows flat against the ground, a finger thick at two hundred years. In the thaw weeks each polygon holds a tea-brown pond a hand deep. There is no other standing water.

The plateau makes its own lid. Its cold surface chills the air above it into a deck of stratus that never clears, and the light comes down pewter-colored and without direction. Nothing casts a shadow. Noon and evening differ by a shade. Moisture arrives as rime and a fine drizzle that wets stone but never runs.

The animals are the thing you notice second, and then cannot stop noticing. You see one, heavy and shoulder-high, its long guard hair the color of the lichen, standing in a polygon with its head down. Then you see another four polygons off, and another beyond that. They stand at every distance, evenly, like a pattern printed on the ground and continued over the horizon. There are tens of millions of them, and no two are close enough to touch. The lichen could not bear a herd, so each animal grazes one dome to the crust and steps to the next.

They all step the same way. The whole population drifts sunwise around the plateau, about one polygon a day. A full circuit takes nineteen years, which is how long the lichen needs to come back. An animal lives two circuits, perhaps part of a third.

They have no voice. Their hooves are broad and fringed, and the crust takes them like felt. Alarm travels as a wave of lifted heads, crossing kilometers in silence faster than anything could run. The hunters are pale and long-bodied. They walk among the grazers at the grazers' pace and are ignored until the moment they are not. There are no thermals under the lid, so the carrion birds walk too, in pairs, unhurried, with their wings folded like coats.

Below that scale the numbers get worse. The crust gives slightly underfoot because it is hollow with vole runs, each lined with shed wool. Old snowdrifts are grey with springtails and seem, up close, to simmer. In thaw the ponds release columns of midges too cold-slow to whine. Moths sit on every stone border with their wings flat, the same color as the stone.

There is almost nothing to hear:

- **Breath:** the animals' breathing, close by.
- **Rime:** it ticks as it falls from guard hairs.
- **Stone:** perhaps once a day, somewhere, frost splits a rock, and the crack carries for an hour's walk in every direction.
- **Grazing:** if you hold your own breath you can hear lichen being torn by a million mouths, a faint dry grain in the air.

The stubs of the old tors are polished to a dull shine at flank height, on all sides, from being rubbed.

Nothing is built here. But about one animal in thirty carries a stamped alloy tag in its left ear, and the numbers run to eight digits. They are tagged at a single notch in the southwestern rim, where the circuit brushes the edge and the lowland crews can reach them without climbing onto the Floor. No one follows them in.

In the thaw weeks the animals molt. The wool comes off in mats, rolls into the troughs, and waits there. Every night the plateau's cold air, heavier than the air of the lowlands, drains off through the rim notches and takes the wool with it. Somewhere below, out of sight, the mills comb it from the wind.

Up here the evidence of the mills is small. At night the underside of the cloud along the southern rim glows a dull orange. Every snowbank, cut open, shows thin black lines of soot, one for each winter of the furnaces. And the voles hoard the tags of the dead, so a run broken open by frost will sometimes spill a dozen bright numbered ovals onto the moss.

Stand still for a full day and every animal in sight moves one polygon sunwise. The view the next morning is the same as before.
