At first, what lives here is only a just-left. You go to the hole and the stick is standing in it. You go to the post and the shavings have not finished curling. The mallet handle is dark, and if you close your hand around it, it is the warmth of a hand, but so is everything. For a long while that is all there is of the one who lives here: someone who was at each place a moment ago and is at none of them now.

The traces will give up a body if you are patient with them.

She is not tall. The panels are her reach, fingertip to fingertip, and the rampart is twice her height and no more, because she measured with the only rule she had. The hole is knee-deep by her knee. Everything in the basin is counted out in lengths of her, so crossing it means walking along her, laid end to end, several thousand times.

She comes down hard on her heels, as people who carry do. One shoulder sits lower than the other, with a pad of callus where the pole rides. She pats bricks with her fingers flat and her thumb held up out of the way, which is why there are four dents and never five. The right hand smooths and the left holds things still. It is the right small finger that is crooked, bent toward its neighbour at the last joint, and in every seam it leaves a slightly deeper line along the outer edge of the stroke.

Her hair is cropped close and badly, in steps, with flint. Nothing else in the basin grows a bristle. There are no pigs or goats, nothing with a coat. When the sky needed doing she had one source, and she used it. It has not grown back, and it won't.

The heat is hers. The air, the channel water, and the clay under the lime all stand at the warmth under her tongue. She probably did not choose this. It is what happens when one body has handled every surface of a place and nothing else has ever touched it. As a result she cannot feel where she leaves off. When she puts her hand in a channel there is no line at the wrist. The water beading on the back of her hand might have come out of her or settled onto her, and the same water beads on the adze. The jars sweat as she does. It is hard to tell the tenant from the house.

She is the one thing here that could accomplish something, and she is not moving. She is on top of the rampart, where the last basket-load went in, standing in the small dark puddle of her own shadow with the mallet by her foot. Her hands are empty. This is new to them, and they don't know how to hang. They are still half closed around a handle's thickness of air. Slip coats them grey to the wrists, neither drying nor running.

She climbed up to tamp the final lift and close the rim. Then she straightened and looked out, to see what she had shut on the other side. It was the mounds, the leaning panel, the stick in the hole. She turned around and it was the same. Whatever the wall was raised against is no longer there to be looked at. Something was, though. Nobody rams earth to twice their own height for nothing.

What she is doing up there is keeping count. It doesn't look like anything, and her lips don't move. But she knows the frogs by number, trough by trough, the catfish to each pan, the turtles to each ledge, and she cannot be brought to accept one fewer. She doesn't begrudge the ibis. It can have the strike and the swallow, the whole motion, the working throat. It cannot have the frog. The frog is on the trough lip because she has it down as being on the trough lip. The leaf lies where it lies for the same reason, and so the damp under the jars keeps its edge. To her, finished means nothing missing, and she has held the place at nothing missing with an attention that does not blink. That may be what the wall was against, something that subtracts.

The weavers tie her knot, or she ties theirs. It matters which, and she can no longer say.

The hole is the one unfinished thing here, and it is hers too. She lifted her own panel and went down through her own matting to her own bricks, four dents each, and under them found matting again with the ends cut green. She is looking for a brick that is worse than the others, a lopsided first one, too wet, made before she knew how. There is no such brick. Every one is as good as the last. Either she never had to learn, or she has taken up everything she made while learning and made it again. Failing a bad brick, she would settle for dirt, plain ground that no one patted.

There is one thing in the basin she did not make, and she carries it with her. The finger was not born crooked. It was broken once and knit that way, and knitting takes weeks. So there were weeks, with nights and mornings. There was a day when something came down on her hand, and perhaps someone who looked at it afterward. All of that is kept in the last joint of one finger and nowhere else. She cannot make a thing without pressing into it the mark of the one event that happened to her rather than by her. It is on every brick. It is the oldest thing here, and the only old thing.

It is quiet because she has not said anything. The frogs have the pulse ready and no call to put through it. The whole basin is warm and wet and closed, like a mouth with a word in it before the lips part. She knows roughly what would follow if she spoke. The frogs would get their voices. The leaf would begin to drift, which means there would be a downhill, which means the water would be going somewhere. The ibis would be one frog ahead. Shadows would lean out from under things. The hole would be deeper by evening, there would be an evening, and her hair would start to grow.

The gourd tendril is a finger's width from its peg. The width is her finger's, since she pegged the cords. That is the size of what she is withholding, and if she lives anywhere it is in that gap. She could close it by laying one finger there. The crooked one would do.

Meanwhile there is the drop. At long intervals one gathers at the foot of a jar, lets go, and falls into a channel that does not rise. She has never managed to count it. It leaves the jar and the jar is no emptier. It enters the channel and the channel is no fuller. In between, for the length of its fall, it belongs to no total. She stands on her wall with her hands open and listens for it. It is the only thing in the basin she waits for, and each time it comes she very nearly says something.
