At first there seems to be no one. The work is everywhere and the workers are not, and it takes a few days in the lanes to understand why. They are not hiding. When one of them hears a foot on wet stone a lane over, she stops and waits to learn whose it is, the way the hares wait. A person in grey felt standing against grey schist is a piece of wall, and it is possible to pass within an arm's length and know it only afterwards.

They are small, or the lanes have taught them to carry themselves small. They walk with one shoulder a little ahead of the other, and go on doing it in the few places wide enough not to need it. Their feet are bare and broad, the soles pale and deeply creased from a life spent in a finger's depth of water. The felt they wear is hare's wool, combed out at the moult, which is why the hares sit in doorways and do not run. Over it goes a cape of plaited sedge that sheds a shower. When a shower comes they do not hurry. They step into the nearest doorway for the twenty minutes and then go on.

They eat sparingly, because there is little to eat: rock lichen soaked in cistern water until the bitterness goes, sedge root, one egg from each nest in the hearting, now and then a hare, always an old one. Fuel is scarcer than food. A fire is willow twigs no thicker than straw, laid on dried moss, and it is the size of two cupped hands. A household sits round it with knees touching and is not quite warm, and does not expect to be.

The hands are what a stranger ends up watching. They are thick through the palm, the nails worn short, and they are never entirely idle. Someone sitting in a doorway will pick up a cobble and turn it over and over, feeling for its faces. Children are handed a stone before they can walk. By the time they are grown they no longer look at a stone to fit it. They look at the gap, and the hands do the turning, and on the third or fourth try the stone goes in with a low sound like a door closing in another room. They own no hammer. A slab the frost has split is two stones now, each owed a place, and a piece too small to show a face goes into the middle of the wall, where the birds nest.

They read walls the way other people read faces. Every waller has a hand, and the hand stays in the stone long after the waller is gone. One liked long slabs set on edge, one chinked more than was needed, one always laid quartzite in pairs. A woman walking a lane can say where her mother's work stops and her mother's mother's begins, and where that gives way to people nobody remembers, though their habits are as familiar as a neighbour's. When she resets a stone she sets it in her own hand. In this way a wall passes slowly from the dead to the living without ever being new.

Lichen is how they count. A child's first stone set alone goes back where it came from like any other, but turned, the weathered face inward and the clean underside out. For years the stone stays bare, and the child is called bare too. Then a grey-green speck appears, and after that nobody has to count. An old man is three fingers across. For the rings wider than a doorway they have no number, only a gesture, the hand opening and moving away from the body.

They speak low and seldom. In still air a murmur carries three lanes, and they seem to dislike adding to what is there. Each household knows the footfall of its own. After sunset they sit on the sleeping shelves, above the damp, and listen to the slabs click as the warmth goes out of the walls. Each click is a stone that has moved a hair's width. The old ones can say which wall and roughly which course. Nobody goes out to it. It has not fallen yet. They only know where they will be walking in a year, or in ten.

The rule has made them careful about finding. A fallen stone belongs to whoever finds it, before dark, so nobody takes an unfamiliar lane with the light going, and nobody wanders by accident into a slumped quarter, where a single glance takes in more fallen stone than a life could put back. They go round with their eyes on their own kept way. Once in a long while someone, usually young, walks in on purpose and picks up the first stone. That is the start of a household, if anyone comes. The way to find a person here is to follow the palest stone.

Their dead are laid in a cell at the edge of the kept lanes, and the doorway is filled, stone fitted to stone, until it is wall. After a generation of lichen no one can tell the difference. So some of what they mend is wall and some is door, and they mend it all the same way.

They have no story about the building. They think of the city much as they think of the showers, as a slow kind of weather that they are part of. A household does not say it lives anywhere. It says it keeps from a certain cistern to a certain leaning dome. At evening some of them climb the through-stones to a wall-top to feel the wind on their faces, which is strange to them, and to watch the bright lines come and go. From up there they cannot tell their own keeping from another's, or from no one's. This does not seem to trouble them.

One household keeps a few hundred lanes near the middle. It has an old woman, a man, two who are nearly grown, and a girl who is still bare. This afternoon, between the third shower and the fourth, the girl found a slab of schist lying in the water film, down from the fifth course. She squatted with it across her knees. She found the gap and turned the stone so that the grey face went inward. It did not fit that way as it had the other, and she turned it end for end and tried again, and again. One lane over a bare foot came down on wet stone and stopped. Nobody came round the corner. The slab went in on something like the ninth try, with the low sound, and she pressed clay into the joint beneath it with four fingers and went home ahead of the fog.

Tomorrow the old woman will pass that way, lay her palm flat on the stone, push once, and go on. No one will speak of it. In ten or twelve years a speck will show near one edge, and the girl, who will not be a girl, will stop on her way past and know she has begun to be an age. Tonight she is in the cell with the others, under the fog, in the dark. A slab clicks somewhere to the north. Her hands have found a cobble on the sleeping shelf and are turning it.
