**The Keeper**

The gauge post is worth a second look. The notches stop at ankle height, but the post isn't blank above them. From the last notch to about the height of a knee, the wood is furred and split in long downward strokes, the newest at the top and still pale. Lower down, the same strokes run over the keeper's numbers and into the floor with them. Around the foot of the post lie small curved flakes of white stone, each the size and thickness of a nail paring.

Everything else on the saddle came here. The cat was brought, and when the people went she was not taken. Presumably they tried. Anyone who has tried to box a cat that is standing on the only warm ground for a day's climb will know how it went.

She is a small grey tabby, not young, and at first sight ordinary. You hear the difference before you see it. Her claws are glazed like the goats' hooves, too thick now to draw all the way in, and she ticks when she walks. The fur of her belly and hocks, where it brushes the wet floor, is beaded at the tips with stone, and when she shakes herself she rattles faintly. Her pupils stay wide all day.

The post is where she takes the glaze off. She stands against it, reaches as high as she can, and pulls down, and the day's stone comes off her claws in parings. A cat's reach is about knee height and the floor rises under her a fingernail a day, so the scratches have climbed the post at exactly the rate the notches were meant to record. The keeper wouldn't have called it a reading. But it is taken daily, and it hasn't lapsed.

In most respects the saddle is what a cat would ask for. The floor is warm. It is dusk at noon. The prey can't sit still, and there are thousands of it. The floor buries whatever is left on it, which suits her, though for preference she uses the hut's gutter, where there is something to dig. The three ferns are doing well.

There is one condition, and for a cat it's the hard one: she can't lie down for long. The stone takes hold of fur quickly. So she sleeps the way the frogs call: a little, then up, a body-length on, and down again. Each place she has lain shows for a few days as an oval, slightly dished and a shade paler than the floor, with a grain inside it like the lining of a hat. Some mornings there are twenty in a wandering line.

For longer sleep she has had the things the people left, and those are going. The windowsill is level with the floor. The bench seat went under long ago, and she lies along the backrest now with her legs hanging down either side. It has a summer left in it, perhaps two. She still marks the survey stakes, every one, in order, though to rub her cheek on an orange cap she has to lay her chin on the ground.

One oval hasn't gone under. It lies just outside the dusk of the nearest mast, and she has come back to it so often that it has become a low grey pad, a hand high and about as wide as a serving dish. It is made the way the liverwort's pedestal is made. Each time she gets up, she leaves some coat stuck in the day's layer. The hairs stand in the wet, the stone climbs them, and the next layer has more to hold. The sheet water parts around it. Pressed with a thumb it gives a little, which nothing else on the saddle does. It is the only thing here she has built, and she didn't mean to build it.

The frogs keep a space around her about two cat-lengths wide. A frog that is moving off isn't calling, so the space is quiet as well as empty. With your eyes shut you can follow her across the saddle by ear, as a small silence going about its business.

Her day fits inside the saddle's:

- **Morning.** She is on the hut roof, in the only direct sun there is, drying. The stoat works the frost line below. She doesn't go onto the old rock and the stoat doesn't eat on the new, and they look at each other across a hand's width of steaming frost. The goats would lick the salt off her coat if she let them. She lets one.

- **Midday.** When the cloud comes up and the midges rise, she goes to the edge of a dusk and waits for martins. For half a wingbeat, a martin in the slow air is something a cat can reach. The difficulty is that her jump slows by the same amount. She goes up at her own speed and, a pace further in, comes down like something being lowered on a string, with all four feet arranged long before they're needed. She gets one martin in thirty this way. Sometimes there is no martin and she jumps anyway.

- **Late afternoon.** In the open the rain would soak her. A few paces from a mast, each drop can be seen coming, and she sits there and keeps more or less dry by moving her ears. Now and then she takes a drop out of the air with one paw and looks at the paw. She goes further into the dusk than the martins do, as far as a certain line, and not past it.

- **Dusk.** The stone sets. While the rattle crosses the floor every frog is silent, so one more silence gives nothing away, and her claws can't be heard over the ticking. She goes with it from mast to mast. This is when she eats. Then the frogs start again, one or two short.

- **Night.** The fog stands at the boundary and the frogs call. She walks the bench, the stakes, and the post, and pulls off the day's glaze. Then she goes to the pad, turns around on it twice, and lies down, and it begins to take hold of her a hair at a time.

She will get up before she has to. So far she always has.
