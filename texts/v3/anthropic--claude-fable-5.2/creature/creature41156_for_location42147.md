Counted by heads, the bats live here. Weighed, it is probably the cockroaches. By seniority it is the swiftlets, whose calendar everyone else obeys. But the bats and the birds are commuters, gone for half of every day, and the things in the floor live a season and are replaced. If the question is who stays, the answer gets small quickly.

When the last skip has gone up and the men after it, the diesel is shut down to save fuel and the string of bulbs goes dark. One stays lit. The turbine at the falls can carry a single lamp without trouble, and from the top of the incline the hamlet across the valley has become one window. Someone is home.

A new man knows her first as that light. Some days into his first week he knows her as a shape on top of one of the fallen blocks, under a square of tin. The block is the size of a house and she has made a house on it: a bamboo platform, a notched pole for a stair, and the tin, which keeps off the drip and the droppings. Then a lamp flashes at him twice from up there, and he looks around to see what he is doing wrong.

The paybook lists her as watchman, the form having no other word. Inside, nobody has a name. A man is a touch on the shoulder or a lamp pointed at his chest. But she has a sign, and she did not choose it: the hand held flat, palm down, pressed twice toward the floor. In the men's signing it means *wait*, or *not yet*. It is what she has most often said to them, and over the years the word and the woman became one gesture.

She was here before the track. Her mother's family held the walls, row by row, the way other families hold fields. She first came down the sinkhole at eight or nine, carrying torches for her uncles, when the only works were the poles. When the company came for the floor, the family kept the walls, and the rule about the fledging stayed with the walls, and she is how the rule is kept. She no longer climbs. The damp has got into her knuckles and her grip is gone. Her cousins' sons go up the bamboo now, and she stands at the foot with a lamp and shows them which rows.

She does not judge the fledging by the nests, which are high in the cloud. She reads it off the floor. While there are chicks in the rows, some fall, and the crabs and roaches mass along the foot of the wall in a band you could trace with a stick. When the band thins and the crabs go back to wandering, the young are flying. She waits three days more, then lifts her hand, and the poles go up. One foreman, in a year of high prices, sent climbers up early. The climbers looked across at her block and came down again.

She is small, and has gone the color of things that live here. She works barefoot, because she reads the floor through her feet. Her eyes are red-rimmed from the ammonia. She had the cave fever in her second year, as everyone does who breathes this air long enough, and lay six weeks with it, and has not had it since. New men still get it. She can tell from how a man climbs the incline in his third week whether he will be back.

She cannot hear. It went so gradually under the roar that there was no day she could name, and down here it has cost her nothing. What she has instead is the block she sleeps on, which stands on the floor, and the floor carries everything. The falls are a steady shudder, and she knows the river's height from it before she looks at the paint. The generator is a quick tick. When a slab comes off the roof in the night, it arrives as a single knock under her spine. In the morning she walks out and finds it, lying where it wasn't, and stands a bamboo beside it with a rag tied on, so the men will know the floor has changed.

She does not trust the white line. The floor has risen and the river has moved since it was painted, and it now means later than it used to. Her own mark is a notch cut in a boulder at the nearest braid, and it sends the men out half a day before the paint would. The whistle lanyard hangs where she can reach it. She feels the blast in the cord and in her breastbone, and she sees it work. Across the floor the lamps all turn at once and start toward the incline.

She eats rice that comes down in the empty skips. She leaves the pink fish to the eels, and takes the eels in a bamboo trap. She does not eat the nests; nobody who harvests them does.

Each year, the day after the harvest ends, she pushes a stake into the mud at the edge of the channel nearest her. The row is thirty-odd stakes long now and runs toward the east wall. The earliest stand in midstream or are gone. Her block lies in the river's way. She has worked out, in stakes, when the water reaches it, and the number is larger than the years she has left, which is all the margin she asks.

Her bulb is the last on the cable and has not been moved since it was hung. The green beside it is the oldest on the string. It has grown past a film into a cushion as deep as a hand, with a fern in it. When the cable was restrung some years ago, she stood under that lamp until the lineman understood. She does nothing for the moss. She only keeps the light where it is.

At dusk she walks to the foot of the daylight column and sits among the ferns. It is the one hour she spends looking at anything far away. The rope of bats crosses the disc of sky, and as the roof empties the pressure in her teeth eases, which is the nearest thing she has to silence. The birds come in underneath and take the walls. Then she goes home.

Twice a year she goes up, to settle the nest account and to see who has been born. She sleeps badly there. She lies with her palm on the floor of her niece's house, waiting for something to come through the ground, and nothing does. The men say the world above has less in it. She would say only that up there the ground tells her nothing.

The cave is young and has decided very little. The river has not picked its channel, the roof is not done falling, and the floor is still coming up. She is the oldest settled thing in it. She has outlasted five foremen, two winches, and most of a meter of floor. She saw the old skip go over, and it was her hand that told them to leave it.

Last thing at night she lays that hand on the block, palm down, and presses, to take the river's level through the stone. It is the same shape as her name. There is no sign for telling her so. Then she sleeps, with the one lamp burning, and around her the floor goes on rising by a thumb's breadth a year.
