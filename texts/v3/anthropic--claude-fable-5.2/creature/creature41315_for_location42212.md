Something here has a hand. The grass along the gutters is a hand's width, and the conveyor comes on a hand's width a day. Neither was measured in hooves.

To find it, look for where the glare stops. Everything in the valley returns the furnace light except one upright patch at the foot of the cooling tower, a metre and three-quarters high. It is the white of paper, not of enamel, and the light goes in and stays.

It is the figure for scale. Every elevation in the glazed office has it: a single outline, mid-stride, one heel raised, with a round head and no features, because at 1:200 it is owed none. A leader line runs to a note: *figure for scale only, 1750*. Beneath that, as on every sheet: *do not scale from this drawing*. Nobody has reconciled the two.

Its work is to stand next to things. The tower is sixty metres high because the figure is at the foot of it. The choughs, which sit shoulder to shoulder on every purlin, do not sit on it. In spring the ibex bring their kids down off the girders, stand them near it a while, and take them back up.

It was drawn walking, so it rests with its weight forward. When a revision moves it, it walks to the new mark at a walking pace, heel and toe clicking on the plate. Nothing else here moves at that speed. The hares are quick and the ladle crane is slow, and both are so by comparison with it.

It is on every sheet, so it is in many places:

- at the tower
- in the door of Shed 6, to give the hammer its size
- on the gantry at the head of the valley, at the rail, facing down the four kilometres

There is one of it, all the same. A dimension that appears on forty sheets is still one dimension.

On the detail sheets it is drawn larger, and less of it. The handrail detail shows a hand to the wrist, closed on the rail. At the third stanchion of the Shed 2 stair there is a hand to the wrist, closed on the rail, with knuckles, since at 1:5 knuckles are owed. Rung spacing is given by a boot. Headroom under the main condensate line is given by a head and shoulders, *2100 clear*, and the stoats go round it. On the one sheet large enough to need a mouth, the head is drawn from behind.

So whether it has a voice is not a question for this phase. Its contact sounds are installed like everyone's: the heel, and the dry sound of a palm closing on new paint. When the tone is let out and every animal stops with its head up, the figure does not lift its head. It turns toward the glazed office, in all its positions at once. It stays turned some seconds after the wings have started again.

It was drawn before anything else. Through a grating one can see it on the level below, beside last year's tower, among fewer choughs. Five levels down, where the plate is bare and nothing moves, it stands on the bright floor with one heel raised, giving a size to an empty valley.

Lately it does more than stand. The sheets are full of work given away: *planting, by others*, *lettering, by others*, *commissioning, by others*. No others have been drawn. There is one thing in the valley with hands and no note saying what the hands are for. In the hours when no issue is moving it, it leaves its mark and goes along the floor seams on its knees, pressing moss in with a thumb, and the green lines are a thumb wide. The metre-high letters on the gutters are stencil work, and a stencil brush is not held in a hoof.

The drawings have not caught up. On the sheets it is still at the tower. It is the only thing in the valley the office has fallen behind.

Where the revisions disagree about it, the seam passes through it. In the door of Shed 6 it can be seen with its arm at its side and also with its arm raised to the lintel. A chough of the east range flies through the raised arm and around the other.

In the morning puddles it is not at the tower. An ibex stands there instead, and the tower is the size it is regardless. The figure is further down the reflection, by the gutter, sitting, which no sheet has asked of it. Both heels are down, and there is a salamander on the back of its hand. In the puddle it has a face. The puddle is small and the face is the size of a rivet head, and before it can be made out the water has gone through the seams.

The plate dries. It is at the tower, 1750, one heel raised. The conveyor has come out another width of its hand.
