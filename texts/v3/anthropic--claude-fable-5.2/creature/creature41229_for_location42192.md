Most of what moves on the floor does not live there. The stilts, ibis and herons roost in the forest and walk out at first light. The butterflies come for the salt and go when they have it. The kites keep a tree somewhere to the east, and the swifts are only crossing the top of the place. Count what is still on the rock at midnight and the list is short: shrimp, fish, the skinks down in the joints, the bats in the figs, the frogs.

On the first day you would say the frogs. Nothing else is so plainly made of the place. An animal does not trade its voice for a heel and a white foot in a few seasons, so the quiet has to be far older than the habit. They are what the quiet made, and for a while that seems like the whole answer.

Then the frogs show you something else.

You know how the stone changes color ahead of you and changes back behind. Stand still under the last tree, early, and sooner or later you see it happen out on the open floor where no one is walking. A lane of pink and grey opens in the green-gold, somewhat narrower than the lane you make, and closes behind itself at the pace of someone with nowhere to be. It crosses a swell. It goes around a pan instead of through. It stops. Then the frogs drift back in from every side, as they do around your own feet if you wait, until all that is left bare is two long shapes side by side, wider at the front than a foot ought to be. Sometimes there is only one shape, and it stays one for a long while. The herons do the same. I don't know which had it from the other.

That is the one who lives here. You find it the way you find the line in the air, by what settles on it or gives way.

Once you know to look, there is evidence all day. It starts most mornings from the rectangle of dark stone and goes out along the sixty-pace groove, exactly along it, which is probably what the groove is. Gneiss does not take a path from a few lifetimes of walking. Where the groove ends, it idles from pan to pan. On still mornings a few butterflies will be sitting a hand's width above the wet rock at a pool's edge, wings shut, in two small clusters, drinking from nothing. So it has something like ankles, and there is salt on them. When it reaches the line, a ripple goes down the row of dragonflies, each lifting as something passes and dropping back into place behind. You would get the same ripple by trailing a hand along a rail.

In the dry hour the frogs pull back to the wet margins and the lane is lost. Look at the ring instead. Somewhere on its clean edge a notch appears, the width of a wrist, and the raised water does not spill through. The notch stays a long time. The herons waiting there for fish stand closer on that side than on any other.

Only the rain gives it an outline, and only roughly. For forty minutes the drops strike something that is not rock and run down it, and there is a shape beside the post: narrow, a little taller than you, with a long back. The back is bent over the skinks stacked on the dry top. It is nearly always bent. Nothing here rises higher than a knee, and most of what there is to look at is the size of a thumbnail, so it has spent a very long time looking down.

That was when the three built things stopped being strange to me. A table that stays dry, a raised basin of water, a line to perch on. They are what anyone puts out who wants small animals to come close and stay, and it used so little because that is all the job takes. The figs are spaced too evenly to have arrived by bird. I think it chose a floor with no slope left in it, where the land itself had finished, so that the small quick lives would be the only thing happening. Even the quiet belongs to this. Sound that ends at two arm's lengths is the hearing of someone crouched over a pool, who wants the thing in front of it and not four hundred paces of everything else.

It does not take sides. A heron's neck goes out and comes back within a pace of the bare prints, and the prints do not move. And it never leaves the rock. The lane comes to where the trees begin and turns, the way the shrimp turn at the edge of the raised water. Whether that is a wall or a preference I can't tell.

At dusk it goes back to the dark rectangle, which is its house, with no more wall to it than the ring has. The frogs know whose it is. When the disc of water holds the light and every flying thing on the floor goes to it, a few moths do not. They circle a spot above the dark stone, low down, about where a head would rest. So it keeps a little of the day too, less than the water does, too little for your eyes. It lies there with the whole floor ticking under it.

You can get close. Follow the lane and walk into it from behind, slowly, and at two arm's lengths you are suddenly in its room. There is breath, slower than yours. And there is a voice: low, unhurried, not built to carry, going on steadily in the tone you would use to a skink on a post, if you were someone who talked to skinks. It has probably been talking like this the whole time, to whatever was nearest, and some of the quiet may be simple manners. It does not stop when you step in. It pauses for about as long as it takes to look up, and then goes on in the same voice, and it takes you a moment to understand that the voice is now meant for you.

Then you step back, and it ends in the middle of a word, and you hear the nearest three stilts instead.
