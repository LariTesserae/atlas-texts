The first thing to know about the people is that they are never still. Someone standing quiet in the green reads, through every foot nearby, as a hole in the noise, and a hole in the noise is how the felted hunter arrives. So they tap: a heel on the platform, a knuckle on a stem, a flake of chert clicked in one hand while the other is talking. Each of them has a rhythm, learned before walking, and it is the same thing as a name. It is tapped on wood when alone and tugged down the cord when tied to someone.

Their feet are broad and bare. They scrape the soles thin with the edge of a wing-case, since a callus is a kind of deafness. They walk slowly and flat, the way a finger moves under a line of writing.

They oil their skin against the dust, and the chaff sticks to the oil, so by adulthood they are furred pale all over. In the shadowless light they look cut from felt. Only the eyes and palms are kept clean. The palms are for speaking, and they flash when a person signs.

They have voices and use them daily, though never for words. A person feeding a stand leans chest-first against a trunk, arms around it, and pushes one long open note into the wood until the breath gives out. From behind it looks like someone holding on to someone. None of them has ever heard it. They judge a shout by what comes back up through the feet, and by the pods a week later. In their hand-speech, eating and shouting are one sign.

A household's standing is how loud it can be and for how long. The old, short of breath, drum with split-stem clappers. Only in the sleeping-basket does the tapping stop.

All of that is true of perhaps sixty people. It is not true of the one who lives in the black.

She is old, and she is still. Her soles have grown thick again, because the glazed floor gives a foot nothing to read. Her left forearm is smooth and hairless to the elbow, with the same shine as the standing stems. Nobody asks about it with their hands.

Her house has not moved in years. It is the only house in the hollow lashed with fixed knots, pulled hard as knuckles, which children elsewhere have never seen hold. It is also the only house with things in it: a row of cherts, a stack of wing-cases, and a child's wrist-cord, short, hung on a peg.

She keeps the count. Everywhere else a notch stretches, and a tally becomes a smear as long as an arm. In the black, a notch cut at a child's crown stays where it was cut, and the child is the one that moves. Each household has a stem there. They come to the edge with what they want kept, such as a birth, a death, or a house-move, and they pluck the cord.

The cord runs from the last living stalk at the edge, across the glaze, to her wrist. It is how she is called, and how she feels the hollow at all, as a faint grain in the fibre like a pulse taken through cloth. When the green advances, the cord goes slack. She walks out, reties the far end, cuts off the spare, and brings it home. The offcuts hang in a bundle by her door, hand-lengths and half-hand-lengths. She has never notched this count, but she keeps it.

Most mornings she walks the cord out anyway. Where the floor begins to hum she stops her ears, kneels among the beetles at the margin, and drags a wing-case along the black for an hour, as anyone would. Visitors have asked why she helps it come. She answers with the one sign, eat-shout, and goes on scraping.

She has measured the distance between the edge and her house against the bundle by the door. It is more than she has years for, though by less than it used to be.

At night, when the roof goes blue, she takes the wax out of her ears. No one else in the hollow does this, or could. She picks up a chert and strikes the glazed stem beside her door once.

It rings with one note, alone and clean, and then it thins and is gone. She holds her breath until it is. She is the only person here who knows that a sound has an end.

Then she lies down with the cord on her wrist. Through it, small and steady, comes the drumming.
