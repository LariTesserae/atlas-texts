No one lives here in the sense the question hopes for. The people come in pairs, wearing ear defenders, and stay for the length of a logbook entry. One carries a blue crayon. One, once, had a can of yellow paint. They are how the residents arrived, on boot treads or down the shaft behind them, but they are visitors.

What lives here is six patches of green, and in one of them, a moss.

From the doorway each patch is a stain, the kind a leaking gutter leaves on a house. Closer, it has structure. The formwork boards left the wall ribbed with fine ridges, and the water travels in the grooves, so the green is laid down in the grain of timber that was stripped and carted away before the first cell landed. It is brightest a hand's width under the lamp glass and gone within an arm's length. The concrete beyond that is just as wet and bright enough to read a gauge by, which is not enough to eat by. Each fan is a map of two things overlapping, lamp and weep, and it stops where either stops.

At the weep itself there is nothing. The water has come through the dam under the weight of the reservoir, and it arrives caustic, loaded with lime, so the white thread it lays down is bare. The green begins a little below, where the water has been in air long enough to turn mild. Every fan has that pale, uninhabited stem running into it from above.

Nobody has put the film under a lens. It will be single cells, a few kinds of green algae and probably cyanobacteria among them, sitting in a slime of their own making that holds them to a wall that never stops shaking. They have no way of knowing about the noise. To them it is the cross-hatch in the puddles, in a film of water thinner than paper, tightening a little in the early evening and slackening late at night. That is the only clock they are offered. The lamps have never gone off, so there has never been a dusk to divide by, and each cell splits when it is ready and not before. The cloud the fan draws down, the popped ears and the smell of snowmelt all pass over them as slightly more or slightly less light.

Nothing eats them. No snail has left a track and no mite works the edge. The dead stay where they are, under the living, and the fan of green thickens by a layer nobody could measure.

The six colonies can be seen from one another and cannot be reached. Each grew from whatever happened to land there, so they are not quite the same. The one nearest the door, where the boots come in, is a yellower green. The one by the extract fan has a blue cast and a slick surface, and is spreading fastest. They are six separate accidents, each a few metres from the next.

The moss is under the second lamp. It sits inside a blue crayon circle, beside a date in a fitter's handwriting, and it is younger than the date. It is the size of a coin and the only thing in the room that grew a shape of its own, with a base, a crown, and shoots packed like the pile of a carpet. Every shoot leans the same way, up and outward, toward the glass. Outdoors a moss answers to a whole sky. This one has a single small sun at a fixed bearing, and it has grown like a compass needle.

The weep feeds it directly. The cushion takes the water in and holds it between the shoots, where the shaking cannot get at it. The drops under the penstock are thrown off before they are full, and the puddles never settle, but inside the moss the water is still. It may be the only still water in the room.

The base of the cushion is already paler and stiffer than the top. The water gives up its lime on the way through, as it does to make the straws under the crane beam, and at about the same pace. If it is left alone the moss will go on turning to stone from below while it greens from above, climbing its own crust toward the lamp. It has put up no capsules. It may never, and it does not need to.

The reservoir is still rising. The circles will get company, and so will the green. The maintenance schedule has no line for any of this yet.

Every eleven minutes or so the second green lamp comes on, and the water in the gutter begins to move. It takes with it a few cells from the lowest edge of each fan, through the grating into the sump, where it is dark, and then out along the discharge line. Wherever that lets out, there is daylight, which none of them has ever had.
