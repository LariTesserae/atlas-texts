The first answer is no one. The sand says so, and so do the bare ledges and the clean cracks.

The second answer is the green things: the ferns, each on its own crack, the algae below them, the rushes, the moss. They do live here, but they are more like the furnishings of a household than the household.

The third answer takes longer, and it starts with what is wrong about the first. This is open water in dry country. A basin like this should be a crossroads, with fox and hare prints stitched to its edge, wasps on the moss, swallow mud under the lip, and a spider in every crack. Instead there is one white ring around the rim, which means that in all the time it takes a mineral line to form, nothing has lowered the water by a mouthful. That is not vacancy. The alcove is occupied the way the basin is full, exactly to the line, with no room left over.

So something lives here, and most of what can be said about it comes from what it leaves alone.

It is warm. The alcove faces away from the sun, and the back wall has never had direct light on it, yet the stone gives back a mild heat like a wall that was sunned earlier. That heat belongs to the resident. It is not the warmth of a den or of anything that has been running. It is the warmth of a place where someone has sat for a long while without getting up.

It has edges, and they are precise. It ends at the varnished lip, the way the ferns end where the damp ends. It does not go out into the wash, and the two gray shrubs have no dealings with it. Inside, it is everywhere except within a pace of the seep, where the air stays cool. It keeps that distance from the water on purpose. It lives beside the seam and does not lean on it.

It eats, in a manner of speaking, but only what has already left. It takes what lifts off the basin into the air, which is the same amount the seam brings in. That is its whole appetite, and it has never once been hungrier than that.

It moves very little. A few times a year it shifts, the way a sleeper turns, and the faint ripple on the sand is the mark of the last time. The low tone at the lip is not its voice. That is only the wash breathing across the opening, though it listens. What it does with sound is carry it. The drip is a small note, and the resident holds it up and hands it along the curve of the roof to the mouth. That note is the only thing it sends outside. An animal coming up the wash hears it, understands that the room is taken, and goes on. It does not leave out of fear. It leaves the way one doesn't walk into a room where someone is resting.

It has had company once. The people who came cut nothing and shaped nothing. They chose their wall stones for fit, and kept their fire small and at the far end, near the open air. They set their grinding slab near the basin, inside the cool pace, the one part of the alcove the resident does not fill. They worked in the room it left them. Either they asked or they noticed, and it amounts to the same thing. It let them stay for many seasons, long enough for a stone to wear a hollow as smooth as skin.

It did not grieve when they went, because it doesn't work that way. It keeps things. It has sealed their soot under glaze so it can't be rubbed off, and it is slowly bringing the top of their wall into the same dark varnish as its own lip. Eventually the wall will be something the alcove has always had.

The four cobbles are different. They are the only things in the alcove from somewhere with more water than the air can take. The resident has no experience of that. It has never seen a surplus. But it attends to those stones more closely than to anything else on the floor, and the sand has not been allowed to cover them. And at the basin's edge, where the overflow would go if there were ever any, it keeps a mat of moss in good order. This is the one thing in its character beyond exactness. It is perfectly content with the amount it has, and it keeps a place ready, without impatience, for an amount it has never had.

If you walked in, your feet would make almost no sound, and it would draw back into the far corners, where the shade has no hard edge, to give you the floor. You would feel it only as the warmth, and perhaps as the sense that the quiet was being held, not merely happening. If you drank from the basin it would not stop you. It would go a little short until the seam made up the difference, and it would not hold that against you. Then it would smooth the sand.

Mostly it does one thing. The drop falls, the rings spread to the white line, and for the second or so before the next drop the surface is flat and nothing in the alcove is owed anything. It lives in that second, every four seconds, and has for longer than the varnish has been growing.
