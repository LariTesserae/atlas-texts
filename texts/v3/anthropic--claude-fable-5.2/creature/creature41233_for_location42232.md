Start with what is here all the time.

The skin on the walls is alive, and it isn't spread evenly. A hand moving in from the crack finds it on the knob where the passage turns, on the two floor cords that take the knees, and on a stretch of wall beside the cleared patch at the height of a leaning shoulder. Between those places the rock sweats clean. Stone this new offers almost nothing to eat, a little iron and a breath of sulphur, and where the film has only that it stays as thin as the mineral bloom. Where it is thick it has been fed, on salt and the oil of a palm. The room has not begun to make soil of itself, but soil is being started on its behalf, one handhold at a time.

The thing on thread legs grazes those patches and walks between them. That is why it crosses the back of a hand: the hand is resting where hands rest. It takes the hand for a rise in the pasture, tastes it, and goes on along a route that runs from the entrance to the seat by the same holds in the same order.

The roots hang at the level of a face going in sideways. They draw across the leading cheek, and shortly after, the cold stops. They are the first thing in the room to touch whoever enters and the last to touch whoever leaves.

So the few small lives in the blister turn out to be an outline. They are arranged around someone.

It is someone narrow, or able to become narrow, and short in the back. Over the seat the drips are snapped to clear one head and not a finger more, by a person who did not want to take more roof than they would use. The arm isn't long. From the seat it reaches the wall of marks, and from the newest mark it reaches down to where the cobble lies. The fingertip is the size of the pits. Nine pits make a row because nine fit beneath one spread hand, thumb to smallest finger, so a row can be read without the hand moving and losing its place.

These are hands that can't hold two things without comparing them. The drips were all a finger long when they hung. Broken off, they differ by the thickness of a thread, and they have been laid in order anyway. They are hands that clean up, too. A basin and a hundred and four pits were pecked out of this rock, and there is no grit anywhere. Every grain the cobble raised has left the room.

That is partly the cup's work. In the valley the cup was a lamp, and it came up the mountain as one. Up here it has never held anything but water. The groove where a wick would lie fits a thumb, and water tipped across it leaves as a single cord instead of a sheet, so it can be aimed by feel. It rinses a fresh pit until the edge reads clean, then goes out through the crack carrying what was rinsed.

Whether the eyes of the one who comes are any use down below, the room can't know and has no cause to ask. Nothing here was made by someone struggling with the dark. No mark is oversized, none is struck twice, and nothing was fumbled and put right.

The room knows its visitor mainly by heat, since heat is the thing it is exact about. Rock, air and water have held one warmth since the roof set. Then a coolness comes in sideways through the crack, skin with the wind still on it. For a few releases of the ceiling it is the only cool thing the blister has ever contained, and the drops that land along its back are warm and distinct, each one felt. Then the body comes level. The drops go on landing but can be told only as touch, and the edges of the arms and legs loosen into the air.

This is the stretch the work is done in. Find the cobble. Find the last pit above it. Move one fingertip along, or if the row is full, drop down and begin again under the thumb. Strike.

It is slow. The air is thin, the stone is glass-hard, and the blows have to be small. A working body makes heat, and this air will accept none of it. Sweat runs and cools nothing. Everything else in the blister gives back exactly what it gets; the visitor is the one vessel here in which something collects. So it collects, and at some point the ceiling lets go and the pat across the shoulders is, faintly, cool.

That is the signal, and one pit is about what fits before it. The fingertip checks the edge. The cup rinses. The cobble goes down under the new pit, an arm from the seat. There is a drink from the basin. Then come the knee cords, the wall hold, the knob, and the roots across the other cheek, and the room's knowledge of the person ends a body-length short of the cold.

Nobody lives here, then, in the sense of staying. To stay past the cool drops would be to fall asleep inside a warmth that feels like one's own and isn't. The one who comes lives here as the drop lives on the tip of the stone: gathered slowly from elsewhere, hanging a while, gone.

The blister has two rhythms. The short one is forty heartbeats long and exists only when there is a heart in the room to measure it. The long one is the space between pits. No one has counted that. It is however long it takes, down among the river stones, for a person to fill.

With what, the room can't say. It can say what is left behind. Each pit is a clean cell the size of a fingertip, and by the next visit it holds a bead of warm water, like every other cell in the froth. The lava blew its thousands in one hour and in no order. This one adds them singly, in rows, with a stone the river made. It is the same work, continued more slowly by something that can count.

Whether it has always been the same someone is another thing touch won't settle. The seat was cleared once, to one height. Either one back has fitted it all along, or several have learned to.

And the room cannot tell a pause from an end. The fifth pit of the twelfth row is as crisp as the first pit of the first, and a reloading can't be heard. All that can be found is this: the film is thickest of all on the smooth end of the cobble, where the grip goes. The thread-legged thing visits it there. The cobble lies where it has been set down a hundred and four times and picked up a hundred and three.

Overhead the ceiling fills.
