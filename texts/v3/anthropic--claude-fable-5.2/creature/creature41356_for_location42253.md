At first the answer seems to be the islands, about a hundred of them. Each is easy to take for a separate creature. The rosettes hold themselves stiff, the ferns slouch, the small trees stand like people who have been told to wait. Each keeps its own stack of shed years underneath it. A visitor walking the gaps would feel they were among a hundred hermits who had agreed long ago not to speak.

But a hundred wills would not produce gaps that equal. Hermits would crowd the seeps and thin out toward the rim. Here the spacing is the same everywhere, which is the evenness of one thing arranging itself, like the fingers of a spread hand.

The body is in the gaps. The stone that looks bare has a nap, and a bare sole would find it, something like wet felt. It is made of colorless threads finer than hair, lying in the film and combed outward from the seeps toward the rim. The water is a hair deep because that is the depth of what holds it. Seeds do not take in the open for the same reason a seed does not take on the back of a hand. And the water climbs the walls because it is being drawn. What lives here drinks at the floor and breathes out at the lip, one long breath a day, and the ring of mist at noon is the end of that breath.

So there is one resident. It is sixty paces across and a hair thick, warm as blood, lying face-up in a bowl it fits exactly. Whether it settled into the bowl or the bowl wore to fit it no longer matters. The islands are the few places where it stands up into the air.

It has never been eaten, so it has no thorn, no bitter sap, no flinch. It has never lost anything except as mist. It keeps every leaf it has dropped, in order, down to the pale net on the rock that was the first. Its flowers must be a habit older than the hollow, inherited from something that lived among wings. It opens them and sets seed that falls on its own skin and cannot grow. It was not waiting for anything. It had no idea there was anything to wait for.

Its only sense is the film. A leaf landing is felt across the whole of it at once, and for most of its life that was the only touch it knew: one an afternoon, always from a piece of itself coming to rest.

Then there were feet. They were the first thing ever to put weight down on it and take the weight up again. After the feet came the grinding, through the slab and into the rock for hours, a thousand times louder than a leaf. Its useless seed was carried to the east side, broken in the cups, and taken out over the rim, where only its breath had ever gone.

They came for longer than one life, since seven cups are not worn smooth in less. They built no fire on a floor already warm, and no wall, because there was nothing to keep out. Once, one of them cut the largest tree. It had never been cut and did not know to mind. It closed bark over the notch without filling it, and it keeps the notch as it keeps the leaves. It has laid mineral over the flake on the ledge, thin as a fingernail, and the edge beneath is still keen.

They seem to have known what it was. Under the overhang, the one place its water does not climb, they left five strokes a finger wide: a hand, set flat on the only stone that is not its skin. That reads as a courtesy, an open hand answering a cupped one. It is the one thing they left that it cannot reach.

Then they stopped. The pestle lies in the fourth cup like a thing set down in the middle of a task. It cannot know why. It only knows the touch ended, and it is a thing that does not clear away.

So lately, meaning in the last few centuries, it has done something new. In the seventh cup it has stood up a rosette closer to itself than it allows anywhere else, breaking its one rule in the one place made by hands. The rosette is a small green thing on wet stone in a hollow shaped like a palm, a copy of the whole the size of a fist, placed where the meal used to go. It may be an offering or an imitation, and the resident probably does not distinguish the two. Its flowers smell of warm bread, and perhaps they always did.

It has also sent moss up the pestle from the seep side, in a collar where a grip would close. At a finger's breadth a decade, the collar will meet around the stone in two or three hundred years. It will not be able to lift the pestle, and it does not know what lifting is for. It will hold it anyway.
