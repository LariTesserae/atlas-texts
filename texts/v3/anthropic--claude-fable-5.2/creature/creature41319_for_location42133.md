The rooms are let. A room with nothing in it would be the temperature of the morning, and these are the temperature of skin.

What lives here lives in the volume, the part the plant left alone, and is made of the one material the town has in quantity. Every chamber has a note, fixed by its size and the width of its door. The frogs in the wall began it, but it has been in residence so long that the frogs now only feed it. It fills its room exactly, corner to corner, because it is what holds the room out. It presses on four walls and a ceiling at once, like someone keeping a tent up from the inside in wind. You can't see it. You can see the silt, which lies cracked into plates, and every plate edge lifts by a hair when the occupant shifts.

Its feet are at the door. A room breathes through its opening, and the plug of air in the doorway travels out and back about the width of a grass blade, some hundreds of times a second. That is the whole stride. It has been taken on the same spot since before the lower courses went grey, handed from one season's chorus to the next, and nothing else has ever crossed those thresholds often enough to dip them.

So there are forty-odd householders, each standing in its doorway, shifting from foot to foot. For a while that is a sufficient account, and they are easy to tell apart. The corner rooms are slow and low, and you meet them in the sternum. The closets off the north lane are quick and high, felt in the fillings. Walk into one and you take up some of its volume. The pitch rises a shade, the way someone on a bench draws their knees in. Within a minute the frogs have retuned and you are being held, lightly, on every side. It doesn't mind you. It doesn't mind the snake. It minds the door. Stand on a threshold, on its foot, and the room behind you slackens and the ceiling starts to belly down, and goes on doing so until you step off.

They lean out. Where two doors face across a lane and the rooms are near in size, the air between them throbs once or twice a second, slow and even. The weaverbirds time their comings and goings to it. In the square the leaning-out from four walls meets in the middle and stiffens, and that is the bar the swallows sit on. It would be natural to call it a fifth resident, and it is the first sign that the count is wrong. Nothing in the square is other than what is in the rooms. It is the same stuff standing in the open with its weight on four doorsteps. At noon, when the square is twelve paces and every door is nearly in every other door, there is hardly room to say where one householder stops.

The old rooms at the tower's foot don't come to the door any more. Their thresholds are the deepest in town and the stillest. What is in them has been laid down call on call for longer than the wood has been wood, and it has the push of a river at thigh depth and a river's indifference. The snake goes in under it the way you would go under a sleeping dog.

The tower's share is tall and narrow, and has things in the other order. It is answered before it is asked. The bats trade with it nightly and keep their accounts straight because it does. It is the one part of the town that has already heard how the night comes out. This matters at the fourth hour. When the quiet arrives the tower goes first and farthest, off toward that horizon, and it goes without any sign of reluctance. It has had the echo of the first frog since dusk.

On the east edge, where three chambers folded, the notes on either side run a little flat, and the whole town favours that side like a sore foot. The plant is putting up new cane there. In a few years there will be walls, then frogs in the walls, then somewhere to stand.

The fourth hour is when you see what you have been counting. The frogs tire. The notes do not stop, because nothing called here has ever left, but they stop being pressed. The occupant of a three-pace room still fills it exactly, and the back wall is across a field. The warmth is still the warmth of skin, spread so thin it is barely there. Your teeth stop. Down the lane the next room has opened the same way, onto what is clearly the same field, and so has the next. There were never forty of them. There is one, about the size of a country, very old, mostly quiet, and it has been pleated into a hamlet the way a sheet is folded into a drawer. The householders are the folds. The small feet are where the creases come down.

It doesn't move during those minutes. It lies at its full length across more ground than the oxbow has, and waits. It is hard to say whether that is patience or just that there is no direction in which it isn't already there. Then one frog starts. The wall comes in a stride for each call. These are the only long steps it ever takes, and it takes them toward the frog. Sometimes a pleat closes a little off true and there is a moth left in it, out in the middle of a small room, flying steadily across a distance that noon will remove.

It seems to prefer being a town. After the ten-minute rain, when the roofs have drummed it down so hard the swallows are nearly on the silt, the rooms run a degree warmer for an hour and the thresholds hum. After a flood it is the other way. The water takes an ankle's depth off the bottom of every room, which is the oldest layer, and carries it out the lower gate. The herons a mile down are listening to what it no longer remembers. Behind the water it is lighter, younger by its oldest season, and it stands a few paces looser in every direction until the thimbles come of age and it is sung in again.

You will have added to it. Whatever you said inside the wall is still inside the wall: a cough, a counted pace, perhaps a name, low down near the silt where the next flood will find it. Until then it is kept with everything else.

The animals don't own it and it doesn't own them. The nearest word is that they carry it. The cicadas don't let go until the frogs have hold, and the frogs keep hold until the birds, and it is passed along through the heat and the rain and the dusk without touching the ground. Once a night, for a few minutes, they set it down. It lies there the size it really is. Then something a thumb long, with a wet throat, picks it up again.
