Mostly it is the plant that lives here, and the plant lives only in its top hand's-breadth. The leaf-scales are alive, and the pale course on each wall, and the stems of the present floor. Under that it is its own timber, and under that its own compost. It has kept to this one height for as long as there has been a height, and all of it has left, many times over.

Everything else here lives the way the plant does, or doesn't last.

The slug is the easiest to find, though it is not easy. It is clear enough that the leaf-scales show through it, slightly enlarged. What gives it away is that a bead of water that size shouldn't be going anywhere. It keeps to the pale course because new stem weeps a little where it is laid, and the film there is faintly sweet. But the pale course is never the same course for long. The wall is far slower than the slug, but the wall doesn't rest. A slug that rests wakes on green stem and has to climb. One that rests too long is found a course lower, gone milky, and after that is not found. So it climbs for its whole life and stays where it is. The rectangle has one. It travels the top of the wall from one side of the door gap round to the other and back, because it won't leave the pale to cross. It is the only resident the house has had, and it has never been inside.

Something made the galls. Each has a bore in its side the width of a quill, and behind the bore a chamber, and in the chamber nothing. Whatever asked for that room left it a long way down. The plant has gone on laying the gall at the surface ever since, hole and all. It keeps a room the same way whether the tenant has gone or has not come yet.

Whatever does the rotting lives too deep to meet. Its warmth comes up, and so do its flies, small and pale, rising through the weave with the heat. They fly weakly and the wind takes most of them over the rim. They come up thickest where the floor is lowest and wettest, which is in the lanes. So the lanes are where the bird walks.

It is the size of a fist and the brown of the third floor down. Its feathers lie shingled like the leaf-scales and shed water the same way. Perched on the lip of a rib beside the bud, it has the bud's size and the bud's outline, and a count of buds along a row comes out one too many until one of them looks round.

It does have a voice. It will let you come within arm's length, since nothing here has ever given it a reason not to, and from there you can see the throat work and hear a tick like a wet twig snapping. At ten paces the roar has it. What the bird has in place of a call is the rib. It drops in feet first with its wings shut and sits in the mouth like a cork, and its note goes out of the chord. It lifts, and the note comes back. In, out, in: a long gap, a short one, a long one. Far off in a lane another bird stops walking and raises its head. That is the song. It is sung by taking something away, and it carries further than anything a throat that size could make, because what was taken away was the loudest thing on the plain.

Each bird has its rib and keeps it. It nests a forearm down the bore, where the wall thickens into a shoulder, on a pad of leaf-scales and its own down. The rib is rising through itself like everything else, so by the next season the pad has been carried lower, and the bird builds on top of it. Under any sitting bird is a column of nests, each flatter and darker than the one above, going down the bore past the line of the floor and on into the old floors. The bird sits on all the birds before it, at the same depth below the lip that they sat.

The column matters to the sound. A rib speaks from its mouth down to whatever stops it, and what stops it is the top of the stack. The lived-in ribs are short pipes and sound high. The ribs no bird has taken are open a long way down, and sound low. Of the five or six notes over the plain, the bottom two or three are vacancy.

Toward dark the birds come in from the lanes one at a time, and the chord thins from the top. Each note that goes out is a bird accounted for. A high note still sounding after dark is the only bad news the plain can carry, and it sounds every night until some other bird takes that rib. Not every rib is taken. There are fewer birds than ribs, and there always have been. Full silence, every pipe corked and only the weir going underfoot, would be the chord resolving. It has not happened.

The bird's feathers go into the floor singly, a day on the weave and then a scale's depth under. Eventually the rest of it follows. The door gap has come up through thousands of courses without changing width, and so has the hole in the chord from the rib to the east. The birds that made that hole have gone down one after another, and it is still the same pitch, heard from the same stair.

At night what is left is the roar, and over it the low pipes, the ones nothing has lived in, sounding until morning across the plan of the town.
