The crust is the simplest thing here to call alive and the hardest to be sure of. It does not live on the stone. Scraped from a joint and pressed into another wall at that wall's noon, it is powder within a few breaths. Pressed into a joint where the shadow lies long, it has taken hold by the time the shadow has moved a finger's width. What it needs is the slant. It lives on low light the way moss lives on damp, and the wall is only where the slant happens to land. When noon reaches it, it lets go in flakes finer than the dust. The tide carries them down the streets to the houses entering their afternoon, where a stain appears in the joints, orange on the edge that faces the hour to come. So there are not many patches of crust. There is one, very thin, going round the day in two bands, one ahead of noon and one behind it, and the dust tide is how it gets from house to house.

The grazer is never seen close in its own hour, but it can be seen close through a window. A window keeps a different hour from the room it opens on, and sometimes that hour has a grazer in it, on the facing wall, three paces off. Seen that way, it has no fur. Its hide is small grey plates, no two the same shape, lying edge to edge with nothing between them. When it bends, the joints open dark and then close exactly. The legs fold twice. The feet are two hard toes that spread on the coping. The mouth is underneath, a disc it sets against the stone and turns.

It does not look about. Its head stays low and a little to one side, toward its own shadow, and it steers by that. It feeds while the shadow is longer than it is. When the shadow draws in to its own length, it sets off along the wall-top or out along a dry channel, and keeps going until the shadow swings round and lengthens again. Noon is the one thing it avoids. That is why it is always far down a street. It is not shy. It is some hours away, and walking toward it changes which hours.

It is always alone because there may be only one. The grazer in the window has a plate missing behind the left shoulder, a dark notch the shape of a thumbnail. So does the one at the far end of the street outside, and so does the one two streets over, where it is late afternoon. Counted from the western rim there are nine grazers in the city. Counted from the east there are fourteen. One animal going round its day would look like this, in a place where the day is laid out to be walked through. So would a great many animals all missing the same plate.

The pellets are the crust made back into stone. Each has seven or eight flat faces, none regular. Set one on a wall-top and look away, and at the click it is in a joint that was not there to be filled, and the course is that much longer. The wall leaves its windward face as grains, crosses its own width as dust and stain, goes through the grazer, and is laid again on the far side. The house moves east through the animal. It is not the only way a house moves, since whole slabs are seated too and no grazer passed those. It is the way that can be watched.

Then there are the doorways. The city has thousands, and its interiors are dim at every hour, noon included. Nothing has ever fallen on this saddle, and night has not either. It does not come down on a house. It is kept inside. Through the day it stands back from the sills, further in than the light should allow. Toward evening it comes up to the doorways and fills them level, like the dust in the cisterns. At a house where the sun is down, it is out in the street. It reaches as far as the kerb and stops there in a straight line, because past the kerb the paving belongs to other hours. In that house's morning it goes back in. This is what the doorways are for, and why a town with no people has them.

So the fourth doorway is not hard to account for. It appears on a house late in its afternoon, low, on the side where the shadow is longest and the crust thickest. That is the one time in its day the grazer comes down off the wall-top to feed along the foot of the wall. The night in that house has come to the wall early, found no opening there, and made one. The jambs are properly laid and the lintel is a single slab. The grazer's shadow, running long ahead of it along the wall, reaches the opening first and goes in. The animal has steered by that shadow all its day, and it follows.

Whether this is hunting is not clear. A door and a mouth are the same kind of opening here. But at a house in its first light, a grazer is sometimes standing on the coping where there was none, and for its first few steps it has no shadow at all. Then one arrives from the correct side, long, and the animal lowers its head toward it. It has the notch behind the shoulder. It is perhaps thinner. The house beneath it has the right number of doorways.

That leaves the houses. Each one keeps a night inside it, lets it out, and takes it back. Each holds an hour and does not give it up to a neighbour. Each keeps its shape, sill and lintel and stair, while every stone in it is replaced from one side to the other. Each moves, slowly, away from where it is being worn. A building does none of this.

The spacing is not a town's spacing either. It is how animals stand on thin pasture. There is one day's light to go round, each house takes its portion at an hour no other house is using, and four hundred paces is how far apart the portions lie. It looks like streets only because they keep their intervals so exactly. It is a herd crossing from west to east, with its nights inside it and one stain and one animal going round among its members. The saddle is only what is under it at present. The oldest pale rectangles begin in the air. By that evidence the houses came in level, and by the scree below the eastern scarp they do not go out level.

Toward the middle the herd behaves differently. House after house settles toward the same hour. Houses on the same hour draw on the same light, so they stand further apart, eight hundred paces, then a thousand. They are gathering, in the only way they can, around the square with the low wall.

That wall's lack of shadow is not noon. The sun never stands quite overhead, and noon leaves a hand's width of shade under every other wall in the city. This wall has no shadow because it has no hour. It takes nothing from the sun at any position. There is no crust on it, and no grazer has been seen going toward it, since there would be nothing to steer by. The dust in its square does not tide. There are no pale rectangles behind it, so it has never moved.

Counted from a rooftop, it has eleven courses from the north and eleven from the south, the only wall in the city that gives the same number twice. The click comes from it more often than from anywhere else, and it carries. Everywhere else the sound dies within a few arm's lengths, but this one reaches the rooftops. Sometimes it comes twice in the time it takes to look away and back, and the count is still eleven.

It may be a new one of their kind, begun before there is an hour to give it. The day is a closed round and every part of it is taken. Or it may be something older than the herd, which the herd has come upon. Either way it is not getting higher. It is getting longer, and at its southern end the newest slabs no longer lie in line. They have started to turn.

A wall alone has two faces and no inside. A corner is the beginning of an inside, and an inside in this city is never empty. Whatever comes to be kept in a house with no hour will not be night, since night is let out at an evening and this house will not have one. The nearest houses have each opened one doorway more than their plan allows, low, on the side facing the square, as a house does when something is about to come along the foot of its wall. There is no doorway in the low wall yet, because nothing has needed to come out.
