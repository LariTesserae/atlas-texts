Very little lives here, and most of it is found by ear.

The amphipods came up from underneath. The rock is open to the sea all the way down, and they rose through it with the water, out of whatever dark seams they kept before there was a basin to rise into. They are white and eyeless and they do not hurry. Each pipe holds its own few, which ride the tide up and down the bore twice a day without swimming a stroke, grazing the slick on the glass below the waterline. In the brimming pipes of the centre they can be seen from above, turning under the grey disc of sky.

The orange film is alive in about the way rust would be if rust had to eat. It takes iron from the new rock a little at a time and competes with nothing. It keeps the basin's only record of touch. A heel set down in it shows for most of a week.

The birds are petrels, soot-dark, small enough to turn around at the bottom of a pipe. They spend the day far out on the water and come in only when the light has gone. Each finds its own pipe among the thousands by smell, a musk of fish oil that the fog holds low. They drop in with wings half shut and leave before dawn the way a swift leaves a chimney, scrabbling up the glass. A pair has one egg on a bed of sifted grit, then one chick, grey and fat as a fist, which sits at the bottom of the note it is muffling and waits.

A petrel can live thirty years and more. The rock has not. Some of the dozen pairs hatched in the ground that is now under the floor.

That ground was peat, a low sodden flat at the sea's edge, and it was riddled. Tens of thousands of pairs had burrows in it, each an arm deep. On summer nights they called from inside, a dry purr with a hiccup at the end, each bird to its mate overhead. From the rim it sounded as if something very large were asleep under the turf. Then the lake came and boiled the water out of the peat, and the water left by the only way open to it.

The birds that were at sea that year came back and found stone. Most went elsewhere. A few went down the nearest dark hole, because that is what a petrel does with a hole, and those and their young are the dozen pairs. They still call from the bottom. Nothing comes of it. The purr is pitched above the rim's groan, at the note of pipes that stand about half full, and there are thousands of those farther in, all sounding. The call sits inside what the basin is already saying, a great deal quieter, and it does not get out of the pipe. No passing bird hears a colony here, so none lands, and the number stays at a dozen.

The one who stops the pipes is a woman of about sixty, who was grown when the lake came. She learned the peat as a girl, belly-down on it, with her arm in a burrow to the shoulder. Her people took fat chicks from the flat every August, by hand, one hole in four, and she was good at it. She does not make much of this. They were food. She would reach in and the chick would be there, hot and oily. At night, from her bed beyond the rim, she could hear the ground purring.

She has come in along her cairns most days for eleven years. She built them in her first season for the slack of the tide, when the field goes quiet and there is nothing to steer by but dripping. The rest of the time she does not look at them. The height of the roar tells her how far in she has come. The concussions from the cliff tell her which side the sea is on, and the stream in its throats gives her a line across. With those three, in fog, she knows where she is to within a few pillars.

She walks the way the place has taught her, slightly side-on with her elbows in, like someone getting through a crowd without touching it. Her coat is patched with hide at the shoulders and knees, where the drip-shelves are. Her boots are resoled every winter. Her hands are the part of her the basin has had most of. The palms are cross-hatched with fine white scars like a cutting board. The fingertips have gone hard and smooth, and she notices small cuts only when the clay stings in them.

She carries the clay in from a streambank beyond the rim, one lump at a time, inside her coat against her stomach so it stays warm enough to work. She brings nothing else. The stones she finds where she is.

The room came first, in her second year, because she had to have it. Hearing wears. The top of hers is gone already, and the shrill of the centre reaches her now mostly through her teeth. The middle she guards. On the slack she sits on the slab and eats limpets from the raw cliff, uncooked, and drops the shells where she has always dropped them. No gull has found the heap. She swept the floor with the edge of her hand. She broke the one pillar with a stone, and the sound it made going over was bad enough that she has not broken another. She stays until her ears come back, which takes about as long as the limpets.

On cold days she goes on to the warm pipes and holds her fingers in the breath of them until they will bend. She knows those pipes by their flat notes before she sees the fog thin. They are the nearest thing she has to a hearth.

At the start of each season she fetches the note. She goes to a rim pipe with a flaw in it, kneels, folds her sleeve against the glass below the lower shelf, and lays her head there. Through the wall she can just get the purr. She takes its pitch into her closed mouth and keeps it.

Then she walks inward on the rising tide, when the whole field breathes out evenly and nothing gusts, humming. Near a pipe whose note is close to hers, the hum begins to throb against it, a slow beat in the bones of her face. She follows the beat as it slows, and where it stops the two notes are one. She drops a pinch of grit down the bore and listens for water, because the birds use only dry pipes and she has never stopped a dry one. She lays her palm over the mouth to be sure. Then she finds a stone that nearly fits, and another that makes it fit, and packs the clay around them with her fingers. On a good tide she stops two.

Rain lifts the water and brings new pipes into the note, and she takes those as well. So the gap is a band, not a line, and it widens every wet year. She knows the arithmetic. She also knows the grit is doing the same work everywhere, more slowly and without preference. She is only asking it to hurry in one place.

Far out, where she has worked longest, the gap can be heard on a steady wind by someone who has been told to listen for it. It is narrow, like one slat out of a long fence.

On such evenings she waits at the rim. The birds come in low under the fog and drop. One by one the soft places open in the groan, and she counts them to twelve. Then she hums the note with her mouth shut and waits for something to beat against it that is not a pipe.

Twice this summer something has. It was a slow throb in her jaw, a breath long, from overhead where there are no pipes. She has not counted it as anything yet. Both times she went back in the next morning with more clay.
