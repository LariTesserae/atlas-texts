The first thing a visitor notices about the people is that their mouths are shut. They breathe through the nose, slowly, with the deep chests of people born at this height. They talk through lips barely parted and laugh in the nose. A child who gapes is tapped under the chin by whoever is nearest.

**Bodies**

They are small, short in the limb and broad in the foot, with toes that spread on a ladder peg. Their skin carries a grey bloom from ash washing, darkest in the knuckle creases, and over it a shine of goat butter rubbed in to hold the water under the skin. Their palms are black from the bridge poles. Their pupils stand wide all day in the dusk, and on the rim they wear a fringe of goat hair across the brow. By thirty their teeth are worn flat by quern grit.

Nobody runs. Children run and are told they are throwing it away. Adults move as if carrying a full bowl, whether or not they are carrying anything.

**Voice**

Because the cleft repeats everything, there are two ways of speaking. One is the carrying voice, pitched to pass under the swifts, used for bargaining, calling goats, and disputes. The other is mouth to ear. For anything between those, two people climb the stair and say it on the rim, where the wind takes it. "Going up into the wind" with someone can mean courtship, conspiracy, or apology.

They do not hear the low note of the cleft, but every song they have sits on it. The coppersmith tunes each goat bell to a different pitch, so a woman can tell in the dark whose animal is eating her roof. He will melt down a bell that "fights," and he cannot say what it fights with. Infants are quiet in the windy half of the year and fretful in the still half. The few who have gone down off the plateau with traders and come back say they could not sleep down there.

**What they keep**

The customs go further than the three a guest is told. Spitting is the one true obscenity. Weeping is allowed, but someone will come and sit beside you with a cloth, and afterward the cloth is theirs. Friendships are reckoned this way, by who holds whose.

Each household carries its night water in a lidded pot to its own fig and lets it into the crack at the root. This is not counted as pouring out. A family and its tree have been drinking each other for generations, and the tree is spoken of as a relative on the wall side.

Most houses own one jar that has not been opened in living memory. The beekeeper renews its wax each year. It is rude to lift another family's jar and unthinkable to shake one. A sealed jar means a family that has not yet had to open it.

A dispute over a dew-heap may be in its fourth generation. It is conducted across the gap in the carrying voice, a few sentences a day, during the still months, with the whole town as witness because the town cannot help it. No one hurries it. Both sides know what the heap collects. It is the town's own air, last night's talk and sleep and cooking, risen and gone cold on the stones. The owner of a heap drinks her neighbours, and they know it. She holds the first mouthful at dawn without swallowing until she has looked down into the cleft.

A stranger who stays is not said to have arrived until her breath stops coming short. By then a month of it has gone up to the heaps and come back down in other people's cups.

**The other tenants**

The goats turn aloe into milk, and are understood as doing exactly that. Kids and children are weaned in the same season on the same roofs.

The figs have a tenant the people have never properly seen. It is a wasp the size of a grain of ash, living its whole life inside the fruit. They know only that a fig opened too early is full of "dust that flies," and that it must be left alone.

The swifts are the only residents who leave. Each day they go off the plateau to the low country, feed and drink on the wing, and come back at dusk. What falls from their nests whitens the walls and feeds the roots, so a little water comes up each evening. A little goes down the other way, in the vultures. Of a dead woman the people say she has "gone down with the birds." Of a good fig year or an unexpected guest they say it "came up with the birds." They do not seem to put the two sayings together.

The children sent up for beetles are the town's licensed thieves. What a child licks off a beetle's back before dawn is the only water she will ever drink that has not been through someone else first. The right ends at about twelve, when she stops being sent. Every adult remembers the taste, which is cold and faintly bitter.

**Down and up**

The young build at the top, where it is cold and the light is better. As people age they trade downward, house for house, toward the warmth and the dark. The oldest live in the lowest tier, with the floor at their doorsills, and sweep out each morning a little of what drifted in at night. They do not mind. They say it is quiet down there, though it is not.

At the end a person is carried up the whole height of the town in one morning, past every roof she has lived under, to the platform. The bones crack there in the heat and frost like everything else on the plateau. In a few years they are grit, and the wind puts the grit back into the cleft. This is why no one lives on the floor. They do not call it sacred. They say it is occupied.

**The stones**

The market is held on the floor. The weigher is always old, on the theory that the old are past being tempted. She lifts each cobble out of its dent in the dust and puts it back in the same dent afterward. People buying capers or copper pins hold the weight a little longer than the weighing needs. A palm that has known only wind-worn rock, which is matte and pitted, can feel the difference in a stone that water has finished.

They have no story about where the river went, and the question strikes them as strange. On this whole dry height the only water is in skins, in four hundred people, their goats, the figs, the fat leaves, and the beetles on the rim. It passes between them by milk and breath and cloth. It leaves a little each day in a vulture and returns a little in a swift. It rises with the floor, a hand in a lifetime, toward the rim.

Ask the weigher about the river and she does not point anywhere. She taps the cobble twice against her breastbone and sets it back in its place.

There is one moment before first light, in the still half of the year, when the swifts have not started and the geckos have stopped. The heap owners wake for it. From the top tier, if no one coughs, they can hear the only sound of water on the plateau. It is a drop falling from stone into a clay basin, then, after a long wait, another.
