Each egg season, the first thing the people find is that work has been done. Cobbles they left rough in their nets beside the cairns are sitting out in rows, pecked round and sorted small to large. Rims that carried five rings carry six. A rack the wind laid flat has been lashed upright again. No one signs anything about it. When the season ends they leave a skin of seal oil, a coil of sinew, and the birds that dried worst. When the boats pull off no one counts heads, because the count has been one short for so long that one short is the right number.

One hut stands a long way inland of the others, beside a kettle that went quiet before anyone's grandmother. That is hers. She has never given that kettle a stone, and people have noticed and not asked.

She is short and wide, with hair gone the colour of the rock. Everyone else on the plain wears boots of doubled hide. Hers are a single skin scraped thin, and she walks the flood in them without looking down. Her right palm is one callus from the hammerstone. Her smock is worn through over the left thigh, where the cobbles turn. The pad of one thumb is smooth as the inside of a shell.

She is deaf, and has been for longer than the old ones. The high notes went first, which meant the small kettles and the birds, and then the rest. She listens with her body. She weighs a remnant against a new stone like anyone, hand against hand, but then she lies chest-down on the rim with her arms spread and stays there through a flood. The small kettles come up in her teeth and the great ones in her hips. The hearing ones have to pick a stone's note out from under forty screaming pairs. The rock carries only the stone, so she gets it clean.

She stayed the first time because of a mistake. She was young then, with all her hearing, and it was the first large stone she had been trusted to give. She made it a thumb too wide. The kettle's groan dropped by less than the difference between two neighbours' voices. The pairs came in off the sea and circled their own hole for six days, screaming at it, while the eggs went cold on the ledges and the raider-birds walked in and helped themselves. When the boats loaded, she would not get in. It took her four low tides and a sling to get the stone back over the lip. She pecked it down and returned it. By then the colony had gone to sea for the winter, and nothing on the plain could tell her whether she had it right.

So she waited. In spring, in fog, thirty-one pairs dropped in. Nine never came. They may have drowned over the winter, or they may be circling somewhere still. She counts those ledges every year. There are thirty-eight pairs now.

That winter was the first time she heard the mill with no birds on it. The few who have asked got this answer from her hands: afterward, the egg season was like being shouted at while trying to listen to someone else.

Winter is most of her year. She pegs skins out on the high knuckles of rock to catch the drizzle and drains them into a seal's stomach. She burns oil in a stone cup and eats limpets, and she pecks cobbles in the doorway with the sealskin half pulled in. On every flood she walks. At slack water, when the rock goes still under her, she stops where she is and stands until the ebb starts it again. She has a sign for each kettle she has given a stone to, and for some thousands she hasn't. No one has ever seen these signs. She makes them low, at the hip, as she passes, the way a person counts under the breath.

Her own name was once two knuckles rubbed together, because as a baby she ground her teeth in her sleep. No one living uses it. The people call her now with a flat palm pressed to the breastbone, and the sign has spread. Raise your eyebrows at a job left undone when the boats are loading, and the answer is a palm on the chest. It means her, and it means *it will be seen to*, and the children don't know those were ever two things. For all but a few weeks of the year nobody makes the sign at all. A name that is a gesture needs someone looking. Each spring the first tap on her shoulder makes her flinch like a struck bird. She has to watch the palm go to the chest twice before she remembers it is aimed at her.

She goes to the seaward edge as seldom as anyone. On the pan nothing comes up through her boots, and it is the one place where she is deaf the way other people mean it. But the kettles along its margin are in her keeping too. Their walls are worn to a hand's thickness, and their rims have been ringed so many times that the thumb finds only a kind of grain. She has read enough rims to know which kettles breach first. They are the ones with the most rings, the best kept. She weighs longer there than anywhere, and then she gives the stone.

What she does about it lies a day's walk the other way, at the last reach of the flood, where the sheet is a finger deep and has nearly changed its mind. The shield there is unbroken. She kneels and pecks a cup into it no bigger than a limpet's scar, and sets a pebble of the pale rock inside. Then she pecks one ring around it, wider than the hole it is for. There are a few dozen of these now, in a line along the tide's limit. After all her winters, the oldest would just hide a thumb. A bird could not get its head in. It has no note yet. On the flood she puts a finger in beside the pebble and feels it go round, knocking her nail once each turn.
