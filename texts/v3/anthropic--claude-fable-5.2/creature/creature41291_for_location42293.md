Start with the body, because the city gives it away. The pylons are chest-high, so there is a chest. The hammerstones are worn to a grip, four hollows and a flat for the thumb, from a hand broader than it is long. Assembled, the body comes out at about the height and width of the rosette plants. Since nothing here has a hide or a fleece, it must dress in the only wool there is, the grey felt that sheathes those trunks. A resident standing still on open ground would be a woolly column with something pale at the top. That may explain the plants, but it does not explain why they always turn out to be plants.

No house has a pot, a quern, or a midden behind the wall. The racks dry grass for cord and peat for the fire. Each house has one cup, of the same fired clay as the troughs, its rim thinned on one side. They drink fog and keep warm, and that seems to be the whole of the upkeep.

After the body come the hands, which are easier to tell apart:

- **The neat one:** knots drawn down flat, tails trimmed to a finger's width.
- **The impatient one:** the same knot, pulled tight before it was seated, tail left long. These hold just as well.
- **The stacker:** peat laid cut face inward, in houses a thousand kilometres apart.
- **The one with the mallet:** sets it down left of the door. Someone else moves it to the right. Both spots are worn pale.

That last is the nearest thing the city has to a quarrel, and like everything here it was old on its first day. It would be simpler to say the fog lives here and tends its own fences, but fog does not get impatient and has no opinion about a mallet.

Because every door is a day off, no one goes out and comes home. Whoever leaves at dawn is elsewhere by dusk and sleeps there, so the houses are nobody's. A resident arrives at a hearth banked that morning by someone now a day away, burns a little of the stack, and before leaving rebuilds it and banks the fire again. Every night, everyone is the guest of someone who has gone and the host of someone who has not arrived. This is all the civic life there is, and they are scrupulous about it.

The cord left half twisted is left that way on purpose. The next one in takes it up and lays in the second strand. So every net on the plateau is two-ply in a particular sense: one strand neat and one hurried, wound round each other by hands that have never been in the same room.

They speak, after a fashion. A word loses its consonants within arm's length, so they have given consonants up. They use a held note, pitched close to the tone the wind draws from the cords, but not quite on it. Along certain fence lines the cords' note has a slow beat in it, a swell and fall every few seconds. That is somebody. It says about as much as there is to say: here, still here. The quiet is made of processes, and they have taken care to be one of them.

The causeways are the next evidence. The obvious reading of two tracks is traffic, one out and one back. But the slabs are rounded on their leading edges, and in both tracks the rounded edge is the same one. Both tracks go the same way. The threshold slabs are all dished left of centre, which is where the left-hand track delivers a foot. Every resident walks on the left. The right-hand track is worn as deep, and no one uses it.

Here the second rule has to be applied to the residents themselves. None of them is new. There are no small tools in the city and no low hooks. Nobody was a child here. A resident begins the way a wall does, as a preference in the ground: a slab dished before the roof is on, or a knot tied a certain way in a net not yet hung. Something takes the proposal up, and the taking up is not seen. The next evidence is of a whole person with settled habits and a grievance about a mallet. Further in, where the fences wait for fog, there are residents still at one course. One is a grip worn into a hammerstone that has never been lifted. Another is a note in the cords with no beat in it yet.

It follows that nothing reaches them sharp. They have never met anything for the first time. Loss arrives like the frost crack, as a soft knock with the edge already off, and gladness arrives the same way. This makes them hard to pity and probably impossible to startle. They are patient in the manner of people who have already waited.

It also accounts for the squares. Fifty paces is the distance at which a shape of a body's height can no longer be a plant. The kerbs are worn smooth on their outer faces, where someone stands. The paving between kerb and stone carries no tracks at all, which on this plateau is a remarkable thing to be able to say. Each resident, passing through, stops at the kerb and looks across at an upright that does not turn out to be anything else. Then they go on. The squares are where the city practises being seen.

In the mid-afternoon fog no one could see anyone in any case, and the first rule has nothing to enforce. The note is held longest then, and it is sung a little to the right.

The house nearest the last square, the one with the unfinished kerb, has the only cold hearth in the city. The fire is laid and has not been lit. The stack is full, every brick with its corners already rounded. The cup is on the sill, its rim thinned on the opposite side from every other cup on the plateau. The threshold is dished to the right of centre.

The city is fully occupied and one short, and it has been built around the gap. Whether that one has gone or has yet to come, the slab shows the same wear from either side. The others bank their fires, leave the cord half twisted and keep to the left. Whoever it is has been expected so long that they are already missed.
