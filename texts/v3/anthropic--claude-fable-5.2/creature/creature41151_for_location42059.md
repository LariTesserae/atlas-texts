At first you would say a potter lives here, and you would be nearly right.

The evidence is all hands. The paddle's handle is warm in a band the width of a palm. A hank of cord has been bitten through where the shell knife was out of reach. In the basket of river clay there is a scoop where someone pinched out a sample to test for grit. You build her from this without meaning to: a small woman, thick through the forearm, slip drying gray at her hairline where she pushed the hair back with a wrist. You almost catch her. A leaf is swinging when you turn, and a dipper rocks on its cord.

But the paddle-stripes go all the way up the dome, over the part where no one could stand, and they are no fainter there. The thumbprints do not hold a size. Low on the wall they are as broad as yours. Follow a coil upward and they narrow, with no course where the change happens, until at the blowhole they are the size of a seed. Either there are many potters, each smaller than the last, or there is one, who is whatever size the work is.

It is the wasp. Watch her and not the patch she is leaving, and she stays a wasp. She is black, with a waist like a knot in thread and wings pleated shut along her back. She lands with the pellet held under her chin and walks the rim backward, drawing it out. She taps the fresh clay fast with both antennae, the way a potter raps a wall with a knuckle to hear how thick it is, and where it sounds wrong she goes back. She is not fierce. If your hand is on the wall she walks over it. Her feet are cold, the only cold thing in the room.

That should tell you. She has been outside. She drinks from the drip on the jar shoulder and eats nothing. In all this green there is no place pressed flat by sleeping. She is the only thing here in a hurry, so much so that her paddle sounds before it lands. She builds the room and stocks it, but she does not live in it. When the last course is on she will close the blowhole from the outside with one pellet, and the note will stop.

The frog is a voice in a jar, and the answer is the same voice from a jar of plain water. Lift the hide and there is your face, and rings. A vessel says back what is said into it. The frog may be only that, the room practicing the sound of something living in it.

The caterpillars are alive, but they are kept, which is different. They lie head to tail among the pot feet, each curled the way it was when stung, skins tight and cool. Now and then a pulse travels down a back. They have been breathing as long as the drip has been falling, and they are no thinner.

That leaves the pale thing. Nothing in here was found, and everything was made, except this. This was laid. It has no thumbprint and no paddle-stripe. It is the color of the inside of a gourd, curved like a nail paring, and dry.

The thread is the part to study. The thing hangs because its food is alive. Stored creatures shift, and a caterpillar arches once in a long sleep. Anything lying among them would be rolled on and crushed. So it hangs over its larder, and when it opens it will not come down. It will let itself out headfirst, still gripped at the tail by the split shell. It will reach the nearest soft breathing thing and bite, and if the thing heaves it will draw back up and wait. That is how it will first know the room, on a line, in the rain, tasting and retreating.

It will have no eyes and no legs. It will be the color of slip. It will have a mouth, and along each side a row of breathing holes, each a blowhole with a faint note of its own. It will eat the caterpillars. Then it will be a hand long and no longer a grain, and it will drop and eat the gourds, the ferns from their baskets, the moss off the cords, the wet-rope climber down to the lattice. One evening the frog will call after rain and only the answer will come.

And it will wear things. It will drag a track in the ash floor where its belly goes. The jar shoulders will take a polish where it squeezes by. This is why nothing here is worn. Wear is what living in a place does, and no one has lived here yet.

When it fills the room, curled once around the wall with the heat inside it at last, it will stop. It will go soft all through, as unsettled as the daub. Then it will harden, jaws first, then the black waist, then the pleated wings. It will be the first fired thing here. Count the jars then and they will come out the same both ways.

It will chew through the finger of mud into the cold. The mountain will be in by then, or it will not. She will stand on the frost feathers, and the first thing she will want is wet clay. She will find a rope going down.

So count who lives here sunwise and you get a wasp, a frog, a row of sleepers, and a thing on a thread. Count the other way and there is one creature at several sizes, not in order. The oldest thing in the room is still wet because she has not hatched yet. Meanwhile she is behind a leaf with the paddle.

For now she hangs. Get the firebox behind her and you can see a darker curl inside the pale, with two points at one end already working in a slow spiral. She turns slightly in the steam. Each time, she turns toward whatever nearby is warm and breathing.

Just now that is you.
