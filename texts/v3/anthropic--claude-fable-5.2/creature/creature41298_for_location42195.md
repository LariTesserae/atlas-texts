Start with the grazers, because they are what the eye finds first: a dot, a hundred metres off another dot, and so on across a quarter of the horizon.

Close to, a grazer is the size of a big pig and shaped like a wedge. The nose is the thin end, carried a hand's breadth above the felt. From there the back rises in one unbroken slope to the hips, and the gale goes over the animal as it would over a low dune. The legs are short and set wide. The coat is the felt's own trick done again in hair, hollow colourless shafts that throw the light back. From above there is no animal, only a black wedge of shadow lying on the glare with nothing to cast it.

The ears are small, plugged with hair, and useless. A grazer listens with its feet. The pads are soft and far broader than its weight calls for, and what comes up into them through tens of metres of dead mat is the knocking from the gypsum. Where the knocking is thickest the roots are winning the most water and the sap above runs fullest, so the animal walks toward it. A herd over a season is ten thousand animals steered through their soles by a quarry none of them will ever see.

It does not bite. The lower teeth have fused into a single blade, and it planes off a strip of felt a palm wide. The wound wells and the tongue is already there, because there are perhaps three seconds before the resin skins and the plateau closes again. Shave, lick, step. No grazer has seen water standing and none passes any. What it leaves is a dry pellet like a knot of string. The cost of feeding this way is resin. It gathers on the lips and chin, darkens and sets, and by middle age an animal carries a bib of amber under its jaw. In an old one the bib is thick as a fist and has chaff in it, moth scale, now and then a whole beetle with its legs arranged.

On the rump the hairs are longer and rooted in a disc of muscle. Laid flat they are dull. Raised, they are the white that can be read across a morning's walk. Every animal faces the wind, so every rump faces away from it, and a flash can only be read from behind. News in a herd runs one way. Those on the windward edge look out at empty felt and are watched by everyone. Those on the lee edge see all the herd knows and can tell no one, and that is the edge the hunters prefer. No grazer chooses its place or keeps it. Twice a day the wind reverses, the herd turns in its tracks, and the last are first.

The hundred metres between them is partly a matter of lanes. An animal walking behind another gets its dust and its sealed leavings. It is also the charge. By afternoon each grazer is a charged body, and two that touch do it with a crack and a stink of singed hair. Only the calves are spared. A calf presses to its mother's flank when the wind rises and does not break contact until it fails, so that the two are one charge all day. A calf that lets go in the afternoon finds out what coming back costs.

The ground birds are harder to describe, because for most of the day a ground bird is not visible. It is fist-sized and felt-backed, and it crouches in the lee of a vent-stem and works along the seed lines the bales have dropped. It has nearly given up flight and keeps the wing as a shutter. They live in pairs, and a pair's ground may be twenty kilometres long, so the two seldom see each other except as light. One flick, and far off, an answer. The glitter in ones and twos is mostly this, couples keeping count. A flick that gets no second is repeated at longer and longer intervals for days. They nest under the hem of the mat where it has come unpegged, in the pocket between the living and the dead. It is the one place out of the wind, and it is where the next sheet will lift.

A hunter is mostly wing, and the wing is mostly edge. From below it is a line, and it only becomes a bird when it tilts. It does not fly so much as decline to be moved. It takes ground birds, and calves from the lee edge, but mainly it takes what spills. It knows a failing bale by the sag and the heavier shedding, and above a bale about to break there will be a dozen hunters stacked in a column where the sky would normally hold one. They lay their eggs on the dark bands, where the warm ground does the brooding and both parents can hang all day.

The bale is the only thing on the plateau with an inside. The small birds that ride it glue their nests in with resin taken from its torn edges while it is still wet, and glue the eggs into the nests. The chicks hatch already gripping, since the floor comes round overhead every few seconds. The mouse-like animals keep to the core, where the turning is slowest, and move outward to feed on pod and seed when the bale stops. It stops at dusk, and then the night wind rolls it partway back. A bale's hundred kilometres is a ravelled line, two lengths forward and one back, and its passengers are born, breed and die on a journey that keeps undoing itself. When it breaks they have a day to reach a scar edge, another bale, or a dark band, with the hunters stacked overhead.

The moths spend the day down among the hairs of the felt, holding on. The rip of a sheet lifting is the one sound on the plateau that anything listens for, and they are what listens. Those downwind of a tear never reach it. Those upwind let go and are carried to the bleeding edge.

The dark bands are the plateau's towns. The honeycomb under them is dug by the same mouse-like animal that rides the bales, settled now, thousands to a kilometre. A town cannot stay where it is. The fire moves a few metres a year. Ahead of it the ground is cold, and behind it is ash, which will not hold a tunnel. So the colony digs forward into the warmth and loses its rear at the same pace, and advances about its own body-length a month. No animal in it has lived anywhere but the same warm stretch, though the town itself has come from beyond the horizon and is older than the species that crops the roof over it. New blood arrives when a bale breaks across the band.

The grazers come here at night. The herd that was a field of dots by day draws into a single line tens of kilometres long, each animal lying on the warm strip with its nose to the night wind. They lie among the hunters' eggs.

Then there is the hour. When the wind fails, the charge drains out through ten thousand pairs of feet, and the grazers do what they cannot do at any other time, which is walk toward each other. They stand flank to flank. They mate. They gnaw each other's amber, since no animal can reach its own bib, and an old one with no one to gnaw it will end unable to open its jaw. The calves let go and run in circles.

The hunters have no gale to stand in and come down. They sit on the felt with their wings half open, too long to fold properly, and the ground birds walk past them. The ground birds fly, once, straight up. Each pair meets in the air for a few minutes and is two flashes again before the hour ends.

The moths go up from the felt and out of the pipes. Their pupae winter in the snapped stalks, wedged partway down, and a stalk with a pupa in it has a shorter column of air and sounds a higher note. The top of the chord all day has been moths waiting. This year's flower opens for them now, low and pale and half-raised, and by next year it will be a pipe.

Then the first air comes from the wrong quarter, the hair lifts along ten thousand backs, and they step apart.

Nothing here rots, and that applies to the animals too. What the hunters and beetles leave of a grazer dries where it lay, and within a season the felt has closed over it from the sides, brighter than the old. The dead layer holds all of them. In the scarps at the plateau's edge they show in section between the mat bands and the ash lines: the wedge, the wide feet, the bib of amber still clear enough to catch the sun. They lie far apart, a hundred metres or so. Each lies as it lay down, nose to the wind. Some face one way and some exactly the other, and you can tell of each whether it died by day or in the dark.
