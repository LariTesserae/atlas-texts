Most of what lives here lives on the wall, and the wall is full. Swifts, geckos, moths, snails, bees, and frogs each hold a band of height or a patch of damp and keep to it. They are tenants of the edge. If the hollow had no one else, you would say it belonged to the geckos, because they are everywhere and the last hour of light is theirs.

But stand at the rim at noon, when the chamber has gone flat, and count the marks floating on the white. There is the black-green fan, the amber stain, and the grey tide line of nests. There is the ring of ochre dashes, a hand apart, all the way round. And there is one dash that is not in the ring. It is the same healed pink and the same length, but it lies far inside the circle, near the middle, where there is no wall to mark.

The next day it is somewhere else.

It is on the cone. At first it looks like part of the cone, a rounded lump of the fallen roof with crystal grown over it like all the others. It is about as long as a forearm. It sits on the west flank in the morning, when the oval of direct sun crosses there, and by the shadowless hours it has gone into a scrape on the north side, under a tilted slab. The floor of the scrape is worn as glossy as the handholds on the south wall.

It is a tortoise. She is white, though not by birth. Gypsum has crusted in the seams between her plates and regrown there, so that she too looks poured. Her growth rings are packed with flour and cannot be counted. Her eyes are black and wet, and nothing else in the middle of the hollow is. She keeps them shut through noon. Perhaps this is why the centre does not trouble her. She does not judge distance. She knows the cone by the feel of it under her, each healed face of it.

The pink is on her highest plate. It has been laid on in many coats, thick enough to stand proud of the shell like a ridge, and the last coat has faded exactly as far as the dashes on the wall. The people who needed to know where the wall was seem to have needed to know where the middle was too. Whoever painted her had to cross the flour to do it. Their tracks are gone, so tracks do not last forever here, only longer than anyone has been back.

How she came to the cone is the one thing the floor ought to record, and it does not.

She lives on what the sky drops. Seeds arrive in droppings. So do feathers, a moth that misjudged, eggshell from the nests, and now and then a fledgling that failed the spiral. She eats the grasses rarely and never to the root. At dawn, when the hollow sweats, the cone sweats with it, and she walks from face to face licking the crystal before it dries. Certain faces are polished from this, like the snails' trails, and catch the light.

On the summit there is a shallow dish the width of her own shell. No drip made it. She wore it herself, turning in place, and she keeps it clear of grit. When rain comes it comes as a single column onto the cone, which is to say onto her. She is always at the dish before it starts. She stands high on straightened legs with her neck out, and she drinks until the water is gone into the rubble. Then she does not drink again until the next rain, which may be a year away.

When she moves there is a dry rasp, like a quern turning, and the walls swallow it.

She has one path. It circles the foot of the cone where the rubble meets the flour, and she walks it turning the same way the swifts turn. They wheel down the wall and she goes round below them, far more slowly, on the same axis. Nothing on the wall seems afraid of her. Perhaps the middle was empty, and so it was where she could be. Perhaps the middle is simply taken.

At dusk the scrub above goes dark, and for most of an hour the hollow holds its even paleness. The walls get their curve back, and the ochre dashes stand out all the way round. In that light the geckos begin to move. She comes down to the lowest stone of the cone, sets her forefeet at the edge of the flour, and puts her neck out to its full length. She stays that way a long while, facing the north wall, where the drip is.

The flour in front of her is smooth. She turns, the way she always turns, and climbs back to the scrape.
