Take the one with the chipped scute, since she is the one you will keep meeting.

She is fourteen paces from jaw to tail, and you cannot see over her. The plate is laid in courses like roofing, each lapped over the one behind, so that dust runs off toward the tail. The chip is in the fourth scute back from the stack, on the left: a piece the size of a hand gone from the trailing edge. It is old damage. The broken face has scaled over the same blue-grey as the rest, and the rivets around it are worn nearly flush. Elsewhere they stand proud, in rows that are only roughly rows, the way teeth are.

She has no eyes that face outward. Two lenses the colour of smoked mica sit low on the skull beneath the boss, angled down and in, toward where the work goes. Walk in front of her and nothing changes. What she attends with is her back. The scutes along the spine are never quite still. A fine shiver runs through them from shoulders to tail, separate from the slide of walking, and it goes on when she stands. She is taking the roar in through her plate, all of it, as you are taking it in through your teeth.

You can put a hand on her. The flank is as hot as a pan handle left near the fire. Under your palm is the walking, and under the walking is something slower, a swell that passes rearward along the upper flank every ten or twelve seconds and returns along the lower. That is the water. She was filled once, by her dam, and has not been filled since. When a beast dies the others open its seams and drink before they eat, so the water in her has been in every one of them there has been. It never fell as rain. There is one quantity, and it goes round.

At the anvil-stone she stops with her forefeet in two hollows that fit them. The throat works backward in three long swallows. The loaf comes up between the jaws, dull red at one end, and she lays it in the dish of the stone with the flat of her tongue. Then she raises the boss a hand's breadth and holds it there. This is most of what she does. She holds for the better part of an hour, spine shivering, and then the skull comes down and you feel it in your shins. The loaf looks the same. She turns it a few degrees with the side of her jaw and raises the boss again.

She cannot hear her own blow any more than you can. What she can hear is every other blow on the flat. Among them, a kilometre up the groove and a kilometre down it, are the blows struck on this same loaf by a beast with a chip over the left shoulder. She has never heard herself any other way. Watch long enough and you will see her hold a stroke back, plates rippling, and then let it go as if into a space. You will not hear the space.

Between anvils she walks and grazes. The tongue is grey, as wide as a shovel, set with backward points that are replaced from the root. One pass takes the varnish and a finger's depth of shale and leaves a pale stripe. The shale feeds the fire, and what the fire leaves is the start of the next loaf. The third leg on the left comes forward late and sets down short. In the left rut, at every stride, there is a dull patch the size of a saucer where the gloss is scuffed, and the patches run the whole length of the groove, in front of her as well as behind.

She moults a course at a time, tail forward. The new plate over the left shoulder comes in with the chip already in it.

At this anvil she has two calves, one in each rut behind her. They are the size of handcarts, with plate like sheet lead that takes the print of a thumb. They have no stack, only a sealed knob between the shoulders, and no fire. Their heat is hers, from following close. Their tongues are still soft. When a bead forms at one of her seams and dries, it leaves its ring of salt on the stone beneath, and a season later that stone has orange on its lee side. The calves find every one. They also worry loose her shed plate and swallow it. Nothing she drops stays in the groove.

Walk on to the next anvil and she is there, with the same chip. The loaf is narrower. The calves each have a first row of rivets coming through behind the jaw, still pale. At the anvil after that, the knobs on their shoulders have split and each trails a thread of smoke. At the one after that, she is alone. Two shallow grooves leave hers at an angle, one to each side, ankle-deep and unpolished, and out on the flat there are eleven plumes. If you turn back to check, the beast at the anvil behind you is whole across the shoulder and walks evenly. There is no scuff in its rut, and it has never calved.

This is why the counts disagree. She is at every anvil in her groove, a little further along at each, and the kilometre between is also most of a season. The ear has it right. It is counting her wherever she has stood, and wherever she will.

At the rim the loaf is finished. It is a third smaller than it began, and it has a skin on it like the varnish on the stones. She sets it down among the shells and pushes with the flat of her muzzle until it tips. She has nothing to watch it go with. Then she stands for a day with her jaw low over the edge.

Each shell is closed by a skin across its mouth, as thin as the skin on hot milk, and the skin is always moving. Every blow arrives at the rim entire, as it arrives everywhere, and here it goes in. The shells are built of layers finer than paper, more than can be counted, and it is likely that each layer is a blow. If you pry one loose, a thread of the roar leaks through the gap, and you can follow it a few metres down the escarpment before it frays out. The underside of the shell is lined with a millimetre of orange. By next season the gap holds a shell the same size as its neighbours.

While she stands there, a bead forms at the hinge of her jaw, and for once it falls. It is the only water she gives up. The water has passengers, and this is where they get off.

Twice a year the whistle comes. Her boss stops where it is. The shiver along her spine stops, which it does at no other time, and at the rim every skin in every shell goes still. The calves, at the anvils where she has them, turn their heads, each a different way. She does not turn hers. Then the roar is back and she is at the next stone with her forefeet in the hollows and the loaf already up, and the dust between the two anvils is unmarked.

Whatever whistles does not live here. Its note is the only sound on the tableland with a direction, because it is the only one that comes from somewhere else, and the rim is on every side. The loaves go out in silence. Twice a year, one note comes in.

After dark she is a row of orange seams and a heat on your face. Put your hand back on her flank. The swell of the water passes under it. The boss comes down, and you feel it in your boot soles and hear nothing from her, while the air around you thunders. Somewhere in that thunder, at full volume, is the stroke she just made. Behind her in the ruts, the two calves lift their flat heads when she lifts hers and bring them down an inch short of the stone.
