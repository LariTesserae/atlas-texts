Most of what lives here can be counted, and none of it meant to come.

The midges are the most numerous. Each lamp has its own cloud, and the clouds do not mix, because thirty paces of dark is farther than a midge will ordinarily fly. A few are carried across on the draught after a defrost, which is how each new lamp gets its first. So there are forty separate nations of midge, the western ones older by a thousand generations. The moss follows the midges and the ferns follow the moss, and that is the whole succession.

The eels are olive and brass, the colouring of an eel that has not yet been called away. In a river they would turn silver one autumn and go down to the sea. Here there is no autumn and the only way out is up, through the pumps, so they have not turned, and an eel that does not turn hardly ages. The ones that came through the screens thin as bootlaces are now thick as a wrist and likely as old as the first pours. They travel the whole grid, but sooner or later they all lie up beside the warm bays, the way cattle stand at a gate.

The bird is a wagtail, and it does not call. On a riverbank a wagtail calls constantly. This one has given it up, or was never answered often enough to keep the habit.

None of these lit a lamp. For whoever did, go east.

At first the east end reads as a crew just stepped away. The forms are oiled, the steel is tied, the skip is ticking. It is work on a scale that suggests thirty hands and a foreman, but no one comes back, and the evidence thins toward one. There is one tally, in one hand, chalked at a height that puts the writer's shoulder near a tall person's breastbone. The strokes are pulled right to left. Under them are the grey ghosts of older tallies, wiped and rewritten, the same hand every time. On the bay poured the day before yesterday, warm and just hard enough to bear, four struck form boards lie side by side with a folded coat at one end.

So there is one person, small and left-handed, who sleeps on the newest concrete that will hold them. They never sleep twice in the same place, and they always sleep in the same place, one bay behind the wet edge. The bed has been moving east at the speed of the hall for longer than the ferns have been rooted.

The tally is easy to take for days. It counts skips, so many to a bay, and when the bay is full it is wiped with a sleeve and begun again. Days are not kept. The bays keep the only count that matters, and they keep it themselves.

Other things follow from the place. The hands will be like the floor near the shaft, pale and lime-cracked, with the knuckles scarred by the keeper's own arrises, since nobody else here makes an edge. They will long ago have stopped hearing the pumps. What they attend to is the water hammer: they stop mid-stroke when it hits and stand until the blow has run out westward, because a blow that stops short means a main has gone. They know the rain. The drops fall in rows a yard apart, and someone narrow enough can walk the whole hall between two rows and arrive dry, and this one does. There are no eel bones anywhere on the floor, which settles one question, though not why.

They have one habit the work does not require. Around midday, when the green band wakes near the springing of the arch, the tally shows a gap, a wider space between one stroke and the next. They stop for it. They probably sit on the form boards with their back to the pour and look up at the only light they did not strike themselves, until it goes.

They may not have come here on purpose either. The likeliest story is the eels' story: a child sent down with the first crews to carry chalk and tea, small enough for the work between the steel, who was below when the others stopped coming. By the time anyone might have thought of the shaft, it was a long walk back and the work was the other way. The siren would tell them how long ago that was, if they wanted to know. When they were young, the second note came back almost on top of the first. Each bay since has delayed it by a hair, and now there is a wait.

What shows only at this end is that everything else in the hall lives off this person and does not know it. Each lamp they light draws its green circle, and the midges and the bird follow the green. The pour gives off its week of heat, and the eels come and lie in it. The hall has weather, but it has only one season, a narrow band of spring that moves east one bay at a time, and this is the person who carries it. Behind them it settles into moss and lamplight and stays that way. Ahead of them there is nothing yet.

Ahead is also where the brine pipes have only begun to whiten. For some hours in every bay, the ground on the north side is not yet held, only promised, and the keeper ties steel beside it with whatever the hall was dug to keep out standing unfrozen a pipe's width away. Nothing in the tally suggests they work faster then. The strokes are evenly spaced.

Two miles of wet floor carries a footfall a long way, and the bird moves ahead of a stranger from lamp to lamp, so the keeper has known for most of an hour. That is why the last stroke is fresh and the skip is ticking and no one is in sight. Look at the lee side of the nearest column, where the lit fog hangs still. Every so often a small breath of steam comes past the edge of the iron, at about the height of the tally, and thins away. It comes slowly. They are not exactly hiding. They are doing what they do when the siren goes, which is to stand where the column covers them, without hurry, and wait for weather to pass.
