About forty people live here, and for the first hour they are hard to tell apart. They are short and deep in the chest, with the broad, weathered cheeks of people raised in thin air. They dress in goat wool left its own colors, gray, dun and a brownish black, so a person standing still against a wall is mostly noticed when they move. They tie their hair back with sedge cord and wear soft hide shoes. They walk the polished middle of the lane even when it's empty.

They look like one family but are eleven households, loosely cousins. In every generation a few go down into the cloud with the wool and the cord and don't come back, and now and then someone comes up with the green cobbles and stays. One woman came that way twenty years ago. She still speaks at a valley pitch, and her voice goes down a lane ahead of her and turns the corner. Nobody hushes her anymore. The children can imitate her, and do, quietly.

What the rest have in place of loudness is hearing. Each door turns in a hole it ground for itself and has its own note, so they know who has gone out. They know one another's whorls and footfalls, and they know by the drip which cistern is low. A quarrel here is conducted in murmurs and can last years, since nothing in it is ever said loudly enough to be over.

Their hands are gray in the creases with schist dust, with a callus on the heel of the palm from the hammerstone. They touch walls as they pass, laying a palm flat for a moment, to feel for damp. In a house without fire the joints never pale. When the dark spreads from the seams into the faces of the lowest course, somebody says the house is asking, and within the month a household moves.

They own what can be carried in two trips, and no one says "my house." Their word for living somewhere is their word for burning, and they reckon a life that way. An old man will say he has burned in nineteen houses and that the one under the drain is the worst, because of the sound. He has been saying it for fifty years.

There are seven or eight children at a time, rarely more. They know the town by its roofs and can cross it yard to roof to yard without touching a lane, the cliff-birds walking ahead of them complaining. There are no graves. The dead are carried down the south side, the way the cloud goes, and what is kept of them is their share of the dish in the thresholds.

They aren't solemn about any of this. Putting a stone back is no more a rite than shutting a door. A man will reset a slab badly, a neighbor will redo it at dusk without a word, and that is the sort of thing the murmured quarrels are about. What is new here is only what wears out, the cloth, the cord, and the pots with the thumb marks of whoever coiled them. When a pot breaks, the sherds are ground under a hammerstone and worked into the clay of the next.

The goats keep the town's hours more strictly than the people do. An old nanny with a chipped horn brings them down the lane at the same grayness every evening, and the people look up at the sound to learn the time.

The pair of birds on the store are the only residents who have never moved house. It has always been a pair. The fledglings leave, and when one bird dies another arrives from somewhere within the season. The people find this unremarkable, being arranged the same way themselves.

The short-tailed thing in the walls has no name except a word that means the corner of the eye. It lives in all seventy houses at once, warm and cold, and is the only creature that does. Its runs through the joints are what loosen most of the chinking slivers, so a good many of the stones put back the same day fell because of it. This has gone on so long that it's hard to say which of them is keeping the other in practice.

The cushion plants are older than all of them. An old woman will point to one with her spindle and say it reached the second paving slab when she was born. It still reaches the second paving slab.

But apart from the cushion plants, the longest-lived things in the town are the fires. There is almost nothing here to start one with, no wood for a drill and no tinder that stays dry through a morning of cloud. So fires are not started. Each is banked at night under its own ash with a brick of peat pressed into its heart, then uncovered and fed in the morning.

When a household moves, the fire goes first, before the bedding, carried by the oldest in a pot kept for nothing else. If one dies in the night, through sickness or careless banking, someone walks two doors along with the pot and comes back with a piece of a neighbor's. So there are eleven fires, and there is one. Nobody knows who brought it up the mountain, or when. Presumably it came as the terrace soil did, carried. In the store is a striking-stone traded up from below, and two or three people have been told how it is used. None of them has ever used it.

Once this is noticed, the people look different. The houses stand thirty generations and the fire has burned as long. It's the people who are replaced. The fire needs feeding twice a day and covering at night, and every few years it needs carrying down a lane to a cold room. They do that for it.

If the town could be watched over three hundred years with the days run together, the people would hardly show. The smoke would show, standing over one capstone and then over another a few doors off. It would go around the town at about the pace the cushion plants grow, and every morning the cloud would cross through it. The cloud leaves water on the stone and moves on, and the fire takes the water back out, a house at a time.

The people know this well enough to joke about it. When a move falls in bad weather they say the fire has decided to go visiting and they are its goats.

Tonight the newest hearth is on the upper lane, in the house whose yard is the roof of two others. A woman, her brother and two children carried the pot there nine days ago. The room last held fire before the woman was born. The walls are still giving up their damp, and the slabs tick as they dry. The air smells of wet slate and old ash. The thing in the walls has already shifted its runs to the warm side. The inner joints are still dark, but by next season they will have paled.

She has banked the fire for the night, pulling the ash over with a goat rib and pressing the peat in. Under the ash is a red thing the size of one of the terrace tubers, with no particular shape. It is the same one her grandmother covered in another room. In the morning, while the cloud is coming through the fog-walls and the first drip starts in the cisterns, one of the children will kneel, push the ash aside and blow, and it will be there.
