About four thousand people live between the tors, and the number has not changed in anyone's memory. They are the obvious answer, though not the whole of it.

The place has made them slow. Nobody runs, because the air will not pay for it, and nobody strides, because the paving is slick again a week after scraping. They walk flat-footed in nailed boots, placing each step, and a stranger watching a street will sooner or later think of the toad. They are short and deep in the chest. They wear felt and waxed cloth, and everything they own is baked in a blower house once a week or it goes green at the seams. "Dry" is their word of praise. A dry coat, a dry house, a dry man: something kept up, something that can be relied on.

Their hands give the trade. Pit and pug-mill workers have white in every crease that no washing shifts. Stokers are black to the wrist. Wire drawers carry fine pale scars, and the netters, who mend the mesh and knock the rime off it on clear nights, have split red knuckles. But everyone's hands are green some of the time, because scraping is not a trade. It is the rota, one week in eight for everyone over twelve, and the works manager takes his hoe with the rest.

Outsiders misread what this has done to them. They do not expect anything to stay done. A roof, to them, is something you do, not something you have. They tell no stories about beating the green and feel no grief about losing to it. Those who come up from below take this for resignation and find after a year or so that it is closer to housekeeping. The lowland idea of a thing built once and left to stand strikes them as faintly careless.

Newcomers come up in the coal buckets, there being no other road. They arrive black and are sick for a week. A few people go down in the empties, packed in straw like the ware. Those who come back report that the lowlands are loud, that people there stand a long way apart, and that the rain hits you on the top of the head.

They speak low and close, with a hand on the sleeve first, since a word said from two metres goes into the wall. Conversation happens at a distance where you feel the other person's breath. Shouting achieves nothing, so nobody learns it, and quarrels are held at the same closeness and pitch as everything else, which is harder to bear. The one sharp sound in the town is in the sorting sheds, where the tappers strike each insulator with a peg and listen for a ring or a clack. A child's first echo comes inside a cold kiln at relining, the only room on the plateau that gives a voice back. Children are taken in for the purpose, the way lowland children are taken to see the sea.

They are used to one another's faces lit from below. Elsewhere that is how you frighten children, and here it is how your mother looks.

The lamps have got into their speech. A person getting ready is at amber. A person at red is not to be interrupted. Green means finished, and they use it for everything finished: a kiln drawn, a job given up, a roof gone over, a pit closed in. When someone dies, the household hangs a small green lamp at the door, meaning drawn and safe to open. Ask whether the green of the lamp and the green of the moss are the same word, and they look at you as though the question answers itself.

They live by the kiln calendar. Every street has a private season: a week of summer when its kiln is at red, a slow autumn through the cooling, winter when it stands empty. The old carry stools from row to row, following the red lamps. The cats do the same without stools. They belong to the kilns, not to households, and people climb up with scraps for them. Babies are aired against warm brick. Any group standing in a kiln yard settles into an arc with its backs to the cold, like the cabbage beds.

It would be easy to say the people are one more band of growth on the kiln's flank, sorted by temperature like the mosses, and it is partly true. But the moss does not light the kiln. They are the only thing on the plateau that makes heat on purpose, and everything else that lives here, themselves included, leans toward what they make.

The tors are their addresses. One lives under Sixty-One, or between Ninety and Ninety-Four. Children learn to count from the stones, so the numbers have faces: forty-one is the long low one behind the wire mill, and it stays that way all their lives. In the clear hours people climb onto the tors, which are the only open ground in the town and the only places above the roofs. The rule forbids building, not standing, but in practice nothing is left there. A dropped glove is carried down by whoever finds it. They stand with their hats off and say little. It is the one time in the day they are lit from above, with shadows falling the right way.

Their dead go into the floor of a pit when it is worked out. That is why the pits are left alone, and it is one reason they do remember. A grave is known by its nearest tor, and mourning is told by the stage of the green. "My father is under Two-Twenty, at moss." Then at cups, when the rosettes arrive and the toads walk in. Then at trunks. After forty years nobody visits, because the place can no longer be found. Only the number remains.

Then there is the other resident. Nobody has seen what eats the moths. The wings are swept from the sills each morning, and the thing has no name beyond "the sill," when it is mentioned at all. Yet most households leave one lamp unshuttered in a back window overnight. For the moths, they say, though nobody wants moths. Nobody keeps dogs. This is the nearest thing.

That may be the truest thing about them. They keep faith with what they cannot account for: the bare tors, the numbers, the lamp in the back window, the gutters hung upside down at the rim. They are not incurious. Asked why, they consider the question honestly, fail to answer it, and carry on. Reasons get overgrown here like everything else, and it is the habits that are kept scraped.

One office holds all of this, and it has nothing to do with clay. The number painter is a woman at present, as it happens. She carries a wire brush, a ledger, and a pot of white slip from the pits, bound with size. Her round takes eleven months, and lichen takes a numeral in under twelve, so the first is going grey as she paints the last. She has never been finished and does not expect to be.

She is the only person who goes regularly into the old ground. The ledger in the works office lists six hundred and twelve tors, and she paints past it. Lichen grows thinner where paint has been, long after the paint is gone, so on a stone lost for a lifetime she scrapes and finds the figure waiting as a paler shape, and fills it in. Out past the forty-year pits, past any digging anyone's grandmother knew of, the figures are still there. The furthest are in no hand she recognises, with sevens crossed and fours left open. Beyond those are marks she cannot read as figures at all. She paints those too, following the edge exactly.

She has never found a tor without a mark. She says she has not gone very far, because the cloud comes back.

To see who lives here, look in the clear hour of mid-afternoon. On every numbered stone in the town a few people stand bareheaded with their faces up, not speaking. The cats are on the kiln shoulders and the swifts are working the rim in silence. Somewhere out in the dark pelt of the forest, too small to see, a woman is tracing a white shape that someone made before her, and someone before that. Then the next bank climbs the cliff. They go down between the stones, the lamps come on in their globes, and whoever's week it is picks up the hoe.
