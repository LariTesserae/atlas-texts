By the obvious count about a thousand animals live in the hollow: a gross each of seven kinds, and an egg. The obvious count is out by a factor of a hundred and forty-four. There is one pika. It has a nick in its left ear and a burr in its fur, and it is at every tussock but one. There is one frog, one snail, one chough, one midge, one trout, and one grey bird that is never seen except in the act of taking. That makes seven residents, and seven is still too many.

None of the seven lives on its own account. The pika cuts sedge and does not eat it; the stem is standing. The trout have the egg and are no fatter. Each is warm and gives nothing off. An animal runs at a loss, and these do not. A fingernail on the snail, a wet hand on the moss, the wind off the choughs on the back of the neck: it is the same shudder at the same pitch each time. They are teeth, switch, belt, stack and winding. Counted honestly, they come to one thing, a hundred and fifty paces long and forty metres high, cast in a mould of black rock and roofed with cloud. Its pulse comes up through the tarn. It makes one egg and eats it, and on the strength of that egg it makes the egg. It has one second to its name, which it has had since the mountain opened and has not finished.

That is the works, and a works is a place. Something lives in it, and the evidence is small and all over the floor.

The burr is the first of it. Saxifrage, lady's mantle, willow, sedge and moss are all that grow here, and none of them makes a burr. It came in over the rim on cloth. It is still here, in a place where nothing can be left behind, so whatever carried it in has not been left behind either.

The rest of the evidence is what is known. It is known that the rock will open a palm. It is known what the snails feel like, all hundred and forty-four, to the limit of a fingernail. Somebody held a wrist under the drop. Somebody shouted and noticed the order things came in. Somebody carried a pebble over the rim and felt in the pocket.

That one went on down the far side, and presumably got home, and has a scar across the palm by now. But nothing can be taken out. What lives in the hollow is the one that was lying where it lay.

It is at the tussock by the lip of the tarn, an arm's length from the flat stone. It kneels, since nothing here stands higher than a knee, and kneeling it is easy to miss. It is the grey-violet of everything else and has no shadow to lift it off the moss. Its coat is soaked through, no heavier for it, and will never go at the elbows. One pocket hangs inside out. The left palm is open along the heel of the thumb, a clean cut with one bead standing in it the way water stands in the lady's mantle. It does not run and it does not close. With no shadows the face has no depth and could be anybody's. The mouth is open on a number.

It is the hollow's gauge. Everything else here does something; this one checks that it has been done. It walks the floor, six or seven metres and a halt, six or seven metres and a halt, touching each left ear in turn. It is also kneeling at its tussock and has not moved. Both are the case.

It counts on the beat. Each number comes back off the walls slightly before it is said, and it says the number anyway, to agree. It has never been wrong. The number it is caught on is always the last, which is itself. It breathes out with the pikas, and one of the hundred and forty-four puffs is larger than the rest and a little higher off the ground.

The works did not need it in order to run. But to say of a place with no wire and no wheel that it is a works all the same is a judgement, and this is the only part the hollow has for making them.

It has tried the other thing a works wants, which is somebody to tell. It raised its voice, and the answer arrived first, in its own.

Its right hand is out over the water, cupped under the edge of the flat stone. This began as a measurement, thumb joint against egg. The egg tips. The egg is in the hand, warm, with a real weight. The fish have it. The egg is on the stone, tipping. All three are the case. The hand goes out on every beat, as the pika's teeth close on every beat, and it has caught the egg every time. Whether it minds cannot be read from the face.

On the rim the marmot is whistling. Of everything in the hollow, this is the only one that has heard that note somewhere else and knows what it would mean. It hears it now. Its lips are on the number, its hand is out, and it does not look up.
