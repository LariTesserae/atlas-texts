People live here, about six thousand of them, though the figure depends on who is counting. The pay office counts hands, the temple counts hearths, and the co-operative counts ration books. The three totals have never agreed.

They mostly see one another from the opposite slope: a diagonal of coloured capes crossing the table, a pole rising over a channel, a figure stepping off a wall top into the tea and back up. At that distance they look like part of the mechanism, and they would not entirely object.

Their people were brought up from the plains the way the soil was, by the load and on contract, five and six generations back. Nobody's ancestors chose the valley. They were sent to it, then they built it, and somewhere in the building the difference between being kept here and keeping the place wore away. Most households still owe something in the co-operative's book. Most could not sleep anywhere else. Often it is the same household.

Up close, the first thing is how they walk. A child here learns on forty centimetres of wall top, so nobody swings their arms and nobody looks at their feet. They can step down backward into the tea without turning. Their calves are hard and their voices are low, because the cloud takes a shout at ten metres. What they have instead is touch and signal:

- A sluice man raps his pole on a penstock, and the powerhouse hears it through the steel before the water arrives.
- A rag on a stake means a bulging wall, but the knot says how bad it is.
- A veranda lamp moved to the left post means someone in that room is ill.

They also listen through their feet. Anyone standing on the factory road can tell which rollers are running and whether the big dryer is on its second fire.

Their manners come from the wall tops. The loaded pass and the unloaded step down, and this has spread into everything. A field officer with empty hands stands in the bushes for a girl with a sack of leaf. To walk any distance carrying nothing feels faintly indecent, so people pick something up, a stone for the gang or a fallen oak branch for the stack. The usual greeting is "What have you got?", and it is polite to answer even when the answer is obvious.

Each trade knows the valley through a different sense.

- **The pluckers** have forefingers and thumbs stained brown-black to the first joint. They know the table by resistance, since a shoot that is ready snaps and one that isn't bends. They talk the whole time, quietly, along the line.
- **The sluice men** carry one shoulder higher than the other. They read water by colour, which runs red from the upper terraces and nearly black from the oldest soil by the river. They can say which slope a squall is sitting on without looking up.
- **The wall gangs** use no string. The courses above and below give them the curve. When a length is relaid, each face stone goes back with its black side out, so a wall rebuilt forty times is still the wall their great-grandfathers built.
- **The loft hands** watch the hygrometer because it is the rule, but they trust a fistful of leaf, closed and then released, and how slowly it opens. They go home smelling of their storey: apple from the withering loft, copper from the fermenting floor, toast from the firing room. A husband and wife on different floors can be told apart in the dark.
- **The linemen** own everything above the grease line on the trestles, where the green film stops. There are fewer of them than of any other trade, and they are looked at differently.

Dryness is the real currency. Nothing is ever fully dry, so the rack space over the dryer vents is allotted by a rota older than the present factory and is argued over like land. The old are given work in the firing rooms when their knees go, and that is understood as a pension. Babies are walked up and down in the warm spill beside the sleeping dogs, and people court there. The dogs belong to no one, are named for the vents they lie under, and are fed by whichever shift is on.

The children run messages and learn the drains. Before dark they walk the back drains in pairs, clearing weep-holes with fingers small enough to fit, and they come home with leeches on their ankles and a count of what they found. A blocked drain reported is worth a sweet from the store. A blocked drain missed is remembered against the family until the next monsoon.

What is theirs and not the valley's is small, and they are particular about it. The veranda rails carry rows of ghee tins and paint tins planted with chillies, a curry-leaf sprig and marigolds for the temple. It is the only growing thing here that is neither crop nor moss. The temple has its wet flag and a priest who also works the weighing shed. They take a private satisfaction in the whistling thrush, whose song wanders like a boy with no errand. They call it the one resident who carries nothing, and nobody disturbs the flume at dawn. The forest on the ridge is kept by rule for the head tanks, and by something else for themselves, as the one place nobody has to tend. Nobody cuts there or goes far in. When the barking deer comes down, the lorry driver dips his lights and waits, loaded as he is, because the deer does not know to step down.

The dead are burned with firewood from the estate stack, entered in the book like any other issue. The ashes go into the river below the last weir and past the tailrace, where the water is no longer sent anywhere.

Some leave. They go out over the pass the way the tea does, and it is said that more than one went the first stretch in a bucket. They write home that the plains are loud and that the ground there lies still in a way they find hard to trust. Some send money, and some come back and say little about it. For the first week they sleep badly, until the ticking of the ropeway works its way back in.

After a while it becomes plain that no one here holds the valley whole. A plucker knows her section bush by bush. A sluice man knows his four boards and the temper of the channel between them. A woman on the lower lines knows forty metres of drain so well that she wakes when it changes note. The valley is known in pieces that overlap by a few metres at each edge, as the stones are passed hand to hand. When a family goes, a stretch of wall or water turns strange for a season, until someone else's attention grows over it.

So what lives here is a people, but also the thing they make between them, a single wakefulness shared out among six thousand heads and never all asleep at once. In every room on the lines someone is awake or sleeping lightly. A lamp goes along a flume at two in the morning. The night loft hand closes a storey of louvres against the cloud.

It can be seen whole only when the ropeway stops. The fifth rule says the cable halts for a man on the line, and the phrase covers both a lineman riding out to free a grip and the other thing. The ticking ceases. The valley has not been without it for months, and the quiet reaches people the way a sound would. Pluckers straighten up and sluice men hold their poles. Sleepers wake in the lines and come out onto the verandas. All along both slopes, faces turn up toward one man none of them can see, somewhere in the grey between two trestles, and they count.

Then a grip passes over a sheave. Drops patter from the cable, and everyone bends again to what they were carrying.
