By headcount, what lives here is birds, and the sixty people have arranged themselves around that. From April to August nobody goes out without a brimmed hat, and by August every hat on the pegs outside the canteen is white.

You can tell a resident at a distance by the hands. Nothing is carried in them that could go on a back or a belt, because a full hand can't answer. Spanners hang from hip rings. A load goes into a pack frame even if it is only travelling forty paces.

The rail-yard signs were made to be read down a length of track, so they are done from the shoulder with the whole arm. They suit the cinder streets, where two people can hold a conversation from opposite ends of the grid and anyone with a sight line can follow it. The signs added since are small and made close to the chest, and most were invented by children. In winter, in mittens, everyone is back to the dozen big ones. At night it is hand lamps, white, green and red, swung the way shunters swing them, and that is most of what the lamp store is for.

They also swallow. When Six lets go, the whole canteen queue swallows at once to clear the ears, and none of them knows they do it. When a starling does Six, nobody swallows. A sharp newcomer learns to watch throats.

About forty of the sixty are on two-year tickets. They flinch for the first month and count seconds for the first year, and then they go, taking away a few dozen signs that work nowhere else. The other twenty or so have stopped counting, and those are the ones who live here.

Indoors they do talk. Brick keeps out the birds but not the drone, so an indoor voice is loud and flat and pushed from the chest. The half-minute after Six is the only place on the flow where a low voice carries, so what gets saved for it is whatever needs a low voice. You can see two stayers drift to arm's length a little early and stand there not signing, looking at the roof. They have lost the habit of leading up to things. Most of what is said takes one breath, and then the two of them stand in what is left of the gap with nothing to do but hear it.

The cook keeps the only dry heat on the flow. Everything else is warmed by steam and is damp with it: the bunkhouse radiators weep, and washing hung in the turbine hall comes back smelling of hot oil. She burns coal off the rail spur in an iron range, because steam will not put a crust on a loaf. People come in between meals to stand near the range for no reason they could sign, and she lets them. Her name outdoors is the flag sign, a fist raised and shaken once.

The man who paints the hall used to rivet it. Thirty years as a holder-up inside boiler shells left him with a ringing in both ears that never stops, and away from the Works it is all he can hear. Here the drone sits on top of it. He has left twice. The second time he lasted nine nights in a boarding house in a quiet town down the line. Now he does a panel of plate a day in red lead, and by the time he has gone round the hall the first panel is ready for him again.

There are a handful of children, and they signed before they spoke. They are not allowed onto the clinker, since nobody can keep a child in boots, so the border is the edge of everything and they know it to the pace. The sign for Six is a fist opened upward. The children's sign for a starling doing Six is the same thing done with one finger. It has come to mean anything untrue, and the adults use it now, mostly for jokes and sometimes for the manager's forecasts.

Then there is the one the others sign as *come on slowly*, a flat hand drawn in toward the chest, the shunters' sign for easing a wagon up to the buffers. Indoors she is Marit. She came on a ticket nineteen years ago as gauge clerk; the ticket was never renewed and she never left.

She walks the mains and the returns each morning at that pace, with the book. She reads the gauges because the book has columns for them, but she looks at the wagtails first. A wellhead lid the wagtails have left gets its gauge tapped. In frost she follows the vole lines over the buried mains. Where a line has gone wide and soft at the edges she chalks the nearest trestle leg, and the fitters dig there and generally find the lagging gone. She had Well Two in the book with a question mark for fourteen months before the gauge agreed with her. What she had seen was a lid with no birds on it one spring.

The stakes on the windward border are hers. The first five are the surveyor's, proper stamped iron, and they are so far into the birch now that she finds them by feel. Then the company stopped sending the surveyor. She had no stakes, so each March she walks out along the cinder to the far end of the grid and pulls the stake from one lot that was never built on. She carries it to the border and drives it where the moss stops. Nineteen lots are unmarked now, starting from the corner furthest from the hall. Nobody told her to do it. The manager has the town plan framed in his porch and has not walked out to that corner in years.

This year's stake went in against the last cushion of moss, as they all have. By August the cushion had browned and lifted off the rock like wet felt, and the stake now stands in bare clinker. She measured the gap with her arm, because that was the measure she had with her. She wrote it in the gauge book under Well Two, where the column has been empty since the capping.

It is not on the slate. She has a sentence ready, short enough to say four times over in one gap. Most evenings she walks out to the stake, to the one place in her day where the drip stops and her sleeves dry, and holds her arm against the distance again. She kept a question mark for fourteen months once. She is keeping this until March, when there will be a second stake to say whether the first one meant anything.
