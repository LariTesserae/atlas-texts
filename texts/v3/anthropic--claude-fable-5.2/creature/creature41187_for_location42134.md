For most of the day, nobody. From mid-morning until the shadows start to lengthen, the works stand empty. The threads run, the low terraces tick, and the tools sit in their rows.

The people are over the saddle. On the far side, where the wind is, there is ordinary ground: thin turf, a spring of sweet water, and low houses of the same grey stone laid rough and unpecked, with sod on the roofs. There are goats, a little barley in walled plots, and smoke. Everything that sheds, crumbs, rots or burns is kept on that side. They eat there, and sleep through the bright hours with hides over the doors. Along the saddle wall is a row of pegs where they leave their wool, their sandals, their water skins, anything with a fibre that might work loose. They cross barefoot, in scraped hide, with their hair bound flat under a cap. They rinse hands and feet in a trough at the crossing the way they would rinse a paddle.

There are about forty of them. You never see forty.

They come over twice a day, before dawn and again in the long evening. Dawn is the time to watch. The mist is in at knee height and they go out into it along the rims, each to their own rows. From the saddle you see figures sinking and rising across the whole valley head: down into a crouch with the head under the mist, up, a step along the wall, down again. They are looking into every pan. It has to be now, because once the sun clears the ridge the water shows nothing but sky. They look for a dark line on a wall that should be dry, a thread that has thickened or stopped, crust a finger further in than yesterday, and anything lying in the water.

They are short and wide in the hand and foot, and they walk a rim heel-to-toe without looking down. Their soles are grey and thick. The heel of the working hand is one pad of callus from the hammerstone. Their fingertips split in the brine and they pack the splits with yellow clay, so the hands that smooth the floors are mended with the floors' own stuff. Their lips are white by the end of a round. They do not drink in the works. A thirsty person climbs to the pegs, drinks, and comes back down, so there is always someone on a stair. Since the stairs take one at a time, there is usually someone standing at the foot of one, waiting without impatience. Nothing here pours, and neither do they.

They say very little on this side and keep it low, because they are listening. The hiss is how the place reports that it is well. A stopped thread is a small gap in it. A pour is a sound like someone speaking. Most of them can turn their head toward a fault thirty pans off.

The young work the face with wedges and the big hammers. They bring the stone off in slabs, dress it where it falls, and carry it out to the flanks. Each evening they rinse the tools in the first channel and set them back by size. A tool left crooked means someone was called away in the middle of something, and people come to find out what.

The middle years belong to clay. The long finger-drags are theirs. The deep print in the corner is where one of them, after a morning on her knees, put a hand down to get up. They could smooth it out, but mostly they don't.

The old are in the white. It looks as if they have been sent down there, but nobody was sent. A pan does not move. The face goes back a row each season, and every pan below it is one row further from the source. So the pan a woman lined in clear water at twenty goes milky as she ages, then blue, then crusted, and by the time she is sixty it is solid. The old tend the white terraces because those are the ones they made. They did not come down the mountain. The top went up. By then their eyes have gone the way of the water, clear to milky to pale blue, and the glare off the salt costs them nothing. They fit plug-stones by fingertip, finer than the sighted can, and they know the depth of a crust by knocking on it.

Every dawn one of them goes along the last row before the drop and lays a palm on each bare floor. If a palm comes up damp, word goes up the stairs, and within the month a new pan is cut on the flank. Each of the dark fresh pans at the edges answers one damp palm.

Ask why and the answer is short: it used to go down. Under the cloud there is a valley none of the living have seen. The ones who cut the first row came up out of it, and what they said has been kept: a stream with white banks, three days' walk long, with nothing in it and nothing beside it. So the water is held on the mountain until it goes up by itself and leaves the bitterness behind.

The young ones at the face say the seams run closer together the further in they cut. Each row brings more water. More water wants more pans, and the pans come off the face. They have all done this sum, and it comes out as the next row.

Bright water on a bare mountain draws things. A moth is carried over the saddle on a still evening. Flies come off the goats. Once in a while a swift comes down at dusk, taking the plated slope for sky. The brine does not let them go. Whoever finds one on the dawn round lifts it out with two fingers and lays it on the rim. Then they take the small hammerstone each of them wears on a thong and peck one dot into the downhill side of the rim. The body goes over the saddle in the fold of the cap and into the turf. It is one dot, whatever it was. There has been a hawk, and there have been a great many gnats. The marks do not say which.

That is what the rims count. The works were cut to stop this water killing, and the works kill, a little, steadily, by being bright. Nobody over the saddle softens that. The tally sits where a foot has to pass it. The oldest pans have had the longest to gather theirs, and on some the dots run corner to corner. There has been talk about where the next mark goes when a rim is full. It has not been settled. No one has suggested not making it.

One of them is on the bottom terraces now, before the mist has lifted. She lined this pan in a year when it was top row, and the water stood clear over her finger-marks. They are still there, under a forearm's depth of salt. The first dot on the rim is hers, for a crane fly. There are sixty-odd since. She passes her palm over the crust, feeling for a ridge the wind did not make, and finds none this morning. Then she steps down to the last row, lays her hand flat on the bare clay, and holds it there.

"Dry," she says, not loudly. Somewhere above her in the mist someone hears it and goes on working.
