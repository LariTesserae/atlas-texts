For a long while the answer seems to be lizards. A city this loud ought to show its people, and the eye goes hunting for doorways and smoke and finds six pale animals in a row.

Then small things. The scrapers on the ledge by the kilns have no dust on them. One ladder has a pale new lashing among the horn-colored old ones. The cuts on the resin stems, where the amber beads stand, are all at the height of a hand.

Then one of them moves, and she has been in plain view the whole time, standing in a stripe of green halfway down the ramp. She is rubbed from scalp to heel with the grey bloom off the leaves, and at noon she glares exactly as they do.

There are about forty of them, in a city of several hundred jars. They are lean, slow-moving, dry-skinned people who keep to the gardens. They stand, sit, and sleep along the same lines the lizards use, and their routes ignore the streets and follow the quiet. When a bare band has to be crossed they cross it fast, one hand flat on the breastbone. They drink from the plants, a stem cut at dawn and held to the mouth. Nothing is ever poured.

Their ears are stopped. Each ear holds a plug of leaf wax capped with a bead of amber, fitted in the first days of life and remade as the head grows. Nobody speaks. They talk with their thumbs: one takes the other's forearm and presses along it in rows, hard or light, quick or slow. It is the same motion that bonds a coil to the coil beneath.

They live in the kilns. A kiln is a jar with a stoke-hole knocked through its side, and a jar with a hole in its side cannot sing. These are the only mute vessels in the city and the only ones with anything in them: a coil of cord, a hammerstone, a heap of grey fibre, a sleeper. Before a firing everything is carried out. Afterward the ash is swept and everything is carried back in, and everything they own is black.

The singing jars were never houses. Each one is a person.

A jar is made once in a life, by a child of six or seven and one adult. The clay is dug from under the basin floor and kneaded with pounded stem-pith in place of water, so it comes to the hand warm and smelling of turpentine. The child stands inside and the wall goes up around it. The adult lays each coil and presses it down from outside. The child presses from inside, at the same spot. After the first day they cannot see each other. On a finished jar, every thumbprint on the outer wall has a smaller one behind it.

When the shoulder has closed over and the neck is drawn in, the child comes out on a knotted cord: one arm, then the head, then the other shoulder, turned sideways. The mouth is finished to the width of that head, on that day.

The note is not chosen for the child. The adults find it in the basin. The husks there turn with the whole chord, and when an old voice has gone, the drift turns lopsided, and somewhere up the ramp a stripe of garden is left out in the roar. The new jar is shaped to put the stillness back. A child is given whatever note the city has lost, and it is the nearest thing to a name that anyone here has. No one has ever said one.

It is made a little flat, on purpose. The grit will grind the lip and bring the note up, and a jar made true would be sharp before its maker was grown.

After that the child may go inside. At noon, when there is no other shade, the children are in their jars, curled on the floors. In there the note is not heard. It is a slow grip, closing and opening on the whole body. A child takes up room, so the jar sings a hair high while it is occupied. An adult who lays a palm on the neighboring jar can feel the two beating against each other, and knows the child is in there and roughly how much it has grown. Nothing may be left behind. A bead would rattle. Bedding would eat the voice. The cord comes out last.

This lasts until the shoulders stop passing. There is a particular day. After it the person sleeps in the kilns and stands in the gardens with the others, and the jar goes on without them.

It goes on a long time, far longer than its maker. Most of the jars in the city belong to people long dead. A body is carried up the ladders and out onto the salt, and little is made of it. The jar is left to sing. The newer ones stand high and the old ones low, and the pipes carry breath down from one to the other. A jar is left until the wind has flared it past sounding, or a rosette has closed it, and no one here has ever pulled a plant from a neck. Then it is broken with hammerstones, crushed, and packed into the terrace where the next will stand. That is the funeral, and it may come a hundred years after the death.

So the forty are the smaller part of what lives here. The larger part is the chord, several hundred people with no bodies, kept exactly as loud as the living. The forty dig for it and build for it, and each will join it in the end. They do not tune it. The plants and the grit do that.

At dusk, when the wind falters and the voices go out from the top down, the forty stop where they are. When the last drone at the bottom quits, they take the amber out of their ears.

For those few minutes they can hear the resin ticking, the lizards' feet, and one another breathing. Then, one at a time, not together and not loudly, each of them hums. They do it under the breath, the way a tongue checks a tooth. Each is humming the note they slept in, which went into the bones early and stayed.

The old hum flat. They are humming the jar as it was when they last fit inside it, and the grit has had fifty years at the lip since. The older the person, the wider the gap, and the oldest are most of a tone below themselves. The dead make no sound at all. For these minutes it is possible to hear how small the living part of the city is, and how full of holes.

Then the air begins to climb out of the pit. The small jar near the rim catches it. It was made this year, a little flat, by a girl who is sitting on the terrace beneath it with her stoppers in her fist, humming. She is on the note exactly, and she is the only one in the city who is.

The others are already pressing the amber back in when the rest of the jars join.
