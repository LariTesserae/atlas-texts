The first answer is the salamanders, and it is close.

Take the one nine jars out from the spring. She lies with her chin in a dip on the rim that other chins made. Her skin is thin enough that yesterday's shrimp shows through her flank as a grey thread. She has no eyes, and the hall comes to her anyway. The cooled water creeping back along the floor passes through the low tier last, so everything shed in a day, whether a moult, a hatching, or a death three stacks off, arrives at her jar mouth as a taste. Once a day a shrimp that starts across her rim does not reach the other side. That is the movement.

She is about seventy. She has been in this jar eleven years, and she was not hatched for it. A hand going jar to jar along the low tier would find them graded, smallest toward the walls and largest toward the warmth. She began as an egg stuck inside a fist-sized jar in the top tier, by the cold wall. She spent a decade at the length of a thumb among the shrimp broods, then came down, and she has been travelling inward since, one jar at a time, whenever a death ahead of her opened the line.

When the animal nearest the spring dies, the shrimp have it clean in three days. Over the following season every salamander in the row shifts one jar inward, into a polish slightly loose on it, and grows until it fits. Nothing is replaced. The vacancy travels outward along the row to the wall, where it ends as an empty jar the size of a fist and, eventually, an egg. A jar belongs to a size, and the animals pass through the sizes. The low tier is better counted as one line than as forty animals.

The order has a plainer cause than it seems. For a long time hands came down the stair and took things. They took what was easy: the fish idling across the stacks within reach of the steps, the salamander lying out on open floor. What kept to its lane or its jar was passed over, and bred. No one needs to have meant it. After enough lifetimes, everything left is descended from the ones a hand did not close on. The hands stopped so long ago that nothing here fears one, and the shrimp will climb a lowered wrist. The fear went. The keeping to place stayed.

The line of salamanders ends at the largest open jar, whose mouth gives onto the bowl. The animal in it is the oldest in the hall, and she has spent twenty years facing the sealed jar from an arm's length away. It is the next size up. There is no move for her to make. No salamander has ever grown to what that jar would hold, which is a body the height of the people who carried the cobbles, with the knees drawn to the chest and the head put down on them.

That is what it holds. The hall was sized throughout by one kind of body, in palms and fingers and forearms. The largest jar, nearest the warmth, was made for the largest animal that used the room. Others seated the lid and puddled the clay, went up the ramp while it was still steps, and closed the door from the far side.

The hall draws one distinction, between a jar that is held and a jar that is vacant, and this one has never come vacant. So the shrimp go round it. The fish leave the water above it alone, as they leave the stacks alone. The line of salamanders stops short of it and waits behind someone who is not going to die. By the only reckoning used here, the one in the jar still lives in the hall. It has been longest in residence and has the best place, and it faces outward, which for a jar set upright means facing the lid.

The oldest salamander does not behave as though any of this matters. When she dies the shrimp will take their three days, and through the season the line will come in by one. The next will settle its chin in the dip and face the same way.
