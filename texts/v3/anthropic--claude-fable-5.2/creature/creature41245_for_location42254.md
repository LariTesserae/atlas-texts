Most people who climb the escarpment meet it first as dung.

The scat lies on the whalebacks and nowhere else. It is tar-dark and twisted, about a finger long, felted with grey fur and set with bones: frog thighs as fine as fish ribs, the keel of a nestling, the orange front teeth of a young whistler. It is always on the highest part of the bare stone, often against the foot of a cairn. It has been placed. On a table with nothing on it to mark, the animal uses the same few knuckles of rock that the people use, and for much the same purpose.

Beyond that there are few sightings, and they do not agree. Something poured from one pore into another across a hand's width of open hide. Something lay along the trampled margin of a black pool at first light and was gone, without having seemed to move, when the cloud slid off it. A woman who came up too early one year found a line of prints crossing a whaleback in new snow, small and paired, the pairs set far apart, and she stood and watched them melt. Those who have seen the animal itself say it is an arm and a half long and the same thickness from end to end, with no neck or shoulder or hip to interrupt it, and legs so short that it seems to travel by some other means. Some say it is the tan of the dead stems. Others say it is darker, and wet-looking.

There is one pelt. It hangs inside a shelter in the oldest-used part of the plateau and has hung there longer than any skin in the roof above it. Resin smoke has turned it black, so it settles nothing about colour. It does show the rest. The fur is short and dense and gummed flat, like a seal's. The eyes were small. Where the ears should be there is a slit on each side, with a lid of skin to close it. The whiskers are still in place, stiff as bristle and longer than the head is wide, and there are more on the chin and on the backs of the forelegs.

The people have a sign for it. Quiet is a flat hand pressed downward. The falcon is the same hand carried across the body at arm's length. For this animal they draw the tip of the smallest finger slowly along the opposite palm.

Everything else has to be worked out from what the ground does.

It lives its whole life inside, in the warm straw of the plant's past, and it has no need to dig. Slumping has made the galleries, and it is narrow enough to use all of them, including the pipes where the frogs sit. Wherever it goes, they stop. Nobody knows whether they smell it or feel its weight come along the stems, but the ones within a few lengths of it shut their throats and wait. So the animal moves through the loudest place there is inside a quiet of its own. The noise stands off from it on every side at a short, constant distance, giving way in front and closing in behind. It steers by the draughts in the chimneys and the feel of the walls. It finds whistlers and birds by their warmth and their smell.

The frogs are harder. A frog that has stopped calling is the temperature of the gallery and the colour of the gallery, and it does not move. This is why some of the small silences wander and others go out. A wandering one is the animal travelling. One that goes out is the animal lying down. It puts its chin on the straw, closes the lids over its ears, and stays. After a while the frogs forget. One starts, then the one beside it, and the note closes over the place where the hunter is. Then it is lying in the full din with a frog knocking a whisker's length away, and it takes it. People who have spent many summers here do not worry about a patch of quiet passing under their feet. They notice when one fills in.

At dusk it leaves the frogs alone. While the air above is solid with wings, it works the necks of the burrows from below, where the incoming birds are heavy with the day's feeding and the nestlings are waiting. It kills more than it eats. A man reaching into a collapsed pore after a dropped bone hook once brought up a handful of birds instead, a dozen of them, dry and almost weightless, each bitten once through the back of the skull. The resin in the air had kept them. There are thought to be larders like this all through the hide, and nobody knows how old the deepest are.

They live singly. Each keeps a tract around one or two whalebacks, and since calling is useless here, they leave word for one another on the stone. In the last warm month a second, smaller scat sometimes appears beside the first. That is all anyone has seen of how they meet.

The young are born inside, two or three together, in a fist-shaped chamber lined with whistler fur and down. They are born into their mother's quiet. When she goes out to hunt, the knocking comes in over them, and when it stops, she is back. For every other creature on the plateau, quiet means that something is coming to kill it. For these it means milk, and later meat. When they are grown, each goes off carrying a quiet of its own.

One of them is known. Its tract takes in the whaleback where the pelt hangs, and it has been there longer than the youngest of the adults who summer at that hearth. Its scat is at the foot of the cairn most mornings. Now and then a hair snare at the edge of the rock is found drawn shut on a tuft of grey fur and nothing else. In the evening the people at that hearth put the bird guts out on the stone, and they are gone by morning. Nobody has ever signed whether this is payment or housekeeping.

At night, when the fire is down to its orange point, the people lie on the bare rock and the knocking comes up into them through hip and shoulder. Some nights a part of it thins. Along one edge of the whaleback, where the hide laps against the stone, the note drops away, and the gap moves slowly round the rim of the rock, under the foot of the cairn and past the wall of the shelter. Whoever is awake can follow it with their eyes shut. Then it goes out. The animal has either moved on beyond feeling or lain down, and there is no telling which from above. Some time later there is one frog fewer under that edge of the hide, or there is not. Either way the knocking is whole again, as far as anyone lying on the stone can tell.
