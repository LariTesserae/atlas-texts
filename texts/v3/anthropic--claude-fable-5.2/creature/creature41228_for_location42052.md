The vine lives there. Nothing else does.

The town does a great deal that looks like living. It draws breath every afternoon and hums in its jars. A door knocks in the eastern quarter as if someone wanted in. The flame stands up and roars on schedule, and the pitch moves down the lanes with what could pass for purpose. All of that is heat, wind, and pressure in the rock.

The dust is the record, and it holds seed-pocks and the drag-lines of fallen leaf pads and no other mark. Whoever polished the quern, pressed the tally marks into the trough rims, stamped the jar plugs, and bound the adze left their things in good order and is not there. No one empties the pits or tends the fire. Nothing small has moved in after them either, since nothing gnaws and nothing flies.

That leaves the vine, a sealed and patient plant. In a place that has never been wet it keeps everything behind wax and resin. Its leaves are small, black, and waxy, its sap closes a split stem within a day, and its pods are as hard as fired clay.

It feeds at two ends. Its roots lie in the troughs, in the pitch, and its leaves go to the roof-holes. Its whole shape is the shortest line between those two things. It runs thirty paces of bare grey stem along a wall, wasting no leaf on the dusk, then puts up a tuft where there is a rod of light to climb toward.

It is greedy, and it pays for that on a short cycle. A shoot reaches a hole, spreads one pad across it, and takes the entire rod, so the room below goes dark. Within days the sun has cooked the pad brittle and it falls in pieces. By then another shoot is at another hole. The shifting of the dim patches from week to week is the only way to see the vine move.

Over a longer span it has become the building. It was there when the people were, as something they used. The cord on the peg is its fibre, and so is the binding on the adze. Now the arrangement runs the other way. Its stems are the lintels where the old ones failed, and its roots hold the bricks where the mortar has gone to powder. A good part of the town stands because the vine is in it.

It has no company of any kind. Nothing eats it and nothing carries its seed. The heat splits the pods, and the seeds skitter and lie where they stop. On that dry floor most of them presumably stay seeds. It got into every lane somehow, though whether it is one plant or several hundred could only be told by tracing stems through the walls.

It keeps one limit. At the five doorways of the round room the stems arrive and turn back. The description does not say why, but a plant rooted in pitch and full of oil has good reason to stay away from the flame.

At night it does what the pitch does. The troughs stiffen around its roots and hold them until noon.
