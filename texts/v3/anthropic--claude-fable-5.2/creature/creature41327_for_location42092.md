Almost everything in the pan is either rooted or in transit. The fan-bearers stand where they settled. The walkers pour, the swimmers seethe, the fliers keep their layers. One animal here has an address, and has it because it makes the address itself.

The drummer is about the size of a sow, and lower. Its back is a dome of overlapping plates, grey as the stone and running with the same sweat. In the rust-light it is one more rounded thing on a pavement of rounded things. Four short legs splay from under the dome and do very little walking. Two forelimbs lie along the front, long for the body, jointed once, each ending in a knob of lime as dense as a river cobble.

It is natural to start with the knobs. They grow from the base and never stop, and the stone wears them down about as fast as they come in. A drummer that quit beating would, within a few dozen slackenings of the wind, carry clubs too heavy to lift. So it does not quit. It beats while it feeds, and it beats in whatever it has in place of sleep, slower, with one limb.

But the knobs are the lesser half of the animal. The greater half is underneath. The belly is a single bare plate, slightly dished, and the four legs exist to keep it pressed flat to the slab. This is what it hears with. It has no organ for sound in air, because under the chord such an organ would be a wound. A row of pits along one flank registers the rust on the rim and nothing else, which tells it which way it is facing. Everything else it knows comes up through the stone.

What comes up is its own stroke and the slab's answer, and the shape of that answer tells it whether anything is standing on its stone. The humming hunter can arrive inside a patch of sound that seems whole, but it cannot put weight on a ringing slab without flattening the ring. The drummer feels the note go dull at the first foot. Then there are the neighbours. Each nearby slab reaches it twice, once through the rock, almost at once and very clean, and again when the airborne sound arrives late and sets its own slab faintly shivering. The gap between the two is the distance.

A slab crowded with fan-stalks will not ring, so the drummer weeds. Any young fan-bearer that sets its foot in the slab's cracks is pulled and eaten before it can raise a comb. A drummer's slab is therefore a bare, pale floor in the fan-field, and dry at the centre, because the work keeps the animal a little warmer than the rock and the stone beneath it does not sweat. The walkers' troughs bend to pass the edge of every such floor, since the walkers travel from note to note. The drummer does not hunt. Between strokes it reaches into the trough, lifts one walker out of the river, and the river closes. The shell goes over the side into the fissure.

That would be enough to explain the animal as a toll-keeper on a road it maintains without knowing it. But the note is not really the drummer's. The note belongs to the slab, to its thickness and span and the depth of the hollow under it. A hundred animals in succession have sat in the same saddle and made the same pitch. The saddle deepens, the slab thins, and the note has been sinking by some amount too small for any one life to register. Everything in the pan learns the map fresh and never knows it has drifted.

What belongs to the drummer is the time. One beats steady fours. One beats three and rests. One beats a long, limping figure that takes forty strokes to come round. The walkers do not care about this, but the other drummers do. Through the rock, pitch says where and rhythm says who.

The rhythm is learned under the slab. At one edge of every ringing slab is a crack that the drummer keeps clear, and through it the eggs go down into the hollow. The young hatch soft and limbless and fasten to the underside of the slab by the belly-plate, hanging from the ceiling of the loudest room in the pan. They are fed by what the fissure water carries in. For the whole of their growing they are beaten on from above by one pitch, in one pattern. When their knobs come in, they begin to tap the ceiling from below, in time. The drummer above feels this, a small late echo inside its own stroke.

When they are too big for the hollow they leave by the fissures, during a slack, and what they carry differs from what they leave. The pitch stays behind, because it was only ever a place. The rhythm goes with them, so lineages move across the pan while the notes stay put. Every drummer on every slab is beating its mother's time on a stranger's stone.

Most never get a slab. They live in the fissures, mute, coming up in the slackenings with the other hidden things to eat the folded fans. They knock on whatever stone they cross, testing, and solid stone gives back a thud that carries nowhere. An animal built to be a landmark, without a slab, is nobody at all. So they listen through their bellies for a rhythm that has begun to fray, and they move toward it a little more each slack.

There is a slab a third of the way in from the lee rim, low-pitched, with a saddle worn a hand deep. The drummer on it has plates chipped along the left side, and its left knob is worn flat on one face. Its time is two close strokes, a gap, one stroke, and a longer gap. The longer gap has been getting longer. Under the slab the brood taps along, a shade behind. For the last three slackenings there has also been a knock on the dead stone just past the weeded margin, a patient four that it does not recognise.

When the wind fails and the chord drops out, the rock goes clear, and for that short while the drummer can hear past its neighbours to the whole pan, every slab at once, each in its place. Far toward the windward rim, on a high thin note it has never been near, something is beating two close strokes, a gap, and one.

It does not turn, since its pits face only the rust. It lifts a walker from the trough, drops the shell, and keeps its own time against that other one until the thin note starts at the edge, the fans open, and the chord closes over the rock again.
