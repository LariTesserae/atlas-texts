Seen from above, it is a hairline. A swimmer lying in a lane and looking straight down will miss it, or take it for a hair on the surface. It shows only to someone who puts their head under and looks sideways, out of the quiet toward the wall of noise. There, an arm's length off, hangs a band of water as deep as a hand is wide, in which the columns beyond do not quite meet themselves. Each hexagon edge is carried a finger out of true as it crosses the band, the way a window-bar jumps in old glass. The first guess is a layer of colder water. The second is the cable, somehow come up off the floor. Then a thickening travels along the band from one end of sight to the other, and it is an animal.

It is a strap of clear flesh hung on edge. It is thinner than a finger, and it has no fins, no scales, and no colour at all unless it has fed. It hangs in the full roar, where an amphipod lasts a few seconds. The amphipod carries a grain of lime in a sac at the base of each feeler to tell it which way is down, and outside the lane that grain is shaken until the animal goes slack. The fish have given up their ear-stones and still keep to the quiet. The ribbon has nothing of the kind anywhere in it: no bone, no shell, no grain, no bubble. It is the same weight as the water all the way through, and the sound passes through it as through more water and finds nothing to move. It is doubtful the ribbon knows there is a sound.

What it knows is light. Under the stippled surface the daylight comes down broken into a fine shiver. Under the lane it comes down whole, in a stripe. A row of pigment pits along the ribbon's upper margin, each smaller than a pinprick, is enough to tell one from the other, and the ribbon keeps the clean stripe at a fixed distance on one side.

It appears to patrol. A swelling starts near the front and runs back along the strap at walking pace, and the eye follows the swelling and reports a thing moving down the lane edge. Pick a column on the floor and watch the body against it. The body stays. The sea drifts toward the lip here at a rate that has not changed, the wave drives the ribbon up-drift at the same rate, and the two cancel. A ribbon hangs over the same row of hexagons for years.

The face turned to the lane is tacky. An amphipod that wanders over the boundary goes slack within its own length, fetches up against that face, and stays there. Along the lower margin runs a groove lined with something finer than hair, and the groove carries the catch forward to a slit near the front end that serves as a mouth. An amphipod taken near the tail arrives days later. A ribbon that has fed shows its gut as a grey pencil line down the middle of the glass, a second thread beside the lane's own, much thinner.

The whole lane-ward face is the net, so there is no advantage in being short. Thirty metres is ordinary. Far up the basin, where the drift is weakest, a swimmer can keep pace beside a single ribbon for four or five hundred metres before it ends.

The front end is blunt and closed, like a thumb. The hind end is square, in every ribbon that has been looked at. It is a cut that has healed.

The floor here is all edges, and none has ever dulled. When a ribbon reaches a certain length, it lets its hind part sink until it lies across the column-tops, and it goes on passing its wave. Between the wave and the drift, the flesh is drawn back and forth over a rim of basalt or a blade of obsidian, and in a day or so it parts. The forward piece rises and hangs as before. The hind piece goes lipward with the drift, turning over slowly along the lane edge. In that time it closes one end into a thumb and opens a slit behind it. Where it comes to bare edge it starts a wave of its own, and stops.

Nobody has found an egg, or seen two ribbons touch. Every ribbon on a lane edge is the cast tail of one farther up, which was the cast tail of another. When the edge is full, the pieces find no bare place and keep going, past the last station, past the hall or through it, and over with the sheet. Being clear, they cannot be seen to go.

The builders stood their caissons astride the longest null, and the turbine intakes in the legs open up-drift, just outside the quiet, which is where a ribbon's tail trails. A tail goes into an intake about as often as one would expect. The rotor does in a moment what obsidian takes a day over, and does it many times. Lipward of each station the lane edge is hung for a hundred metres with short ribbons, finger-lengths and arm-lengths, each with a square end and a new thumb and a small wave already running. These are the only short ones in the sea. On the first three stations out from the hall, someone bolted a grating across each intake. From the fourth station on there is none.

When a piece goes through, the rotor checks, and the drum in the gauge-house stops for a few seconds with the pen resting on it. In the labelled rolls this shows as a point on the straight line where the ink has spread a little. There are a good many such points. None of the careful hands wrote anything beside them.

Follow the longest null the other way, up-drift, to where it meets the back scarp and the collar of ice. The ribbon hanging there has none above it. Its front end is the one it started with, and it is the only piece on that edge that was never a tail. It is not especially long, having shed most of what it has grown. Its pits are turned to the stripe of whole light, there is a grey line in it, and its wave goes back along it at the rate the sea sets. From there to the lip is six hundred kilometres of the same animal, with gaps.
