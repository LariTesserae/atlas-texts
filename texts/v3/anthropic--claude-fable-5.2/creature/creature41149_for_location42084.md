Start by counting, because the place invites it. There are two hundred and twenty-four petrels, half of them present at any hour of daylight, and eighty-odd pairs of gulls. The bats run to thousands, but nobody has counted them, and the pelt does not come apart into animals until it is in the air. There are two falcons. There is one fox, the only animal in the count with nobody to relieve it.

The count is accurate and it misleads, because most of what it counts lives somewhere else. Take the petrel in the fourth row of the plate, ninth tube from the left. She has sat at the back of the dark for three days without food or water, and she will sit two more before her mate comes in. She is about twenty years old, older than the fox and either falcon. In those years she has spent a few hundred nights on land, all of them in this tube, and she is bad at land. Her legs will not hold her upright. She gets about on her shanks with her wings half open, like something dragging itself clear of an accident. Everything she is good at is over the horizon, where she patters on the faces of waves and picks oil from the surface film. She comes here for the one thing the sea will not allow, which is to put an egg down and find it where she left it.

The gulls are the same, on a longer and angrier shift. So are the bats, whose real country is the air above a river none of them has seen by daylight. They carry their living in and carry themselves out again, and what they drop between is the hollow's entire income.

The ones that live here are the ones that cannot leave. A soft tick in the dust under the roost is eyeless, dust-coloured and flat as a lentil husk. It feeds for twenty minutes on a warm foot, lets go, and resumes waiting, and it may eat six times in fifteen years. The darkling beetle that climbs the rivet line before dawn has made that climb every morning of its adult life, and has never been farther than the length of the drum from where it hatched. A gecko holds a yard of rivets and barks if another sets a foot on it. A lizard's range is a few squares of gulls, with the gulls on them. The scorpions in the clinker do not come out for the dusk, since they have no need to. These are the residents. The first rule still covers them, because each descends from a stowaway: a tick in bat fur, a beetle egg in breast-down. The geckos probably came in a crate of firebrick, which makes theirs the only line in the hollow that was here when the boiler was lit.

The dead have longer tenure than any of them. Nothing rots, so nothing leaves that way. Behind the hen in the ninth tube is a foot of packed feather and bone, and it is in order. First comes last year's chick, husked and hollowed. Behind it is an addled egg, and behind that an adult that came in with a broken wing before she was hatched. She sits with her tail against it, and it keeps the draught off. Under the bats the terraces go down the same way. At the bottom, a finger thick, the first season's layer lies directly on boiler scale. The last thing the works left and the first thing the tenants made are pressed together with nothing between. By weight, most of what is in the boiler is former occupants.

Then there are things with no body at all, which outlast everything that has one. The bats use the firebox door. The manhole is nearer the roost and stands open, and no living bat knows what is wrong with it. Perhaps a falcon once learned to sit on its rim. Perhaps the first bat in came by the firebox, and every bat since has followed the one in front. The reason is gone and the rule is in perfect health. The gulls' grid is argued out afresh each season by birds who took no part in the last argument, and it comes out to the same squares. The petrels arrive when the light has thinned to exactly the point at which a falcon begins to miss. No animal holds these habits, and all of them obey.

The oldest of these is the chord, and it is older than the tenancy. It began the day the smokebox door came off and the wind first crossed the tubes. For some years after that, the boiler hummed in its hollow to the stones. The note is made on the spot, out of wind and iron. Only it and the dew can claim that, and the dew is gone in the first hour of light.

It cannot be proved, but the likeliest account of how the place began is this. Petrels looking for somewhere to breed do it at night and largely by ear. They quarter the dark ground listening for other petrels, on the fair assumption that where a colony is purring there are holes worth having. A hundred and twelve pipes sounding together on a night wind are not a colony. To a young bird crossing the plain in the dark, they were near enough. The first one came down to join something that was not there, and found that the holes were real. The next came down to an iron hum with one purr in it. After that the rumour was true.

So the birds are pitched to the iron because the iron called first, and they have been answering ever since. Their purr sits a little off the chord for good reason. It is close enough that the plate takes it up and carries it, and far enough that a bird hovering at the honeycomb can tell mate from pipe. A voice dead on the note would vanish into it and never be found. The interval that never resolves is the width of a petrel's chance of being recognised, and each generation keeps it.

The chord is not standing still either. A shortened tube speaks sharp, and the plugged ones have crept upward, the worst of them by the best part of a semitone. What was once one note is slowly becoming several, retuned from behind by the dead. Meanwhile the windward plate goes on thinning. One winter the wind will come through the silver side, and the note will change or stop. Nothing in the hollow knows this.

Under the hen in the ninth tube, against a patch of skin she has plucked bare for it, is the egg. It is white and blunt, a quarter of her weight, with a faint ring of rust-coloured freckles at the wide end. Everything else in the hollow is on its way down, through mouth after smaller mouth, to mineral. Inside the egg, fish oil is going the other way. The chick is two days from breaking out. It has its bill in the air pocket at the blunt end, and it can hear. It hears her purr, close, through the skin. Around and behind that, it hears the iron sounding as it did before anyone was listening. The chick will know that interval before it knows light.

In about seventy days it will shuffle to the mouth of the tube on a moonless night and leave for a sea it has never seen. For three or four years it will not touch land. Then one night it will come back over the plain in the dark, low, listening for the note that was here first.
