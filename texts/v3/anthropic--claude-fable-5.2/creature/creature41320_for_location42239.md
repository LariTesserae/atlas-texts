Very little lives here, and nothing that lives here is in any difficulty.

**The cushions**

There are about thirty of them, each in its own broken jar, rooted in a spoonful of grit. A cushion is a dome of scale-leaves packed so close that the wind finds no edge to lift. New leaves come at the centre, a few a year. The old ones on the outside turn to grey husk and stay on as a coat. Before dawn the shards around it go cold enough to take a film of damp out of the air, and that film is its water for the day.

There are no insects to carry anything for it, so it gives its seed to the wind. In some years a cushion puts up a few threads the colour of its own leaves, which you would have to kneel to see, and lets go a seed as fine as dust. Almost all of it falls into sounding jars, is lifted out again with the rest of the dust, and goes over the lee wall onto the salt, where nothing comes of it. Once in a long while a grain drops into a jar that has cracked and gone still, and stays.

So the plant lives exactly where the sound does not. A map of the cushions would be a map of the silences, and the thirty of them sit in the chord's missing teeth.

**The lichens**

The lichens are on the lee shoulders of the big jars, rust-orange and a green that is nearly white. They take nothing from the jar but its surface. Each is a rosette the size of a coin, growing at the rim about as fast as the wind sands it back, so it stays the size it is. That is its whole life, a small steady making at the edge.

**The chord**

These are the lodgers. What fills the room, wall to wall and down through the tiers no one can count, is the chord.

It is fair to say it lives here, because it lives nowhere else. The wind does not, since it only crosses. The jars stand as they were set. The chord is what happens between them, and it stays.

It has a body with a shape. It lies heavy in the north corner, where the great jars are, and that part of it is felt in the breastbone. Along the east wall it is thin and bright as a reed. It has depth too, because every mouth was left open. The buried tiers cannot feel the wind, but they answer the jars above them, each giving back its own note more quietly, so the sound goes down as far as the brightness does. Somewhere in the middle of it is the slow beating where two jars coiled by the same hands came out a hair apart. It resembles a pulse but drives nothing. It is only those two jars, not quite agreeing.

None of the air in the chord now was in it a moment ago. It is made again continuously and comes out the same. It has no memory and needs none, because the jars remember for it. Their windward lips are worn to the exact curve that gives this note.

It has no phrase because it is not saying anything. It does not call across the pan. That it can be heard at a hundred paces is only its size.

If you stand on the windward rim, your body shelters a strip of mouths and the chord thins by the width of one person. When you step away, it closes. That is the whole of its dealings with you.

**The hands**

You will look for someone else: whoever pressed the bricks, coiled the jars, chinked the last gaps with shards, and limed the walls year over year until the lime stood thick as rind. Their palms and the drag of their fingers are everywhere, but there is no track on the salt. They made a room in which every space was filled and every mouth left open, and then the room needed nothing more from them.

When the light goes and the court turns pewter, every rim shows as a ring. The cushions take their trace of damp. The lichens add at the edge what the day took off. The chord holds, north corner low and east wall bright, with the same slow beat inside it and the same thirty small gaps, each with something grey and alive in it.
