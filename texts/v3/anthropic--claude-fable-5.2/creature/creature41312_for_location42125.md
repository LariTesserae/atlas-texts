Beavers, to begin with, and more of them than beavers normally come in. Forty-odd lodges at five or six animals each makes well over two hundred, in a space where ordinary country would hold three families that never met.

They are heavier than a visitor expects, twenty kilos or more, built like a loaf. From the rim at dusk you see only the top of a head, with nostril, eye and ear set in one line so all three clear the water together, and behind it the V. On land they are slow and bow-legged. The canals, the slides and the ponds lapping at the foot of every tree worth cutting are all ways of not being on land.

Elsewhere a pair holds a mile of stream and drives strangers off it. Here a pair holds one pond, and the border is the dam. Every crest carries scent mounds, fist-sized heaps of bottom mud clawed up and marked, and on a still evening the lower basin smells of them, somewhere between leather and birch tar. Neighbours pass within a body length on either side of a dam without looking at each other. Whatever first came down the rubble chute was probably a single pair, so every animal here is some degree of cousin to every other.

Two-year-olds, who anywhere else would leave along the water, have no water to leave by. The chute leads up to limestone that holds nothing on its surface for a day's walk. So they search inside the basin for a gap between two ponds wide enough for a third, or a wall seep not yet stepped. The best openings are made by the walls. A fallen panel is unclaimed ground with deep water on its lee side, and its first lodge is usually built by two young animals from opposite ends of the basin.

Inside, a lodge is one dark chamber above the waterline, floored with shredded wood and entered from below. Kits are born there in late spring, furred and open-eyed. For their first weeks they are too buoyant to dive out through the plunge hole, so they bob in it like corks while the yearlings mind them. In the bottom pond, where the level rises every year, the floors have been raised again and again. Each family there sleeps on top of the drowned rooms its grandparents slept in.

The ants look like the same kind of thing, but they are not. The hundreds of domes on the talus are one colony. Queens are shared, workers walk from dome to dome, and the trails carry brood and food between them. There is no border anywhere on that slope. It is also the one place in the basin with a constant sound, millions of feet on dry thatch, like light rain that never arrives. Put a hand near a dome and the surface tilts toward it, and the air turns to vinegar.

The two cities meet at the birches. The tallest birch stands on ant ground, and a felled tree takes its aphid herd with it. The beavers do their heavy cutting in late autumn, for the winter rafts, when the ants have already gone below for the cold. In summer the slides up the talus go unused.

The caddis build with what drifts down to them. Each wood chip in a case has two shallow grooves side by side, the mark of a pair of incisors. The larva drags that signature across the silt until a water shrew, silver with trapped air, cracks the case like a seed.

The tenants live off side effects. A char does not hear moving water, it feels it, and it hangs wherever some thread of current survives. A char in a canal is a sign of a leak the beavers have not found. The bats that leave the wall fissures hunt by sound, low over the surface, and ripples blind them. Nowhere on the plateau is water this flat, so they have been handed a perfect floor, and a tail slap ruins it for a minute. The otters take kits in the weeks when they cannot dive. The willow at the upper end is cut from below by beavers and clipped from above by elk, and it lives in the band between the two.

In winter the ponds close. The beavers stay awake under the ice, swimming in the dark from plunge hole to raft and back, and never surface. From the rim the basin looks abandoned, a white staircase. On the coldest mornings each lodge shows a thread of steam at its peak, where the breath of the family comes up through the sticks. A metre down in the talus the ants are packed in a ball around their queens. The caddis keep walking.

All the building in the stepped floor comes from one thing in the beavers. Running water bothers a beaver the way a dripping tap bothers a person in a quiet house, except that the beaver cannot decide to ignore it. If the sound is played from a box on dry ground, the beaver will bury the box. This basin has held enough beavers for long enough that they have used the sound up. Most animals here go weeks without hearing it. They cut, haul, eat, groom and re-mark the mounds, and the thing they are made to answer is nowhere.

Then a panel comes off the wall. The wave goes down through the terraces, a dozen dams open, and for the first time in months the basin is loud with water falling from pond to pond. Every beaver comes out, even those that should be asleep, and animals cross crests they have never crossed. They carry mud against the chest, held under the chin with both forepaws, walking upright up the face of the breach like people carrying full basins. Sticks are towed by the butt end and jammed in. Nobody is driven off anything that night. By morning the gaps are dark with mud, the sound is gone, the mounds are freshened and the borders are back.

There is one place where the sound cannot be used up. The mound over the throat belongs to no family. Under it the water goes down into rock, a hollow pull that is felt through the feet more than heard. Animals from the bottom lodges come to it every night, and every night some of what was put there the night before has gone into the hole. That has been happening for generations. Somewhere under the plateau, in a passage nothing has seen, there must be a jam of peeled sticks with paired tooth marks on their ends.

They are winning slowly. As the bottom pond rises it covers the lowest seeps, and a seep under water stops weeping, because the water outside the rock now stands as high as the water inside it. The lake is climbing toward the level the plateau's water held before the floor fell. Nothing here is aiming at that.

On any night at the throat, a beaver walks up the mound on her hind feet with mud against her chest. She presses it in with her nose and both hands, then stops with her head a little to one side. The sound is still there, so she goes back down for more.
