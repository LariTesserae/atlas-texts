At first you would say the toads live here. They are the largest things in the hollow and they have the best seats. But each toad has one cup and has never left it. A toad could not tell you what a snail looks like at rest, because it has only ever seen one in transit.

Then you might say whoever came with the chalk. But a hand put in spills a hand's worth of woodlice over the far lip, and a whole person would empty the place. The counter belongs to the fringe, with the beetles and the small crab, one more thing at the edge facing inward.

What lives here is the empty place.

It has no size of its own. In the lower wall it is the size of a banded snail, and above that the size of a woodlouse. On the shaded wall it is a slate-shaped patch of bare slag between wings. It is always the shape of whoever has just left, and for a breath it keeps their leavings: a dab of slime, a little wing dust, a patch of stone that is drier than the stone around it. It has never been in a room that was not just vacated.

It has never taken a step. Each animal that moves into it sends it one place the other way, so all its travelling is made of other creatures' small advances. It goes backwards through them like a guest handed down a table.

It keeps a round. At dawn, when the river air beads on the walls and the snails are loose in their cells, it climbs through them. By mid-morning it is among the woodlice, and you can find it by a break in the whisper. The firebrats pass it along so fast it hardly exists there. On the chain it waits longest, as a single open link among the capped ones, until a wasp has finished building. It crosses the moths in the afternoon, one wing lifting and settling at a time. On each rising tide it is lifted with everything else, and twice a day it comes within a row of the rim, where the wagtail is. So far it has always come back down.

Twenty minutes after a pour it hurries. In the steam after rain nobody can see it, and when the white clears the three toads are facing three different ways. Then, one after another, they correct.

It is the only thing in the hollow that anything pays attention to. It is also the only thing that has been everywhere in it. A snail knows its own cell and perhaps the next one. The gap has been snail-shaped in every cell of the lower wall and has lain among the fish as a fish-length slot of plain water. There are only three places it has never been, and those are the cups of the toads who watch it.

It has had siblings. Each time the young slag ticks and opens a new cell, a second absence is born, sharp-edged and clean and warmer than anything around it. It lasts as long as it takes a beetle to come over the lip. A toad's swallow also makes an absence, and that one is closed from the finger-wide channel before the swallow is finished. There have been hundreds, and none has lasted a minute. They are what the fringe is waiting for. The fringe cannot reach the first one. Anything inside the hollow that fills it only moves it along. Something from outside could end it, but when it rides high on the tide and something at the lip leans in, a woodlouse has always shifted first.

It was here before any of them. When the two tongues of waste cooled, it was the whole hollow, a kitchen-sized pocket and every cell in the walls besides. It was the heap's own breath in the shape the gas left when it went. Then a spore landed at the waterline, and the first snail came down the channel. Each arrival took a piece of it exactly its own size. It has been made smaller ever since, and it has stopped at one, which is as small as it can get and still be there.

I think that is what the chalk is about. The tally comes to the same total every time because the total is everyone else. The sum on the slag plate is its outline. Whoever keeps coming back to write the number again is not checking on the snails.

The cricket may be the same creature heard from the other side. It is one voice passed around a silence, as the gap is one silence passed around a crowd. There are never two of either.

It will not last past the heap's cooling. When the warmth goes, the firebrats will leave, the fish will thin, and cells will stand open by the dozen. Then there will be nothing for a toad to face. It is only someone while the hollow is full, and it depends on that more than anything else here does.

For now, toward evening, it comes down the wall to the waterline and a single fish turns. It spends the night in the pond, thumb-length and blood-warm. It lies in the rank with its head toward the seep like the others. An hour behind the tide, the water lifts it a hand's width and sets it down again.
