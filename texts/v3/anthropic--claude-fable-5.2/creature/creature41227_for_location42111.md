There is one more resident, and the chamber hides it the way it hides the rock.

Go in along the left wall with a hand on the nap at shoulder height. Around the sixth pace, just past where the daylight clogs, the wall gives a little more than it should. The smell changes too. Under the nuts and ash there is something sour and close, like the lining of an old hat. The moths there are shingled like all the others, heads tucked, several deep. They rise and fall slightly, ten or twelve times a minute.

From outside it is only a lump in the nap, larger than a lizard makes. It stands with its back to the wall for most of the day, and the moths roost on anything that keeps still.

Under the moths is skin. It is dry and loose, and so worked with scale dust that it has a nap of its own. A fingernail drawn across it leaves a pale line. The hair has felted into a cap. The body is the height of a grown person and about the weight of a child, because the air takes water from the living too, only more slowly. Age cannot be told from a body like that. The eyes are shut. The dust is easier that way, and there has been nothing to look at for years. It does not start at a touch. Things walk on it all day, and grazers have crossed its feet and left bare stripes that closed behind them.

It is a woman. She came up from the lowlands behind the moths in a year when the grubs took the whole root crop. She walked for weeks after the thing that had eaten her field, meaning to eat it back and go home.

People used to do that in numbers. They stacked the wall, which gives the warm air more cold stone to freeze onto. They ground moths in the dish and carried the cakes down, and they built the cairns for three who did not get down. She still drinks at the wall, half slumped as it is. She has no use for the dish. A cake is for carrying, and she carries nothing anywhere. She stayed through one leaving, and that was a good many dark layers ago.

She keeps to a band like everyone else. Hers is the mouth band, between the grazers and the first lizards, where the air is as warm as fevered skin. She has stood in that fever so long that she would feel its absence as cold.

She eats moths off the wall beside her head, a few at a time, rubbing the wings off between her palms. The bare patch closes before she has swallowed. But a moth is fat and little else. What she needs from the chamber is water, and the water is in the lizards.

That is what the strip is for. A lizard under the nap could be anywhere on forty paces of wall, but a lizard in a pit is at a known place. She goes in with her left hand on the strip and comes out with her right. At each pit her fingertips go in, and if a lizard is there she has it by the head before the moths above it have stirred. She walks so slowly that most of her own moths stay on.

Nobody dug the pits. Fingers went after lizards in the same slight hollows until the hollows deepened, and a deeper hollow holds a lizard better. She did not start this, because one life does not polish andesite. Others stood where she stands, and the wall does not say how many, or whether any two of them ever met. They are in the floor now, dried and chewed and pressed, somewhere among the dark layers and the pale.

She does not walk the strip whenever she likes. She waits for the knock. It comes up through the path her feet have trodden in the felt, pressed as hard as the old layers underneath, and she feels it before anything on the walls does. Then she goes in to meet the heat. The bands are sliding toward her, the lizards are moving under thin cover, and they fetch up in the pits. She takes two, sometimes three. She walks out ahead of the front with the cloth sound of the moths going along the wall beside her, and she eats where she stops, the wet parts first.

The strip runs twenty-six paces. Past the middle the polish thins and the pits grow shallow. The last is barely a dimple, at a place where a palm can stay on the rock for one breath. Those are the pits of the pale years. When the moths have gone and the eating has started at the crack and worked outward, the near pits are empty, and she has to go in after what is left. She comes back from there with her fingertips split.

Before dawn she goes out with the walkers. She kneels in the lee of the wall and puts her mouth to the rime. They stand over her hands with their legs laced through her fingers. In the dark years she leaves them alone, and in the pale years she does not. She is inside again before the sun touches the ridge, which she has not seen by day since she came.

When the cold breaks she stands in the slot for the three or four nights it takes, and the moths go out past her face. It is the only time in the year that anything touches her face. They are going down to lay in fields like the one she had. She does not follow them.

The first pit on the strip is at the fourth pace, where the last of the daylight gives out. It is the deepest of them all and takes a finger to the first joint. It is too near the mouth and too cool for a lizard, and none has ever lain in it. She puts her fingers into it every time she goes in and every time she comes out, and so did everyone before her.
