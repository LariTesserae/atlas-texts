The toad is what the keeper would name, if the keeper named anything. It is there every week on its bar, facing the same way. But it is facing down. The bar it has chosen is the one over the deepest part of the well, and it sits there the way someone sits at a window.

Under the grating the lamplight falls in slats onto the sump. The water is smooth and very slightly tilted, sliding toward the intake. Beneath the stairs, where the flow turns a corner and leaves a slack place against the brickwork, something dark lies along the foot of the wall. It looks like a length of suction hose that was dropped and never fetched, and it has passed for hose with more than one keeper. Hose does not keep one end pointed upstream.

It is an eel, and from the size a female, since the males never get so big. She is longer than a man's leg and as thick as his forearm. Her head is broad and flat, with the lower jaw pushed a little forward. A single fin begins behind her shoulders, runs down her back, round the tail and forward again along the belly, and a slow wave travels the length of it even when she is at rest. Nothing drives that wave but her. For most of her life she was the colours of the room, liverwort green along the back and handrail brass underneath, with eyes no bigger than lentils.

She came in through the west wall. The high drain is on the other side, with the river beyond it and the sea beyond that, and one spring it carried glass eels, each the length of a finger and clear enough to read a date through. They will climb anything wet. One of them found a joint in the lower courses where the mortar had gone and the water was coming through, and she came through with it, thin as the thread from the gland. She crossed the floor in the film, followed the channels cut in the concrete as the water does, and went down into the sump. A hart's tongue grows in that joint now. She could not get her head into it.

She lies with her nose to the culvert, and the country goes past it. None of the land can be seen from inside, but all of it can be tasted: peat, lime, iron from the deep cuts, the sourness after the beet is lifted, and in some weeks something that numbs. She knows it has rained when the worms arrive, pale and drowned. She eats those, and sticklebacks, and the red larvae of the midges, which she noses out of the silt, and perhaps twice a year a frog. She does not eat the toad. His kind taste of a warning, and he stays above the grating while she stays under it.

Behind her the intake draws. There is a line in the water past which a thing does not come back, and she learned where it runs when she was small enough to be nearly wrong about it. She keeps a hand's breadth short of it, as the moss keeps its distance from the motor. What goes past her goes up through the volute and over the sill into the high drain, which is exactly where she would need to go. It does not arrive there in a condition to continue.

She has never been in water without the hum in it. The row of pores along her side, which is how she feels a stickleback turn in the dark, takes the motor's note as its zero. She does not hear the pump house, but she would hear it stop.

The room has no winter, but the water does. It comes in off the open drains at whatever temperature the land is, and for some months it is cold enough that she hardly feeds. In each of her ears is a stone the size of a rice grain, which grows as she grows, and every cold season lays a dark ring in it. There would be some eighty rings. They are the only record in the building on which one year can be told from another.

Her kind are not built to stay. In a certain autumn they stop eating, the gut withers, the eyes double in size, the back blackens and the belly goes from brass to silver. Then, on a night with no moon and the water rising, they leave for the sea. Every autumn the cold and the taste of leaves begin this in her. She goes off her food and something swells behind the eyes. She waits for the rest of the instruction, which is darkness and a rising level. The lamps stay on in their cages and the water stands at its mark. After some weeks the change subsides and she eats again.

It does not subside all the way. Each autumn has left a little behind, and she has had eighty of them. She is no longer the colours of the room. Her back is the black of the ironwork under its oil, her belly is nearer the white of the tiles than the brass of the rail, and her eyes are wide and dark, made for deeper water than there is anywhere in this country. She has been made ready over eighty years, a little at a time, in a building maintained so that the last of it never arrives.

What is missing is small: one dark night, and a rise in the sump of about the width of the recorder's ink line. The lamps and the motor are on the same supply, so the two would come together, along with a silence she would feel down the whole of her side. The chart for that week would show a circle that did not close. The floor is wet all the way to the door, and she has crossed it before.
