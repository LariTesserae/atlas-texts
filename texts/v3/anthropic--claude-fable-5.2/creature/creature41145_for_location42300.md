**The flies**

The smallest things that live here live in the stump pools. The pale flies spend most of their lives as grubs under the skin ice, working along the pith wall. In the bright hours the skin thins, and the grown ones come up through it and hang over the stump in a thread, like smoke from a fire that isn't there. Few go anywhere, because the next pool is half a day off. A fly hatches, breeds, and drops back into the water it came from. Now and then a drifting frost-fall picks some up and sets them down elsewhere, and that is all the travelling the kind does.

**The fliers**

The white fliers live at the other end, against the roof. They nest where the dark leaf keeps the ice wet, and they drink there. Whatever they eat is up there too. None has been seen alive on the floor.

A joint starts to open some time before its column goes, and the fliers leave then. They don't follow the fan down. They circle at the height where the nest was, around nothing, while that piece of roof slides on with a pale patch where the leaf tore away. By the next day they have crossed to another fan. Their eggs are in the wreck.

**The browser**

The long-bodied animal is white, about as long as three people lying head to foot, and no higher than a waist. Its coat holds frost, so when it lies still it is one more ridge in the mat. It has a narrow head and a mouth that cuts on a slant, and the shoots it leaves look like trimmed quills.

It can eat only what has come up since yesterday. Anything older is over its head and already hardening. A gap in the fans feeds one animal for one day, and then it has to walk to the next patch of light. So it is alone, and it is always leaving.

The bitten shoots keep growing, and the cut rides up on top of them. Some reach the roof still carrying it and open two fans instead of one. A doubled pad on the ice means the animal passed underneath when that column was a day old.

**Falls**

Everything here waits on the falls. A column comes down and light reaches the floor. Shoots come for the light, and the animal comes for the shoots. The stump fills, and in time the flies have it. The fan lies out on the mat a long walk from its own foot, with its leaf, its fiber and its nests, and the people come for that. They arrive first. They have watched the lean for days, and the frost cloud is often still hanging when the first sled pulls in.

**The people, far off**

The people are few in the way the columns are few. A household keeps to one column and its water, and the next household is at the next column. From a distance, a person is a dark upright mark on the frost, the only dark thing down here besides the stones, and is usually walking toward a lean.

They gather only at a fall. Sleds come in from several directions over a day. Eggs are divided, stone is traded, leaf is cut and rolled, and matches are made. By the next morning the shoots are up through the wreckage, and everyone has gone.

**Closer**

They wear fan fiber quilted over down from the fallen nests. They rub soot around the eyes and tie a strip of split cane with a slit in it across them, because the light comes from above and below at once. They speak quietly, since nothing here asks for more.

Each adult carries an adze, a cane joint with a live pith ember packed in ash, and a small stone on a cord. They look at the stone on the cord more than anything else they own. Held at arm's length beside a column, it shows the lean. When the cane stands a hand's width off the cord, they have some days. At two hands they load the sled.

Every stone they own came through the roof and landed near someone. When the roof gives its low note, they stop and stand where they are, since there is no telling which way to step. Afterward, if there is a new black mark out on the frost, someone walks out to fetch it. The heaps at the stumps are these, sorted into small ones for cords, middling for adzes, and large ones left for whoever comes next.

**What they learned**

The stretched steps and the rooted route are old work, from when they still made things the way people do on rock. A step cut as a step is useless within the week. A pole stood as a marker is a column in a season. Those marks are high overhead now, with everything else that is old.

Now they cut for the stretch. A ladder begins as a row of shallow nicks a finger apart, at hand height, on a young column that has no water yet. They cut on the side that will face up once the column leans. The cane pulls the nicks apart as it lengthens and lifts them out of reach, and someone cuts another row beneath them every morning. By the time the fan has opened and the film is running, a ladder stands from the ground to above the glaze, and no one climbed to make it. It took a season of standing in one place.

A morning missed is a gap. It rises through the ladder a little each day, and every climber has to make that long reach until the gap has passed above the water.

They plant the poles on purpose now. The first line was an accident, but the ones since run down the slow lanes, each pole stood a season after the last. A household walks along its line as the columns come ready, and plants at the far end.

They don't draw. A picture is pulled out of shape in days. They count, because five marks dragged into five long scars are still five. Anything that has to keep its length they tie into cord.

**The dead**

A body is bound at evening to the tip of a new shoot. Someone sits beside it that one night to keep the browser off. By morning it is above reach. For some days the household can still pick it out, a speck on a pale line, and then it enters the fog. That is the height the fliers come down to. The mourning ends there. A season later the tip opens against the roof, and whatever is left is under the fan, in the light.

**One of them**

The new steps in the middle of the hollow were cut by a woman whose hip no longer lets her climb. She stood that column herself as a pole. She cuts each morning's row for a boy who is now as tall as her waist, because he will be the one going up when the water comes. She keeps his stride on a knotted cord and reties it as he grows. The nicks she cuts are spaced for the stride she thinks he will have by then, so she sets them a little wide. If she has guessed long, he will feel it on every rung for a season.

When the row is done, she hangs the stone and looks. The cane stands true to the cord, so this piece of roof is slow. Then she puts her palm flat on yesterday's row, already level with her shoulder, and waits for the tick. When it comes, the row moves up under her hand by the width of a finger.
