A visitor would say the animals, and for a while that seems to be all. There are a dozen pale rowers the colour of the rooms, each with four or five lamps to keep. One finishes an edge, clicks once, and moves on. The clicks come a few times an hour, never evenly, and they are the only thing in the dome that happens. Anyone who came in through the skin, waited as long as the cold allowed, and left again would report twelve small animals and an empty town.

But there is not enough breath. Nothing dissolves here and nothing rises. If the people of fifty rooms had breathed the way lowland animals breathe, one day would have packed the dome with silver from floor to crown. Instead there are strings along the rails and clusters at the doors, a few thousand beads in all. Either the town was lived in for an afternoon, or whoever lives in it lets a breath go about as often as they do anything else. The rooms were not built in an afternoon.

So they are here. They are in the dark, and the dark is nearly all of the dome. All the lamps together light less water than one room holds. The rest, several million cubic metres of it, is unlit, and that is where they hang, a little off a rail or just outside a door, each one exactly where they last let themselves stop. Water at its heaviest has no reason to move. Most of the time, neither do they.

They are built for the rails. The arm is long, and the hand closes all the way around a line as thick as a finger. Below the ribs the body narrows and trails and is used for little. They have no preferred way up. Two of them meeting at a door talk at whatever angles they arrived in, one lying across the other's line of sight like crossed tools. They are seen only in pieces. A lamp is two arm-lengths across and stops sharp, so what enters it is a wrist, a jaw, the flat of a back. To be seen whole, one of them would have to fold up inside a sphere, knees to chest. It is not known whether they ever do, or what it would mean between them if one did.

They know each other by the line. A hand on a rail feels who else is on it, how far off, and hauling which way. Each has a pull as particular as a gait. Every rail runs through the hoop and comes out a few degrees turned, so every pull arrives slightly other than it was sent. They allow for this the way one allows for an accent.

The cold goes a certain depth into them and stops, as the light stops. Inside that line they keep whatever warmth they have. They are edged like this all through. One of them attending to a vessel attends to the vessel, and whatever hangs beyond the reach of the arms is not there for that while. That is why the dozen containers stand in a spiral around the worker's place and nowhere else. That is also why one of them can own a single toothed object, keep it at the exact centre of a room, and find the room full.

The first rule has shaped them more than the cold has. Nothing they have put anywhere has ever fallen, rolled, sunk, blown off or been buried. They have never had to finish a thing before it spoiled, or pick anything up. So a task can be left in its middle without unease, because the middle will hold. The line with its last loop half-uncoiled was not abandoned. Whoever was paying it out opened a hand, and will close it again on that same loop. Between the two there may be a hundred clicks or ten thousand. The doors are open because open is where they were put.

Breath is the one thing they make. It is not drawn from anywhere, since there is nowhere to draw it from. It gathers in them slowly, the way a nail grows, and when there is enough of it and a reason, it is let go. After that it is there for good, at the height of the mouth that released it. So a breath is spent with care. The clusters by the doorways are conversations, every one still present. Someone arriving later can tell from the sizes and the spacing roughly how each one went: the big slow beads, the tight scatter where something was funny, the long gap before an answer.

On the rails they let one go every body-length, so that whoever follows in the dark knows the line has been travelled by a living hand. They let them go closer together on the long spans, where that is easier to doubt. The rail toward the crown they travel with everything held. Up there is the skin, and the note in it is strong enough to feel in the wrists. They do go. Whatever they make of it, they have agreed not to leave it hanging in the water.

The ring near the floor is how they decide things. The matter is carried out to open water that belongs to no room, and put there. They go out to it in a chain, each with a hand on the next and the last with a hand on the rail, and take places around it. What is said stays where it is said. When it is settled someone carries the thing away and the ring remains. It is the nearest they have to a law, because it can be gone back to and gone around. There is only the one. In all the time of the town a single matter has needed it. The ring does not record what the matter was, only that hundreds of breaths were spent on it, more along one arc than the other. There is one gap, a body wide, with no bead in front of it.

The gap is hers. She is the one who is not on the rails.

To get where she is, she had to open her hand on a line and not close it on another line or on anyone's wrist. This is not done. Off the rail no hand can find her, and she is the only one in the town whose whereabouts nobody knows by touch. She rowed down the way the animals row, with whatever she had to row with, past the lowest lamp, and stopped where the rock made her stop. On the rails she was known by a short double pull, the second lighter than the first. It has not come down any line in a long while. The others have noticed, and so far noticing is as far as it has gone.

What she found there is the ridge. Everything else in the dome is where some hand released it, the rooms included. The ridge was never put. It comes in under the skin on one side, runs straight across the floor with its top edge still glass, and goes out under the skin on the other. It is the only line in the town that does not trend inward and does not pass through the hoop. When she understood that, she said one thing, and it is still there beside her mouth.

Her hand is open over the edge, its own width away. A hand that closed on that edge the way hands close on rails would be opened by it. She may know this. Either way she has not closed it. Nothing will decide for her. The water will not carry her nearer or farther, the bead will not rise, and the rock will not dull. The clicks reach her whole even down there, one and then, much later, another. Everything she has ever taken hold of was let go by someone first. She is taking her time over this, and the place has given her all of it.
