The first resident you meet is a hand. It closes on your forearm near the head of the approach valley, where the roar has already taken the sound of your own boots. It goes through your pack and pockets without apology and removes anything that could make a light. Then it sets your palm flat on a shoulder, and the shoulder starts to walk.

**The bodies**

They are people. They are shorter than you expected, deep through the chest, warm and damp to the touch. They wear felt beaten from flier down and left oily, so the fog beads on it and runs off. It smells the way everything here smells. Their hair is cropped or greased flat. Their soles are hide from the street-lickers, set with crushed shell for grip.

Nobody here has ever run, because the air won't pay for it and the paving won't allow it. The fastest gait in the city is a firm, unbroken walk, and it gets through a crowd by contact: a palm on the back ahead, a hip against a hip, a stranger's fingers turning you by the elbow at a corner. Being alone is something they do on purpose and not for long. Households sleep in one room, in a heap, against whichever wall faces the Tap.

Their eyes work. Outdoors they keep them shut against the ammonia; indoors they open them or don't. In the grey months children climb to the top terraces and come down to report that the grey is back, the way children elsewhere report that a puddle has frozen. The word for light is the word for a crush of bodies, because for them light is an accident and that is the sound it makes.

**The hum**

Before you understand anything else about the shoulder you are holding, you feel that it is vibrating. Every one of them hums, mouth closed, low in the chest. The fliers own the high register and the Tap owns the floor, and people live in the band between. The hum does the work a lamp would do. Two walkers hear each other coming and pass without touching. Someone who goes silent in a street gets walked into, and it is their own fault. Going quiet beside another person is an insult, an intimacy, or a warning, and they always know which.

Speech goes under the roar rather than over it. It is spoken mouth to temple, in short phrases, with hard clicked consonants they may have learned from the fliers, or the fliers from them. For distance they call, three or four pitched notes set against the nearest junction tone. Everyone has such a phrase, and it is their name. A lost child stands still and sings its street's interval, and strangers pass it hand to hand, each one moving it a junction closer to where that interval comes true. This happens daily and nobody thinks of it as kindness.

**The glass**

There are no days here and no seasons, and nobody counts sleeps. You agree on where to meet, not when, and whoever arrives first waits, with a net to knot or felt to beat. Waiting is not felt as a cost.

One thing is counted. When a child is born, a knapper takes a blade of the mountain's glass and chips it, flake by flake, until it rings on exactly the note the home junction holds that day. The child wears it on a cord. For years, struck in the street, it vanishes into the tone. Then one year there is a slow swell between the two, like breathing. By middle age it is a pulse. In the old it is a flutter, and they will strike the glass for you, unasked, to prove the street has sunk.

Age is reckoned by that beat, and nothing else is. If you stay, someone knaps a glass for you, pitched to the day you came. By the only count kept, you are a newborn, which is why you are led by the hand, fed, and handed along.

**The work**

Eggers work the ledges by touch, sliding a hand under one sitting body in every dozen, and their knuckles are ridged with scar. Netters stand thigh-deep in the channels beneath the long-fingered rakers. The two have an arrangement that no one made: the netters throw the undersized catch up at the wall, and the rakers no longer rake a netter's hair. Bakers grow film on racks in rooms warmed from the Tap, dry it, mill it with crustacean meal, and bake it flat on warm plates. They have been told what gets into their bread. They say bread without it would taste of nothing.

In the street they shoulder through the lickers and grumble back at them in the lickers' own tone. It is the most common exchange in the city.

At the footings, where the lowest course is still raw, tenders keep the growing edge clear. They say that if you leave your palm on it for a whole sleep you can feel it move. If you ask them how it grows, or how the Tap works, they answer as you would if someone asked how you made your teeth.

**The gap**

Nothing has ever happened to them quickly, so a loud noise means nothing to them. The rock cracks like a rifle and no one's hum wavers. What makes them start is a drop-out. When a hole opens in the roar and travels across a tower face, every person under it stops, and the humming on the high spans gets louder.

Their name for what makes the hole is a pause. They say, "I was on the upper span when", then leave a beat of nothing, then say, "went by." A few have touched it. They agree on one thing only, and they say it with more unease than they give to its size: it was dry.

Nobody will tell you it has ever taken a person. But residents who have been carried down the valley, into sun, say the light was only painful. What they could not bear was the quiet, and the far mountains standing in it, enormous, in plain view, making no sound, like something that hunts.

**What hangs at the junctions**

When someone dies, the body is laid at the footings and has been grown over by the time the household has finished grieving. The newest dead lie in the lowest course, and they lift everyone else.

The glass is hung at the home junction, with all the others. The fliers' wings keep them stirring, so every junction carries above its sinking tone a faint ringing smear of every note it used to be, one for each death. Young districts sound clean. Old ones shimmer. Residents can pick a grandmother out of the shimmer, and they do, in passing, without stopping.

The old complain that their street used to sing higher. They are right, and the street still does, faintly, through the hung glass. In the oldest districts some flier colonies have begun to pitch their calls to the shimmer and not to the tone. They are a little sharp. They call in the key of people who are gone, and they teach it to their chicks.
