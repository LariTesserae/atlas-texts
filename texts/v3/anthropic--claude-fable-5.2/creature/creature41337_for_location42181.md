In the morning, from the rim, you count them before you know what you are counting. Nine or ten dark marks stand on the white field, upright and well apart, like doors left standing after the walls have gone. You look down to place a foot, look up again, and there are four.

Nothing has left. The missing ones have turned toward you, and turned toward you they are a hand's breadth wide. At that distance a hand's breadth is a line, and the glare closes over it.

Up close, one of them is a standing slab of an animal, about shoulder-high, somewhat longer than it is tall, and nowhere thicker than the breadth of your hand. It balances on its lower edge, a single row of blunt pads, each a finger wide. One face is the colour of the marble, grey veining included. The other is the blue-black of the sky straight overhead, a colour found nowhere else below the horizon. Both faces are ribbed from top to bottom, finer than the flutings on the pinnacles, and the ribs draw water up from the crown, so the whole animal sheets and shines like the stone it stands on.

Along the leading edge runs a groove as deep as a finger is long. Whatever it sees with is at the bottom of that. Light gets down there only by being passed from one wall to the other, as it gets to the bottom of a slot, and what arrives is a sliver of the world, dead ahead, with most of the brightness worn off. This is how it stands the place. It also means that to look at a thing it has to point itself at the thing. You are being watched by whichever ones you cannot find.

It probably does not see the plateau, any more than it hears it. Marble, water and sky never change, and an eye kept in a groove has no use for what never changes. What comes down the groove is whatever isn't those: a moth on a crown, a white wing-patch opening, another dark mark. You, on the rim, may be the largest event in years.

The water in them is the plateau's water, a few degrees above freezing, and the dark face is how they get anything better. At sunrise each one stands broadside to the sun and stays square to it all morning, creeping round on its pads as the sun goes round. On still mornings a thread of vapour comes off the dark side, the only smoke the plateau has. They are digesting. What the afternoon brought is still in them, cold, and it takes a morning's sun to use it.

So the sun steers them. Which way the grooved edge points depends on which face is dark. In some it is the left and in some the right, about evenly. A left-dark one, giving its dark face to the east at dawn, finds itself looking south, at the notch where everything leaves. A right-dark one looks north, at the white line where the water starts. Neither chose. By noon the sun has swung the first round to the west and the second to the east, and neither has seen what the other saw. To look the other way, one of them would have to turn its pale face to the sun and go cold.

That cost is what they talk with. Broadside, a dark face can be seen from one side of the plateau to the other, and all of them stand at one angle, the sun's. Speech is a departure from it. One swings a little out of true and holds there, losing heat. A long way across the field another swings out by the same amount and holds. Then both go back. Sometimes one turns all the way over and shows pale, which against marble is showing nothing, and that seems to end the matter.

What they cannot do is show and look at once. The face that speaks is at right angles to the edge that sees. Whoever is speaking is looking somewhere else, and whoever is looking has thinned to a line. So each says its piece without seeing how it is taken, then turns to find out, and by then the other may be speaking, or gone pale.

There is one arrangement that gets round this. Two of opposite hands stand some crowns apart, one due north of the other at dawn, and the sun's own angle points them straight at each other. Neither gives up any warmth. As the morning goes on and the sun swings them, the line between them has to swing too, so they walk. Each slides its row of pads across a gap the way a plank is fed across a ditch, then pauses, then crosses another. They circle each other a quarter of the way round by noon. All that either sees of the other is the narrowest it gets, an upright line with a notch of dark in it. They do this every morning. Somewhere in watching it, you stop thinking of either one as *it*.

After noon the updrafts start, and a slab on edge is no shape to hold in a wind. One by one they go down. Each slides to the lip of a slot, tips, and enters edge-first, like a letter. It holds itself between the walls by breathing in and lowers itself by breathing out, a hand's width at a time, until it hangs a body-height below the crowns. Then the top edge parts along its length into two lips of fine tines, and each tine seats in a runnel of the wall it faces.

The insects come down chilled onto the crowns. The film takes them over the shoulders and down the flutes, and in an occupied slot they stop at the tines. That is all the hunting there is. There are perhaps thirty of them on the whole plateau, and the windfall would not feed more.

You can look down and see one, both faces pressed to wet stone, but you will find it sooner with your feet. A slot with a body in it is a shorter throat than its neighbours and holds a slightly different pitch. Where that pitch lies against the note there is a slow swell and fade in the rock, in time with the breathing. From the rim you would never know. The note holds.

They stay down all night. After dark the air drops below freezing and the water does not, so the film coming down the walls is the warmest thing on the plateau, and they hang in it with both faces covered. There is nothing to see and so nothing to do. If the swell in the rock tells any of them where the others are, they give no sign of it. At first light the crawlers are already at the tines, picking out what the night left, and are allowed.

The young are in the orange runnels. They are slips of clear tissue, a finger wide and thinner than a fingernail, lying in the film and grazing the slick about as fast as it grows back above them. You can see the stone's veining through them. They are found in any month. As they thicken they cloud, then go the colour of marble on both sides, and then one side darkens. Nothing anyone has noticed decides which. Now and then a grown one stands a whole morning on an orange column with no one in its line and nothing being said. That may be all the tending there is.

Nothing collects, and they do not either. One that dies in its slot breathes out and does not breathe in. The body slides to the floor and the drainage takes it inward, slot to slot, over some days, until it comes out where the pinnacles drown and lies flat on the sheet. It crosses slowly, dark face up as often as not, the one time that colour lies open under the sky it matches. The cross-hatching closes behind it. Then it reaches the notch and goes over with the pane.

The crossing takes most of a morning, and while it lasts there are no dark marks on the field. Every one of them has left the sun and turned its grooved edge to the sheet. From the rim the plateau looks empty. It is the most attention the place can hold.

When you leave, look back. If it is morning there will be dark marks again, squared to the sun, and fewer than there should be. Somewhere in the glare a line is standing with its grooved edge toward you, or its blind edge. From here the two look the same. The silence in your ears goes away in a day. Not knowing which edge it was takes longer.
