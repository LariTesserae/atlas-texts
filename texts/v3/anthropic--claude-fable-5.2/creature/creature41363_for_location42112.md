When water starts down a new route and the forty run to meet it, one of them runs a little behind the rest.

She is short and wide across the back, round-shouldered like the houses. Her face is weathered to the brown of old horn, except for a pale band across the eyes where the slit-strip sits. Indoors, with the strip off, she looks as though someone has drawn a wet thumb across her. She is fifty or so. Nobody here counts closely.

Her name is made with both hands, flat, palms down, stopped in the air. It is the sign people throw to each other when a stone lets go of the headwall. It means *hold*. She got it as a child, and not kindly.

Every generation or so the town gets a loud one, and she was it. Her laugh came out before she could catch it, and she sneezed like a slap. At six she saw a weasel take a wader chick and yelled at it, and the town went white to the lip. Her mother was pouring at the time, and stood with both hands in a wall until the sun came round to her. After that the other children made the sign whenever she opened her mouth.

She still has too much tongue for fine work. She cups her palm against a form and clicks, and the form takes, and so do the scoop, the runnel behind her, and the puddle under the next woman's feet. Nobody asks her for a doorstep. They want her when a channel jumps and a whole lane needs stopping at once, and that is why she runs behind. The others slow the water with hides and get their feet clear, then look back at her. She clicks once from the corner and the lane stops. She stands there trying not to look pleased.

Otherwise her days are anyone's. She has a goat with three grey boots and one white, and a mute dog that watches her mouth, the only mouth in town worth watching. She signs from the shoulder, big enough to read from the far end of a lane. Where a neighbor will knot a whole afternoon's grievance into a door-string, she hangs three knots: *up top, back later, feed the dog*. She has no patience for kettles, and hers is always a hand's width out of the light.

What is hers is the dry room. At the hot hour, when the morning's walls are sagging, she lets the door-felt down behind her and the neighbors look somewhere else. A voice here is a body matter, kept in the room with birth and crying. They don't mind that she has one, but they would rather not picture it.

She goes in to keep it in condition. A voice left alone goes soft at the front, and a soft sound sets patchily. For years she simply barked in there, one hard noise over and over into the lining, as the dogs' grandmothers must have.

Then she got bored. She had an hour a day and a working voice, and nothing to say with it. The shelf has no speech to borrow, since hands do all that. So she began with the sounds things already had. *Tik* is the coin a drip makes when it lands. She made that noise until it was simply what the coin was called. The snap of the long-billed bird came next, then a peg going into a seam. That got her a dozen.

The silent things took longer, and most of what she lives among is silent, like the waders' dance and the glitter coming down. For those she had to decide, and she would carry water for days with her lips shut and her mouth trying shapes behind them. The loach waiting in the ice took her most of a winter. She has about sixty words now. They are short and nearly all begin with a stop, because that is how her mouth was trained. There is no word for *and*. There is no word for herself, since she has never needed to call herself over. She says them in the order the day brought them, which is as near as it comes to talking.

The voice is for the evening. The town does not leave its last set to the headwall. A stone at midnight locks the place however it happens to be lying, with a channel half moved and a pool across somebody's door, and no sun to give anything back. So when the sun gets down near the cloud-tops, the ready-sign goes up terrace by terrace, from the lip to the headwall. It means goats in, beards out of troughs, feet out of the water, door-felts down. The last hand is at the house below hers. She stands in her doorway with the ice wall at her back and shouts once. It hits the wall and comes down over the roofs. She is inside before the glitter lands.

It is the only grown voice most of them ever hear, and they have views on it, as they would on weather. They say it was rounder before she lost the tooth. In the dry rooms, children make the shape of it with their mouths and no air.

For twenty years what she shouted was the bark. She can't say when it changed. At some point it began to be one of the words, whichever the day had been fullest of. A hard noise is a hard noise, and nobody could tell. She would lie awake afterward in a town holding the shape of *kettle*, or *weasel*, or the word for a wall that has begun to lean. That was enough for her.

Some evenings the mountain gets there first. A stone cracks on the scree just as the last hand goes up, the set runs through without her, and she is left in the doorway with her chest full. She takes the word to bed and uses it the next day, a day stale.

This year there is a boy. He lives on the low terrace by the lip and laughs like a dropped bowl. They send him up to her at the hot hour, as she was once sent to an old man whose face she can no longer get back. She meant to teach him the bark and nothing else. But he is seven, and she had been saying her words in that room too long to stop for company. He pointed at the lamp and signed *what*. She told him, and he said it back. Nothing had been said back on the shelf in a very long time.

He has about half of them now. He has also made two of his own, which she thinks are bad, and uses.

So the evenings are different. Tonight the hands go up from the lip to the headwall. She pushes the horn strip up onto her forehead and shouts the loach's word, the one that took a winter. It means staying in the ice with your eyes open until it is time to go on. The white runs out, and the ticks run down toward the lip and stop.

In a house down there, a boy who should be asleep hears it and knows what the town has just been told. He says it back, with no air in it, and turns over.
