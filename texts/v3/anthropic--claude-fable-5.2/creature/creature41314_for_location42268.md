The slab in the ring wall is warm.

Nothing else in the basin is. The grit is iron-hard a foot from open water and the air is cave air, yet the dished seat holds the heat of a body. A hand laid on it knows at once what the ring wall is. It is somebody's house, and they have only just got up.

There are eleven cairns. Stand beside any one of them and there are still eleven to be seen across the floor. Ten of those are cairns. The other is chest-high and grey and flat across the top, and it stands as still as they do, with rime down one side in feathers as long as a finger. From a distance nothing tells it from the rest.

It turns when anything comes near. It turns as the spider turns, all at once and squarely, and lifts both hands to the height of its shoulders with the palms out. The hands are empty. That is the whole of the greeting, and after it there is no taking her for stone.

She is small. Some of it is a stoop and some is not. She wears one coat of felted wool, undyed, the grey of the polygon centres, tied at the waist with a plaited cord, and her feet are bare on the frost. Her hair is black on one side and white on the other. The white is rime, and it is on the same side as the rime on the stones.

Her age does not add up. The hands are old, broad in the knuckle, with nails worn square and thick as horn and grey stone dust in the creases that will not wash out. The face has had no such use. It carries the lines of whatever expression is on it at the moment, and no others. Years have occurred to her. They have not been kept.

Cold occurs to her too, and hunger. A pouch of roasted meal hangs from the cord. She wets a pinch of it at the seep and eats it off her palm, and the pouch is no emptier. She is as hungry as someone an hour before a meal, and never hungrier than that.

What she does with her hands stays done. Nothing else here can claim that. The seep cannot fill the tarn and the north wall cannot add one stone to its fan, but a flat stone that she sets on another flat stone is there afterwards. She laid the ring wall and stacked the cairns. She has not done it lately. On one cairn a rosette of map lichen has spread across the joint between two stones, half on each, as wide as two spread hands.

The sound has left very little happening over, and she lives on that little as the spider lives on what blows up from below. She does one thing at a time. When she walks she walks, with her arms hanging and her eyes on the place she is going. When she stops to look, she has stopped. She makes a round of the cairns and lays a palm flat on each capstone. At one of them the spider comes out and lifts its front legs at her, and she lifts her hands back. She sweeps the floor of the ring wall with the edge of her hand, though nothing has fallen on it. The wall was laid for two. She sweeps both halves and lies down only in the one.

She does not speak. A greeting shouted at arm's length is felt in the shouter's throat and not heard, and she watches the mouth make it and gives no sign. A long while later, sitting on her slab, she nods. It is the nod of someone answering. What is said here comes round again in the roar, as the choughs' whistle does, and when it comes round she is listening.

Listening is her work. She sits on the slab facing the opening, with her hands in her lap and her head a little turned. The frost took a very long time to sort the stones of the floor, coarse to the rims and fine to the centres, and that sorting is finished. She is sorting the sound, and that is not finished.

The white pebbles on the wall are what she has got out of it so far, and she will show them to anyone who sits down in the swept grit beside her:

1. She touches the first with one finger, then points at the north wall and holds the point. After a time the crack of a slab letting go stands out from the weight of the rest, single and clean, like quartz on grey.
2. She touches the second and draws the finger slowly down the line of the fan.
3. For the third she taps a fingernail on the capstone three times, lightly.
4. For the fourth she cups her hand, blows across it and looks up at the col.
5. The fifth is the choughs. She waits until they reach the right place in their circle before she lifts her chin.
6. For the sixth she nods toward the seep and puts two fingers to her own wrist, and it is not clear which of the two she means.
7. For the seventh she holds a finger close to a feather of rime, not touching it.
8. The eighth is somewhere under the cloud.
9. For the ninth she touches her own mouth, then points at the mouth of whoever is beside her.

Each one comes clear for as long as she holds it, then goes back in.

At the gap she stops. She looks down at the tenth pebble in the grit, and she does not point anywhere.

It is the only thing her hands have set that has not stayed. Now and then she picks it up, and the rime on it goes to water in her palm. She sets it in the gap, a thumb's width from the ninth, and sits down again to listen. Later it is at the foot of the wall with the rime grown back. It is not seen to fall. She looks at it as she looks at the fan that does not grow, and leaves it where it is until the next time.

She keeps one ear toward the opening. Her shadow lies out through it ahead of her, alongside the shadows of the stones, toward the pallor behind the ridge. Whatever the tenth is, that is where she expects it from. In this place it would be heard before it was seen.
