Most of what is alive on the plateau at any moment does not live there. It came up the scarp that afternoon on the rim lift: midges, aphids, thrips, winged ants, spiderlings on their threads, pollen, grass seed from the lowlands. The day wind carries it inward over the stone, the cold takes it down, and by dawn it is stiff under a coat of rime. On a good day the fall amounts to a few bodies per square pace. That is the whole income of the place, and every resident is built around catching it or doing without.

The crust lichens do without. They grow on the floor against the larger stones, black on the side facing the interior, where the dawn frost forms on the stone above them, and rust-orange on the side facing the rim. The arrangement is the same at every stone, so anyone caught in a dust wall can find the direction of the centre by looking down. A patch the size of a coin has been growing for several thousand years. It spreads until it reaches the line where the stone's shelter ends, and there the wind keeps it trimmed.

The loose lichen is always a broken piece of another. Dry, a curl is rolled shut and hard as a nail paring, and it travels. Frosted, it opens. Between the sun reaching the rime and the rime leaving there are about forty minutes when the curl is damp enough to work, and it does all its living then. It breaks as it rolls, and each piece goes on. It has no other way of multiplying and needs none. There is a fair case for calling it one organism in several billion parts, spread over the whole floor and leaking steadily off the edge. Some of the smoke the lowlands see is lichen.

A cushion plant is a single plant, a dome of rosettes packed so tightly that a thumb will not dent it. Its taproot follows the seam down twenty times the cushion's width. It keeps its dead leaves inside, and they are its soil. The interior stays a few degrees warmer than the air and holds the dawn frost as actual wetness into the afternoon, a few drops, the only liquid water on the plateau. A kind of mite lives in there and nowhere else. Every few years the cushion opens flowers the size of pinheads, flush with its surface, and fertilises them itself. Nearly all the seed goes over the rim. Each plant is the one success among centuries of seed.

The spiders are the size of a grain of rice and the colour of the quartz gravel, and they are the only residents that look at anything. Each keeps a silk cell glued under a stone on the interior side. They come out when the light turns amber, because a veil of dust means air has arrived from the rim and something will be falling out of it. They watch the dust devils and go where a column has just let go. They take the dead as readily as the dying. Every step is made ahead of a dragline glued to the floor, and a spider that jumps without one is delivered, in time, to some other spider.

The air carries nothing for them, but the rock does. A male drums on the floor with his palps and the tip of his abdomen. Polished gneiss takes the signal ten paces along a band and rather less across it, and a female under her stone drums back. The plateau is silent to anything with ears and seldom quiet in the stone. Once he has found her, the rest is done by sight. He approaches on a zigzag with his forelegs raised, each tack shorter than the last, and she watches the whole approach from the mouth of her cell. A male who comes straight in is treated as food.

Whatever set the masts does not live here, and nothing visits them. The spiders have found a use for the ones on the softer bands. The gap beneath those is a finger wide, warm, and windless. The disc around the base keeps nothing loose, so a spider crosses it on a dragline and glues what it builds to the rock. Silk will not hold to the mast, and the cells under one have a floor and no attachment overhead. There is room for three or four. They are the best winter quarters on the open plateau, held by one female at a time and fought over. On the hard bands, where the gap is the thickness of paper, nothing fits yet.

The still places are different, and what lives in them has become different.

No face is windward there, so the frost grows on every side of everything at once. The loose lichen that rolls in stops for good. The top of each drift is alive. Underneath, the curls are brown and dead, and at the bottom they have gone to a dark crumb, a few handfuls per square, the only soil on the plateau outside a cushion. Seed that crosses the line drops at once, which is why the cushion plants crowd the edges. They grow to the size of a head there, and they flower every year.

Everything else in the air drops too. A spider in a square does not wait for amber light. The fall comes to it across all four lines, all day, and lies where it landed. These spiders are heavier than the ones outside, and slower. They live three or four years where a spider of the open floor lives one. Daughters build cells in the same drift as their mothers, and their daughters beside them.

They cannot drum. The powder carries nothing. A male in a square still tries, briefly, at the start of each season, and nothing answers. What he has instead is the surface, which keeps every step. His zigzag toward a cell in the lichen stays behind him after he has gone, a stitched line of tacks narrowing to a point. The next male to come across it follows it, since it leads to where a female was, and where her daughters are now. The line deepens and becomes a road.

The powder is laid in sheets. Each front leaves one, thinner than a fingernail, tan or pale or faintly rose depending on the quarter it blew from, and a square holds several hundred. After each fall the road shows only as a softened trace, and the spiders walk it back up through the new sheet before the impression is lost. Part the powder beside a road and the figure is there in every layer, a little displaced each time, all the way down. The lowest sheet rests on clean stone. Whatever leaves the square leaves from underneath, oldest first, and the oldest figure goes with it.

The figures have stopped being approaches. A female in a square watches from her drift as a female outside watches from her stone. What she watches for now is the road, walked tack by tack with no pit missed. No two squares have the same figure, the squares being days apart. In one, the road closes on a patch of empty powder four hand-spans short of the lichen, where the drift stood before it shifted. The males walk to the empty point, stop there, and only then turn and cover the remaining distance on a newer line.

Spiders from the open floor arrive now and then, dropped at the line by the night wind. A female finds a place in the drift. A male drums on the powder for a day or two. He is quick and lean and comes at the first cell he sees by the shortest way, and he is driven off or eaten. A few last long enough to find the outer end of the road. One of these will stand at it for some time. Then he sets a foot in the first pit, and the next foot in the next, and walks the figure through to the empty place.
