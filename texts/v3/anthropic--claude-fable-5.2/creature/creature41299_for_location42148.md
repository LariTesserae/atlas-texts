Tonight, whatever lives here is doing it without moving, so the answer has to be found by feel, the same way the place is.

The first thing a hand finds is a tree, and the trees are the obvious tenants. They are older than the line and perhaps half as old as the rock. The wind has allowed them three metres, so they have spent their centuries on girth and on reaching sideways, until every crown is gripped by its neighbours. None of them holds the ridge. Their roots run flat through the mat and stop where the stone begins. They stand on a floor made of their own dropped leaves, and they stay upright mostly because the lid is one piece. A tree here is less a thing rooted in a place than a post in a raft.

But the hand on the trunk is not touching bark. It is touching moss, and the moss is nearer the truth. The trees comb the cloud; the moss keeps it. On a ridge with no pool or trickle, it is the only reservoir, and nearly everything else that lives here lives in it or on its water. The filmy ferns have fronds one cell thick and cannot close themselves against dry air, so they exist only where the air never dries. The orchids came as seed fine as dust, and the lichens and liverworts as less than that. A few centuries ago this ground was glowing, and every resident descends from something that blew in and lodged in a crack. The wind taking the notch apart tonight is the wind that stocked it.

There are animals as well. The stillness is not absence. It is what residents do on a night like this, when anything that moves is leaving over the eastern lip. That is why no moths come to the violet beads. The fliers on this ridge are passing through, and what breeds here walks. There are beetles whose wing cases have fused shut over nothing. There are pale, long-feelered crickets that have never jumped higher than the mat is deep. Tonight they are jammed into the bases of the bamboo with their legs folded.

The house has two floors. Above is the wet one, the moss and root. Below, in the air gap and the loose clinker, is the only dry place on the mountain. A small furred thing lives between them, long in the nose and lighter than a wet leaf. Its runways through the mat are two fingers wide and pressed smooth by use. It hunts the crickets in the gap and sleeps lower, in a ball of shredded bamboo leaf between stones that would open a palm. It has felt its ceiling lift and settle all night. When the tree by the north shoulder went, one of its runways was cut mid-length and now opens onto wind. It heard the clinker go downhill like crockery. It has not moved, because moving is for when the roar stops arriving.

A bird is here, a metre off the ground on the lee side of a trunk, pushed into the moss sleeve where it meets a fork. It weighs less than the water in a handful of that moss. Its toes locked when its legs folded, so holding on costs it nothing. It has let itself cool by a few degrees and is spending as little as it can to reach morning. The false rain passes over it and does not get through its feathers. It learned the two-note hum above the canopy when it learned everything else, and it does not hear it.

And in the moss itself, nearer than any of these, is a frog the size of the orchids. This is the one the place has made most completely. It has never seen standing water and would have no use for it. It drinks through its belly, pressed to the sponge. Its eggs are laid in a wad of wet moss and guarded there, and the young come out as finished frogs no bigger than a bead of the drip. They skip the swimming stage, because there is nowhere to swim. On calm nights the males call, a small hard tick like two pebbles touched together, and the notch is full of it. Tonight no female could hear a call over the wind, so none is made. At six degrees the frog is barely more than a cold, patient part of the moss, with its eyes open.

Below the frog live the residents who outnumber all the rest, in the film of water on a single moss leaf. They are mites, springtails, and slow eight-legged specks that can dry to a grain and wait out a year. For them a bead on a leaf tip is a lake, and a gust is nothing at all. By the count of bodies, most of what lives in the notch is having an ordinary night.

The fallen tree is not finished either. Half its roots are still in the mat, on the standing disc. Trees of this kind tend to go on from where they lie, putting up a row of new stems along the trunk, each one starting again at the height of a finger. The disc's inhabitants now live on a wall. The water will drain differently, and over the next days they will walk down to the level or dry where they are. A tree fern will have the hole.

Nothing lives on the line. The nearest people are at its two ends. One of them watches a gauge at the dam, and others stand at the pots in the smelter. Their concern crosses the notch twenty metres up, at the speed of current, and does not stop. The orange smear seen through the tear in the cloud is where they sleep.

Toward grey light the gusts will start arriving with longer slack between them. The long-nosed thing will come up the cut runway, stop at the new edge, and press its face into moss to drink. The bird will warm, shake once, and begin picking along a twig as if the twig were all there had ever been. And from a sleeve of moss beside the hole in the lid, under the hum, there will be one tick, a wait, and an answer.
