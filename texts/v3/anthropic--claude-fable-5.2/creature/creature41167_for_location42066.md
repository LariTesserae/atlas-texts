The first answer is the crowd, and it is not wrong. The lizards live there, and the beetles, the birds, the moths, the browser and the grubs. But the lizard is floor that has not yet lain down, and the bird is a leaf that sews. They are the basin looking after itself, and that leaves the question open.

The second answer is in the measurements. The middle of the floor is lower than the rim by the height of a person. The fence comes to the chest. The tiles go down as far as an arm can follow them. The stems are as thick as a wrist, the tools fit a hand, and the grub is the top joint of a thumb. Nothing that walks the tiles has a chest or a wrist. The whole place has been cut to a body that is not in it, the way a coat is cut, and a coat says something about its wearer.

It is of ordinary height. It is right-handed, because the polish on the scraper's grip sits where a right palm would put it. It works sitting down, at the north rim, facing the basin with the fence at its back.

It wastes nothing. The roof is gut, the lizards are jointed with sinew, the air smells of tallow, the awls are bone and the tines are antler. No creature in the basin has any of those to give. So before the basin there was at least one animal with blood in it, and someone took it apart down to the last cord and found a use for everything. The browser is wicker, and may be a replacement.

It also once cut its thumb. The scar across the whorl is short and straight, the kind a chert flake leaves when it slips. This is the most particular thing known about it, and the strangest, because a scar is a cut that closed by itself. Nothing in the basin can do that. A torn leaf is stitched and a cracked seam is pitched. Whoever owns the thumb is made differently from everything that carries its mark.

A size does not live anywhere, though. The animals do not treat the bench as a place where someone is. They walk around it as they would walk around a pole. Whoever sat there seems to have gone into the basin the way that first animal went into the roof. The height went into the floor and the reach into the depth of the tiles. The thumb went to the grubs, which the lizards now press from the basin's own clay, so even the signature has stopped needing its owner.

The animals do treat one place as occupied, and that is the bowl.

The back of a hand held over the bowl finds it cool. Nothing else in the basin is cool. The tiles for a pace around it tick less often than the others, and the ones touching it do not tick at all.

The bowl is dry, and something is in it. A lizard that has had its head down comes up a shade darker at the throat and under the jaw, as fired clay darkens when it has taken up wet. The dark fades as it walks back to the digging. The browser moves without creaking for a few steps afterward, like wicker that has been soaked. The beetles are glass and nothing can change them, but they come anyway. Each sets its crumb of pitch on the rim, waits, and picks it up again. The moths never come. A moth that settled there would be a smear that stayed a smear, which is why the birds keep the ash away.

It is not water. Water would have left a line, and somebody would have had to make it. Everything else in the basin can be traced: the lizards come from the clay, the clay from the tiles, the tiles from the lizards, and the mark from the grub, which comes from the lizards again. Follow anything here and it leads round to itself. The bowl is where that stops. What is in it was not pressed, plaited, knapped, stitched or sung.

An animal can be taken apart for its gut and sinew and tallow, and one part will be left that cannot be scraped, twisted or rendered. The same would be true of whoever did the taking apart. That part has no use. In a place this thrifty nothing is thrown away, so it was given a bowl, at the exact spot where a person would have to stand to give the floor its depth. It is not possible to say now whose it was.

That is what lives there. It is small enough to fit in a bowl, and it has never stood higher or lower than it stands now. It makes no sound. It cannot be seen or lifted, and it cannot be carried except in a throat. It does no work, and it is the only thing in the basin of which that is true. What it touches does not sharpen. The rim has gone round under all those jaws.

It has the habits of the thumb. The green rush is still plaited, knot over knot, and still one row longer after each crossing of the bright panel. But its newest row is not pale and not stiff. It bends. A bird has stitched veins into its top leaf, as birds do, and by the next look the holes have closed around the cord.

It is getting to be more, slowly. The lizard that lay down beside the bowl has drawn its legs in, but its back has not flattened and its edges have not come up sharp. The beetles have arrived with their pitch and stand along its side in single file, waiting for a seam. A grub has been over it twice and the corner will not take the print. It is too soon to say what it is becoming, except that it is not a tile.

Then there are the birds. A bird that has drunk flies up to the lowest layer of leaves and opens its beak, and for once no twine drops. There is a sound instead, short and low, hardly louder than a tile ticking. The other birds come down to gather it. They walk the floor beneath the place where it was, turning their heads, and find nothing to pick up. Then one of them goes to the bowl.
