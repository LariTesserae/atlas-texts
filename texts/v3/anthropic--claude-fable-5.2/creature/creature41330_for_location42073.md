It is tempting to look for something large. The place seems built for it: forty kilometers of stone lifted into the weather, holes that swallow whole rivers, a boom now and then that carries from rim to rim. But the rock gives too little to keep anything large alive. The hawk only borrows the updraft. Below the hawk everything shrinks: swifts, hummingbirds, a frog to each bromeliad, toads the size of a thumbnail, midges. That slope ends on the surface of the rock, in the black.

The black is what lives here.

It passes at first for the color of the stone, but the stone is pink, as any fresh break shows. The black is a skin laid over it, about as thick as a coat of paint. It covers nearly every surface that light and water can both reach: the towers and pedestals, the walls of the slots as far down as daylight goes, the rim, and the cliff below the rim wherever water runs. In the rare dry hour it is matte and brittle, like scorched paper. Wet, which is its ordinary state, it swells into a film of jelly and becomes the most treacherous footing on the mountain.

Under a hand lens the skin turns into a crowd. There are threads of cells, each thread in a sleeve of jelly, branching and matted into tufts no taller than the nap on velvet. There are round cells in twos and fours, every one in its own clear capsule, the capsules nested inside larger capsules. Fungal strands bind some of this into lichen, and bacteria live on what leaks.

Looked at a little longer, the crowd is a pasture. Mites, springtails, rotifers, and water bears graze through it, animals a thumbnail toad would count as fine print. The toads walk the bare rock because the rock is not bare, and they pick the mites off it one at a time.

Looked at longer still, it is the ground everything else stands on. Quartz holds no nitrogen. The crust takes it out of the air, which almost nothing else up here can do. Nearly all the nitrogen in the peat, in the bromeliads' tanks, and in the frogs calling from them came in through a cell too small to see and was leaked, grazed, or washed into a crack.

The darkness is a roof. In the clear minutes the light at this height is violent, so the cells pack a dark pigment into their sheaths and live underneath it. That is why the rock steams when the sun breaks through.

It is also eating the mountain, slowly. While it works in the light, it turns the film of water against the stone faintly alkaline. Quartz shrugs at acid but gives a little to that, and a grain comes loose. Some of the white sand on the flats was let go this way.

It keeps no hours the animals would recognize. At dusk it stops, and at first light, whatever light the cloud lets through, it starts again. If it dries, it shuts and waits, then wakes within minutes of the next wetting. Lightning leaves a pale star on a tower, and the star is dark again in less than a lifetime.

When a slab peels off the rim, the new face stands pink for decades. The black returns first along the seeps, in stripes like ink run down a page, then closes between them. It has already taken most of what the surveyors left:

- **The plinth** at the upper station was grey when it was poured. Now only its corners tell it from the outcrop.
- **The weir** is black to its crest, and only the paint on the staff still holds it off.
- **The copper rain gauge** is the one thing that has beaten it. Sixty years of overflow have carried a trace of copper down the rock, and below the gauge a tongue of stone a meter or two long is clean pink.

That patch of absence is the whole mark the survey left on the resident. It will close when the copper is gone.

Its age is the strangest thing about it. The sand of this mountain was a beach almost two billion years ago, and this was on the beach. The cells were different, but they were the same makers of the same sheaths, lying in mats on the wet sand at the edge of that sea. Nothing else was there to lie on it: no weed, no shell, no worm.

It was old even then. Its kind had already spent some hundreds of millions of years putting oxygen into the air, and the oxygen had begun to rust the iron in the world's sand. That rust is the pink. The stone is the color it is, under the black, because of what the black did before the stone was stone.

So the tableland holds no fossils, and its oldest resident never needed to leave one. The sand was buried, pressed, lifted, and worn back into the rain, and the crust was on the surface when it arrived, as it has been on every wet, lit surface since. Of everything on the summit, only the rock and its skin are what they were a hundred million years ago.

Toward evening, when the cloud lifts off whole and the other tablelands stand out along the horizon, each of them is black too. The same thing lives on all of them. It crosses the gaps in the wind, a few cells at a time, and it has had long enough. Then the cloud comes back over the rim and wets it, and it goes on with what it was doing.
