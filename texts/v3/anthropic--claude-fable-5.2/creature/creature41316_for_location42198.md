By the knotted cord at the first stack there are a hundred and nine people on the shelf. They are never seen as a hundred and nine. Outdoors each one stands on a flag with a body-length of grey air on every side. A watcher at the cliff foot sees a figure bent over a basin, another thirty paces on cutting heather, a third at a door reading a cord with her fingers, and takes them at first for more of the towers.

They are short and broad in the foot, and they move slowly. A quick step sends a quick adjustment through everything nearby, and hurrying the shelf is the first thing a child is taught not to do. Their hands go bare in all cold, because the hands are for speaking, and the palms are crossed with fine white lines from the back wall and from splitting stone. The inside of every sleeve is left undyed, pale as an underwing, so that a sign made in fog can be read from the next stack. At that distance a sign takes the whole forearm. Across a body-length it shrinks to the fingers. Between two people of one household it shrinks again, to a turn of the wrist nobody else can read.

Their names are signs, and most are whatever the hands do oftenest. The woman who relays slumped stacks is two flat palms set down one after the other. The boy who combs wool from the heather is a rake of bent fingers. The oldest woman is a hand pointed up the back wall toward the old ledge.

Behind the slab they are different. The rooms are too low to stand in. A household sleeps in one heap around the heated stones, legs across legs, and the whisper goes from mouth to ear with no air between. A whisper has no pitch, so nobody on the shelf knows whether any of them can sing. Laughter is breath. Grief is also breath, and longer. The room is built against the stack the way the basin is set under it, and the same permission holds in both.

A child belongs to its mother's body until it is weaned. Outdoors it rides on her back and the animals allow the two of them one space, as they do for a doe with a fawn at heel. Then, on a morning she chooses, she carries the child out onto the flags, sets it down, and steps back her own length. Nobody steps forward. The nearest hares shift, then the voles beyond them, and the widening runs out to both ends of the shelf and comes to rest. After that the child has a sign, and a space that will grow as it does.

Their sign for themselves is cupped hands, which is also the sign for basin. Each dawn, once the fog is over the lip and the stacks have begun to tick, they skim the drowned moths from the water and lay them on the roof slabs to dry. That is most of what they eat, with heather tips, the one egg, and whatever the cold has killed. They reline cracked clay. They count the salamanders on each stack, because a sound stack holds the same number every morning, each a length from the next. When the count falls, a stone has shifted somewhere inside and closed a face. They cut the paths back, bridge the year's crack, and comb the heather. They kill nothing. A person who stops on a path feels the shelf settle around the stop, in the skin of the back, without turning to look.

Asked who lives here, a keeper sweeps one arm from the cliff to the lip and only then, last, touches her own chest.

The rock-sitters are first onto every fallen block and sit with their old men's hands folded, one to a facet. The deer fold down at dusk into the cushion plants, one deer to a cushion, and the cushion keeps the dent all day. There are forty owls for forty-three stack-tops, and the three bare stacks are the three the builders expect to relay next.

The foxes are lean because of how hunting goes here. A fox walks in among the hares and the hares do not bolt. They slide away ahead of it, holding its length exactly, as everything else slides for them. The fox takes the one that cannot slide, the one that has put a foot on an arris under the moss. The rust-colored line leads to it. So the hidden edges do the choosing, the fox closes the space, and the ring widens and resettles. Nothing on the shelf is fat, and very little starves.

None of them holds the spacing by effort. A hare does not glance about to measure. Watched for long enough, it looks less as if the hare keeps its pocket of air than as if the pocket were there already and the hare stood in it.

The people have reasons to think the space comes first. The evening before a block comes off the back wall, the creatures under that part of the cliff draw off from a patch of moss the shape of nothing in particular, and the nearest household carries its bedding to another room. In the morning the block is lying in the cleared place. When a woman is near her time, the snails leave the flags at her door, a second body-length's worth.

When someone dies they carry the body to the lip, set it on the last flag, and step back, the same way they pass a bowl. The dawn fog comes over. When it clears the flag is bare. Nobody has seen an animal come up, and nobody has seen this either. For some days afterward a gap of the right size goes on being held at that person's door, where they used to stand to read the cord. Then one morning there are snails on it.

So they do not call the spacing a rule. They have no sign for it at all, unless the stepping back is the sign: setting a thing down and giving it room.

One space on the shelf has never had anything in it. It lies midway along, between the first stack and the lip. It is round, and it would take eleven paces to walk from its edge to its middle if anyone walked it. The moss there is as deep as anywhere and carries no rust-colored lines and no snail tracks. The path from the first stack to the lip was laid straight. It bows now, and in the oldest woman's lifetime the flags have been lifted three times and set further out. At dusk, when the moths come in and the towers look furred, the air above that ground stays clean, a column going up as far as the fog allows. After dark the copper pinheads stop at its edge.

At dusk, when the fog drains back and the shelf goes to the basins, the hares cross that ground without swerving. It is the only hour they do. At the first stack's basin, where the oldest woman kneels, the animals press in three deep, flank to shoulder, and on her left hand there is a place one drinker wide that nothing takes. Behind it a lane stays open through the packed bodies, straight back from the clay, eleven paces long.

She does not look at it. She drinks from her cupped hands, and when she has finished she leans a little to the right, as one does to let a neighbor up. The lane closes from the far end forward. Out between the stack and the lip, the hares stop crossing.

The children ask her what it is, in the small household signs. She gives them the account she gives everyone: her mother on the old ledge, the rock bare and still warm, the animals already standing on it, evenly, each alone. Her mother could not have told a kept space from an empty one that morning. It was all bare rock. What she could see from up there was which way they were facing.
