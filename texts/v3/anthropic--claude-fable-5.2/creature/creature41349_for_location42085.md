What lives there has no heat of its own. That is the condition of residence, and it leaves a short list.

The frog comes first. It is the length of a thumbnail and the colour of the rock when the rock is wet, which here is always. Its skin has the lava's faint gloss. It is found by its eyes or not at all, two dull copper beads set wide on a head that is nearly half the animal. It weighs so little that it can sit on the rim of a collapsed blister, on an edge that would open a palm, and the edge does nothing to it.

There may be sixty. Whoever built the bench seems to have tried counting frogs and settled for counting calls.

The frog is exactly as hot as the air, the rock and the pools. Anywhere else, an animal of this kind spends its days on errands of warmth, moving into the sun and out of it, pressing its belly to a stone that has kept the afternoon. Here there is no such errand. A frog on bare rock at midday and a frog under a red-backed leaf are in the same condition. So they sit in the open, anywhere, with an indifference that looks like tameness until a hand comes close.

It still has one sense of cold, and the only use of it is to say where the edge is. For an arm's length inside the veil the moss is cooler, because the water there has just come off the boundary. Frogs do not sit on that moss. They keep back from the front and the sides at a distance they all seem to agree on.

The rear edge is the only one that comes to them. The sides slide past and the front walks away uphill, but the back of the garden advances by less than a frog's length each night. A frog that has chosen a crack and kept to it, as frogs do, will find itself one evening on cool moss without having moved. Most shift forward. A few stay a night too long, and the black bird comes for those.

The bird is not a resident. It carries its own heat in through the veil and cannot put it down, since nothing inside is cooler than it is. It stays as long as it takes to walk the rear margin. It has never been seen to take a living frog. Possibly it cannot see them.

The gnats are simpler. They rise from the pools, hang over them and are eaten. They do not bite, or have not bitten the one person available.

The call is a single dry note, like two pebbles knocked together once. A clap of the hands explains why it is single. The dome returns every sound late and a little lower, so a frog that called twice would be calling over a deeper version of itself. One note, then waiting. Sometimes another frog answers. From the bench it is not always possible to tell which kind of answer it was, and it is not certain the frogs can tell either.

They lay in the bowls nearest the front, which hold the poorest water in the garden. The rock there is new and carries only a film of algae and the gnat larvae. The tadpoles are black, slow and few. The bowls in the middle are rich with leaf rot, and almost nothing lays in them. No frog needs the front for itself. A bowl takes about thirty years to cross from the leading edge to the rear, and a frog lives perhaps four. But a line that lays where it hatched is carried backward a few paces every generation until it reaches the cool moss. Lines that stayed are in the wake now, in the bird or in the soil. Every frog inside descends from frogs that went forward to lay.

Finding the front is not difficult. Water crosses the rock in thin sheets, all of it running downslope toward the back. A froglet leaving its bowl turns into the flow and goes. Small frogs do this anywhere. The water explains uphill, but it does not explain why the bowls in use each season sit a little to the north of the centre line.

So what lives in the place is partly sixty small animals and partly a procession. It is a hundred paces long and climbs a few paces a year. Every walker in it is replaced within a few years, and none of them travels farther in its life than the length of the garden. It has gone on long enough to outlast three of the stones. The ridge is more than a century off at this rate.

The person who built the bench does not live there and could not. A human body is the one thing inside that is cooler than the air, and the garden treats it as it treats the boundary. Water forms on the skin and runs. This goes on for perhaps twenty minutes. When it stops, the body has come up to the temperature of everything else, and it is time to leave, quickly, into a wind that freezes wet cloth stiff. Just outside the moss ring, on the downwind side, lies a flat lava plate that is not one of the fourteen markers. Nothing is under it at present. If each stack in the wake stands for one sitting, everything known about the frogs was learned in under five hours.

This season the frontmost bowl in use is one whose rim came inside about two years ago. A hand's length from it is the liverwort that was not there last month. Under the rim, just below the waterline, is a clutch of eleven eggs, each one clear with a black centre the size of a sand grain. A male sits above them on the glass, facing the ridge. At dusk he gives his one note. Something answers, late and a little lower, and then the rain starts, as hot as he is.
