The first brown thing in the basin is a leaf. It lies on bare marl a little way inside one of the plazas, curled, tan along the midrib, some days dead. At the plaza's edge, an arm's length from it, rootlets comb the air and cannot cross. Anywhere else in the town it would have been netted and taken back before it wilted. Here it has been left to dry.

The plazas hold in other ways too. Inside one, a shell throws its shadow away from the sun and nowhere else. Twenty paces across is twenty paces back. The cracks in the floor can be counted from either end and come to the same number. And when a plaza drifts off and is found again two districts away, it has the same floor: the same plates of marl, the same scallop by the same corner, the same leaf. The silence does not pass over the ground. It brings its ground with it.

The town is not its own tenant, and says so with every door. These are the tenants. There are nine of them, or eleven by another way of counting.

None has a body beyond an outline. Each is a straight-sided patch of quiet, open to the sky, which from the scarp looks like a well of sunlight sunk in the roofs. Each keeps its own number of sides and its own proportions, and can be known by them. Sound goes in at the edge the way light goes in at a pupil. A pupil is black because what enters does not come back out, and these are silent for the same reason. Whether anything is done with the noise in there cannot be told from outside, and from inside there is nothing to go on.

They move at about the pace of a person carrying a full bowl. They do not use the doors. The wall in front of one is drunk dry by its neighbours, goes to paper and falls away from it, and the ground behind is paved with stems in minutes, so that it crosses the town as a clearing that travels. Shadows in the basin lean toward growth, and there is no growth inside, so every culm at the rim throws its shade outward. At any hour each of them sits in a ring of shadows pointing away from it, as though it were giving off the light.

They can be told apart:

- **The seven-sided one:** It is the largest, ninety metres across, and keeps to the waterfront, where it lies for days with one long side laid along the quay. Its floor carries more than the others: a drift of brown leaves in the north corner, and plates of marl whose rims have begun to round. Nothing else on the shelf has had time to round. Its floor is older than the ground it sits in.
- **The sliver:** It is two hands wide and forty paces long. It travels point first and prefers the broadest avenues.
- **The five small ones:** These are triangles, none bigger than a tabletop. They go in file, faster than the rest, and the town springs up in the gaps between them and is cut down again by the next in line, so that they pass through a district like stitches. They cross the market every day.
- **The pentagon:** It lives at the foot of the tower that stands in three districts, and so it lives in all three, with the same five sides, the same floor and the same two cockles. A leaf that blows into it in one district is lying in it in the other two.
- **The newest:** It has four sides and has not yet settled on their lengths. It was first seen just inside a gate, and so far it is the one sign that the gates are for anything. Its floor is unbroken salt, like the shelf outside. Out there, where it is silent all the way to the water, it would have had no edge. Whatever it was on the shelf, it was not one of anything until the noise closed round it.

They keep to the places a town has. One will stand a whole morning in the market, and the stalls push up all round it and not in it, and the roar goes on about a hole the exact shape of a customer. Fruit that rolls over the edge stays where it stops, since the town cannot fetch it back. It wrinkles and darkens in the sun and goes sweet, and that, as far as can be seen, is how they take it. They settle over the wells and stay through the heat. One or another is generally in the dry channel, moving down it toward the waterfront.

They also go to the unbuilt sounds. When a tolling starts in empty air, one of them is usually at the foot of it within the hour, and while it stays, nothing can come up there. The bell goes on ringing from nothing. When the resident moves off, the tower climbs, the gourd is hung, the gourd never moves, and the tolling has gone to another part of the sky. It is hard to watch this many times and not conclude that they prefer the bell to the tower, and are keeping it the only way it can be kept.

A person can walk into one. The noise comes off the chest like a hand lifted. The seven-sided one does not appear to notice, and goes on with the visitor aboard, the walls ahead thinning and folding without a sound. The newest stops dead and will not move until it is empty. The pentagon turns. One side swings past, and the visitor is standing in the din again, having been set down.

The town knows none of this. It has one sense, and they are the places that sense does not reach. Yet it touches them all day along every side. Each thorn and leaf-tip at a rim stops on the line, so that the outline of every resident is drawn exactly, in green, wherever it goes. The bract windows that face a plaza show the roofs from high above with a pale shape cut in them, and the veins that look nearly like writing run thickest across those panes. Then the town grows a door a hand high, and a door that would admit a ship. It does not know the shape of an inhabitant, and it is the only thing that gives them one.

One of its guesses is right. The street of doors too narrow for anything but light would fit the sliver to a finger. The sliver passes the end of that street most days and has never turned in. Nothing can tell the town so, and the street will come down with the next front like every other guess.

Toward evening, when the wind drops, they stop where they are. The fog comes over the wall and the town drinks it off every surface, but in the plazas there are no surfaces, and the fog stands in them undrunk. For an hour each resident is a block of white with its own number of sides, as tall as the roofs, and this is the one time the whole of it can be seen. Then the fog settles into the marl. In the morning their floors are dark with damp, the only wet ground in the basin, and the rootlets are massed along every edge. The town has sunk wells thirty metres for what lies a hand's width from it.

By morning the streets run differently. No wall is where it was, and nothing standing is older than the night. The seven-sided one is on the waterfront. The small ones are going in file toward the roar of trade, which has moved. The town in the basin this morning has never stood before. It is the same town as yesterday's only because the same few are living in it.
