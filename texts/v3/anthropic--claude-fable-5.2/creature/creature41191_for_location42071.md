People live there, and they are the smallest part of what does. A household gives its size as a yard: so many of the tall breed, so many of the small, so many pairs in the wall. The people are understood to be however many it takes to keep that going, usually eight or ten.

They are deep through the chest and short in the stride. They walk as the herd walks, flat and unhurried, the whole foot set down at once, and they breathe through the nose. Nobody runs. A child who tries is watched with interest by the adults, to see how far it gets.

Their faces are dark from the sun and shine a little with the lanolin they rub in against the dry air. The hands say more. Clinker opens everyone sooner or later, and by adulthood both palms carry a fine pale hatching. Builders have it to the elbow. Ice cutters have thick knuckles and ridged nails. A knapper's scars are few, straight, and thin as hairs, because obsidian cuts clean and closes clean. Two people meeting for the first time look at each other's hands before they look up.

They wear felt in layers, left the colors the animals came in. Dye is kept for ear tassels and debt cords, where a color has to mean something. The one bright thing on a person is the copper pin at the shoulder, and it is always older than whoever wears it.

Indoors they talk low and close, and at length, since the pot will take all morning anyway. Across any distance they use their arms. Arm talk is large and plain, made with the whole limb against the sky from the top of a dome, and it can be read four or five compounds off. It has no way to whisper. Whatever is said by arm is said to the quarter, so it runs to tallies, weather, births, and insults meant for everyone's enjoyment. Anything delicate waits until someone can walk over and sit down. They are blunt on the roofs and roundabout by the fire, and a stranger who has met them only one way has the wrong idea of them.

News crosses the city roof to roof, in the clear hours between the smoke lifting and the cloud building. A death on the southern front is known at the northern edge in three days.

Everyone has two names. The spoken one is given by the household. The other is a shape made with the arms, given by the neighbors, usually for something done before the age of seven, and it cannot be refused. A grandmother with two hundred animals in her yard may still be, on the roofs, the one who fell in the pit water.

They hum. It costs less breath than singing. They do it treading tubers, shearing, and walking the herd in, closed-mouthed and through the nose, pitched a little under the animals. At dusk it is hard to tell from outside a yard which of them is making the sound.

With no center, nobody rules from one. What a person belongs to is a tube. Those who drink from the same ice answer to each other and to no one else. They marry out of it and carry their dead out of it. Each year after the last hard cold, the mark is pecked by people from the next branch over, who gain nothing if it is set low. They are given a feast of bench rodents, and they go down with a lamp of herd fat and a hammerstone and come up without saying much. Whatever they pecked, the quarter cuts to it.

"Cutting low" has become the word for every kind of greed. It is said of someone who shears too close before the cold, or eats before the old have, or bargains hard with a household that had a bad lambing.

The opposite has no name, because it is only what is done. Children learn it on the wall tops in late autumn, with the cutters whistling at them. One handful for the stalls, two left for the wall. It is the first counting most of them do. Later they find everything is taken that way. Squab are lifted from some niches and not from others. Fleece is left long on the belly. The ice has its mark. None of this is counted as virtue. It is how a wall is kept tenanted, and a tenanted wall is the thing they admire most.

They judge each other the same way. Under every lintel hangs the household's cord, with its debts knotted in and dyed by kind: wool, ice, labor, a lintel lifted, a body carried. Anyone ducking in can read it, and does. A cord thick with knots in both directions means a household with a great deal living in it. A bare cord is pitied. To owe nothing and be owed nothing is what they mean by poor.

The quarters differ. Along the southern front the soil is forearm-deep and the walls are furred orange, and the people are careful, slow to agree, and proud of ice they can no longer cut much of. On the east the frost households get up in the dark. They own stones by the thousand and spend their days restacking them, and their quarrels are about cairns that moved in the night. The rest of the city says the easterners drink everyone's soup, and says it by arm, on clear mornings, for the pleasure of it. The easterners answer that someone has to.

In high summer, pairs go up the mountain's flank for obsidian and come back with their hands wrapped. They are asked what they saw, and they say what such pairs have always said, that the thread of steam was there and was the same.

The oldest stories are older than the rock and say so. They have people cooking on ground that now lies ten arm's reaches under the quarters. They are told in winter, indoors, in the low voice. Arm talk has no shape for the mountain. To mean it, a person lets both arms fall and stands still, and whoever is reading understands.

After the graupel, half the people go out with the herds. On the rim grass there are no roofs to stand on and no walls to keep a voice in. They sleep among the animals behind low crescents of stone left from other years, and they talk aloud, more than they do at home, to people from tubes they would never otherwise meet. Most marriages are agreed out there and knotted on the return. The ones who stay behind are the old, the smallest children, the weavers and knappers, and whoever watches the ice. They have the city nearly silent for the autumn. They lift the tubers and take the third from the stacks. When the pit water skins over, the herds come back along the bands, and for some days the two halves are a little shy of each other.

They count time in lichen. When a child is born, a fresh-broken stone is set in the yard wall, black face out. People in their twenties are still called black. By the time they have grandchildren their stone has crusted gray-green, and so, it is said, have they, and it is meant kindly. No one lives to see their own stone flower. The orange belongs to the fifth generation back, which is about the distance at which names stop being spoken and a person becomes part of the wall. Nobody would scrape a wall for dye. Lichen for the dye pots is gathered out on the open bands.

When someone dies, the news goes north ahead of them. Each quarter carries the body across its own ground and hands it on at the open band to the next, who are already waiting. A person from the southern front passes through the hands of dozens of quarters of strangers, and each one earns a knot on the household's cord. The pin comes back down the same way.

The platforms stand on bare lava just past the last domes, and since the last domes move, the platforms move. Those who clear clinker for a new compound find white splinters in it, where the rust-breasted birds dropped what they carried. The splinters go into the rubble core with everything else. For as long as it has been a city, it has been walking uphill behind its dead.

At the northern edge this year there is a woman called, on the roofs, by the shape for a spilled basket. She is still black by the reckoning, and so is everything she owns. Her dome closed before the graupel. It took six neighbors to seat the lintel column, and that is one knot on a cord that has four. Another is for seed tubers from the quarter below. Another is for the doves, which came up from her mother's wall in two baskets, eleven days on the back of a tall-breed animal. The fourth is owed to her, by a household whose yearling she found on the open band and walked home. She is pleased with that one out of all proportion.

Her soil is a hand deep. Her arms are hatched to the elbow. The tube under her yard has ice nobody has cut. She has been down to look at it, and she says by arm that it is bluer than her mother's, which is a boast and is received as one. After the cold, people from the next branch will come and peck the first mark in it, at the height the ice stands, with clean wall above.

The doves have settled. Finches found the smoke hole within the month. The cutters are another matter. They cannot be carried and will not stay if they are. They come along the walls by themselves from the older compounds, or they don't.

Every few days this autumn she climbs up to look along the wall top for cut grass laid out to dry. In the mornings the smoke lies flat over the city to the south and stops about where her wall does. To the north there is the mountain, with nothing between. She looks at the thread of steam, and then at the wall top.

When a stack appears it will be the first on that wall, and the first stack on a new wall is left whole.
