Four hundred and six, by the ice clerk's count. Hers is the only count that has to be right, because ice is issued by weight and by head.

You do not see them at first. They are the colour of the place, with grey felt and pale dust in the hair and the creases of the face, and they move at about the speed of the bucket chain. A newcomer looking across the gap sees catwalks, cable and haze, and then notices that parts of it are climbing.

**Hands**

The first knuckles of the right hand carry a flat, faintly polished callus from the brass rail. The fingertips are crosshatched with fine white cuts from the rock edges, which never get the chance to close. Everyone wears a felt-wrapped spanner on a lanyard, including people who are not riggers, since a loose bolt belongs to whoever sees it.

They touch brass before they touch a switch, a cat, a sleeping child, or each other. Two people meeting on a catwalk put their knuckles to the rail and then to one another's. The young sometimes skip the rail on purpose and take the spark on the lip.

**Voices**

They have voices and keep them for inside the cells, with the door dogged shut. Even there, talk is low and made at the front of the mouth. Laughing is done with the shoulders. A cough is the thing people fear most in their own bodies, and everyone carries a pad of felt for it. People who work the separator level hold their teeth slightly apart to stop them buzzing and keep the habit elsewhere, so that a third of the town always looks about to speak.

Each person has a knock of three to five raps, given by their first crew inside a month. That is the name that travels. Mouth-names are for the cell. The ice clerk is two slow beats, like blocks being set down. When someone dies, or goes down for good, their knock is sent once along the whole length of the crack and is not given again.

**Pace**

A new arrival hurries, because below everyone hurries. After a dozen rungs he is sitting grey-faced on a landing while the traffic parts around him. People who have been here for years have taken out every spare motion. They do not gesture or turn the head when the eyes will do, and they set a tool down where the next hand will want it. From across the gap it looks like calm. Close up, the hands are moving very fast in very little space.

The ropeway hands are the exception. They work on the rim in goggles and face wraps, inside the shriek of the dynamos. They are a little deaf, and their voices have grown to match. When they come down the ladders, people lay two fingers on their sleeve, which means *lower*.

**Sleep**

Three people share each bunk, one after another, and may go years without seeing each other awake. They know one another by the dent, the warmth left in the felt, and what is kept under the bolster. That might be a heel of ration, or a wallcreeper's feather found in the nets, which is the only red thing a person here can own. The tie is recognised. Bunk-kin stand for each other in disputes and inherit each other's ice flask.

Between bunk and shift there is the transformer ledge. People sit with their backs to the warm casings and their flasks melting beside them while the choughs go through their pockets.

Cells are moved with the sleepers inside. The riggers unbolt, slide and re-bolt, and the sleepers wake a metre west of where they lay down, with the door facing a different piece of the vein. They do not remark on it, and there is usually a cat that rode through too.

**Trades**

- **Wedge-turners.** They are the fewest and the most regarded. After each quarter turn they lay a palm flat on the stone and wait. They are the only people in town who see their own breath.
- **Riggers.** They carry the present shape of the town in their heads, since no drawing holds it. At shift change they hand it over by rap: what moved, what is loose, which joint is near the end of its travel.
- **Greasers.** The roller grease also goes on split lips, so everyone tastes faintly of machine.
- **The glazier.** She cements the glass slips and keeps a ledger of every one that has broken. Places do not stay put, so she records each break as a distance from the east cell, the only thing in town that has never been shifted.
- **The moth-brusher.** At present he is a man whose lungs no longer manage ladders at working speed. He makes a slow round of every bulb with a soft brush, and snowfinches follow him for what falls.

**The eleven minutes**

All three shifts are awake for it. The sun comes down the north face, the vein goes white with its black blades standing in it, and the drills start. Everyone stands at the rails with their faces up, watching the walls. Under the bulbs they all look alike. In the sun you can see who has got old.

Under the noise of the drills some of the mouths are moving, and nobody asks what is said. It is the one time a voice adds nothing to what the rock is already being asked to bear.

**The born**

Seventeen people have been born in the crack. The eldest is twenty-three and a rigger. None of them has ever shouted, and they are not certain they could. They do not flinch at the snap. The only falling water they have seen is a spilled cup, which everyone watches until the dust has it. At about eight years old each is walked to the east end and shown the cell with the creased door, and nothing is explained.

They are the clearest instance of what lives here, which is less the four hundred than a single habit. When a slip breaks, the wedge-turner's palm stays on the stone, the brush stays raised, the cat on the bunk stays flat, and the choughs stand fixed along the cable trays. Nobody counts aloud. They all arrive at thirty within a breath of each other, and a knuckle on the rail says *held*.

The eldest of the born went down once, at nineteen, in an empty bucket. She was gone eleven days. She reported that water ran along the ground with nobody weighing it, and that people took hold of each other without earthing first. In a kitchen someone dropped a pan. She was the only one in the room who went still, and she stood there to thirty while the talk went on around her.

She came back up with the ice.
