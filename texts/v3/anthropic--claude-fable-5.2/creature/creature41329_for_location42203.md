You meet them the way you meet everything here, in pieces. First a slate pattern somewhere ahead, then breathing, then the smell of lime and warm fat on cloth. Then a lamp comes round a corner and there is a face and a pair of hands with nothing between them.

Look at the hands first, as they do. They are wide for the wrists that carry them, grey to the second knuckle with lime that no longer washes out. The thumbs are spatulate, the pads pressed flat by marking brick. A young mason's print is still sharp. An old one's ridges have been eaten away by lime, and the thumb leaves a smooth oval like the back of a spoon. On one middle knuckle, everyone past infancy wears a cap of beaten copper tied on with felt cord. This is the knocker, the nearest thing to a possession anyone has. Its rap on brick is that person's voice at any distance greater than an arm.

The faces are pale and unsurprised. The eyes work, but they are used the way you might use a little finger. A mason testing a wall shuts them and lays her cheekbone to the brick. A man talking to you looks at nothing, with his head tilted to give you his ear. To be stared at is to be shouted at, and nobody shouts either. A raised voice smears against the lid and comes back as mush, so speech is kept low and close, and is mostly for jokes, complaints, and endearments. Anything that matters is knocked.

They are small people, narrow through the shoulder, and nobody can say whether the lanes made them so or they made the lanes. They walk with one hand trailing the wall, the knocker ticking against it every few steps without thought, the way you might hum. Their clothes are a dun felt beaten from the cocoons the tallow moths leave in the stores. It smells of fat and sheds the rain about as long as a rain lasts. They eat the bloom. What the crews scrape off the mortar is pressed and dried on the kiln shoulders into grey wafers that taste of chalk and faintly of mushroom. There are moth grubs, fried. It is not much, and they eat it slowly, and they are thin.

A name is a knock, a handful of beats with at least one rest in it. A mother raps patterns on the wall beside her infant for many firings, and the first one the child raps back is its name. Age is asked as courses: how many have you laid. Children too young to lay are useful anyway. Their flat hands are the gauge between leaning walls, and they are sent into gaps no adult can enter, to knock from the far side and report. They learn the rules there, with brick against both shoulders. When a hand grows too thick for the narrowest gauge, it is given a mallet.

After that a person belongs to a crew, though loosely. The kiln tenders are the only ones with colour in their faces. They have singed brows and a squint, they talk too loud, and the rest of the town thinks them slightly deaf and slightly lucky. The haulers are soft-footed and patient. You will find one standing in a lane with a load of wet brick on her back, her head turning by degrees as she sorts forty patterns for the one that is hers. Then she sets off without having opened her eyes. The cistern keepers sit on the rims with their feet hanging in, and the newts come to their ankles. They are the only people here ever touched by an animal on purpose, and they are a little proud of it.

None of this is settled. People are treated like the mortar. A hauler can be walked into a kiln crew over a few firings if she leans that way, and a name can gain a beat after a bad fall or a good marriage. A quarrel between households is thought of as a wall that came out wrong, to be wetted and wedged and moved a hand's width at a time. Their word for a stubborn person is the word for brick fired too hard, which rings beautifully and breaks when moved.

The callers are the ones the rest arrange themselves around, though they are not chiefs and get no larger share. You know a caller by the stripe of callus on the inner forearm, where the count was struck into her as a pupil. The old ones carry all of it, and it runs in them whether they attend to it or not. Their fingers go on tapping in their sleep. When two of them settle a dispute, the one who falters says nothing, gets up, and goes to one of the doorless rooms. She climbs down the ladder and knocks the count through alone, with her own echo coming back from four walls, until the echo stops correcting her. Sometimes that takes several rains.

Watching them, you begin to think the count is what lives here and the callers are its lodging. It passes from forearm to forearm the way the rain passes down through the floors, by whatever gap is open.

That thought gets stronger when you learn what becomes of the dead. Animals are few, and a newt renders no fat. The tallow in the saucers comes from the people. So do the long bones the floor crews tamp with, which keep their knock-names. A crew will tell you without ceremony that someone's mother is still on the floors. The rest goes to the kilns and comes out as lime. A person ends as light, as a tool, as the grey between two bricks, and as some thousands of thumbprints shut away through the courses. The face you first saw was lit by a face. They do not find this hard. The town is made of the town, and they have never held themselves to be an exception. When a moth drops into a lamp and spits, whoever is nearest raps once on the saucer rim. Ask why and they shrug, because it is only manners.

The grinders on the eastern side know all this best, since they spend their shifts taking it apart. They are quiet even for here. They turn over every brick that comes up and fit a thumb into the dent before it goes to the trough, as a greeting. Mostly the dent is too wide or too narrow. Now and then it fits, and the grinder holds the brick a moment longer, then breaks it like the others. Near the bottom of the workings the dents begin to turn up on the wrong face, and there the grinders stop, as the rule says. They will not tell you whether the prints on that side ever fit. They knock the floor of the pit at the start of every shift, listen with their eyes shut, and go to work on something higher up.

It is not hard to guess who was down there. Somebody pushed those courses up from beneath, thumb against the underside, until there came a course where they stopped reaching up and began pressing down. There they climbed through, stood on their own ceiling, and called it a floor. They have been building in the same direction ever since. Nine storeys on, the basin is full to the brim, and their heads are under the top slab the way their thumbs were once under the bottom one.

That leaves the one that walks on the lid. When its tread starts in the west, every person in the town puts down what they are holding and lays a palm flat on the nearest wall. It looks like fear and it isn't. Watch an old caller while it crosses. Her free hand is still moving against her thigh. She is counting, and the heavy steps overhead fall where the count has rests.

A mason knocks a wall before she trusts it, and something is knocking this one. It comes in the still hours, when nothing is ticking, and crosses without hurry, the way she would, listening to what comes back. The people hold still for it as a course of brick holds still, so the note will be clean. They cannot hear the note themselves, as no wall can. They know only that the tread goes on to the rim and does not turn back, and they take that as an answer for now.

So they are masons second. First they are the inside of a wall that is still being asked. The closing of lanes, the regrinding of the old town into a denser one, and the thumb in every brick are all a long effort to ring true when struck.

By their own rule, one brick in the lid must be laid dry. Children ask where it is. The callers answer by taking the child's forearm and striking a few beats of the count from far beyond anything yet built. Then they stop and let the arm go.

When the tread has passed, there is a sound before the slate that is easy to miss. All over the town, in the dark, knuckles lift and come down once, copper on brick, as each person asks the wall under their own hand. The answer is several thousand notes, tight and high. Then somebody strikes a slate and the count resumes.
