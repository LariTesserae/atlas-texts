Your first guess will be the thing in the sump, and it is a fair one. Kneel at the grating through a few strokes. It is pale, as thick as a thigh, and long enough that you never see both ends at once. When the water drops it rolls and shows a flank, and when the water comes back it settles. It has no fins, and no head either. Leave it for now.

Nearly everything else you can name is a lodger. The crickets, the beetles, the ants, the frogs, and the bats in the six front rooms pay in work or in dung, and none of them could tell you whose house it is. The swiftlets come closest, because they go up the shaft and see the other end.

What lives here is the tree. There is only one. Stair, vault, floor, cottages, and mains are a single piece of wood, and the hall is its lowest room. The rest stands overhead, taller than a tree is able to be. A tree lifts water by pulling on it, and there is a height past which the thread breaks. This one went past that height long ago, on the engines, and cannot come back under it. If the pumps stopped for a day, the top would die. That is why it rests one engine in three and never all of them.

You would expect appetite to be the main thing about it, but it is exactness. It ate a pumping station and could not bring itself to alter a line. In the second cottage a coat hangs on a peg, wood now, with the drag of rain still in its hem. A pair of boots stands under it, one fallen over. Break a splinter from a tread and the grain shows laminations finer than hair, one for every whistle. It has no idea which things matter, so the kettle got the same care as the keystone. Nobody was taken. The men went up the stair, and it has kept the stair.

It made one mistake. Along the engine-house wall is a rack of spares: a piston rod, a set of brasses, coils of gland packing. It took them like everything else, before it understood. The rod is perfect, and it has bowed under its own weight like a longbow. Since then it has not touched the engines, and the lacquer is the care of something that knows it has no spares.

So it learned the work instead, and the roster is still filled:

- **Fireman.** The beetles and the screw.
- **Oiler.** The ants. Their columns keep to the old walking lines between the cottages and the engine house, which is why those are still polished.
- **Beltman.** Not needed, since the lianas see to themselves.
- **Timekeeper.** The clock. The tree regrew its wheels tooth by tooth, and the copy is very good, though not exact. The shift was twelve hours once, and it has settled at eleven and some minutes.
- **Lampman.** No one.
- **Engineman.** The tree, in person.

At each of the three stop valves, a root as thick as a wrist is wound around the spindle like a clock spring. Through the shift it bakes and tightens. When the whistle brings the rain down, a gutter leads the water in. The driest coil drinks fastest and unwinds, the way a stork's-bill seed screws itself into wet ground. It shuts its valve, and a liana run from wheel to wheel opens the next. That quarter turn is the one fast movement the tree makes anywhere in its body, and it makes it once a day.

The lampman's post is the one account that does not balance. The tree cannot make glass or filament. Where a bulb has failed, it has grown one of wood into the socket, pear-shaped and thin enough that the firebox glow shows through it like a fingernail. These do not light. Around the ones that still burn it puts out the white leaves, which feed nothing. It is the only place in the hall where it behaves like a plant. When the last bulb goes, the moths go, and it is hard to say what goes with them.

It has met this kind of problem once before. In the office it copied the ledger leaf by leaf, and in the last column it did the only thing down here that is not copying. Whatever the men were counting down when they left, it closed over.

It does not know you are in the yard. You weigh less than the rain. If you stood still for long enough it would get your boots right, down to the eyelets.

Now go back to the grating. That is a root, the first one, which came down through the ground looking for water before there was any brick in the tree at all, and found the sump. It never met anything to copy. Everything else in the hall is the tree's rendering of something that was there before it, but this is what it looks like on its own: white, blunt, and unfinished at the tip. It has no post on the roster, because the engines do its drinking now. It still reaches after the water when the stroke takes it away, and it is still overtaken when the water returns. If the water ever failed to return, this is where the tree would find out.

It is the nearest thing the tree has to a face, and it keeps it under the floor.
