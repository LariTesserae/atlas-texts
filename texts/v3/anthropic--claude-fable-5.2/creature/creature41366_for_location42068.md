The webs have owners. The eye passes over them on its way to the frogs, because they are very small and they hardly move. A frog's raised foot would cover three of them. Each is dark and glossy, about the size and shine of a flax seed, on legs the colour of weak tea, and each hangs upside down beneath a sheet of its own.

What looks from the hatch like a floor of web is forty or fifty of these sheets, each a palm wide, slung edge to edge in the bays between the fins at the height where the air stands still. The middle of every tier is left open, because the middle belongs to the water.

They came the way everything came that could not climb. A spider this size travels by letting a thread out into the wind until the wind takes it, and at three thousand metres the air is thinly seeded with them, a drifting plankton the condenser breathes in along with the fog it wants. The swifts take most. The rest come in on the blade of daylight, meet the shaking air, and are carried to the nearest shelf and set down there. The tone sorts them as it sorts the water.

So they did not choose the still places, but they are the one animal here that the still places fit. A spider of this kind has eight eyes and little use for them. It knows the world through its feet resting on silk, and what it asks of the world is a tight line with nothing else touching it. Outside there is always wind. Here, for eleven minutes at a time, there is none. The loudest room on the mast holds slabs of calm as wide as the chamber and as thick as a finger, quieter than anything their kind can find outside. A body-length above the sheet the air begins to blur, and a body-length below it does the same. Between, the silk lies like a drawn wire, and whatever touches it is read at once.

Mostly that is midges. Their columns bend where they meet the shaking air, and the strays come skidding sideways along the shelf, into the bays and onto the sheets. The spider runs to the spot from beneath, bites up through the silk, pulls the midge down through the hole, and later mends the hole.

Everything else in the chamber has had to give up sound. The frogs wave and the moths send scent, but the spiders have kept it. A male who reaches the edge of a female's sheet announces himself by tapping, a signal many thousands of times fainter than the shout that cannot cross an arm's length. It crosses the sheet perfectly.

On the fourth tier, in the bay beside the ladder, one sheet is older than its neighbours. It is grey with mends, more darn than cloth, and the spider under it did not spin it. She had it from whoever hung there before and has replaced it thread by thread without ever taking it down. She is large for her kind and has seven legs, the eighth having gone to a gecko. One corner of her sheet is moored to a ladder rung, at the middle, where the hands used to go. Nothing has troubled that line in her lifetime. On the fins at either end of her sheet sit two frogs, raising and lowering white feet she has never seen.

Her day is eleven minutes long, and she has about a hundred and twenty of them between one sunrise and the next. Her night is the pause. For anything with ears, the pause is the quiet part, but for her it is the only noise there is.

The stillness under her feet lets go. Beyond the fin tips the lens falls, and the clap arrives as wet wind that bellies the sheet upward. Then the frogs on her own mooring fins begin to shriek, and every line she owns jabbers at once, as if every midge that ever lived had landed together. She can read nothing, so she draws her seven legs in and holds on.

Other spiders are using the forty seconds. The chamber, no longer drawing breath, lets its warmth go up the shaft and out through the louver, which is why the frost on the rim never advances. On that exhale the season's young let out their threads and rise. Those from the top tier go out into the cold and take their chances with the swifts coming the other way. Those from lower down gain a floor or two before the tone returns and sets them down wherever they have got to. The males go the other way, each on a line paid out from the edge of the home sheet, falling one metre through air that will shortly be impassable and scrambling for a fin.

Then the tone climbs back up from beneath hearing. She feels it before the frogs stop, as the sheet draws tight, the wet wind dies, and the lines fall quiet one by one. The frogs go back to their flags. At the far edge of her sheet, where there was nobody forty seconds ago, something has landed that is too careful to be a midge. In the first real silence it begins to tap.
