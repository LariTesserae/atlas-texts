For the first few days the answer seems to be the crowd: the crickets, the beetles, the ash-drifts of springtails, all sliding east under the light. By weight and number that is most of what lives here.

Then small wrong things start to register. Finger-wide runnels do not stay open across three hundred meters of moving sand, and these are open. The selenite slabs are clouded right through, but there is no dust on their faces. Here and there a thorn stick stands out of a dune with a knotted cord of human hair at its tip. Dig down along it and there is a peg hole, and beside the peg hole the lip of a cistern.

People live here, perhaps seventy. No count would be right, for the same reason no map is.

They are hard to see for the reason the geckos are. Their coats are pieced from bat skins, hundreds to a coat, fur inward, the outsides rubbed with gypsum until they match the floor. Skin and hair carry the same dust, set by the damp. In light that arrives from every side, a seated person has no shadow and almost no outline. What gives them away is their dark eyes.

Movement does not give them away, because in a cavern where nothing holds still, they are the thing that does. A light patch crawls a hand's width every few breaths and covers perhaps a hundred meters between morning and evening. Following it is not walking. A person sits at the western edge, where the crowd is thinnest and slowest, and every so often shifts a body-length east. They take the pellets the beetles have rolled, the straggling crickets, and springtails lifted on a wet palm. They take from the edge and never from the middle. They call this sitting a light.

A household does not hold ground. It holds wells, and with each well the curved band its light has stained on the floor. A holding runs over the old walls and avenues as the patch does, without regard to them. There are four hundred lights and few people, so most lights go unsat on any day, which they consider correct. When a dune lies across a household's band, they sit at a neighbor's and are not refused, since everyone's turn under the sand comes.

The old ones' eyes cloud the way the slabs have, from a lifetime of light with no shade in it. They go on sitting. The air is cellar-cool and the sun column is not, and the back of a hand can find the edge.

They speak low and close. A voice is gone into the sand at ten paces, so nobody raises one. For distance each carries a flake of selenite on a neck cord and tilts it in a column to throw a pale fleck across the floor onto someone else's patch. There are a dozen signals: water open, roof down, come, a death, a birth. The one sent most often means only *I am here.*

At dawn the crust takes a footprint and keeps it until noon. A visit made in the morning is made in front of everyone. Their phrase for doing a thing you would rather not have known is "walking in the afternoon."

Dawn is also when they wipe the reflectors with pads of swift down and wring the pads into their mouths. The cisterns hold plenty, so the wiping is not for thirst. Asked, they say the slabs need wiping, or that it is the sweetest water. Either way a second, paler light still goes down the avenues.

Every cistern has a note, set by its depth and the height of its drip, and children learn the notes before they learn the wells. Someone is always walking the north seeps with a finger trailing in a runnel, listening. When a note stops, a dune has arrived, and they know before they see it. Someone goes out with a stick. When a note that has been silent for a season starts again, the fleck goes round, and within days the water is crowded with shrimp and so is the rim.

These are the only times they gather, and no one can say in advance when the next will be. Betrothals, quarrels, and the trading of sticks and cord all wait for a dune to move. Then they are settled on the cistern steps by people scooping with both hands.

They sleep in the upper chambers of the pillars, above the floor drafts, going up the slumped ramps before the evening turnover. The owls keep the doorway notches and are left alone. The sunk rooms are not used by the living. A dead person is laid in one that stands in a dune's path, and no stick is set. A year or three later the row stands swept clean again, and what remains lies in what was once somebody's house. A patch of light fills the room for a few minutes and leaves it.

It is easy to take them for people who wandered into a ruin and camped. But the keeper of the seeps has an antler tine handled down to a nub. She also has a copper blade worn to the size of a fingernail, which cuts the cord at births and does nothing else. They have no story about the builders, because they do not think of the builders as someone else.

They are what the builders wore down to, the way the dishes are what the rooms wore down to. Whatever was cut shallow in them has gone: walls, doors, the idea that a place stays where you left it. What was cut deepest has stayed. They keep the water moving, mark what the sand takes, turn the light down the avenue, and live far apart. They still grind, too. In whichever row of hollows is uncovered they kneel side by side at the old spacing, working dried crickets and pellets into grey meal.

A few times a year there is a soft thump, and every sitter in the cavern stands. When the cloud has settled there is a new light, and it goes to the oldest child who has none. She sits beside a bare mound for a week, shifting east on schedule with nothing to take. Then the first grey-green shows, and the first cricket arrives.

The ragged lights are bigger and richer than the round ones, and the round-light households look down on them a little. The old keep a cord with a knot for every well, and they have noticed which end of it grows.

One stair still goes all the way up, inside a pillar on the north side, and comes out in a well-throat. A few evenings a year, when the swifts are coming in screaming, they climb out onto the plateau and shout. They shout names mostly, their own and each other's, as loud as they can, into air that carries it. The old go up because it is done. The children go up because they like it, and they are slow to come down.

Then they are back at their lights, and the loudest thing in the cavern is water again.
