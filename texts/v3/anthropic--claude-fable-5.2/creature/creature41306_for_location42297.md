**The few hundred**

From the rim they are small figures crossing between buildings, and closer up they stay a little like that. They are spare, deep-chested people who don't stop in the open. Everyone walks heel-first at a steady knock, and you know who is coming by the knock before you can see a face. Whatever stands still on the cushion gets skinned over, and a dropped glove is a green glove by spring, so the habit of not settling goes in early. Children who sit down on the green are picked up. A man who stops to light a pipe shifts from foot to foot while he does it and doesn't know he's doing it.

They smell of pitch and parsley. The resin gets into their cuffs and the creases of their hands and never fully washes out. It behaves on them as it does on the plant, soft by day and stiff at night. In the morning they work their fingers loose over the stove before they can do up a button.

Most are deaf to ordinary speech by thirty, which matters less here than it would elsewhere. They listen through the legs, standing with the knees a little slack, and any of them can tell you which engine is running, and how well, with their ears stopped. Outdoors they talk in the gaps of the steam hammer, which falls about every four seconds, so nothing they say is longer than that. Visitors take it for curtness. On days the hammer is down for repair they talk over each other and trail off, because nobody knows where a sentence ends.

They give every distance with a date: four hundred yards, this spring. The pencil marks on a doorpost, one a year, are the distance to the neighbour's door. Two brothers who built facing houses so they could talk from their steps now signal each other with lamps, and the younger has started to need a glass.

The trades are the ones the growth makes:

- **The splice gang** goes out every spring with new lengths of rail.
- **The linemen** walk the telegraph with their heads cocked, since a wire sings one known note for about a week before it parts.
- **The fog men** are at the mesh before dawn.
- **The lamplighter** has a round of nine miles, a few yards longer each year. It takes in a number of lamps he has never lit, which are burning when he gets there. He trims them anyway.

Anything urgent is knocked into the cushion with a tamping iron, in longs and shorts, and taken in through the shins on the far side of town. The same message comes up from below around four, a third lower. The clerk enters that one as the file copy.

On still nights they lie and hear their day played back under the bed. The hammerman counts his strokes on shift, out of habit. One night he counted them again as they came up. He had struck two thousand one hundred and forty, and he heard two thousand one hundred and forty-three. He has not raised it with anyone.

They used to bury their dead in the cut. The first cemetery is now a mile from where it was dug and spread over forty acres, and it has more hummocks than the register has names. Since then the dead go to the retorts with the offcuts.

**The finch**

It is the grey of cold brick, sparrow-sized, with a heavy bill. It nests only in the chimneys nobody built, in the joints where the pointing would have gone, and lines the nest with shredded underlayer and lamp wick. It drinks by running its bill along a row of rosettes before the beads go in, and it takes the beetle when it can see it.

It can't be heard over the works and doesn't try. It sings from inside the flue, which carries, a two-note figure that is the shift whistle made small. The older birds sing it a third lower.

There is one pair to each unbuilt chimney. When a new chimney shows on the green there is a pair in it before there is a shed under it, though nobody has seen a finch cross the open to get there. The fog men, who are out earliest, say every cock has the same pale feather in the left wing.

**The beetle**

It is the size and colour of a resin bead and can't be seen until one of the beads gets up and walks. It eats resin off the crowns, off a cutter's cuffs and off the varnish of a door.

It doesn't shelter at dusk. The resin stiffens around it where it stands, and it spends the night set like a flaw in glass with frost over the top. Around nine it walks out. It is the one thing here that can keep still. The plant doesn't take it, perhaps because it is mostly resin already.

The stillmen strain beetles from the first run. What gets through gives a darker grade, sold as such.

**The ones not on the payroll**

The payroll has three hundred and twelve names. Counted from the roof of the hammer house at shift change, the figures on the green come to more than that, and to a different number each time.

It follows the buildings. Those who walk for a living, the cutters and the splice gang, stay single. It happens to people who keep one place: the engineman at the same lever for thirty years, the gatekeeper in his box, the clerk on her stool. For a long while there is nothing. Then there is the sound of someone walking where no one is, a tread crossing the open at the right pace with the right hitch in it. Some years on a figure is standing by itself a hundred yards from where the person works. It is a little shorter. Nobody posted it there. It is warm, its breath shows on a cold morning, and its coat has the same tear at the same pocket.

They don't speak, or haven't yet, but they work. All four engine houses are kept oiled, and the ledger pays one engineman. His wife carries his dinner to the first house at noon. Most days a can is left on the step of the second. Nobody admits to leaving it, and it is empty by evening.

The town treats them as it treats the second chimney, going round them and keeping them out of the book. The growth carries them farther off every year. There is already some doubt which engine house is the ledger's. Nobody has said aloud how far that doubt extends.

As with the buildings, the farther ones are less. There is a gatekeeper in the box and another in the box that grew beyond it. Past that, where there is only a gatepost in the green, a hand comes up with a lantern when the tram goes by. The farthest of anyone is only a tread. Several of these have been followed. They go toward the old quarry and stop at the rope.

**Below**

For years everything known about it fit on one page of the quarry foreman's book. It is broad-winged, it doesn't beat, and it comes about once a season, on a clear afternoon. It takes eleven minutes to cross the hole. The early entries say grey. Nobody would guess its size, since there is nothing down there to hold against it.

The page has filled since. With a glass, the back is a hard green, yellowing along the high lines, and it runs in long low swells. One entry has it white at three in the afternoon. That would be frost, which would last down there, where the tableland keeps the sun off until late.

What were taken for wings are its margins. Up here the rim has curled under like the edge of a loaf. Down there it hasn't yet. It lies out flat and thin on both sides and lifts at the tips when the thing banks. It is the plant, divided and young, keeping under the old one the way the second chimney keeps near the first. The fog that reaches the town at dawn has been combed once already.

The clerk, for her own reasons, has kept bearings on where the four o'clock whistle comes up strongest. It moves, and over some years the bearings make a ring. The hole is on the ring, and the whistle is loudest at the rope on the afternoons the thing goes by.

Last autumn the cutter with the best eyes took the foreman's glass to the rope and held it on the thing the whole eleven minutes. She says there is a chimney near the middle of its back, short, by itself, with a thread of smoke laid over by the turning. She says a lamp was lit near it in daylight, and there was a groove that might in time have rails in it. Someone was crossing between the two, small, heel-first, not stopping.

They asked her if it looked up. She said it didn't, and that it would have little reason to. From down there the tableland is a brown underside the size of a district, with one pinhole of light in it, and nobody up here has seen that side either. What reaches them is the noise of a day's work, coming down through their sky.

The hammerman has decided that what comes up around four is no echo. A shift is being worked down there, four hours behind and a third lower. Somebody struck the three strokes he didn't strike.
