The first sign of it is weed in the wrong place.

The lanes are bare. Nothing roots where the tone cancels. But down one lane, at the edge of the twenty metres, a clump of turf stands upright on the clean rock, taller than it is wide. Then the dredge's plume comes across and there is an arm's length of brown water. When it clears, the clump is further off.

It is not weed. It is something the weed has grown on, as it grows on every surface more than a week old, and this surface is a great deal older than a week. Apart from the dredge buckets and the clock, it is the oldest thing in the valley. Turf covers its back, its shoulders, and the outsides of its limbs as thickly as it covers the switchyard fence. Only the working parts are bare: the palms, the soles, the insides of the joints, a patch around the mouth. The skin there is grey and smooth, and it is smooth from use. Its hands are the one place in nine kilometres where an edge has been dulled.

The shape under the turf shows when the switchyard arcs. For an instant the fronds go to glass with their veins showing, and the thing in the lane lights through as well: narrow in the body, long in the forearm and the shin, heavy in the jaw. It is built to the measure of the machines. The rungs up the side of each lift station are spaced to its reach. The handwheels fill its two palms. The time clock is mounted at the height of its face. Whether the works were made to fit it or it has been here long enough to fit the works, nothing else in the valley is old enough to say.

Those long bones ring. The ground-tone gets into them as it gets into the mains, and into its teeth, which would chatter against one another without rest if it let them. It keeps a time card folded between its back teeth, chewed to felt. This is the only use it has found for its mouth. It doesn't eat. The blades it pushes through close behind it with their edges whole.

The turf on its body is the same turf as anywhere, and grows toward sound. In the ranks, where the pressure swings hardest, every filament on it stands out stiff, drawn toward the nearest pump. It goes through the stands with its eyes shut, because there is nothing to see in there, and reads the pull on its own back. As it nears a lane, the pull evens out between its left side and its right. At the centre of the lane, the whole pelt lies down flat against it at once, and its teeth go still on the card. It opens its eyes. That is how it finds the paths, and that slackening is most of what it has in the way of rest.

Here it differs from the weed. When a pump changes speed and the lanes move, the weed is already standing in the new ranks. It is not already anywhere. It has to stop, let its pelt rise, find which way the fur leans, and walk. It is the one thing in the basin that takes time to arrive, the one thing for which another place is far. The noise is the same at the rim as on the floor, and the warmth is the same from floor to canopy, but it is slower in the seventh hour than in the first.

It makes a round of the machines, though they need nothing. No bearing wears here and no lamp fails. It lays a bare palm on each finned housing for as long as it can stand to, climbs to look at gauges whose needles tremble with the tone, and climbs down. It carries a valve key as long as its forearm, green with turf except at the grip, where the bare patch is the print of a hand a little larger than the hand that holds it now.

Its real work is the cards. Loose things in a standing wave end up where the wave is still. A card let go by the clock drifts into the weed, and within the shift it is lying in a lane, a pale oblong on black rock. It walks the lanes and picks them up. It carries the stack against its chest back to the doorway of LIFT 4 and sets them in the rack, and that is why the rack is never empty. It has learned to come at the doorway from the north. From the south there isn't always a 4.

The cards have been round many times. They are stamped on both faces, stamp over stamp, some of them to solid ink. There was a line printed for a name, and the stamp has fallen on it until the line is gone. Some cards carry times the clock has not reached. It racks them all.

Every eight hours the clock takes one card and gives four seconds. It has never let the rack run out. The clock might drive its stamp down on nothing and the pumps drop all the same, but it has not tried. Four seconds in eight hours is what it is paid, and it will not risk them.

Now and then the room of air wanders over it. It cannot go looking, because the room comes or it doesn't. A wall of standing water crosses the lane and passes over, and it is in rain. The water goes out of its pelt in sheets. It weighs more. The turf on it stays up, dripping, held by the noise like everything else in there. It takes the card out of its mouth and sits down on the floor, which it does nowhere else. This is drained ground, a rail yard's worth of what all of it was built for. It looks up into the rain falling out of the water. Sometimes a card comes down with the rain, stamped by some clock overhead, and it puts that one with the others. Then the other wall comes up behind it and goes over, and it is in the water again. There is no mark in the silt where it sat.

Then the stamp. The tone goes out of the rock, every frond in the valley slackens, and so does every filament on its body, wherever it is standing, lane or not. Its teeth stop. For most of its time here, that was the whole use of the four seconds: it stood still and was let alone.

At some stamp, it could not say how many cards ago, it began to spend the first second differently. It swings the valve key against the nearest main. Two metres of riveted iron ring once. Then it listens with the three seconds it has left, in a valley where, for those three seconds, a sound can come from somewhere.

An answer comes. It is one stroke of iron on iron, from the south, or from above. It is the only sound it has ever heard that was nearer or further away. It may be another like itself. From the south, on some shifts, there is a second clump far down the lane, the same height, also walking away. Or it may be its own stroke, gone up over the sill with the discharge and come back down at the outfall. The motors catch before it can strike twice.

Then the tone is up through the rock again and every filament on it stands. The scarps are elsewhere. The lane it was in is a rank now, with blades as broad as doors closing over it from both sides. The weed is already in its places. It shuts its eyes, bites down on the card, feels for the lean, and starts toward wherever the quiet has gone.
