The people are the ones you see first. They are slow, and it isn't idleness. Nobody born here runs, and nobody lifts what a counterweight could lift. They are deep in the chest and spare everywhere else, and they speak in short sentences cut to fit a breath. Most talk is done with the hands, in a speech that grew out of the shutter code and still looks like it: palm open, palm closed, long and short. Two people will argue across an alley for an hour by lamp while the hammocks between them sleep through it. They laugh with the shoulders only.

Everyone wears gloves, because the stone sees to that. Children are gloved before they walk, and a bare hand is a private thing. What they prize is anything worn smooth. Stone never gets the chance here, so iron carries the age: a latch, a wrench handle, a hammock hook polished by more hands than anyone has names for. These are the fittings that go inward when a rim building is emptied. A door may have hung in nine houses. The household goes with its door, in from the leaning tenement to the newest gap in the innermost ring, and starts again on pale stone. So the oldest families live in the youngest walls. The city moves out through its people the way the spine moves up through the saws.

They reckon age as distance. At a birth a brass stud is set in the paving at the quarry's edge, and it travels a pace a year with everything else. Ask someone's age and they point down a radial street. The studs keep travelling after their owners stop, and in the brown rings the pavements are freckled with them. The dead go out ahead of their studs. A building emptied at the rim is only emptied of the living. The dead are wrapped and laid in the bare rooms, where it is warm, and the building leans its few years and takes them down on a cloud day.

They have no word for finished. A leveller who has been round her street says the street is kept. A sawyer coming off the gantry says the crown is kept. When the crack comes from the spine they count breaths, and the count goes to nine. Nothing has ever happened by nine, so the hands start again.

The spall is weighed because the tally office keeps the mountain's books. Blocks plus spall ought to equal what the spine has given, and every year the sum falls short. The shortfall is the hollows.

The bird is hardly an inhabitant. It comes up through the cloud, leaves an egg in a niche in the black walls, and is gone the same hour. The chick hatches feathered into the warmth of the stone. It walks to the lip of the niche and drops into the air over the rim. Children find the shells, which are grey-violet and the only fragile thing in the city.

The pale ones live here in a different way from either.

A pale one is the colour the stone would be with the violet taken out. It has a body of overlapping plates no longer than a thumb, and seven legs, or eight with one kept folded. Accounts differ because nobody leans close. It is as hot as the rock it came from, and on a cloud day it walks in its own thin steam. It sets each foot flat on a face and never on an edge. In the simmering puddles near the spine it will stand for a stroke or two of the engines. Then it finds a channel and follows it as water would, over the lip of a cistern and down.

Folded on the bottom, it is the size of a fist. The keepers work around it with a lowered lamp and long wooden rakes, drawing off the rust scale, and they count. In the young cisterns the count is easy. Farther out the pale ones take the stain as the stone does. In the oldest courts the keepers count dark shapes on a dark floor by their seams, which stay pale, as seams do only on something that flexes. No one has seen one move underwater. But at every cleaning they are found along the outward wall, packed close like spall in a gutter, though the cistern has gone a pace toward the rim since the last one.

Not all of them meet the saw. The inclines are a kind of scale, and the brakemen can feel a block that rides light. Such a block is stopped at the dressing yard and a ring is chiselled on its face. It is kept in the city, built in low beside a channel, and no one hangs a hook in it. The tally office's reckoning of hollows has always run ahead of the keepers' count, and the difference is in the walls. People sleep a hand's breadth from it.

Once in some years a leveller finds a ringed block with a hole through its face, two fingers wide and smooth at the lip. The nearest cistern counts one more at its next cleaning. Most ringed blocks never open. They reach the rim at three hundred years, still closed and still warm, and go over as they are.

The hollows the saws do open are kept as well. A halved one is set by each door as a basin and fills on cloud days. Someone coming home will take off a glove and rest their fingers in it for a moment, since it is the only stone here a bare hand can touch.

When a cistern court comes due at the rim, its keeper makes the last count and cuts the number into the lid. The book goes inward with the fittings. The court leans, and on a cloud day it goes, with all of them folded against the outward wall. It comes to rest in the talus among the window openings and the iron stairs, on slopes that are made of old city all the way down.

Some keepers hold that there has only ever been one count, and that what walks out of the cut has been over the rim before. It can't be checked, because that would mean marking one, and no one touches them.

At the crown, a wire stops. The sawyers stand back from the cut and wait.
