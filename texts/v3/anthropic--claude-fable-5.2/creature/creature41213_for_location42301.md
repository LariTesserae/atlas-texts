The rime has something in it.

Break a feather of it off a horn tip before sunrise and it snaps clean across. Down the middle runs a hair of glass, finer than fleece, and at the root of the hair sits a black bead the size of a poppy seed. Every feather has one. The bead did not blow into the rime and stick. It was on the horn first, at dusk, with its hair held out into the wind, and the feather is a night of breath frozen along that hair.

The air here is too clean to freeze unaided. Fog needs a speck to begin on, and the plateau has no dust, because the rock flour is damp and trodden into moss as fast as the grinders make it. Left alone, the breath-cloud would stay liquid far below the cold at which it ought to turn, drift out past the edge of the herd, and be gone into the dry. The beads are what it begins on. They are the reason the water comes back down each morning onto the animals that breathed it out.

The bead does it for the heat. Water gives up warmth when it freezes, and a hair taking on ice all night is warmed all night. At the root of a feather the cold holds just under freezing, while the air a hand away is thirty degrees below that. Nothing else so small gets through a night here in the open. A grinder grows its wool once. The bead grows a blanket every dusk and is out of it by mid-morning.

It is dark for the same reason the hooves are dark. The body is iron taken from the rock, and the hair is drawn from the glass rind of the flow tops. Both are eaten as flour.

In the morning the sun reaches the bead through the ice before it has done anything to the ice. The feather lets go at the root. It sags into a drop with the bead inside and the hair folded round it, and the drop falls. The fall is never more than the height of a horn. Every drop of the plateau's rain has a passenger. Some land in fleece and sit out the day in a grinder's garden. Most reach rock, and the drop runs to the nearest column head and stops in its cup.

The cup holds a finger of water over black glass, and by noon the sun has taken the chill off it. There the hair, which was a single strand on the horn, opens into a tuft of thirty, and the bead rows with it. It grazes the brown skin of flour and dung on the floor of the cup. Sometime in the afternoon it divides, and there are two. A hoof coming down empties the cup, and whatever was in the cup is then on the hoof or under it.

The water is earthed. It lies in the joints with the cable animals, and any charge that reaches it runs away down their backs. The horn and iron above have been rubbing all day and are not earthed at all. When the drip stops and the air dries, the bead comes up, twists its tuft back into one hair, and stands the hair through the skin of the water. For a while nothing happens. Then the pull on the hair is greater than the water's hold on the bead, and it goes, the way chaff goes to a comb that has been through wool. It goes to wherever the charge is crowded, and charge crowds onto points and edges: horn tips, the rims of plates, the lips of the columns out ahead of the leading front where no horns have come yet. It lands with a tick too small to hear. Some of the blue on every horn at nightfall is beads arriving.

A bead still in its cup when the cup freezes is frozen to the floor with no feather over it, and within the week a grinder rasps it up with the lichen and wears its iron as hoof. So every bead leaves the water every evening, and none has ever been heavier than a charged hair can lift. Nothing else has kept them small.

That was the whole of it until the valley.

At the bottom of the ring the afternoon melt now stands over the column heads instead of in them. It makes a sheet a few hundred paces across, in among the legs, hock-deep on a grinder along its lowest line. It is the first water on the plateau too deep to freeze through in a night. Dusk puts a lid on it. Under the lid it stays liquid until morning, and liquid water under ice is exactly as warm as the root of a rime feather.

The beads near the top of that water go up at dusk as beads go up everywhere. One grazing the floor when the light fails is under ice before it can reach the air. It stays, and it does not die. It has a night beneath the lid with the floor still to eat, and in the morning the lid melts off and it is where it was, a little larger, with no reason to be light.

There are beads in the pond now the size of a pea, and some as long as the top joint of a thumb. Their iron has begun to lie in plates. The tuft has not grown with the body. It has coarsened into two rows of stout glass bristles, good for rowing. The big ones do not divide in an afternoon. Whatever they do instead they do under the ice, and it has not been seen.

They keep one habit. At dusk they rise beneath the freezing skin and push their bristles through it, as if to be lifted. The charge pulls and cannot lift them, and the lid sets around the bristles and holds them hanging. All night the tips stand in the fog above the ice and seed it. By morning the pond wears a white nap a finger high, in patches, one patch over each animal hanging underneath. Then the sun burns it loose and it drips, and the drip goes into the pond. This is the one water on the plateau that has started to bring down rain for itself.

It cannot stay where it is. The cloud belongs to the herd, the herd turns, and ground behind the trailing front is dry rock within a season. The pond exists only under the herd and creeps around the ring at the herd's pace. The small beads never notice, since they are thrown and scattered fresh every night. The big ones have to keep up by rowing. It is not far in a day, but it is always the same way.

On a still morning, before the lid goes, they can be seen through it from the back of any grinder standing in the pond. They are dark shapes hanging under white fur, every one of them facing the way the wheel turns.
