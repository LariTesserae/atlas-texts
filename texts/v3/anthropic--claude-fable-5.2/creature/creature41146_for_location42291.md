The simple answer is a count. A few hundred fern crowns stand in the moss, each a stiff rosette about as tall as a hand is long, each wearing below its living fronds a grey skirt of every frond it has ever dropped. Some dozens of filmy ferns hang wet from the roof. Then there is the moss, the club mosses trailing through it, and the liverworts on the walls. Counted that way, the room is crowded.

The count does not survive touch. Under the moss beside any crown, fingers find a rhizome as thick as a bootlace, running to the next crown and the one after. Followed far enough, the few hundred ferns become nine or ten. The cushions are all one moss, or possibly two. The liverwort is a single sheet that has gone all the way around the walls and met itself. So the residents are few and large, and each is much older than any of its parts. No frond has been alive more than a few decades, but the thing that puts out the fronds was here when the peat was a stain on gravel.

They are all kin. Whatever came down the bore came once, and no stranger has arrived since. The young ferns in their arcs are the children of ferns a pace up the current, and of each other. They are leathery against cold and sweet against frost, and have nothing prepared against teeth.

The only travelers in the room are too small to feel. A spore that settles on damp moss does not grow a fern. It grows a green scale smaller than a fingernail, flat to the peat. When the water film across that scale is unbroken, it releases swimmers, which cross a few millimeters of wet in the dark, toward a trace of scent, in a matter of minutes. This is the fastest thing that happens here, and the only thing that goes anywhere under its own power. It happens a handful of times a year.

Under everyone is the fungus. It is the one resident that eats, and it eats only the dead, slowly, taking the grey skirts down a strand at a time. It is in the peat, in every root, and in the base of every moss stem. Sugar goes down into it near the thread and comes up somewhere else. It is the only resident that touches all the others. It also runs out past them, under the frost-stiff moss and the dry crust, almost to the hoarfrost. The garden's true edge is where the strands stop, not where the green stops. Once in a long while it fruits: a pale cap the size of a lentil that opens to no one and lets its spores go on the same hour-long roll of air as the ferns'.

That would be the full list. But the longer one stays with it, the harder it is to find where one of them leaves off. The fern's sugar is in the fungus. The fungus hands the fern its own dead back as food. The moss holds the film the swimmers cross. The roof ferns catch the vapor and set it down as drops, in places the moss has shaped itself to receive. Lift any one out and it would not be a smaller garden. It would be a hole in something.

So the room has one inhabitant, and it has a body. It is tallest along one diagonal and tapers in every direction. A hand drawn across the fern tops would feel a ridge like a spine, with the fronds on either side all canted toward it. It has no skin. It thins to stiff moss, then to crust, then to feathers of frost made from its own breath, so that its outermost fronds are ice. It breathes once an hour, down the middle and up the sides. It is colder than the stone around it, which no other living thing is. It lives by being the place where warmth goes in and does not come out as warmth. It has no voice, only a tap, a long gap, and another tap elsewhere.

It is also going somewhere. It climbs a centimeter in a century, roof first, laying down floor behind it. The thread does not climb. For most of its life this thing lived underneath its line, leaning up toward it, with open air between them. Now the line is at its knee, and the moss has found it and gone along it hand over hand. The moss there is the deepest in the room, wrapped around something that returns no cold or warmth to the touch and feels like nothing at all. After all that time it has hold of what feeds it, and it cannot feel that it does. In time the peat will rise over the thread and close. What happens then is not something the inhabitant considers. It has one habit, which is to lean toward the line, and it will go on leaning as the line passes through it.

There is one place it does not go. The moss that climbed a wire has never put a stem across the plate. That palm of floor is the only spot in the room where nothing lives, and it is kept as exactly as everything else is kept.

Every measure of this thing is some part of a hand, and no hand has ever touched it. Nothing has ever touched it that it did not grow. It keeps its dead on and it is sweet where it breaks. Under the tip of its longest frond it has a dimple the size of a thumbprint, where it has let the same drop fall for longer than any one frond has lived. That is the only mark anything has ever made on it, and it made that mark itself.
