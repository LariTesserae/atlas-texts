Begin with a scraper, because the scrapers are in front and you would take them for the owners.

A scraper is nine plates, overlapped front to rear like slates on a roof, over a foot as wide as the animal. It has no head you could point to. The front is the end the tongue is under. It has no eyes and no pits where eyes might once have been. It knows its neighbours by the arc: where its plate edge comes within a hair of the next animal's, the gap stitches blue, and that is all it learns about the animal beside it. The lanes are straight because the current is. Turn a scraper out of line and it would swing back like a needle. You could not turn one, though. It weighs what a washtub of wet stone weighs, and your hands would be white to the wrist before you had a grip.

The tongue is a ribbon as long as the body, folded back on itself inside and set with cross-rows of teeth capped in black iron. The front rows wear to stubs within the hour and go out with the slurry. New rows come up from the back at the same rate. It is the only part of a scraper that is ever new, and it is built from what the animal took off the floor that morning.

The scraper does not eat the rock. Nothing here does. Slurry leaves unchanged by a groove under the last plate, and what keeps the animal going comes up through the foot from the film. Scraping is what the current does with a scraper, as turning is what a river does with a wheel.

The pores along the flank start as slits and wear round, and a round pore whistles. A worn animal carries four or five notes, none in tune with the others. In a lane that runs to the cloud, that is the one way to tell a scraper from the scraper behind it.

The strainers are the same slate underneath. They look pale because they are never out of their own frost. Slurry passes through the belly fringe, each tine keeps what fits it, and the pellets leave by two vents, iron on the left and copper on the right. Seen along its length, a strainer lane is ruled with two dotted lines that smoke.

A smearer is three plates and would sit on a dinner plate. Crack one and the gut is not coiled. It is bent into one fold, a hook or a shut S or a shape like a pinched eye, and that fold is the only one the animal will ever lay. Each piece of the mountain's root is carried by some smearer, and since the smearers move, the roots move. Watch a whorl in the floor against one of the humps and it drifts east at lane pace.

None of the three is ever small on the way to being large. There are no eggs in the film and no broods under the plates. The ones that shoulder in during a lull arrive full-sized and already worn, whistling, tongue down in the middle of a stroke, with the scars of years they were not present for. They come the way the rock comes out of a smearer, old to begin with.

The mites are the first rule in person. They give the film its grain, and rime is what they build. On anything still they settle and lock, leg to leg, and the next layer settles on that. A moving animal shakes them off with each stroke. Every plate edge in the basin carries a fringe of them, waiting.

The joints all connect. No end of an eel has ever shown in one, head or tail, so nose to tail may be a manner of speaking and the hum a single voice.

All of this is below your knee. The floor is a hand high at its highest, the water two fingers deep, the animals the height of a tub. Forty kilometres of living basin would fit under a table. The waders are the exception. A wader's knee is the highest point on the plain.

A wader starts with six feet. Most walk on five, or four. One on three does not walk long. The body is small, a pale lozenge slung low between the knees, and the hammer hangs from the front of it on a neck with a single joint. The striking face is capped with the same black iron as a scraper's teeth. On either side of the hammer is an eye, flat and grey as a rivet head. They are the only eyes in the basin.

They hunt by ear all the same. A wader crosses the grain so that each lane's whistles go past it in order, and it listens for a note gone flat, a pore half shut with rime. It comes down once, on a seam. It takes the tongue, which is the only new thing for forty kilometres, and a wet knot behind the tongue the size of two fists, and nothing else. Then it goes on lifting each foot in turn, as it has been doing the whole time, a little ahead of the mites and a little further ahead of the eels. A wader never runs and never rests. Its walk is the slowest motion in the basin that still counts as not stopping.

At the worn-through places it sees what nothing else here can, which is itself. From above, a wader is a pale back no bigger than a smearer inside a ring of knees. It looks for a long time. Most step away when their feet begin to whiten. A few do more than step away. They walk to the nearest swell, where the film runs thin.

There the wader folds. The knees come down past the back, the legs lie along the body, and the hammer tucks under. Folded, a wader is low, smooth, and the size of a washtub. The mites have it in minutes. Then a smearer lane arrives. The smearers do not go around, because they go around nothing. They go up one side and down the other, laying as they go, a band of grey, a band of pink, each its own fold, into the glaze and through it. By the second rule, what they are building on cannot get higher. It gets older. When the lane has passed, the thing on the swell is a billion and a half years old clear through to the middle, with no shell and no hollow. It is the only ground in the basin that stands more than a hand high.

The scrapers take up what is laid at the rate it is laid, but a hump is laid once. There is no rate to match, so the lead lane splits around it and closes.

The count of humps changes and the count of waders changes. Their sum does not.

A hump goes back the way everything here changes lanes. The hum drops a tone. The frost-smoke is still hanging where the pellets were, and the herd is one lane over, facing the other way. On one swell the film lies flat where a hump was. A little way off, a wader is walking on six feet. Its legs are banded grey and pink to the knee. The banding fades from the foot upward with each step, until it is pale all over, the tallest thing on the plain, and already listening.
