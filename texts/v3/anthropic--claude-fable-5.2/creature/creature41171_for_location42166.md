In a still place two avenues in from the seaward wall, where the silt lies deepest, there is a well with a fish in it.

He is as long as a hand, and most of that is head. The mouth is wide and hinged far back, the lips thick, and the body behind tapers quickly to a ribbon. He has no color. Under a light he would be the pink of the inside of a lip, with the gills showing darker through the cheek, but no light has come this far in. Where eyes would be there are two grey specks under the skin, and they report nothing. Instead he has rows of pits over the snout and along the jaw, each holding a hair that bends when the water moves, and a mouth that tastes whatever it closes on.

He rests tail-down in the well with his chin on the rim, and from there he reads the plaza. Each flat fish under the silt breathes, and he can feel that slow pulse from three lengths off. He knows where the nearest ones lie, and he feels it when one has shifted. Since the day he took the well he has not been farther from it than he can return in two strokes, tail first.

The well is a shaft twice his length, a little wider than his head, lined in courses of pebble and shell. No piece in it is larger than a mouth can hold. At slack water, when the worms are building, he builds too. He noses along the foot of the nearest wall where the terraces shed fragments, takes one up, turns it over with his tongue, and usually spits it out. What he keeps are flat chips of bivalve, which he sets with the smooth face inward. He presses each into place with his snout, and often he takes it out, turns it a quarter, and sets it again.

The well sinks. The plaza silts and the old quarter settles, so a rim that stood clear of the mud one season is level with it the next. He builds upward, and the lining is lost at the bottom at the rate it is added at the top. Sometimes, after a collapse, he digs down and comes to courses he did not lay. There is a band of snail whorls set point-down, placed by some fish who favored them. Below that are plain pebbles packed tight by one who did not care for shell at all. Below that the pieces are locked in pale stone with a honeycomb grain and cannot be shifted. He tastes them, finds them firm, and leaves them.

The door is one thick valve of a clam, wider than the shaft. It is too big to carry and has to be dragged. One edge has a notch where lips grip it, worn deeper than a single fish's life could wear it. He does not wait for the eel before closing. He waits for the worms. When the faint hiss of fans along the avenue stops out of turn, and the quiet spreads from tower to tower, he is down with the door across before the shut band reaches the plaza. Then he lies under the lid in his own stale water until the fans slide out again in order.

On the flood he rises a body-length above the rim, into the edge of what the avenue spills into the plaza, and hangs head-up, snapping at what the fans have missed. After storms he tastes tannin a full day before anything arrives. Then things drift down through the dark. Once it was a beetle's wing-case, which landed by the rim. He took it in his mouth and turned it over. It was smooth on one face, so he built it into the wall. Then the isopods come, and he eats until his belly drags.

He croaks by drumming a muscle against his swim bladder, down in the shaft with his head below the rim. The well gives the sound its pitch, and a deep well speaks lower than a shallow one. Another answers from across the plaza on a different note, and farther off there is a third. He knows the plaza by these notes as surely as by its water. Sometimes a note stops, because the eels are patient. Then the plaza is short a note for a season, until some young fish drifting in off the avenue with nowhere to back into finds the empty shaft. The note returns at the same pitch, though thinner. He came by his own well that way, and the note he sounds was sounded before him.

Once or twice a year, in the fat weeks after a storm, the one who answers from across the plaza crosses it. It is the only journey either of them makes. She leaves a clutch at the bottom of his well, and he takes it into his mouth.

For as long as he holds it, the mouth that chooses stone and hauls the door is full. He cannot eat. He cannot grip the notch, so the door lies where it was left. He stays at the bottom of the shaft, and every so often he works his jaw to roll the eggs and wash them. He does not call. Above him the rim goes under the silt a little at a time.

Then, at the slack before an ebb, in the one hour when every fan on the avenue is drawn in, he rises to the rim and opens his mouth. The young go out, each no bigger than a sand grain, and the turning water takes them.

He tastes the rim and finds it a finger's width low. He goes to the foot of the wall, takes up a chip of shell, and turns it over twice. He spits it out and looks for a better one.
