There are four anti-vibration mounts and five cushions.

The fifth is out toward the west wall, where two of the wide curves cross. From the door it passes for one of the others: fist-sized, grey-green, hard as pumice by the look of it. The light gives it no shadow to betray it. Only an eye brought down to the level of the plate sees that this one stands clear of the floor by the breadth of a hand, and that something is holding it there.

What holds it is legs. They are the dark of the floor steel and about as thick as the wire of the lamp cages. They leave the underside of the body in long shallow arcs and come down on the lichen, each foot on a thread of the figure and none on the bare steel between. They are hard to count against the chequer pattern, but there are six. Five are planted. The sixth reaches out over the swept plate and rests its tip on steel that moves, and its last joint looks the way the leaves on the cable tray look, a blur with a firm centre.

That is how it listens. It has nothing that could be called an ear, and an ear would be wasted here, since the air has only ever carried one thing. What it attends to is the floor. Along the lines the plate neither rises nor falls, but it does rock, tilting a hair one way and then the other at the pace of the note, and the five planted feet take that rocking in the ankle. The sixth takes the stroke itself. Between them the animal has the whole figure and its own place in it, the way a person knows where their hands are in the dark.

Seen close, the body stops resembling moss. It is a low dome of small plates, each about the size of one of the creeper's leaves, fitted edge to edge and coloured like the lichen. In the seams between them is a tint that runs straw, then violet, then blue, the same colours as the welds on the skid and just as unweathered. The front rim of the shell still carries its burr. Under the rim are two eyes, black, dry and lidless, since there has never been a dark here to close them against. Whatever it breathes out, it breathes out dry. The panel above it is as clean as the rest.

It moves, though not so that it can be watched. The cold has set in its joints as it has in the grease, and it goes at the speed that allows. A foot lifts from a line, crosses the bare plate without touching it, and settles on the next line in about a tenth of an hour, so that the animal and the last white digit of the hour meter keep the same step. Each foot ends in a point as fine as a scriber's and comes down between the flakes of the crust without breaking one. Out among the wide curves the animal travels spread and low. In the lattice near the skid, where the lines crowd together, it has to gather its legs steeply under itself, and it goes through that part tall and narrow, like a hand walking on its fingertips.

Its round takes it to each of the four mounts in turn. The moss does grow. It puts out a pale edge at the rim, finer than a chalk line, and the animal takes that edge off. Under the front of the shell are two flat blades that meet the way the jaws of side-cutters meet. With these it works its way round a cushion, cutting only what is new, and what it cuts is all the food and all the water it gets. Then it sets out for the next mount. By the time it has been round all four, the first has an edge again. On the way it crops whatever lichen has crept off a still line onto steel that moves, though the floor does most of that work itself.

When the wheel on the door begins to turn, the turning reaches the animal through the floor as a small wrong tilt in one ankle. By the time the door opens, the sixth leg is drawn in and there is a cushion standing at a crossing. Whoever came with the chalk drew the ring round the largest cushion as the animal keeps it, and wrote the date. They came back later to find the moss filling the ring exactly, and went out through the door with that answer.

To the animal the lines are not the shape of a note. They are the ground, and they have always been where they are. One day a bearing will start to wear, or a valve somewhere far below will be reset, and the note will move by less than an ear could tell. The figure will move with it, a few millimetres, out from under the lichen. The animal will feel that in five ankles before any needle stirs behind its film. Nothing has happened yet to prepare it. The sixth foot stays out on the bare steel, blurred at the tip, where it has been since the first hour on the meter.
