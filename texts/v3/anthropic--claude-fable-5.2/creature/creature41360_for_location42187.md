The dozen animals don't live here. The pale walker and the glider are entries, kept the way the fractures are kept. The walker is on the north edge because the north edge with a walker on it is a single fact, and a fact does not live anywhere. To live in a place, a thing must be able to be elsewhere in it a moment later, and the basin permits that in one place only.

The pan looks empty. The drift lies level and pale to the trees on every side. But a foot set down in it leaves a print, and the print is still there at the next glance. The flakes are what the forest has let go, and it keeps no account of them. So the pan takes marks, and it has some already. In places the flakes overlap all one way, like shingles, in a band about a pace wide that wanders out across the pan and back.

What lays the bands is hard to see. A dark stroke stands on the drift a few hundred metres out, bronze-green, upright, thin as a post. At the next glance there is no stroke, and a slight shine hangs a little left of where it stood. Everything else in the basin is where it was when looked at again. This is not.

Closer, it is flakes. Some thousands of single laminae stand on edge in the drift, a hair apart, in a loose sheaf. Edge-on the sheaf is dark and solid, like the side of a book. Face-on it is almost nothing, and the far trees show through it. When it travels it goes long and low. The hindmost leaf tips onto the next, and that one onto the next, and the motion runs up the file like cards falling. The drift it has crossed is left lying one way. When it stops it draws up to about chest height. Whichever end is going seems to be the front.

Standing, it works. A flake comes up out of the drift at its foot and is passed leaf to leaf up through the sheaf until it stands at the top with the sun through it. It is held there a few seconds. Then it is either drawn down in or let go, and it falls straight and lies where it lands. Nearly all are let go. This is the whole of its feeding, and it has a kilometre of drift to go through.

Every flake on the pan is a layer that once registered something happening and was put out for it. It might have been a settling, a grain coming loose, or the sun moved by a hair. Nearly all of them disagree with the basin alone, each in its own way. A few disagree alike, and the sheaf is those few, gathered. It is a second agreement, small and slow. When a new leaf is taken in, a riffle runs the length of the sheaf and back while the leaf finds its position, and sometimes another leaf drops out. The forest agrees faster than anything can disagree, but this agreement can be watched.

Under a lens, its leaves show the scarp a little east, where a scarp would sit after slumping. The cleft that is not there is drawn fine, like a crack just opened. A stipple in the corners of the clefts could be grit. The glider is on the right-hand crown. The walker has turned the corner and is partway down the east edge. The shadows are all a little shorter, and the sun stands slightly above two-thirds.

So it is not another basin. It is this one, later. And the leaves are not all equally late. At one end of the sheaf the sun has barely moved, and at the other it has moved by about the width of a thumb at arm's length. The leaves stand in order. The sheaf has no front, but it has a first leaf, and it is about a quarter of an hour long, with gaps. What lives here is as much of the rest of the morning as has been able to collect.

It behaves accordingly. It leaves the pan by the clefts and goes about the forest on its own ground. It pours over the real scarp without a pause. Then, a few metres east on flat plate, it stops, makes the slow leaf-by-leaf rearrangement it uses for a descent, and arrives at the height it started from. It waits under the right-hand crown of the glider's gap with its top leaves turned face-up. To rest, it goes to a certain unbroken plate and lays itself down in a line across it, every leaf on edge, bedded in a cleft that is not there.

It casts the only soft shadow in the basin. The leaves do not quite agree on where the sun is, so the shadow is blurred along one side by a quarter-hour's worth of sun. It also makes the only sound that lingers. When it moves, a tick runs down the sheaf from leaf to leaf and arrives at the far end slightly after it began.

It comes to visitors, or to what they leave. A torn leaf is whole on the stem, but some layer noticed the tearing, and that layer is on the pan, lying uppermost. The sheaf finds these first. It does not keep a visitor's position any more than the forest does. It keeps what they did. In its late leaves there are leaves that stayed torn and a stone out of its socket.

Its first leaf differs from the basin by one thing. The sun has not moved, the scarp has not slumped, and the glider is still mid-gap. But on a plate near the western wall there is a single line a hand long, the kind made by testing an edge with a knife. Whoever made it is not kept. Every leaf after the first has that line in it.
