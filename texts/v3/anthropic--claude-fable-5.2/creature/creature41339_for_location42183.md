The obvious answer is the grazers, since they are the ones who sleep in the room.

One can be lifted off a ledge. The moment is just after the green has jumped, at the tail of a file, when the animal's whole attention is on the rump in front. In the hand it weighs less than its fur promises. The nose is dry and hard as a thumbnail. Each eye has a clear inner lid that stays down in the open air, and an outer one that blinks more often than seems necessary. The mouth is shut and stays shut. It breathes through slits, shallowly, about once to your five. It struggles the way a wound spring lets go, all at once and not for long. After that it lies still and stamps one hind foot against your palm in a slow count, telling the rock where it is.

It has never drunk. Neither has any other animal here, nor any ancestor of theirs back to the day the pins went in. There is no motion in it for lapping. A cupful, if one could be shown to it, would mean nothing. Set down again, it does not look for its file. It looks for green, and the file is where the green is.

A grazer leaks. Each breath it lets go is taken at the nostril, and so is whatever stands on the eye between blinks. What it holds at first light is mostly gone by dark, and has been made up a mouthful at a time off the wall. If it misses the green twice, it slows. If it misses a third time, it stops on its ledge and the file runs over it. By the time the last animal has passed, there is a husk waiting for the next gust.

So the animal in the hand is not quite the animal that was on this ledge yesterday. The fur, teeth, and bone are the same, but the wet part has been changed out. A file is less a line of animals than a length of pipe, relaid each time across the floor. The same is true one remove along for the wall-birds, who have theirs from beetles that had it from leaves, and for the narrow-winged bird, which has it from wall-birds. Followed back, all of it comes off the wall. It comes onto the wall from the pins, and the pins have it from everyone's breath.

The crust is the exception. The crust can do without for as long as it must, and most of it, at any moment, is doing so.

That leaves the question of what is actually resident. The animals turn over every few seasons and nothing marks the change. The crust waits. The thing that has been here the whole time, in use every hour, is the sum.

Its size can be worked out roughly: one house front of leaf at its fattest, several hundred grazers, some thousands of birds at a wad of paper each, and the beetles, moths, and eggs. Gathered in one place, which cannot happen, it would not fill a horse trough. The closest fair comparison is a body. It comes to about what one large person carries. That, too, was left unexplained.

It has habits, like anything else that lives here.

It is never all in one place and never still. Wherever it stops being used, it is withdrawn. It has no colour of its own. It is green for the length of a meal, rust on the ledges, and grey in the flutes. It does not travel between these. It is out of a leaf on the west wall and in a leaf on the north, and nothing crosses the air, and the pins do not sweat.

It has one sound, the tick, made when a flake comes over the lip and is taken in. Against the noise of the walls, you must be within arm's reach to hear it.

The last thing takes longest to notice. In all the time since the pins were set, it has not had a surface. It has not beaded, dripped, pooled, or run downhill. It has not lain level or shown the sky to anything. Any other water does these things the moment it is left alone. This water is kept at work in the dark of leaves and bodies and has, as far as can be told, never once been set down.

At dusk the grazers come off the walls and file into the room. The green goes on without them. On some nights a house front of yolk and rust opens on the east wall with only moths to see it.

Inside, the sound ends, and for the first time in the day the sum can be heard. Several hundred small chests work slowly and out of step. There are stomachs, and a young one swallowing. These are the only wet sounds in the hollow, and it takes a room that stops every other noise to make them audible. This is as near as the sum comes to being gathered: perhaps a fifth of it, warm, flank to flank, lying down.

The grazers fill the floor to the walls and lie under the bench and two deep in the corners. Around the basin they leave a ring of bare floor a little wider than one of them is long. A young one that rolls into the ring in its sleep is nosed back out by whichever adult is nearest, without either of them fully waking.

The basin is the one shape in the hollow that the sum would fit. In it, the water would lie level and hold the doorway upside down. Perhaps it once did. Perhaps a measure was put out at the foot of the bench for someone who came down the steps often enough to wear them. The basin doesn't say. It is dry. The grazers' ring is the only sign that the place is being kept.

On some afternoons, behind the pin that leans, a little of the sum gets out. It is not much, what a wall-bird holds, or two. It lies on the shaded rock as rime, a hand across, and for those minutes it is in nothing and is nobody's. It is white and cold, with an edge you could trace with a finger. This is the only time it looks like itself.

The wall-birds come down and stand around it. They leave the same gap the grazers leave around the basin, a little more than a body's length. They can have no use for it. They have only ever met it as beetle, and nothing in them could know that this is what they run on. They stand all the same, heads into the wind, wings shut.

Then the pin catches up. There is the tick, and the water is back at work, first in a leaf on the north wall, then in whatever eats the leaf. By dark it is in the room, lying a body's length from the basin, where it can be heard breathing.
