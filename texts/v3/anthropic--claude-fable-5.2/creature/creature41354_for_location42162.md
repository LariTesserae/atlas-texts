The people of the terraces are known first by the jaw. Light here comes from underfoot, off the cloud by day and out of the furnace mouths at all hours, so a face is a chin, the underside of a nose, two lit ledges of brow, and darkness where the eyes should be. They recognise one another across the rock by the lower face and the walk. Nobody owns a hat with a brim. Collars are cut high and stiff, and a person looking at something far off shades the eyes with a hand held flat beneath them, like a tray. Children drawing a house put the sun under it.

They are deep in the chest and slow to begin a sentence, and once begun they do not spend air on ornament. They are mill hands, cokemen, gas-men, the pump crew, the cable crew, and the trimmers who walk the trestles changing carbons in the arc lamps and kicking the banked moths from the posts. Everything they own came up in a bucket or was rolled on the saddle. Nothing they have made has gone down. They watch the empties sink into the cloud as people elsewhere watch a river go by, with no expectation of news. On the wall of the cable station hangs an old sheave whose groove was worn by a rope running level. The crew oil it once a year and do not discuss it.

Between the works they walk under the pipe-runs, on the moss, which is the only soft ground on the mountain and one person wide. Two who meet must each put a foot on bare rock, and there is a courtesy about who takes the cold side. The pipe is warm overhead. A walker's shadow travels along its belly, upside down among the drips, with the hooves of the ibex going the other way on top. In the dark between lamps they keep a hand out for each trestle leg as it comes and do not speak. Talk is for the islands.

They are older than the ground they stand on, and they behave like it. When a slab parts under a wall, nobody curses the rock. Someone lays a palm on the fresh fracture to feel whether it is still warm, and says it will learn. They are the same with water. Each household has its tin gutter and its canvas, and the pitch of a gutter is a family matter, argued over, because the water has to be shown. A child's first chore is to stand at the barrel end in the evening and tap the tin with a spoon until the beads agree on a direction.

It is a strange seniority, though, because everything else on the saddle already knows the mountain and they do not. The ibex know where the spur is. The pikas know the ledges. The people have to be told.

So the trade held highest is not in the mill. It belongs to the levellers, who set the brass marks. They carry a level, a staff, a stopwatch and a notebook of animals. They take a height off the ibex bedded in the air beyond the eastern slab, sighting on the folded knees. They note the bearing where a pika goes into a hole that is due, and return the next year to find the hole. They plot the line the choughs bank along and ink it in as a cliff. And they time the echo. The oldest notebooks are in a hand that is hard to read now, and the figure for the echo in those is the same as this year's, to the tenth of a second. It is the one fixed measurement they have. From all this they say where the ground will be and when, and the framers hang houses on their word. They have not been wrong by more than the thickness of a doormat.

A family given a house in a hung terrace goes in by the back gantry. The front door is opened only to scrub the step, which is done weekly, kneeling, with someone holding the scrubber's belt. Eleven metres at a doorstep a year is a working life and more. The child born the winter the frame went up is the grandparent in the chair when the rock at last presses the moss under the threshold. On that day the door is opened wide and the oldest person in the house goes out first, in stockinged feet, onto stone that is still warm.

Not every house in the new terrace has a family. The city is built ahead of itself in this too. There are more pegs in the mill's changing shed than there are hands, and in the hung terrace fires are kept in houses nobody has been given. A neighbour banks them. The lamps are trimmed. Asked who for, people say *those due*, in the voice they use for a survey mark, and expect the matter to be understood.

Sometimes those due are a little ahead of the levellers. A kettle is found warm on a banked stove. A step has been scrubbed that no one scrubbed. A jaw passes the open door of the coke battery, lit orange, that matches no one on the saddle and resembles several. On the moss under a pipe-run a walker will have the plain sense of someone coming the other way, and will put a foot out on the rock for them, the cold side, and go on.

Children are told not to shout at the summits. Those who do say that what comes back is their own words in a grown voice, lower, and in no hurry. Some never shout again. Some go out past the last lamp every week.

When the horn sounds, the mill gates open and the shift comes out lit from the chin, and nobody looks up. Then the echo arrives, late and deep, and the choughs rise, and across the whole saddle the people stop where they are for its length. They tip their heads back toward the place where the peak will be, and each face, as it lifts out of the light from below, goes dark. For as long as the note holds, the cokemen, the levellers, the old woman in the chair by the front door, and whoever warmed the kettle in the empty house are all facing the same way as the birds. Then it ends, and they are early again, and the next shift goes in.
