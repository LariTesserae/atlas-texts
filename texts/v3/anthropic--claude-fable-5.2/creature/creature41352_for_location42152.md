What lives there is water. Perhaps five liters of it, going by the basin, and for a long time that looks like the only count the place will give twice.

It is natural to list it as a fauna, since it arrives looking like one. But no louse here had a louse for a parent, and no fly will leave a fly behind. They are less like species than like postures. Spread thin across the film on the walls, the water is lice. Left on its own surface with nowhere to go, it is rafters. Hung from the roof, it is flies, long-legged because a drop is long just before it lets go. Stood in a current, it is worms. Where it has started to come back together, it is a hunter. That is why a hunter's catch is added and not eaten, and why its legs will not count: it has the legs of everything it has collected, and uses the ones it needs.

So nothing here is born and nothing dies. The vent pays in. The opening and the lip take out. What lies between is spent as bodies and spent again, and it does not run down as long as the slit keeps breathing.

After dark the vent slows, the paying-in nearly stops, and the water does what any small sum does when it is no longer being spent. It collects. The long animal in the basin is all of it at once. Its back is the rafters' backs, pale and dry, with the seams between them closed. It has no front end. When the vent manages a breath, the animal leans toward the opening and settles back. This is the same motion the raft makes by day, except that the animal is already the length of the basin and has nowhere to slide. It does nothing else. Before dawn it comes apart along the old seams, starting from the end nearest the heat. By the time there is light to see by, it is a simmering pool with a skin of rafters, and nobody is left who was it.

That would be the whole answer if the grey ones were not there. Everything above is false of them.

They can be counted. There are nine, and counted again there are nine. A grey one crosses a palm of wall in the time a palm of wall ought to take; for them alone the blister is the size of a cupboard. And they drink. Nothing else in the hollow drinks, and nothing else would have a reason.

They are grey because they are not clean. Every other animal came out of steam and is as pure as water can be. A grey one has sulfur in it from the scored crust, and silica, and the fine dust of the glass it handles. It is a solution, and two things follow from that.

The first is the frost line. The hand's width of cold that whitens a louse in mid-step only slows a grey one, the way a salted puddle stays open beside a frozen one.

The second is that it has a size. Clean water can be any amount, a pinhead or a pool, and be none the worse. A solution has a strength. Thin it and it is only water again; let it thicken and it is crust. A thumb's worth is the amount that holds together at this strength in this heat. So a grey one is the one inhabitant with something it can lose while still being present.

Nearly everything they do is upkeep. The yellow they drink from the scored needles keeps them strong enough to go out on the lip. The plain meltwater from figures that thaw to nothing keeps them loose enough to come back. They stay away from the rear of the hollow, where steam would condense on them and water them down. All day they take bitter, then sweet, in small corrections, and the grey of a given animal shifts a shade lighter or darker as it goes.

It can be got wrong in either direction, and both show. One that has gone thin pales, and its fingers run together. It puts its flake down. By afternoon there is a hunter where it stood, or a wet place. One that goes the other way slows, and then is still. Low on the right wall, the dry one, there is a knob of grey-yellow crust the size of a thumb, with a sliver of glass standing in it edge outward. It is the only thing in the hollow that has ended and is still there.

The flake is the only hard part a grey one has. Working, it holds the glass in its fingers. Resting, it pushes the flake into itself, and the glass shows through its back as a dark line, like one bone, kept in or out as needed. Choosing takes most of their evening. They draw each candidate edge down the rock and it makes a small tick; a share of the ticking put down to the cooling walls is this. An edge lasts a few mornings, because ice dulls glass. But the cold that builds the rime figures on the lip also spalls the walls, so each night makes both the work and the tools for it.

They do not quite live in the hollow, or outside it. They live in the hand's width between. Scalding is on one side and frost on the other, and neither would keep them long.

That band moves. At dusk it backs in through the opening as the vent fails, and the nine back in with it, facing out, a little ahead of the thickening fringe. Near midnight they are ranged along the rim of the basin beside the long animal's flank. They do not touch it. At first light the band goes out again and they follow. An arm's length in and an arm's length out is a whole life for anything else, and they walk it twice a day.

On the lip they treat the figures that will walk and the figures that will run exactly alike. It is tempting to read patience into this, or indifference, but it is neither, because there is nothing to tell. A figure is not one thing or the other until it is warm. The grey ones are the single decided thing in a place where every drop is still making up its mind. They got that way by being impure, and they stay that way by labor. It is also why they are in before the sun. The sun settles whatever it finds on the lip, one way or the other. They have arranged to be neither, and they do not wait to be asked.

Under the lip, on the side the slab shades, there is a crack that neither the grey ones' reach nor the sun's gets into. Now and then a rime figure forms there and stays. Days pass over it. The wind brings it glass dust off the flow, the vent's breath brings it fume, and it goes from white to the color of old snow beside a road.

When a warm afternoon finally finds it, it does not run down the rock and it does not step briskly back in. It comes in slowly, on forelimbs that end in something not yet fingers, and begins turning over the flakes on the floor. It picks wrong ones. The nine go around it.

Next morning it is out on the lip with them. Among the few dozen clicks there is one that lands flat and dull, a bad edge on ice. Counted that day there are ten, and counted again there are ten.
