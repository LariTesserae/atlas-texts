The ones who made the roads are the only animals in the foam that carry no light. That is how they are found, when they are found. In a chamber speckled from floor to ceiling there is a gap in the speckle about the size of a seated body, and all the hair around the gap is leaning in.

They are small, and there is very little on them. A body at blood heat in air at blood heat can shed the warmth of its own work only as water, and water is the one thing here that cannot be spent. So they do nothing at a pace that would raise sweat. A blade is knapped three flakes at a sitting. A doorway is opened one chip, then a rest, then one chip, and the grinding of its rim passes from one pair of hands to the next for longer than any of them lives. The patience in those rims is a matter of heat. It is exactly as fast as a body here can afford to go. None of them has ever run.

The roads are a forearm wide because they walk with each foot set directly ahead of the other, the left hand on the cord, the right raised with the wrist turned out to part the hair. In the crowded rooms they walk with their eyes shut. The finest threads come loose at a touch, and an eye is a bag of water with nothing over it. Seeing and moving are separate acts for them. They stop, wait for the threads to settle, open their eyes, and look at the lights for a long time. Then they close them and go on by cord and by note. Most of the old ones have one eye left, and they guard it.

Their skin is dry and bare. Body hair stands up in the charge and gathers splinters, so it is scraped off as it comes. What is left is ruled all over with fine white scars, narrow and straight, because this glass cuts cleaner than anything made. They bind felt on the feet, the shins, and the backs of the wrists, and wear nothing more, since felt holds heat. After any walk they sit in pairs and go over each other with their fingertips, the whole surface, feeling for glass. It takes as long as the walk did. This is most of what they have for company. When a splinter comes out with a bead of blood behind it, the one who found it takes the bead with the mouth, since water left on the skin is lost to the air.

Their mouths stay shut. An open mouth is a leak. They grease their lips with something rendered from grazer moult, and the lips split anyway. The hum is made behind closed lips, in the chest and the bones of the face, and it is all the voice they use. What cannot be hummed is said by hand. The knots on the cords are the shapes of a language made first with fingers and pressed into another's palm or the inside of a forearm: *turn here, low rim, thin floor*. Nothing can be said across a room except a note. They have never shouted. Each of them knows what a shout would do to a chamber and to everyone in it, and carries that knowledge the way they carry a blade, wrapped.

They scrape the rind alongside the grazers. They take a grazer here and there, never two from one wall. They tap the lowest drifters with a small cut for a few mouthfuls, then pinch the cut closed. Afterward they count the lights. They have always counted, and the count has always come back the same, and they go on counting.

There are no other bones in the foam. The grazers are plate and felt, the hunters are jointed shell, and the drifters are skin. So the pegs wedged in the fractures were once inside the makers, and so were the cords. When one of them ends, the body is taken apart with the same slowness as everything else. The water goes into the ones who are left. The sinew is drawn and twisted, the long bones are split for pegs and stoppers, and the ribs are kept whole. The knots that say *thin floor* are tied in someone who knew that the floor was thin. The cord runs at hip height, which is where a hand hangs to be held, and nobody walking a road is walking it alone. The sleeping baskets are ribs, felted over. A child sleeps slung inside someone.

Each of them is named for a note. There are as many names as there are chambers along the roads, and no new name is ever made. A child waits, unnamed, until one comes free. Then it is carried to that chamber and held against a chest that hums the room's pitch, over and over, until the child's own chest gives it back and the wall answers them both. To call someone is to hum them. In their own room the glass joins in. It goes on answering after they are cord and pegs, and it will answer for whoever has the name next.

Infants are the difficulty, since an infant cannot be told. There is one room for them. It is small and thick-walled, and pitched too low for a cry to move it. Whatever hair it had came down long ago under some first crying, and the floor has been felted over the needles to the depth of a hand. It is the only place where a person can stand upright with both arms down. The grown ones do not like it. Nothing in there leans toward them.

The only blue they know is breaking glass. At a tick and a spark, every one of them goes still with eyes shut and stays that way until the rasping starts again. Children learn this before they learn the cord.

They cannot tell by warmth whether a basket has just been left, any more than a stranger could. They tell by the hair. Threads lean toward a body, and they take a long while to hang straight again after it has gone. A basket in straight hair is empty. When one has hung in straight hair long enough for everyone to be sure, a child is begun. Everything else in the foam stays the same without trying, since the rind and the lights come back by themselves. The makers are the one thing here that has to stay the same on purpose. They patch felt with felt of the same grey. They tie the old knots to the old tune. They keep their own number the way they keep the count of the lights, except that nothing keeps it for them.

As nearly as the hand-language can be followed, they believe the melt set all at once and everything set with it, except a little that stayed soft and is still moving. That little is them. It explains to them why they are the temperature of the walls. It explains why they alone wear. Skin wears, and felt, and the body that holds a name. Their palms have worn the doorways. Every one of them who passes through a ground rim lays a hand on it. It is not clear whether this is greeting or apology. The rims are the only evidence in the foam that time passes, and the makers put it there.

Once in a life, each of them walks to where the roads end. The great bubble's note is too low for any chest. It is the one room that cannot be made to answer, which is why there is a cord. The one who has come sits at the rim with open eyes for longer than they have ever kept them open. They draw the cord in hand over hand out of the hair and find the single knot. They watch the decks go by at walking pace, with the dark lanes between. They stay until they are sure. There is a shape in the hand-language for *other*. It is used for the wrong note at a wrong turning. No one has ever tied it here.

Then they let the cord back out, shut their eyes, find the hip cord with the left hand, and start humming the way home, each room answering as they enter it. At the rim the hair goes on leaning toward the place where they sat, long after they have gone.
