The short answer is that nobody lives here. The silt would have kept a track, and there are no tracks. But that leaves out most of the place.

The residents you can count are the reeds, and each is an individual in a way no reed in a sunlit marsh ever is. A stem here has a fixed address and a history you can read with your fingers. The kinks give its age. A long up-valley segment was a long thaw, and a short one was a summer when the river ran thin. Now and then a single joint veers off the common axis, toward something loud that happened once to one side, and then the stem corrects. A reed does nothing but attend, and since it also hums, it is part of what its neighbours attend to.

The moss is slower and probably older. A pillar is a few millimetres of living skin over a house-height of its own past, breathing out beads. The dishes are the quick ones. They are the only things in the lake that move daily by their own doing, a few degrees each evening and back by dawn.

Still, the stem is the wrong unit. The thing that lives here is the grove. A grove feeds on what it gives off, which makes it closer to a fire than to a plant. It catches where there is current, thickens, smothers its centre and burns outward as a ring, very slowly, across plains that record its passage in felt. The chord changes as it goes, because old stems are longer and sound lower. A grove in its last century is a wide, thin circle holding a deep note around a silent middle.

That would be the whole census, except that there is a fourth kind, and it is not a main one. It grows only on the felt inside the frame gardens, under the dishes, where a survey would miss it. It is a tuft of stiff threads a finger long, each tipped with a bead as hard as a cherry stone. It does not grow in the shaking forest by the falls, though that is the loudest water in the lake. The roar is no use to it. It needs a blow, a note with a hard front edge.

The beads drop when they are ripe, and since nothing eats them and nothing rots them, they stay. The felt of a frame garden is full of them all the way down, a metre of harvests that no one took. A hand pushed in anywhere comes up holding a fistful.

The same beads lie thinly through the silt of the entire basin, dormant. That is what the line of pale shoots after a rockfall is. The concussion wakes them along its path. They come up, lean toward where the blow came from and wait for another. None comes, and they are dead by the freeze. That is the plant's wild life: it wakes once in a decade or two and starves while listening.

Somebody watched that happen. They went to the headwall and took the same rock that falls from it, the rock that rings. They hung it where a cobble would strike it every time the current turned. A frame is a rockfall made small and regular. The crescents are the older and simpler idea, a way of thickening what already grew, and they were for the reeds, whose fibre is in every lashing. The frames were for the bead, and the quern on the terrace was for the bead too, or else for something that is no longer here.

About the growers I can say less than I would like. The walls stand chest-high to a person. Seven hearths would have meant forty people, perhaps fifty. They hauled pine up from below the treeline, a long climb for each pole, and there are more heaps of basket-stones on the valley floor than there are frames still standing, so they did it for generations. They knew where a curve throws a sound. They slept in the one place where the valley let them hear each other. Their charcoal says they had air, but their walls were built for water, and I cannot make the two agree. One of them ground a single face of a green axe, put it down and did not come back to it.

They are gone, but the arrangement is still running. Each working frame is somebody's decision about where the food should be, and it is still carried out several times an hour by a cord and a shift of current. The bead plant cannot tell the difference. It has been fed without a break since the day the frame was set. As far as it can tell, nobody ever left.

So what lives in the lake is half of a household. It is the crop without the mouths, tended by the last thirty things its people made. The number only goes down. When a cord finally parts, the garden holds for a few seasons on its own chord. Then the reeds and dishes thin back to what that distance from the falls allows. The fourth kind goes first, in a single summer. Its last threads come up pale and lean toward the tipped frame.

In the thaw months it is most plainly there toward evening. The falls swell, the current quickens and the cobbles swing more often. For a few hours hard clear notes sound all down the valley, unevenly, the way work sounds. One frame stands near enough to the ridge that its note bends over the top and reaches the terrace, very faintly, among the seven hearths. By dawn the river has shrunk and the knocking has slowed.
