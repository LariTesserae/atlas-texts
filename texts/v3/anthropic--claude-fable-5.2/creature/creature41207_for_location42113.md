Start with the people, because there are three hundred thousand of them and you could miss them.

They walk from the knee. A child here learns the set of the foot before it learns its letters, with the heel laid and the weight rolled forward. By six it can carry a full pail up a plank ramp without the ramp knowing. Boots are soled in felt and resoled monthly. Visitors from beyond the rim are called stampers, without malice. For their first week they trail a few anxious neighbours and a line of small grey scabs that the district will have to cut out of the pavement.

They are a people of forearms and inner ears. A line hand can read tension through a gloved palm on a cable, and tell you which footing has drifted and roughly when. Shimmers wear a spirit vial on a cord and seldom need it. A shimmer at someone else's table will fold a card and slide it under a leg without pausing the conversation. Every household keeps a marble on the kitchen table, and where it sits in the morning is the first news of the day. Nothing they own is nailed. Every adult carries a spanner, and addresses are given by pylon number and date.

They do talk, indoors, behind moss, in a speech that has lost its hard edges over the generations. The stops have softened and the stress evened out, so that an argument sounds like someone sorting linen. Names are chosen to be said quietly: Oon, Ensa, Halm, Iwel. The hands do the rest. Flag grammar has leaked out of the pressing halls into everything, and people who have never worked a shift still finish their sentences with their wrists. Courtship is two windows blinking shuttered lamps at each other across a street that may not be there next week. This is felt to concentrate the mind.

For anything louder they go up onto the blue. The kept islands are where the city stores its noise: the lying-in wards, the infant schools, the taverns, the courts, both concert halls. Children are born on kept ground if it can be managed and spend their loud years there, so the oldest districts are also the youngest. A couple who need to have it out take the tram to the blue and have it out, on ground as hard as basalt, among other couples doing the same. The fine cracking in those pavements is officially put down to over-ringing. Everyone who grew up there knows how much of it is schoolyards.

By statute nobody shouts at fresh ground. In practice there is a bank behind every pressing hall with crusts on it. They lift off whole if you are careful, shallow brittle dishes, ridged inside like the roof of a mouth. Some are names. The setting crews say a fair number were made by the bereaved in a single breath, and that you can tell those because they are deeper than they are wide. Nobody keeps them. The bank slumps, the wire comes through, and the cry goes to the presses with everything else and ends up as an insulator blank or forty metres of pipe. Most people find this bearable. Some find it the point.

The ringers are the exception to all of this. They are stocky, deliberate, and half deaf by thirty, the only residents who strike anything for a living. They hold the interval by breath, in shifts, for days. They are fed at any door and not often asked in. To ring is to decide what the city will still have in a hundred years, and they have been wrong before, and they live among the cracks that prove it. Their trade has given the city its words for people. A child is tender. So is an apprentice, and so is anyone a month into love. The old are kept, and so are the married and the stubborn. "You don't ring what you've known a week" is said over cradles and contracts alike.

What they lack is surprise. A street tilts, a works goes up four metres, and the woman carrying cabbages adjusts her stride. What unsettles them is the opposite: a thing that stays where it was, or a sound with no shutter on it. When the geese go over in autumn, calling the whole way, whole streets stand with their faces up. They are not afraid for themselves. They are waiting for something to answer.

The hares come next, and they have been here longer. They are big and pale and unhurried, and they are never eaten. A hare is a survey instrument and a neighbour, in that order. One will settle on a patch of yard, ears laid along its back, facing nowhere, for a day or two, and then the yard goes up. The clerks who count them are mostly young and mostly patient. They say the animals do not seem to be listening. They look more like something keeping a seat.

The crows have changed the most. A crow on a pylon arm will lean forward, open its beak, and go through the whole posture of a call with nothing coming out. The young learn the posture. They bow, flick a wing, and walk the tips heel-first like residents. The blind white beetles they hunt chew their way through the tender, and the chewing glazes each gallery as thin and hard as eggshell. A worked block breaks open on a tangle of fine grey straws. The presses reject these and children collect them. The beetle is the only creature here that leaves a permanent record of everywhere it has been, and it is blind and will never know. The voles overhead are often the only footfall in a house. The foxes use the cable trenches as if the trenches had been dug for them, and nobody is certain any more that they weren't.

The bed a hare presses for itself has always been called a form.

That is the word the pressing halls use for what comes up in the tender: the flutes, the sockets, the blind arches. The hares had the word first, and they have always sat where the ground was about to arrive. So the question of who lives here now has to include the households in the north quarter, who live in rooms nobody built. The recesses came up at the pitch of the windows. The district rang them and moved in. The walls are smooth and bluish and curve a little at the corners, like the inside of a bowl. There is a ledge at the height where a person sets down a lamp. The people who live there say the rooms are easy to be quiet in, and they don't explain further.

The survey office has an account. The city is quiet but not silent. Transformer hum, felt-seated wheels, and three hundred thousand laid heels make one long, low, patterned vibration. Over decades it is enough to half-set the deep paste in the city's own shadow before it rises. By this account the forms are a footprint taken from underneath, coming back up. It is a good account, and it covers the tram ridges and the window recesses.

It does not cover the last few years, in which the forms have begun to be slightly early. A flight of shallow risers surfaced on a slope the district would not need to climb until Thursday. Gauge ridges curved off the depot toward a quarter that has no line. The rail-layers were a tenth of the network behind, as always, and laid track along them because the grade was already made. A row of recesses appeared at the pitch of a storey nobody has added. The city thinks of itself as the thing that adapts, and it has started, here and there, to follow.

So something else lives here that has no body anyone has cut into. It has only the room it leaves. It does not seem to want the city gone, or louder. As far as anyone can tell from the shapes, it wants the city to fit.

Take one household, in the north quarter, fourth recess from the east. Seven people live there, with a marble, a spanner on a hook, and kale in a crate by the warm wall. The grandmother, Oon, is kept in every sense of the word. One corner of the floor was left unrung, as is customary, so the room can breathe. Last month that corner came up in a low welt, and in the welt was a form: a smooth, cool, slightly damp oval, as long as someone lying on their side with their knees drawn up. It fits nobody in the house. Oon has measured it with a knotted string. She says only that it is too big for the youngest and too small for the next, and she has not sent for the wire or for the bell.

Most mornings there is a hare in it. The family eat around it with their voices down and their hands moving, and they watch the marble.
