Nothing lives in the middle of the room. Whatever lives here is found by taking away the path of the air, and what remains is a rind a few paces deep around the walls, where everything alive is holding on to something.

Most of it is green and had no choice. The moss keeps a front along the scoured floor as straight as the edge of a cut peat, and it has leaned on that line since before the footholds were pecked. The hart's-tongues could live elsewhere. They grow in well-mouths and on north walls all along this coast, and here they are only doing well. The filmy ferns could not. A frond one cell thick has no skin to close against dry air, and an afternoon of ordinary wind on the heath would turn it to brown thread. Down here it is rained on every ten or twelve breaths and has never once been dry. It cannot move a body-length toward the slot. If living in a place means being unable to live anywhere else, the room is theirs, and everything that follows is a lodger.

The lodgers, then, from the smallest.

In the moss are springtails, grey, smaller than a woodrush seed. A springtail's one defence is to throw itself into the air when touched. In the deep shelves this serves. Along the front edge of the moss, half the directions it can jump lead into the middle of the room, and the ones that take those are out over the heath before they land.

The herald moths come down on the last mild nights of autumn, take the ceiling of the recess, and do not move again until spring. They eat nothing. Fog beads on their folded wings, the beads fall at the blow, and the moths stay. Whatever hearing a moth has is made for bats, for something thin and high. What this room produces is too large to be a sound to them, and it shakes them along with the rock they grip. They are as near to absent as a living thing can be. The two red points in the lamplight are only the lamp's own light handed back.

The spiders are awake. Each hangs within a leg's reach of her sac and will not go toward light. Lift the lamp and she draws back along her thread. The young are another matter. When they come out in spring they do the one thing their mothers never will, and walk toward the grey. They go up the wall shelf by shelf toward the seam of sky, and somewhere short of the rim they walk out of the lee. No one has watched what happens then. But there are cave spiders in every sea-cleft and well shaft along this shore, and the room breathes out toward all of them.

There is one wren, or there is always a wren, which from below comes to the same thing. In hard weather the heath is locked, and the moss shelves are the only unfrozen ground for a long way with anything on them to eat. The wren is a brown movement in dark green, tail up. It has learned the room as the people have. At the hiss it flattens into the moss with its feet clenched, and in the lull it works. It roosts high in the recess, in a joint of the rock it has plugged with moss. And it sings. On the heath a wren's song is absurdly large for the bird and carries across a hillside. Here the bill opens and closes on each phrase, the throat shudders, and it goes on to the end of the song as though the song were arriving somewhere.

The otter is known by its leavings. A sea-going otter must rinse the salt out of its coat in fresh water, or the fur clots and stops keeping it warm, and the only fresh water under this stretch of cliff is the brown basin in the slab. Some mornings the algae on the basin's seaward lip is rubbed through to stone, lower than a person kneels. So it likely comes for the water and eats while it is here. Its route can only be the throat, taken in the gap between one swell and the next. Somewhere under the cliff, holding its breath in a tunnel full of rolling cobbles, it counts.

Nobody sleeps here. The people live up on the heath, in houses whose floors carry the knock, and they would call this place a larder. But the knock is in their bedding all winter. At the lowest ebbs they wake in the night because it has stopped. Their children tap each other twice on the shoulder for "get up" and do not know where that comes from.

One of them is down here for part of nearly every day of the dark months, since hung meat wants turning and the lamp wants oil. At present this is a woman past the middle of her life, with a girl she is bringing on.

She is broad and short in the stride. She goes down the cups backward without looking, ice and all. Everything she carries is tied: the knife at her wrist, the oil in a bladder at her neck, the fish strung through the gills to her belt. She stands with her knees loose and her feet set across the bedding of the rock. She no longer braces in any way that shows. What she does is breathe out just ahead of the blow, so that it finds her lungs already empty and has nothing to take. The girl has not got this yet, and after each concussion her face shows it for a moment. She went down once without the wool, as everyone does, because no warning given above ground can be made to mean anything.

The woman smells of tallow about the ears. On the heath she speaks seldom and slightly too loud, and she touches people when she wants them to look.

The newest scratches on the wall are hers. The man before her took her wrist one day, drew her hand down the stone in a single stroke, and let go. What the stroke was for, he had no means of saying. She might have asked him at the rim. But the heath sounds thin for an hour afterward, and by the time it had thickened again the question seemed to belong below. He is dead now. She makes her stroke on the day the last of the meat goes up the rope in spring, which is her best guess at what that day had been. The hands before his may each have counted something different, each guessing from the one before. The marks look much alike regardless.

She does not take the wren for a bird that fails to notice it cannot be heard. In her own first winter she stood in the recess and shouted into the blast, to see what would happen. Nothing went out into the room. But she felt the shout in the hinge of her jaw, in the bone behind her ear and across the top of her skull. It was her whole voice, kept in. Nothing down here hears itself any other way.

Some days, when the turning is done and the lamp is filled, she does not give the girl two taps at once. She stands for a few blows with her back against the wet wall, mouth shut, and hums as low as she can, feeling for the note of the room. The girl sees her throat moving and looks away. If the man before her ever did the same, the note he found was a shade higher than hers, and neither of them could have known it.

Two shelves up, the wren does not stop for her.
