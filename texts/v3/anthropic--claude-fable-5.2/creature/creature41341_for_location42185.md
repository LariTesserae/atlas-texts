Three kinds of thing are alive in the room, and only one of them has a place in it.

The stain was first. It came down as a few cells in the rain and stays where the temperature allows, which is a line, not an area. It cannot go past the orange on one side or the green on the other. When the pool rises it is under water, and when the pool falls it is under air, and it does the same in both. In a dry spell it hardens to a varnish on the salt ring and waits. It has been here longer than any animal and takes no notice of them.

The flies are passing through, even the ones that hatch here. A fly lasts about two weeks. It walks the band, feeds, lays in the green, and never learns the room. Now and then one goes toward the brightest wall, as flies go toward any brightness, but the brightest wall is the one above the floor crack. Those do not come back. They dry where they drop, and the next sheet of water carries them down to the pool.

The spider is the resident. It came down the roof crack when it was small enough to ride a drop, and it has not left since. The chip it keeps to is an old one. The film closed over it long ago, so it is as bright as the wall around it, a shallow scoop the width of a thumbnail with a sharp upper lip. The water running down the wall splits at that lip and passes to either side. Inside the scoop the glass is damp but not running. The spider sits there head down with its legs spread, facing the stain a hand's length below.

Like the lava teeth, it has no shadow and so no thickness. It looks like a small flaw in the glass.

Some of its grey belongs to the room. The spider sits on a wall warmer than the air, a little heat passes up through its body, and over the weeks the film finds it as it finds everything. There is never enough to shine. There is enough to take the darkness off, so that against the lit glass the spider shows only as a dim place. After each molt it is black as a fly for a month, and in that month it catches less.

It cannot outrun a fly that is ready to fly, so it uses the fog. When the crack exhales and the whiteness comes down, the flies stop, and the spider stops too. But when the fog settles, it settles on everything. The flies come out of it beaded, and a fly has to wipe its wings before it can use them. For the few seconds that takes, it is an animal on foot. The spider is beaded as well, but it is always on foot. If a fly has stopped within reach of the chip, the spider goes then, straight down the wet glass, while the ceiling's rain is still pattering.

So its day is cut into windows. It gets about seventy chances a day. In most of them no fly is close enough. In some it misjudges, because a fly on bright glass is a dark shape with nothing around it to say how far off it is. Perhaps one rush in two hundred ends with a fly.

It carries the fly back up to the scoop and feeds there through the next cycle or two, holding on while the fog comes and goes. Then it lets the husk drop. The husk lands in the pool, drifts as the pool fills, and goes over into the floor crack with the next tongue of water. The spider's shed skins go the same way. There is no litter under the chip, and nothing to show how long it has been there.

It knows the wall mostly through its feet. The tick of a falling flake reaches it through the glass before it comes through the air, and the spider flattens. It walks around a fresh scar, since bare rock is dry and much hotter than filmed glass. Days later, when the bright margin has closed over the patch and the wall is wet again, it crosses without pausing.

When the rain stops, the windows stop. The pool goes to salt. The flies thin out, and the few that remain are dry and quick. The spider does not try for them. It draws its legs in and sits in the scoop under the steady light, for weeks if it has to, growing a little flatter.

The first change is at the roof crack. A drop falls and strikes salt, which makes a duller note than a drop on water. Some hours later the pool has an edge again. The spider's legs spread, and it turns to face down the wall.
