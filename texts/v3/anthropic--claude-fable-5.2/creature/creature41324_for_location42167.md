What lives on the plateau is heard long before it is anything else. The patter comes down the rail from the direction of the kiln house, quick and even, four beats so regular that for the first minute it could be taken for another part of the machinery. It is a long time arriving. When it passes there is a small movement in the warm air at shin height and a faint smell of copper, and then the patter is going away toward the winding tower at the same rate it came.

A hand laid on the rail will be walked over without a break in step. The weight is less than a hare's would be. The feet are bare pads, dry, set down one directly in front of the other along the rail-head, and it is these that have polished the steel. The skin is hairless, fine and a little loose, and exactly as warm as the stones, so that the hand cannot tell by temperature where the animal ends and the ground begins. The hind legs are much the longer pair and the back slopes down to the shoulders. The head is narrow. Where eyes would be, the skin runs on without a seam or a dint. The ears are small and flat to the skull, and they turn toward the wire.

One can be seen only where a caged lamp hangs low over a bus bar. The animal rises under it to feed and the crown of its head comes up into the hand's-breadth of visible air, pale as the inside of a wrist. It stands upright more easily than it walks, heels down and back straight. The forefeet have five digits, the inner one set apart from the rest, and they close around the bar. The tongue makes a dry sound like a fine file drawn slowly. The animal works from one clamp to the next, leaves the copper bright, drops to all fours and goes on. Inside the halls it breathes once to each stroke of the engine, and out on the pavement it keeps the same count.

Between buildings, where there is nothing to hear, it makes a sound of its own. This is a single thin note held in the throat at the pitch of the wires. It stops when the true note comes into hearing.

Each animal keeps a round. It goes out by the rail and back by the conduit trench, through the generating hall, the kiln house, the winding tower and the sheds, and all of them go the same way about. They are spaced along the round as the buckets are spaced on the rope, though far more widely, and they do not overtake. None has been found lying down. At the last pylon the rail ends at a stop-block a body's length short of the scarp. They turn there without slowing, and the block has a bright place where each one sets a forefoot to come round. They do not face the drop.

They do not use the doors. Beside each door, where the conduit passes through the wall, the edge of the port has been worn back smooth, and they go in there. At the generating hall they climb the iron steps up the dished middle, and at the top each one leans against the stanchion of the handrail as it turns. The stanchion has a faint polished waist at the height of a hare's shoulder. Above that there is another worn place, deeper, and above that another. They climb the post at lengthening intervals, each deeper than the one below, and the highest is the rail itself, thinned to the width of a finger.

In the long sheds a row of iron pegs runs along one wall at the height of a person's shoulder. There are thirty-one. Each is worn thin on its upper side where something used to hang, and nothing hangs there now. The conduit runs along the foot of that wall, and every animal that passes through slows once along it for a few steps, below one peg. No two slow at the same place, and no peg goes without.

No lighter step has ever been heard on the rails. Nowhere on the pavement is there a bone. The same ones go round, and they are polished as the rail is. Their pads are as smooth as the varnished stones, with no ridge or line in them. The peaks came down to swells no taller than a house, and the gravel came down to eggs. What these came down from is recorded on the stanchion. Sight has left them, and height, and every sound but the one note, and what remains is the round.

At the plate behind the generating hall each one rises and lays its tongue in the hollow, which fits it. It takes the film of moisture and goes, and the film has returned before the next one arrives.

Inside the hall, below the switch panel, each of them stops. The handles are a meter and more overhead, set where an arm would find them. The animal lifts one forefoot from the floor, holds it up for a single stroke of the engine and puts it down again. Then the patter resumes.
