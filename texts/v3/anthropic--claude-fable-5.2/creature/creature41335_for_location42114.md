Most of what is visible in the basin arrived after the roof fell. The sickle-winged birds are plains birds that found a ceiling to build on and a meal that does not run out. The flies would come to any warm, wet margin anywhere. The waders walked in, the wall-crawlers followed the flies down, and the people climb out again with their bags. All of these are living off a windfall.

Two kinds were here when it was still a cavern. One hangs under the largest arch and has kept to what remains of the dark. The other is all over the floor in full daylight and has not registered that anything changed.

The people who rake the shallows call them grain, and talk about them as they would about weather: the grain is on the upper terraces today, the grain has left the north pools. Nobody speaks of one, and it takes some effort to look at one.

A single grazer is the size of a thumb, slightly arched, with something over twenty pairs of legs that move in a wave from back to front. The shell has the give of a fingernail soaked in a bath. It is white the way a root is white, never having needed to be anything else. It lets the light through, so the gut shows as a dark thread down the middle, tinted by whatever the animal has been eating. A herd working a pink slick turns faintly pink from the inside. At the front are two smooth swellings where eyes used to be, and a pair of feelers longer than the body, which never stop moving. Underneath is a rasp, and the click is the rasp lifting off the rock.

It is blind, and what it has in place of sight is in its feet. Each leg ends in a soft pad that reads the heat of whatever it stands on. A grazer will stop dead at a line in a film of water that no eye could find, front legs lifted, back legs still walking. The slicks grow best in a narrow band of warmth, and the grazer is fitted to that band exactly, so the herd is always the same shape as the right temperature. It rings each vent at a set distance, spreads in lobes where warm water spreads, and thins where the water cools. From the stair, the white patches on the floor are a chart of where the heat is this hour.

When a pool begins to heat, the legs nearest it lift, and the lifting passes back through the herd faster than any one animal can walk. The whole mass pours downslope and over the rims. This works as long as the heat comes slower than a walk. When it comes faster, or the water simply leaves, they have nothing to answer with. Grazers on a drained terrace do not go looking for the next pool. They crowd into the last wet patch and climb under one another. When the wall-crawlers have finished and gone back up, the ones still alive are the ones that were at the bottom.

The water here lays stone on anything that keeps the same surface for long, so the grazer does not keep its surface. Every third or fourth day it wedges itself against a rim, splits behind the head, and walks out forward. The old shell is left standing on all its legs, with a grey frost of mineral already on the back. For an hour afterwards the animal is soft as curd and cannot click, and that is when the birds take most of them. The husks lift off in the next rise of water, drift to the lip of the dam, and by morning are part of it. A grazer that lives its full two months leaves fifteen or so of itself in the rock, and then the last one, with the animal still in it.

Nothing can be laid here, because an egg stuck to this rock would be sealed over before it hatched. The females carry theirs in a fold under the body, thirty or forty at a time. They let them go as finished animals the size of rice, which get in under the legs of the adults and graze what the big rasps miss. They are full grown in three weeks.

Everything about them was settled in the dark. Under the roof there was no light for a slick to eat, only a thin grey film that lived on the minerals alone. Whatever grazed it must have been sparse, a few animals to a terrace, clicking so as not to lose one another across the black. They still click, though each one now has others touching it on every side. They still go rigid at the sound of falling rock, legs braced, as if there were a ceiling left to worry about. The warm rain lands on their backs and they treat it as drip. They have no means of knowing there is a sky, or that it is full of birds, and when a sickle-wing takes the animal beside them they close the gap and go on eating.

So far their only answer to daylight has been number. The light brought the slicks, the slicks brought more food than their kind had ever had, and they turned it into more of themselves, six or seven generations a year. The birds eat them like berries, and it makes no difference to the herd.

That might be the whole account, except for the eastern floor. The creeping fissure has rearranged the pools there more often than anywhere else, and the herds have been stranded, culled, and reseeded from the survivors again and again. There the grain is not quite white. It has a grey cast across the back that is not stain, since it comes through again after every moult. The swellings at the front of the head are a little larger, and in a few animals something dark sits at the centre of each. The salt-gatherers have noticed and call it dirty grain.

The roof has been off for a few hundred of their generations, which is not long. Like the rock, the animal is still warm from being made.

At the foot of the northern wall, grazers cross the line of handprints most days, working the slick out of the palms. They leave husks in the hollows, which is partly why the oldest prints have filled. In the newest there is a finger's depth of warm water, flies around the edge, and three or four grazers rasping at the heel of the hand, one of them grey across the back. A bird goes over low. The white ones keep eating. The grey one stops, flattens, and waits for the shadow to pass.
