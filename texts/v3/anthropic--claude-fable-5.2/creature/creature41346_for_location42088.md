The first one looks like a piece of the room that has fallen off.

It lies on the flank of a ridge, where the pale drift thins toward the dark lane. It is a length of something black and glossy, as long as a foot, with the horn shine of the ribs. A broken rib would lie like that. But there is another four ridges on, and another by the pool, and they all lie along the room, one end in a still band and the other pointed at the middle of a lane. There are nine, and none lies anywhere else. Nothing else uses that ground. The light animals are pushed off it and the hunters step over.

It is not rib. A rib is solid and this is hollow, a shell shaped like an overturned hull, closed and pointed at the end in the drift and open at the other. In the open end are pages. Thin dark leaves stand face to face across the mouth, with gaps too narrow to admit a hair, and their outer edges are furred with frost. There is usually a grazer on them.

So it is a gill come loose, or a blister dropped from the wall. That is nearer, but the pointed end is warm. The pages do in the shell what the crown gills do in the roof. Sound goes in, and heat is handed along from the lane side toward the ridge side. Cold gathers at the mouth and grows ferns. Warmth gathers in the bow, two degrees above freezing, and nothing else in the room is as warm as that.

What the warmth is for is under the shell. A flat pale body closes the hull from below, the way the mat closes the room. At the bow it has a mouth, and the mouth is in the drift. Every ridge with a shell on it has a notch eaten into its edge, and the notch never deepens, because the note keeps sweeping the floor toward it. The animal is fed and heated by the same sound. On two degrees it runs a gut, and over some years it can ripen an egg.

It appears not to move. Behind each shell, though, is a pressed track in the mat, healed over at its far end. The ridges travel as the room lengthens, a finger's width a year near the old end and most of a forearm near the front, and each animal keeps its bow in its drift. All nine are walking toward the front, and none will reach it.

They have no call. When the grazers set the room throbbing and the hunters fold their legs, the shells lie as they were.

But a shell is a room, with a closed end, an open end and pages, and a room of that kind holds a note. Its length sets the pitch. A shell that reached from the middle of the still band to the middle of the lane, half a stride, would hold the room's own. These fall short, by a little or by a lot, so what they hold is higher. While the room sounds there is no hearing it, even with an ear against the shell.

They grow. But the lanes widen as the note sinks, and a shell must lengthen by a thumb's width in four years only to stay as far behind as it was.

They start in the walls. An egg is put through the skin of a blister when the blister is a swelling smaller than a fist, one egg to a blister, and the skin heals. What hatches grows up alone in a sealed chamber, around that chamber's note. It crops the lining. Its shell lies along half the chamber's length, so the note it holds is the note it lives in. As the blister widens the two sink together. For as long as that lasts it is in unison with everything it can hear.

Then the blister opens into the vault, and both notes fail.

In the silence the pages have nothing coming in. The bows are still warm and the mouths still frosted, and the trick runs the other way, as it does in the floor gills. Heat goes through the pages and sound comes out. Each shell gives up the note it holds. It is thin, like breath across the neck of a bottle, and it lasts as long as the warmth does, which is a few minutes.

So for the length of the silence the room has nine pitches in it. The newcomer, tipped onto the main floor with shreds of lining on its shell, sounds the note of its blister, which no longer exists. The others sound whatever they have grown down to. The largest sound the note the room has just lost, or within a hair of it, and the old pitch goes on in the vault at a whisper, held in two shells.

They do not go to one another. There is no time, and they are slow. Each lifts its open end clear of the mat, turns it a few degrees, and holds it there. Shells close in pitch throb against each other, the way the grazers throb against the room.

Then the lower pitch takes hold. It comes in beneath all nine. For a while every shell is sharp of it and the room beats, quickly against the small ones and slowly against the large, until the bows cool and they go out, smallest first. The stripes assemble in new places. Each animal hauls itself to the nearest ridge, lies down on the flank, and puts its bow in the still band. A line forms across the shell where growth stopped and began again. There is one for each silence.

After a silence two of them will sometimes cross toward each other. It takes most of a year, through loud lanes neither has entered before, without warmth. Each is going toward a pitch it heard for some minutes, from a direction it has had to keep since. Meanwhile the stripes go on spreading, and the place it is aiming for is carried off.

And sometimes one leaves its ridge with no egg to lay. It climbs the wall to a blister, fits the mouth of its shell against the membrane, and stays for days. It is not always a blister it has laid in. Off the floor it has only the room's cold. The frost in its pages melts and runs out. The blister's note is higher than anything its shell can hold now, so nothing in the shell answers. That much can be seen. What it gets there cannot. When it comes down it has to find a ridge, and its track in the mat starts again from nothing.

The largest lies at the old end with its bow an arm's length from the boss, where the stripes hardly travel. It has reached the room's note twice. At unison the shell runs exactly from still band to lane, and the sound passes through it as if it were open air. Each time lasted under a year. Then a blister opened, the room went down a step, and it lay on a new flank, short again. Its shell carries more lines than this room has had silences. The first few are crowded at the bow, in shell of a different grain.

The smallest is at the new end, where the mat is patchy and the till shows through. Its shell reaches a third of the way to the lane. The frost on its pages is thin, and the track behind it is a hand long. It holds the note of a room that is gone, and it will be some years before anything can hear it.
