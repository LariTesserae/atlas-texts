For a long time you'll think it's the pale creature, because the pale creature is all there is to see. But it doesn't live here so much as listen here. The large thing overhead only crosses, the way weather crosses a roof.

What lives here is inside one of the silences.

You find it by its direction. Every quiet thing in the lake travels away from the central vent: the beads along the floor, the crouched-body-sized ones more slowly behind them, all of it outward to the rim and through. One doesn't. One goes across the grain. It reaches a cairn and stops for about as long as a pillow takes to skin over, then goes on to the next. Sometimes the next is inward, toward the vent, where no silence has any business going.

At first that is the whole of it: a patch of dry with errands. The grit beneath it lies pale and still, the same as under any other. It visits the five cairns and the wall in a fixed order, once for each remaking of the floor. Ridges it goes round. Your foot it goes over, once, and the foot is dry, and the roar stops in it to the ankle.

Then, at a cairn, something comes out. Two hands push through the edge of the dry into the water, like hands through a curtain, and take hold of the top stone.

They are the grey-green of the light, which is why they took so long to see. They have no lines. The palms are blank and so are the fingertips, worn to the same finish as the stones. Everything else in the bowl is sharp because nothing has had time to wear down. These have had time. The ends of the fingers are stained rust, which tells you the vent mats aren't torn away by the current. They're picked.

Echoes come first here. You feel a knock in your jaw, a stone seating itself. Then the hands lift the stone that will make that sound. As it leaves the stack the roar catches, the lake drops, and the tops of the cairns stand dripping in air. In the dry patch the stone hangs a forearm above the grit, held against something the colour of the grit. The something has a chin, and the chin is resting on the stone.

Then the hands are out again, the stone goes back a quarter-turn round from where it was, and the sound closes over.

You get half a second at a time, and you put her together across a great many of them. Somewhere in that you stop saying *it*.

She is small. Upright she would reach your ribs, and she is never upright. She is dry all over and floured with glass grit, so that crouched and still she is a heap of floor. Under the grit she is presumably the colour of her hands, which the water keeps clean. In the falter she holds the stone against her breastbone with her eyes shut, because that is how sound gets into a body here. The rest of the time she is deaf in her dry. For that half second she has the note from the other cairns coming through her chest and can tell how far off this stone is.

So the falter belongs to her. The note is kept, the way a fire is kept. The floor heaves, the cairns ride it and drift a little off true, and she goes round setting them back. For as long as she does, there is something under the noise for the noise to be built around, and the lake stands above its own rim.

Between cairns she hears nothing, and this is where the pale creature comes in. When she rests she rests beside it, in the lee, and one hand comes out and lies flat on its back. It is exactly as big as her hand. By now you've noticed that everything is. The wall comes to her knee. The frond is the length of her forearm. The lake falls by the breadth of her hand. You had been measuring the place by your own body and coming out nearly right.

Whatever the creature takes in through its open underside, she takes in through her palm, and when she gets up she knows which cairn to go to. She leaves a pinch of rust thread where her hand was. The creature starts after her at its own pace. Her round is a circle, so she is always leaving it behind and it is always nearly catching her.

She works each cairn from the side away from the wall. Her palms keep those faces of the stones clean, and green grows only on the faces she never touches. Draw a line through each cairn from its clean side to its green one and the five lines meet at the gap. It is where she is looking, over the top of the stone, every time she works.

She didn't stack the two that stand wrong way up. She handles them like someone else's work. She takes the big top stone out with both arms, wet to the shoulder, holds it through the falter and puts it back on top, where a large stone shouldn't go. She has never set it underneath.

The stones are from a river and, going by the hands, so is she. The wall is the kind you build in moving water, low and curved against a current. The mountain is younger than most trees and she isn't. It came up under her, the stones rode it like floats, and she went on with the round. The low note may be what that river sounded like going through that wall.

Because echoes come first, she has warning. When the echo of the large thing reaches the bowl she leaves whatever cairn she is at and goes to the wall. She puts both hands into the gap, cupped, and they fill it from side to side. The frond lies across her wrists.

Then the dimness dims. She doesn't look up. From down here it is too big to be a shape and is only less light. It crosses in the water above the rim, the one part of the lake with room for it, the part that would not be there without the note. It doesn't come down. The light comes back to what it was.

She takes her hands out of the gap with nothing in them. A bead of quiet the size of a knuckle leaves the edge of her dry, because she has breathed out, and it goes up the side of the bowl toward the standing face and the rain.

Then there is a knock in your jaw, a stone seating itself, and a moment later her hands come out.
