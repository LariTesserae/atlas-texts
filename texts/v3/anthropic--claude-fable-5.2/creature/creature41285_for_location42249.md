You would find their work before you found them. Along the shaded side of a berm, grey plates have been pulled from the stack and leaned at a steep slant, forty in a row, then a gap, then forty more. At night there are dark shapes on the glowing floor beside the berm: bars, hooks, rings, a line of them a few paces long. These are places that were covered through the day and have nothing to give back. Someone laid felt and bundled stems there in the morning and lifted them at dusk. By the next night the shapes are gone and others lie elsewhere. This is the only writing on the plain, and the night is bright enough to read it by.

The people are small, dust-colored, and absent by day. Each sleeps through the glare in a slot in the berm, one body long, floored and roofed with dead plates, which hold no light. The slots are the only real dark there is.

They wake to the crackle coming from the east. For the few minutes the film lasts, everyone is out along the leaning rows with a pad of felted thread-leaf, stroking each grey plate once from top to bottom and wringing the pad into a skin flask. A plate gives about a swallow, and forty plates keep one person. The beetles stand tilted on the same plates, drinking, and are stepped around.

Nobody calls out. The plain takes a voice the way it takes a footstep, so a shout is gone within a few paces. They speak with their heads together, in a soft language that is mostly vowels and breath, or they speak with their hands. A hand held over a live plate leaves its shadow there for several breaths. Signs are made downward, and a conversation lies on the ground between two people as a heap of prints, the oldest palest. To interrupt, you put your hand over someone else's.

They know each other's faces best at night, lit from below: the chin, the nostrils, the underside of the brow. A face seen at noon, lit from both directions, looks blank to them, like a stranger's.

Their real work is lifting. The berm sinks one course every five or six years, and whatever soil lies on the bottom course goes under with it. So before a course goes, they scrape its dust into skins, carry it up, and spread it on the newest plates at the top. They move the leaning rows up and rebuild the slots one plate higher. The scrub climbs on its own, and they climb beside it. Everything they have ever made is beneath them, in order. They count age in courses: a child of two courses, a woman of seven. The dead are left in their slots and go down when the course does.

When a child is born, someone walks out onto the plain and chooses a garden, a dead plate drifting about fifty body-lengths out, and that garden is the child's. It will take roughly three courses to come in. The child walks out to it on bright nights and learns its plants, and each year it is a little nearer. The near plain is a calendar for them. Anyone standing on the berm can point to a dark patch and say whose it is and how long they have left to grow.

When the garden arrives, the plain lifts it onto the top of the stack. Its owner leans a row beside it and builds a slot under it. Then it begins to sink with everything else, ten courses from top to bottom. Most people do not outlast their plate.

Some do. The word for them means *loose*. Nothing in the berm is theirs any longer. No one sends them away, and they wipe and lift like anyone else. But the loose tend to start looking outward, and now and then one of them takes up a cage.

One of them has been loose for two courses. She goes out at dusk, when the sky dims and the ground does not. She carries a flask, a felt, a slit visor of lizard skin, the dried skin of a glider's wing, folded, and a cage woven of rust stems with eight or ten beetles in it. She walks east. From the berm she is a dark upright stroke on the glowing floor, visible for miles, since nothing else out there stands. The others watch until she is no taller than the knuckles. Then they count knuckles to remember which mark is her.

She steers by the seams and the knuckles and, when the dust pillars close in, by laying a hand flat and feeling which way the heat is going. She makes no sound walking. She kneels at each garden she passes and looks. The ones within a night's walk of the berm have been visited before, by others, long ago, and beetles live on them. Farther out the gardens hold only crust and the wiry plant. When she finds one of those, she opens the cage and tips out three or four beetles onto the dust. Then she waits for the day there.

That garden will reach the berm in something like a thousand courses, and she knows it. By then the berm she left will be far beneath whatever berm receives it. But every few courses a garden comes in to the berm with beetles already on it. They are paler than the berm's beetles and longer in the leg, a strain that has been alone a very long time. When that happens everyone turns out to look. It means that someone, a thousand courses down, walked out with a cage. Such a garden is the one thing they possess that is older than the bottom course. Nobody claims it, and its dust is lifted before anyone else's.

She might call what she does repaying, if asked. She seldom is, and over the years it has become plainer than that. She has stopped keeping track of which gardens she has stocked. She revisits a few. On one she finds twelve beetles where she left four, and she sits beside it most of a night. On another she finds none, and leaves four more. She can carry water for two nights out and two back, and a garden gives her one swallow each dawn, which stretches it a little. She has learned which plates lie under the sinking air between the pillars, where the dust will not find her. She can tell by ear, cheek to the ground, where the seams are running hard toward a thin place. She does not write any of this on the floor when she comes home, and the berm does not ask her to.

A live plate keeps her shadow for a few breaths. A dead one keeps nothing, and she sleeps better on it than she ever did in a slot. Through the day she lies curled on the grey with the wing skin over her, on the one tabletop within miles that does not shine upward.

The dawn crackle reaches her before it reaches the berm, passing under her and on toward her people. By then she is kneeling at the edge of the plate. The beetles she tipped out the evening before are already standing tilted with the film running down their backs. She wipes the half of the plate they are not on, once, top to bottom, and wrings the felt into her mouth. Then the sun clears the horizon and she lies down and pulls the wing skin over her head. The garden carries her and the beetles a hair's width closer to the berm.
