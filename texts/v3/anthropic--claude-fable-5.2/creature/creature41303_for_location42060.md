The people come last, and that is where they put themselves. A child here is taught the order on the fingers of one hand: mat, fly, shrimp, bird. The thumb is for people.

There are some millions of them. The city knows the figure no better than it knows the birds'. The nearest thing to a census is the water ledger, because the one thing short on a plateau that sweats from every surface is water fit to drink. The seep is bitter and the rain comes off the roofs white. Drinking water is steam, caught in condensers at the boiler-houses and issued by the measure, and it tastes of iron and nothing else. Everyone has a number at a condenser, and that is how the city knows how many it is.

From a distance they are pale. Cloth comes up the incline from the lowlands dyed, and a year of washing in soda takes it down to the colour of the walls, so the only bright people on a street are the newly arrived and the newly married. They wear smoked glass over their eyes against the glare of the pans. Glass is cheap, since the plateau has soda, lime and heat and buys in only the sand. They wear a cloth at the throat that goes up over the nose at dusk when the midges rise. They smell faintly of fish. That is the grease on their hands, rendered from culled birds. They are careful about it, because the water cracks skin, and a cracked hand here is what a sore throat is elsewhere.

The hand language is the public one, and it comes in two sizes. Across the terraces it is made from the shoulder with the whole arm, slowly, in thick gloves, and can be read through haze at two hundred metres. In the street it comes down to the wrists and fingers and goes fast. Either way anyone with a sightline can read it, so whatever is said outdoors is said to the whole street, whether bargaining, courting or insult. What people want kept goes indoors and is spoken, low, because the rooms are small. "Come inside" is a serious thing to be told. Most of them are half deaf by forty and lose little by it. Visitors from below are known by three habits: they shout, they wave at the flies, and they lean on walls and come away wet.

Nobody local leans. Nothing is stood against a wall either. Beds, tables and iron chests sit a hand's breadth out into the room, because a thing left touching a wall for a few years belongs to the house. Every floor has its groove where the threshold water goes through, and the colour in the groove says where on the seep line the house stands. It is orange near the fault, where the rooms are hot and close in fastest and the rents are low. It is purple toward the tail, where it is cooler and the midges are worse. People say of one another that they are from the orange end or the purple end, and they mean something by it.

They eat the lake at one remove, as the birds do. Fly pupae are skimmed off the pan margins in season, dried on the kiln roofs and pressed into grey cakes that keep a year. Shrimp go to paste. Eggs belong to whoever holds the ledge. Flour, oil and onions come up the incline against soda going down. A green thing is a luxury. Some households keep one onion sprouting in a jar on the sill, watered out of the drinking ration, and callers are taken to look at it.

Each person has two names. The hand-name is given in childhood, usually by other children, and the street uses it for life. The spoken name is given by the household and stays in it. Children's heights are cut into the doorpost, as they are anywhere, and the cuts fill. By the time the child is grown the marks are dimples that a thumb can find and an eye cannot.

Most of the work is at the present edge, on the pans and the kilns and the mains. The trade that belongs only to this city is recutting. Every district keeps its recutters and pays them by the doorway and by the letter. They take lintels and jambs back to a line, knock the tongues off gutters, and recut the street names. The city's letters have been shaped by this. Over the centuries the script has lost its closed loops, which fill first, and its thin strokes. What is left is wide, open and wedge-cut, and a fingertip can read it in the dark.

In the oldest quarter on the western pans, where the corners have gone round as loaves, there is a recutter of sixty-three whose hand-name is the sign for a jar with the wrist turned over. She got it at seven, for having to be made to put the fish back twice in one day. She has made perhaps forty children do it since. She has never given a reason, having never been given one.

She carries a measuring rod, a pitching tool, three chisels and a brush. When she opens a jamb, the cut face shows its bands, and she reads them without meaning to. There are thin white years, and a brown line where a main failed above the door. There is a grey decade when the lime kilns burned bad coal. Under the last band she comes to the stroke marks of whoever cut the doorway before her, sealed over and sharp as the day they were made. She knows three of her predecessors this way. One was left-handed. One took too much and left the jambs hollow. One worked for years with a nick in the chisel edge, and the nick printed a fine ridge down every stroke. She has not marked her own tools. Her habit of finishing a jamb upward will do.

Today the doorway belongs to an old man who has been turning his shoulders to get into his own house for a year. The last cut was made before she was born. She takes it back two fingers a side and three off the lintel, and it takes her until noon.

Then she turns to the name on the corner, which is down to five shallow dents. The street has a hand-name, a forearm crooked like a bent pipe, and everyone uses that. The carved name is the spoken one, and nobody outdoors has had cause to say it aloud in years. She asks on her hands and gets three answers from four people. The ledgers that would settle it are in the district office, in an iron box packed with quicklime, and the office is a tram ride away.

So she goes inside with the old man, and he says it into her better ear, and she cuts that. It may not be what was there. The quarter has a Salter's Row that the oldest ledgers give as Sister's Row, and nobody knows which recutter did that, or whether she misheard or was told wrong.

At dusk she goes home to the purple end, to a room she has had for forty years and which is two fingers narrower than when she took it. From her step she can see the bats leaning out over the pans. The fox that dens in the dead main opposite comes out, looks at her, and goes off along the ledge. Inside she scrubs the stone dust off and greases her hands. The lamp is already furred with midges.

Her spoken name is Kesta. Four living people know it. The one who used it most is on the eastern scarp, eleven years in, and still has shoulders but no longer has hands.

One of the four is her granddaughter, who calls her something else. The girl is sitting on the floor by the groove with a jar on her knees. There is a fish in the jar, finger-long, and the lamp shows through it. They are indoors and the woman could say it. She turns her wrist over instead. The girl looks at her a moment longer than she needs to, then kneels and tips the jar. The fish goes along the groove, under the far wall, and into the house next door.
