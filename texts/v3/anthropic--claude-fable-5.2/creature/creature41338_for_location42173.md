Most of the paths over the rim are worn in both directions. One is not. It leaves the widest of the descents just below the frost line and runs level around the bowl, nine kilometres more or less, and it is narrower and deeper than the others, the way a path gets when a single set of feet has made it.

The feet belong to one of the grazers. It came down at dusk with the rest, some years ago, carrying a hind leg, and in the morning it could not get up the scoria. Outside the rim that is a matter settled in two nights. In here the nights don't settle anything. The animal stood at the bottom of the path until the herd had gone over the lip, and then, since it was not cold and there was food at its feet, it ate. The raptor came down out of the plume, sat on a boulder for most of a week, and went back to the rodents.

The leg knitted short. The coat went next. It was a coat for dry cold, as fine as anything grown, and in the wet it felted, loosened at the roots, and came away on the ferns in sheets. What is left is a bib on the chest and a ridge along the neck, and those are green, because moss covers every surface that holds still and she holds still a great deal. The skin underneath is slate-coloured and always wet. The legs are stained orange to the hock and olive above it, from the lake. On the three good feet the toes are worn square. On the fourth, which carries little, the horn has grown out long and curled like a slipper.

She keeps to the top of the green. Lower down the air is too thick for an animal built to hold its heat, and near the centre the ground is too hot to fold her legs on. Her resting place is on the tide mark itself, among the swollen cushion plants, with warm ground under her belly and the cold spilling over the rim onto her back. In the afternoon rain she stands there with her face up, and some of what lands on her nose is still snow. She sleeps with her jaw flat to the ground. The pulse comes up through the bone, and she would know at once if it missed.

She drinks from the big leaves, tipping them with her nose. The lake is for standing in.

Once a day she goes down to the works. Her kind have front teeth that never stop growing, and outside, the wire grass keeps them short. Nothing here is tough enough, so she grinds them on the flues. Certain stacks along the fracture have a band at muzzle height rubbed white and smooth through the rust. While she is there she licks the iron stain, and her tongue is the same orange as her legs. Nobody was ever here to count the cycle, and she is not counting either, exactly, but she has never been at a flue when its turn came. She lifts her head and steps back a length, the stack breathes out, and she steps in again.

The place has found uses for her. The hummingbirds pulled the shed fleece out of the ferns and still tug at the bib while she stands, and most nests in the fern belt are lined with it. Flies graze her back as they graze the mats. Her dung pile (her kind keep to one) is the richest square metre in the caldera, and something large-leaved has come up out of its proper band to grow beside it.

Before the heavier storms, when the herd is up on the rim, their coats lift and crackle with the charge. Hers is too wet. She watches the violet come up on the rocks and nothing happens to her.

At dusk they come down. They arrive with the cold still in their fleece, and for the first hour the wet air beads on them and runs off. Nothing beads on her. They smell her over, struck match and moss, and then let her stand among them in the shallows. In fog to the shoulder she cannot be told from the rest. Before the sun is high they go up. For a long time she tried the slope behind them every morning. Now she walks them as far as the line and stops, and it is no longer clear that the leg is what stops her. It hardly matters. What she would need up there is lining nests.

This year the level path has a second set of prints in it, small. Some dusk or other accounts for that. The calf was born in the afternoon rain at the top of the ferns, wet, like everything here, and it has not yet been dry. It has not been cold either. Its coat is already coming away on the fronds. It has two good hind legs, and in the mornings it goes up the path with the herd's young, fast, well ahead of her. At the frost line it stops, as an animal stops at the edge of water. It puts its nose down to the white, jerks its head back, and stands watching the others go up over black rock into the wind.

Then it comes down to her and the two of them move off along the level path. Below them the first flue in the line lets out its breath, and then the next.
