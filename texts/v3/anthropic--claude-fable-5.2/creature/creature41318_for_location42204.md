**Uncle**

The engineer doesn't live there. He arrives at six with the ledger under his arm and goes home at dusk to a house on the market street, as his father did. The foremen have homes too, and the dock men are only in the yard from four until the crusher stops. Ask in the lane who lives at the ice works and the first answer is "nobody." The second, after a moment, is "well, Uncle."

You will have been in the yard some minutes before you see him. He isn't hiding. He moves at about one step to each thud, and the eye does with him what the ear does with the falling water. Then he crosses under the frosted pipe and you realise he has been in view the whole time: a small old man in a singlet and rolled trousers, carrying an oil can with a long spout.

The pay sheet still lists him as *tower boy*. The lane's account is that an aunt who sold dried shrimp in the market brought him in from the flats one dry season, a child of nine or ten with a chest that whistled. She asked the engineer's father if he could sit on the leeward side of the tower while she worked. By the rains he had stopped whistling and started climbing. He was light and the slats needed replacing, so that became his job. Then the ferns took over the slats' work and the job went out from under him. Nobody took him off the sheet. By then he was also doing the oil cups, the strainer and the belt, and those held.

He still climbs. Once a week he goes up through the fig to clear the trough at the top, so the rain falls evenly on all four sides. There is no ladder. After sixty years his feet go where the roots are, or the roots have grown where his feet go. From the shophouse windows a grey head appears at the top of the steaming tree, stays ten minutes, and sinks again.

The rest is the round, which takes forty minutes:

- He fills the oil cups on the compressor.
- He clears the weep holes above the condensers with a length of wire and touches nothing else.
- He lifts leaves and fallen figs out of the basin strainer.
- He runs a hand round the edge of the husk door, feeling for cold.

He does this every two hours or so, day and night, without a clock, and sleeps in pieces in between. At the basin he puts his hand in the water. The engineer has the thermometer. If the two ever disagreed, the engineer would check the thermometer.

The air has done to him roughly what it did to the flywheel. His skin is smoother than his age should allow, from a lifetime in mist, and the rims of his nails have a green cast that doesn't scrub out. His forearms carry a scatter of small pale scars from the years when the pipe joints were worse. His eyes are always a little wet, and they run before anyone else smells a leak. When they do, he fetches a sulphur taper and walks the joints until the smoke turns white.

The tapers live in a biscuit tin behind the switchboard, with his cot, a second singlet, a photograph, and the geckos. Everything he owns that could rot is kept there, and it isn't much.

The lane thinks he is deaf. He isn't. He has stopped hearing the four sounds and hears only what is not one of them, such as a bearing starting to run dry or a belt lacing that has begun to click. If the power fails at night he is on his feet before the flywheel has finished turning.

Twice a month he goes out to the market street for tobacco or a haircut. He walks at the same pace there, and the motorbikes go round him. Spoken to, he waits about a second before answering. People take it for slowness. He is letting the beat go by.

He says no in the same two places the works does. Children who follow their mothers into the yard may wander anywhere except the belt run and the dock path, and there they meet a flat hand. There is a third no, which is his own. A new foreman once took a wire brush to a condenser coil and found fingers resting on his wrist, not hard.

At the end of the rains it is he who cuts back the fig. He sharpens the machete the night before, and the cutting takes ten minutes. The cuttings go into the canal, because anything left on the paving would root.

At four he works the hoist. As the first block goes down the chute he knocks a chip off its corner, holds it to the bulb to see that it is clear right through, and puts it in his cheek. It is the only ice eaten inside the works.

When the crusher stops he takes his tea to the canal step and sits with his back to the yard. On kingfisher mornings the two of them face the same way for most of an hour.

He has never planted anything. He doesn't tend the pots the women leave, since nothing there needs tending. He does know whose is whose, and he points when they come back. The ones nobody comes back for he leaves where they are. If you asked him who lives at the ice works, he would look at the tower.

The engineer has twice raised the matter of a pension and a room in his own house, with a proper bed. Both times he raised it in the engine house, where the available words are more oil, less water, come and look, and tea. Both times he got tea. He hasn't pressed. He has seen the east wall, and he isn't certain which of them is holding the other up. The number in the ledger is good.
