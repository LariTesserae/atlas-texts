At first the answer seems to be trees. Billions of them stand shoulder to shoulder under black crowns, each with its own trunk and its own thirst.

But follow any trunk down and it stops being one. It fuses with its neighbour at the bark, shares a duct with a third, and hands its water to a beam that belongs to no stem in particular. The wood is the same wood everywhere, branching at the same angle. There are no individuals to count. There is one body, and the things that look like trees are closer to its hairs.

So one creature lives here, the size of a continent. Its skin is the canopy, a layer of living leaf and green wood. Under that is the plumbing, alive only in thin sheaths around the great vessels, and under that the grey, the glassy, and the stone. By mass, most of it is finished rather than alive. It lives like a reef, a thin working layer on top of everything it has been, except that a reef does not plan its limestone and this does.

It has a day. In the morning it exhales and closes its own sky. Through the light hours it drinks, hauling rivers upward in columns that snap and re-form. At night it sets, and the day's dead are packed with mineral and added to the foundation. It eats nearly all the light there is. It takes lightning the way other things take salt.

What it lacks says more. It has no thorn, no bitter resin, and no scar tissue, because nothing has ever bitten it. It has no flower, because it has no one to send pollen to, and no seed, because it has nowhere to send one. It does not race for light, because the leaf shading its leaf is its own. Almost every living thing is shaped by what tried to eat it or outgrow it. This has been shaped only by load, flow, cold, and light. It is enormous and entirely unguarded. Nothing in its body allows for the existence of anything else.

It also has no ear. The noise is the first thing for a visitor, and for the resident it is nothing. This may be the loudest place in the world, and its only occupant has never heard it. It registers tension in the water, weight, charge, and the angle of the light. Whether that adds up to anything felt, I can't say, and I don't think the place says either. No part of it looks built for deciding. No part seems to wait for news from another.

That brings me to the surge, and here the creature changes shape again. A body that moves everywhere in the same instant ought to have a centre sending a signal, but there is no signal, and nothing could carry one that fast. The simpler reading is that every growing tip holds the same count, begun at the same moment, and each arrives at the same number alone. There is no conductor, only the same score held separately by every player, none of whom can hear the others. It is one body in its plumbing, but in its timing it is a multitude of solitary things in perfect agreement, each keeping faith with a beginning it cannot remember. The hour-long roar afterwards is the only place that agreement becomes an event, and it passes through the body unheard.

So perhaps what lives here is the count. Leaves are resorbed, wood goes down to become rock, and the creature of an age ago is now bedrock under its own feet. What persists is the angle of the branching and the number being kept. Whoever meant this left no metal and no marker. They left an instruction, and the instruction grew a body and is still being carried out.

Look at what the instruction produces. It produces bedrock, altitude, weather, and a sealed sky. That is a place more than a creature. The inhabitant is building a continent from the top down, out of itself, with no one on it. I keep trying to see it as a thing that lives somewhere, and it keeps becoming a thing that is making a somewhere. It is like scaffolding, or the lining of a room prepared for someone who has not arrived.

Which brings me to the basin. Everywhere else, water is caught within metres of falling. Here the canopy has thinned, the stone lies bare, and for several weeks a finger's width of water has been let go. This is either the first thing the creature has failed to do or the next line of the schedule. I cannot tell which. I suspect the creature could not either, because it has no way to distinguish failing from following.

Either way, water running on sharp new stone will eventually round an edge. Then there will be a grain. It will be the first thing on the plateau that is not the plant, was not sent anywhere on schedule, and lies where it happens to fall. After the grain will come silt, and after silt a pocket of something that could hold a root that isn't this root.

So my best answer is that what lives here is a beginning. It is alone and it is obedient. It has no word for a neighbour and no defence against one, and at one point on its surface it is letting a finger's width of water run, which will in time make room for whatever comes next.
