By count, frogs live here. After frogs come slugs, then crabs, bees, ducks, goats, pigs, dogs, and whatever happens to be crossing the gap that hour. People come well down the list, about four hundred of them at six or seven to a house, and they would not dispute the ranking. They talk about the town as something they crew.

In the valleys they are known before they are seen. The smell of hearth smoke comes down the stairs ahead of them, deep in their hair, their cloth, and the weave of their baskets. Then comes the voice. They speak in the market towns below exactly as they do at home, at a near-shout, and valley people take them for angry. They also stand too close and keep a hand on your sleeve the whole time they are talking. At home, the person you are bargaining with can vanish mid-sentence. A sentence begun in sunlight is finished in whiteness with no change of tone, and the hand on the sleeve is how each knows the other is still there.

Their feet are bare and broad, with toes that spread and grip. The soles are pale and ridged, as if just out of a bath, which in a sense they are. Every evening hearth ash is rubbed between the toes; parents do it for children and children for the old. Their fingertips are permanently puckered, and their knuckles are crossed with fine white scars from leaf-thin knives.

Everyone past the age of seven carries a knife and a hank of fern cord. Nobody walks the upper streets without one hand trailing the rail, and visitors assume it is for balance. It is inspection. Whoever finds a loose lashing reties it on the spot, load still on their back, conversation unbroken. They cannot leave a blocked gutter alone either, and down in the valley towns they have been seen clearing strangers' drains.

Their bark-cloth hangs in the rafters overnight and is put on in the morning warm, stiff, and reeking. They have no word for dry. "Smoked" does that work and more: a smoked promise is one you can lean on, and an elder is well smoked, said with admiration. A child is green. A few people are called stone, the way the stairs and footings are stone, and it is not entirely a compliment.

The town uses a person in different places as they age. The smallest children have the roofs, lying along the eaves with hooked sticks to pull oak seedlings before the roots find the rafters. At about ten they get the goats. They carry kids up ladders, shift the nannies from a grazed roof to a shaggy one, and learn every bell by pitch, until they can say from indoors whose animal is on whose house. In their teens they get the stairs, the tumpline, and the salt coming back up. They also work the hedge, climbing through its interior with cord in their teeth to lash this year's shoots to last year's. Grown, they settle to the nets, the mallets, the hives, or the dye vats. Old, they get a doorway and a dog, and they twist cord, which the town consumes like food.

At each birth a hanging root of the hedge is chosen and fed into split bamboo. It reaches the soil about when the child walks, and by the time the child is carrying loads it is as thick as a wrist. Everyone knows which root is theirs, and nobody makes anything of it. They lean on it while waiting for someone at the gate. The dead go into the ground at the hedge's inner foot, where nothing stays as it was put. The hedge is thicker than a house is wide.

They address animals in the same voice they use on each other and expect about the same rate of compliance. Scraps go down to the pig through a gap in the floor, and the household sleeps above its grunting. In the week after the pig is killed, nobody sleeps well. The macaques are treated as a neighbouring town of thieves, individually known and named, and grandmothers with slings carry on the feud. Nobody flinches at swifts. On market day you can tell residents from visitors because the visitors duck.

The fowlers are the only quiet ones. They stand two to a mast with their hands on the rope, faces turned into the gap, and they hear pigeons as a change in the wind's sound before there is anything to see. They wring a neck with the same half-turn of the wrist they use to finish a lashing. The nets stay down on butterfly days, when the drift settles on anything salty, such as the salt-seller's baskets or the back of a porter's neck. They come down again at dusk for the bats, which tear the mesh and eat the midges.

Nobody is the gong-striker. When the far houses start to go, whoever is nearest picks up the beater, whether that is a child of six or a fowler walking home. They keep the stroke until the cloud blows through or someone takes it from their hand. This works because the town always knows who is on the stairs. Any of the four hundred could tell you who is down, with how many baskets, and when they are due.

At night the hearths cannot be allowed to die. In each house the lightest sleeper, usually the oldest, wakes two or three times to feed the fire. At any hour, a few people across the sixty houses are squatting over coals and listening. Those who trade below sleep badly in valley inns because of the silence. Up here, if the frogs stop along one lane, every person on that lane opens their eyes.

If someone is still out, the gong goes on in the dark, one stroke at a time. It passes without a word between people who come out of their houses to take the beater. It stops when wet feet sound on the root ramp in the tunnel. Nobody greets her. Someone counts the baskets and the beater is hung up. The frogs have the rest of the night.
