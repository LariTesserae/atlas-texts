There is one more. It is left off the lists because it is seen wrong several times before it is seen.

The first time, it is a stone. The hearth ring has eleven stones, and on some evenings twelve. The twelfth is darker than the others, wet, and not blackened on its inner face. By morning the count is right again.

The second time, it is a length of fig root dropped from the curtain and lying across the moss on the shoulder of the mound, root-brown and root-thick. But a fallen root lies straight or lies broken. This one lies in a curve, and an hour later the curve is a different curve.

The third time, it is water. A shine goes up the back wall where no drip runs, more slowly than any drip comes down.

After that it stays an animal. It is as long as a forearm with the hand included, and more than half of that is tail. The body is low and soft and, like the room, has no edge anywhere. The head is broad and flat, with large black eyes set on top of it, made for the brown-green dusk and nothing brighter. The back is the colour of that dusk. The legs are short and stick out sideways, and they end in small flat hands webbed to the fingertips, four fingers on the front pair and five on the back. On those it walks up wet stone as easily as across it.

Along each flank runs a row of fine upright grooves, and water climbs them the way it climbs a wick. The animal has only to stand with its toes in one of the cupped basins, and in a little while it is wet to the spine. It needs to be, because it has no lungs. The fissure breathes and the bats breathe, but the animal takes its air through its skin, and only while the skin is wet. Its throat flutters constantly, which looks like breathing and is smelling. So it has no voice. The crickets gave up a song, but this animal never had the bellows to make one.

Every surface in the room is occupied, so it lives under one. The fallen roof never packed solid. Beneath the flowstone and the black soil the old blocks still lean on one another, and the gaps between them are the warmest wet in the chamber. The door is under a stone of the hearth ring, on the downhill side. It is a slot a flat hand would just fill, and its lip is worn to a gloss.

It keeps inside through the dry rain at dawn, the shaft at noon, and the thick afternoon. It has never been in the shaft. At dusk, when the ceiling comes apart, it puts out its head and shoulders and lies in the ring being a stone. When the roof begins to sweat and the first warm drops come down, it goes out.

It walks without hurry, one hand and then the opposite foot. Every few steps it lowers its chin and touches the ground once, like a finger put to a line in a book. This is how it reads. Sound dies in the moss within a pace, but a taste laid on wet stone stays for days. The chin learns which cricket crossed here and which crab, and where the animal itself went out three nights ago and came home.

It hunts the pale crickets, and the wax-coloured crabs when they come up onto the basin rims. It stops a body-length short. Then the cricket is gone, and its antennae, three times the length of its body, are the last thing drawn in. The tongue is the one fast part of the animal. As it passes, the frogs lower their white feet. When it has gone by, they raise them again.

Its underside is red, the red of the pale plant's leaf-backs. The belly is red, and the throat, and the palms of all four hands. Almost nothing sees this. The animal is under its stone during the green hour, and at night the only light is the glow-worms' blue, which turns red to black. On the wettest nights it climbs the back wall and goes out onto the overhang above the fissure, among the beaded threads. From below it is a dark shape with four darker hands, spread.

If something seizes it, it arches to show the red, and its skin lets out a white glue. When one dies in the open, the beetles, which have everything else gone by morning, take three nights over it.

Some years a second one is in the room for a week, smaller and narrower in the head. It comes out of the fissure, which runs back further than an arm can reach and is warm all the way. The room is probably the edge of their country and not the middle. For a few nights the two of them walk slow circuits of the mound in line, he leading, her chin resting on the root of his tail. Then there is one again.

She hangs the eggs from the underside of a block inside the mound, in a cluster about the size of a fig, each egg on its own stalk. She coils round them for half the year without eating and turns them with her chin. The room has no pool for a tadpole, and the eggs don't need one. What comes out of each is the whole animal, no longer than a fingernail, its palms already red. The young leave by the door under the hearthstone and go into the moss. It is deep enough to lose a finger in, so it is deep enough to lose them. The whip-spiders take most and the frogs take some. Once in a great many broods, one reaches a size nothing in the room can swallow. Some night that one tastes the lip of the door, finds the trail there gone stale, and goes in.

So there is always one under the hearth, and it is not always the same one. Nobody has counted how many there have been since the fire went out. Nor can anyone say whether the ring was built over a door already worn smooth. If it was, then for as many seasons as the people stayed, the one beneath the stone felt heat come down from above. For an animal of this room, that is backwards.

When the bats pour back in at dawn, the ring has eleven stones again, and the frogspawn in it is short by a mouthful.
