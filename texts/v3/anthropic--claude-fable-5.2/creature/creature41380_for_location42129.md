Most of what is alive in the room has been listed already, and most of it does not so much live here as occur here. The fish are deliveries. The amphipods hold on, which is a different thing. The flies and ferns belong to the light and would belong to it the same way anywhere. If living in a place means having an arrangement with it, the room has one resident, and it takes a long watch from the lip of rock to see that it is there at all.

It appears first as a mistake in a pothole. Near the west margin, where the floor shelves up and each drain bares the rock for a minute, one pothole is wider than its neighbours, and the stone in it is wrong. It is too pale and too large, and it turns too slowly, one revolution for every three or four of the others'. The other potholes are pecked rough by their tenants. This one is polished like the inside of a bowl. Then the pool starts to climb, the chord comes up in the side passages, and the stone undoes itself into a length.

It is an animal, something over a meter long and thick as a forearm, built on the plan of a salamander. It has a flat spade of a head, four short limbs, and a tail that makes up a third of it, finned above and below. It has no eyes. Where they would be, the skin simply continues. Behind the head on each side is a small tuft of gill, dark red, the only strong colour in the room that the spray did not make.

It ought to be white, as everything the cold river brings is white. Underneath it is, faintly pink where the blood runs close. But its back is the soft grey of wet slate, darkest along the spine, and the boundary runs down each flank as cleanly as the surge line on the wall. This is the seam's doing. Skin of this kind keeps the means to darken long after the lineage has lost any reason to, and the light has had all the time it needed. So the one creature that cannot see the room is the one creature the room has coloured. It came from a place with no day to a place with no night, and it has no way to tell them apart.

What it does is go round. Nothing swims against this water for long, and it does not try. It lets the rise lift it out of the pothole and carry it east across the pool, steering with the tail and putting a hand into a scallop now and then, the way a person steadies along a ship's rail. At the top of the surge, the water has it most of the way up the east wall. It climbs the last half-meter itself.

This is the only time it is seen entire. It lays its head and chest into the gap between the fourth and fifth studs, always that gap. It is far too heavy for the firmness to hold, and its hind feet never leave the rock. But the air there takes some of the weight of its head, as a draught takes some of the weight of a hand. The spray bends around it, and its skin goes matte. For four or five seconds it is the largest dry thing in the room.

Sometimes it drags a flank along the firmness and a rag of old skin comes away. That may be the whole reason. Sometimes it only rests there, against the one thing it knows that pushes back without being water or stone. There is no telling.

Then it lets go, and it lets go before the boom, always by about a breath. In water it is all ear. A row of pits along each flank reads pressure, and the chord that makes speech useless must be, for it, the plainest statement in the world of how full the room is and how soon it will not be. The surges are not regular, and it is never caught. Watch long enough and you take your own warning from it: when the grey shape drops from the wall, set your teeth.

The drain takes it south with the gravel, toward the throat. At the same point each time it folds once and goes through the ring. It comes out as the water does, smoothed, and hangs in the tongue of glassy flow among the white fish. The quiet is about a body long, and it fits with a little to spare. The fish do not move away, having no means of knowing what it is. Now and then, after it has gone, there are three where there were four. It eats perhaps that often. An animal like this can make a meal last a season.

Before the turbulence takes the calm back, it is already down in the lee of the pedestal, belly to the rock. The next rise carries it west along the floor to the pothole.

The room is doing to it what it does to the pebbles, only more slowly, because the animal grows back. Its toes are blunt. The edge of its tail fin has been frayed and healed many times, with one notch a third of the way from the tip that has not closed. Its belly, where it rides the bowl, has the sheen of a much-used handle. How old it is cannot be guessed. The grey took years and the polish on the pothole took longer. There is only the one. There may have been others before it, but the room keeps nothing.

It sleeps there, coiled nose to tail-root, turned by the eddy, in instalments of three or four minutes. It has never slept longer than that. When the chord begins, it is already uncoiling.
