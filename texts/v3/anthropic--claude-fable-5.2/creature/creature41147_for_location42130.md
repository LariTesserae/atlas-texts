The first answer is the plant. It has the whole town to itself, and for most of a first visit that seems to be all there is to say.

The second answer touches you on the face. It happens in the lanes, where the walls are close. Something like a dry grass stem is drawn across your cheek and is gone. A few paces on there is another, across the forehead. You take them for rootlets or loose fibre until you catch one between finger and thumb and it pulls back with more strength than a straw should have.

They come out of the jars. Each is a feeler, jointed, as long as a forearm, stiff at the base and hair-fine at the tip, held out across the lane in the moving air. Most jars show two. Follow one back to the clay and put your fingers in the mouth, and you meet a flat disc that feels like unglazed pot, slightly dished, fitted to the neck as closely as a lid. It was not there a moment ago. Take your hand away and the jar does not start humming again.

Neither do the jars on either side of it, or the ones beyond those. The stoppage runs down the lane to the turning, jar by jar, faster than you could walk. The reeds go on sounding, but the lane is thinner and plainer for a hundred breaths. Then the jars come back, the farthest first.

This is what they have instead of a cry. Where the plant makes all the noise, nothing is gained by adding to it. The only signal that carries is to take a note away.

Not every jar is tenanted. The empty ones hum lower, since a body takes up room and a smaller hollow sounds higher. As a tenant grows, its note creeps up. The jars are tuning themselves upward like the streets, and for the same reason: something is slowly filling them.

You meet the rest of them in the slack. The chord sags and drops out, the pods go still, and the ticking starts. Some of it is the wood. The rest is claws on bark plate. They come down the walls face first and spread out over the floor, and they do not sink in the dust as you do. One crosses your foot with the weight of a handful of leaves. A feeler finds your shin and climbs to the knee. Then the animal folds.

Folded, it is the size and shape of a fallen pod, hard-shelled and almost weightless. You will have nudged several with your foot already and thought nothing of it. The difference is that a pod rattles.

In the hands it does nothing at all. The front is the lid, round and rough. Behind it are overlapping plates, stiff and dry, cool as the wall. There are more legs than touch can sort out, each folded into its own groove, and the feelers lie wound against the face like watch springs. The underside is a comb. Knock on the back and it answers hollow. Like the mound, it is mostly air. It smells of pepper and old cupboard, and it may be that the town smells of them rather than the other way round.

What they do on the floor is comb it. Cork crumb and shed scale go in, along with grit to grind them, and the grit comes out finer. The brick may have gone to grit by itself, but the grit went to flour through them. Every handful of the dust has passed through the residents many times.

They are back up the walls before the air turns. When the draft touches the back of your neck, the feelers are out across the lane again, trailing the other way. Why they keep to the slack is a guess. Moving air is drying air, and they have no water to spare. They seem to live on what is bound in the cork.

Whether the builders kept them in the jars or could not keep them out, the walls do not record. Storage or sound, the guess went. A granary pest that outlasted the grain would fit the first. And a street that goes quiet ahead of a stranger would have been worth something to the people living on it.

The damp at the throat they use for one thing. The lip is smooth because it is used. Set into the callus along the lower edge is a row of pits, and in some of the pits is a bead the size of a lentil. A tenant makes the trip rarely, down the middle of the main street in the slack before a cold indraw, and one from the far lanes may make it once. The hatchlings are flat and thumbnail-sized and would pass for shed scale. They walk in with the cold air behind them and find a home the way you found the town, by ear. A jar that hums lower than its neighbours has no one in it.

So the young settle near the throat and the old are deep in. None of them moves house. The population moves anyway, a lane at a time toward the door, keeping ahead of the closing.

In the tightest lanes, where you go sideways, the wood has come in over the jar mouths as it has over everything else. A mouth that would take a fist near the throat takes two fingers here, then one. The tenants behind these are the oldest in the town, and their voices the highest, thin as a whistle. They can no longer come down in the slack. A single feeler still comes out. Pressed to the rim of each hole there is usually a pellet of cork crumb the size of a pea, tacky with resin, and fresh. Something brings these and sets them where a feeler can reach.

Past that the rims are flush and the wood is dead to the knuckle. In the dust under some of the sealed rims there is a small heap of pellets. The ones underneath are hard. The ones on top are not.

There are fewer tenants than jars, and fewer open jars with each long lifetime. The main street will have the last of them, as it will have the last of everything.

From across the pan, the chord you walked toward had a faint grain along its upper edge, above the reeds. That was them. And it is true that you can walk the whole town and leave without making a sound of your own. But you will have been heard all the way, as a few jars' worth of quiet moving down the lanes ahead of you and filling in behind.
