**Evidence**

By day there is nothing to see of it. There are three kinds of sign, and none of them lasts.

The first is the film. On certain ridges, in the first hour, the iron sheen on the pools is already broken into plates, pool after pool, in a line that can run for half a morning's walk. Nothing has touched the water. By the next dawn the film has closed again.

The second is in the frost: a single line of prints along a ridge top, each as wide as a spread hand. The steam hour takes them.

The third is in the rosettes. Here and there a sleeve of dead leaves bulges low on one side, and the leaves there are polished and lie closer than the rest. The grazers don't crop at the foot of these plants. The fox stops, takes the air, and goes on after the herd.

**The night**

The schedule has a gap. After the small birds lock up and the crowns close, there are ten hours or so in which, by the account of anything that works by day, the plateau is shut. The sky is clear, because the cloud belongs to the day. Frost comes down hard, and the top two fingers of moss set into a crust.

The animal that lives here is built low, with its belly a hand's width off the ground. In wet moss it would plow. On the crust it walks clean.

**The animal**

It is about the weight of a half-grown grazer and less than half the height. The coat is the color of the leaf sleeves and hangs straight down in flat locks about the width of a dead leaf, so that when the animal backs into a sleeve it is one more layer. The feet are broad and bare-soled, pale underneath. The eyes are small. The whiskers are stiff and longer than the head is wide.

The front teeth are deep orange. The iron is in the enamel, and it gets there the way it gets into the seeps, by way of the water.

It is warm. On a still night its breath hangs over the ridge behind it in a row of small clouds, one at each rosette, because it stops at each rosette. This is the only cloud the plateau gets after dark.

**Rounds**

It walks one ridge a night, out from its sleeve and back by first grey. At every rosette it stops, pushes its face in under the dead leaves, and lays its cheek against the trunk for a slow breath or two. A living rosette keeps some heat in its core through the night. One that has flowered and finished does not.

The warm ones it leaves. When it finds a cold trunk it strips the sleeve, sets its teeth to the frozen stem, and cuts. This takes most of a night and makes a dry scraping that carries a few paces. The trunk comes down along the ridge. The animal eats the pith and the beetle larvae in it over the following week, and what remains is under moss within a few seasons. A seedling usually takes the gap.

The effect is of maintenance: a line walked, each stack checked by touch, the dead ones taken down. Nothing depends on it. Dead rosettes fall on their own in a few years, and the ridges were here before the animal and would stand without it. Pith is the richest food on the plateau, and this is the one way to find it in the dark.

**Weight**

Peat this deep and this wet is not quite solid. A heavy step sends a slow swell out through the mat, and the pools on either side tremble. That is what breaks the film.

The animal reads these swells through its soles. A herd bedded down to the west is a long confused pressure. Another of its own kind is distinct: four beats, then a pause the length of a trunk. They live far apart, one to a set of ridges, and seldom meet. But on most nights each knows where its nearest neighbors are, how fast they are going, and whether one has stopped to cut.

In the season, one will stand in place and rock its weight from forefeet to hind feet, slowly, for an hour. Many ridges away another will stop walking and take it in. The frogs raise a foot to be seen. This is the same kind of message, sent through the ground.

**The day, from inside**

At first grey it backs into its sleeve, and from there the day is a series of things that happen to the plant. The crown overhead warms and the flies leave. Later the leaves grow damp from the outside in. In the afternoon the ticking starts directly above its head.

The other animals move off the ridges. This one stays where it is, in the one dry place on the plateau, with the hair along its spine lifting a little. It never dens in the tallest plant on a ridge. Then the rain, which it does not hear; a few drops work down through the leaves.

It has been inside the cloud every day of its life and has never seen it. The plateau it knows is clear and hard, with stars over it, and nothing else moving.

**One**

They live a long time, fifty years or more. A female bears a single young every third or fourth year. It spends its first winter in her sleeve and its second walking behind her, learning the ridge trunk by trunk. A young one's teeth are pale yellow, and they darken a little every year.

Two terraces below the largest whaleback there is an old female whose teeth are nearly brown. She has walked the same ridge since before the oldest grazer in the herds was born. There are some three hundred trunks on it. Many of them she first passed when they were no taller than she is, and they stand in gaps she cut.

This year the rosette she sleeps in flowered. It is the only one she has ever slept in. Each dawn before she backs into the sleeve she lays her cheek against the trunk, the same as she does for any other. It is still warm. When it isn't, she will cut it down and eat it, and sleep in the next one along.
