Nothing lives here that the flowers could use. No one is behind the tenement doors or on the tram, and no hand rests on any valve wheel. The engines were built to go on without one, and the steam is free.

But the plateau is inhabited. The inhabitant cannot be met in the towns, where there is only noise: the stroke in the knees, the stacks, the valve, the dynamos, each machine separately itself. It has to be walked out to. By ten kilometres the moss has taken the valve's scream and most of the roar. By twenty, all that is left of the town behind is the stroke and the low B. Then a second town comes in from another quarter, and a third. The second B is not quite the same B, and the second stroke lands a fraction after the first.

It lives in that fraction.

At first it seems to be a tide in the sound, the swell that rises and goes about every eleven seconds. But the shiver it puts on the standing water crosses. It takes one hollow, then the next, then the one beyond, so it has a front and a bearing. It comes from somewhere and goes somewhere. Apart from the tram it is the only thing on the plateau that does, and the tram comes back.

Then it seems to be many, a file of swells crossing the open country eleven seconds apart, like a herd moving through. That is closer, and still wrong. The swells are footfalls of a single animal, and its body is the whole figure they belong to. The towns stand to one another as the columns stand in the scarp, each with six neighbours, and the sound of every town reaches every part of the duct, minutes old. Between any two neighbouring towns some thirty-six strokes are in the air at once, in transit. Laid together across the tableland, all of it makes a single pattern. In some bands the strokes agree and the air leans on the ground. In others they cancel, and for four or five seconds the place is as near to quiet as it ever gets. The pattern is one thing. Its far side is under a different part of the sky.

It moves because the towns are not, after all, the same town. They were built from the same drawings. But one governor weight came out of its mould a few grams heavy, one borehole reaches hot rock some metres sooner, and one belt was laced a hand's width short. So one engine makes twelve strokes to the minute and another makes twelve and a hair. Nothing wears here, so these errors never grow and never correct. They are the only differences on the plateau, and the inhabitant is made of nothing else. Without them the figure would stand still, one more fixture like the rime on the railings.

Instead it walks. And because the engines' rates stand in no simple ratio to one another, it does not walk a loop. The water goes round and the tram goes round, and the sun does not go at all. The figure never returns to an arrangement it has held before. The swell crossing a given hollow now does not have the weight of the one that crossed eleven seconds ago, or quite its bearing, and neither will come again. It is the one thing here with a before and an after. With the sun pinned, its passing is the only passing. It is no clock, since a clock repeats, and it is more nearly a life.

It spends that life touching everything, lightly and without preference. The moss swallows the high frequencies and the low ones pass through, so it goes down through the cushions into the basalt and comes up again through the sleeved trunks of the rosettes. Each cupped silver leaf holds its water, and each swell puts a ring on that water and lets it go flat. It gets into the flower spikes. Every open floret is rocked by less than the width of one of its own pollen grains. It is the only visitor they have had. It leans on the tilted slabs of the pressure ridges along the breaks that would still fit, and on every sharp-edged column in every scarp. It cannot push harder at one thing than at another. It only arrives again, slightly changed.

The works were built against water. Nobody built anything against this, and the engines that keep the rock sharp are the same ones that made it. Nothing has fallen yet, and no grain has left its anther. But it is the only thing on the plateau for which "yet" has a meaning.

It can be seen best in the country midway among three towns, some thirty-five kilometres from each. The strokes arriving there are a hundred seconds old, and no town is more than a smudge of plumes. In the lava hollows the finger of clear water goes from glass to a fine grain and back to glass. The grain comes in from one side, is crossed by another, holds for a breath and clears. It never shows the same grain twice.

At the rim it becomes plain what the thing is. The moss stops a metre short of the lip. The last gutter turns through its elbow and takes its water back inland. Everything else the plateau has is kept. But the duct has no wall there, and the inhabitant goes on, past the lit lamp on its post and out over four and a half kilometres of dry column, into air with no lid on it, where it spreads and thins and is lost. It is lost there continuously and made again continuously at the six stacks of every town. Unlike the water it does not hold its level by going round. It holds it by passing through. It rises in the towns as from springs, gathers in the open country, runs along the cold layer under the cloud, and goes over the edge without stopping.

The plateau was built so that it would have no river, and it has one in the sound. The river's fall is at the lamp. Whatever lies below receives the stroke and the low B, and about every eleven seconds a swell.
