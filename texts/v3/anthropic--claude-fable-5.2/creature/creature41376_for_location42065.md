Start with what is alive in the ordinary sense, because it doesn't take long. There are moths at the lamp, gone by morning. There are beetles in the sump, turning over in the warm water. Neither lives here so much as puts up with it.

Then the plants, and here the counting goes wrong. At the dyke you can name them: willow, alder, fig, hop, bindweed, ivy. A few paces in, the names stop being useful. Willow bark runs into alder bark without a seam. The bindweed winds with the hop. Near the engine every joint on every stem is the same distance from the last, as if growth were paid out by the stroke. There is one thing here, and the species are only its materials.

It is an acre across and four metres deep. It is warm all the way through, with one pulse, one circulation, and six or seven breaths to the beat. For a while "animal" is the word that sits in the mind: a big slow one lying in the fen with iron in its chest.

The word doesn't last. Animals want things. This has never put a root through the dyke or a seedling over it. The hops carry no cones and the willow no catkins. It makes gas from its own rot and burns it to lift its own water, which falls back through it to be lifted again. It takes in daylight and gives out noise. "Heart" is wrong too, because a body grows its heart. Here the beat came first, and the body gathered around it as far as the beat could be felt.

Look instead at what it has done, item by item:

- **Fuel:** When the coal stopped coming, it found gas.
- **Pipe:** When the pipe rusted through, it grew a stem of the same bore, no wider.
- **Belt:** When the belt perished, it supplied a bine of the same length, and it lets the leaves be cut off every revolution rather than run slack.
- **Bearings:** When the grease ran out, the fig gave latex, a little each turn and no more.
- **Bed:** When the bed stone sank, it took the weight and kept the level.
- **Walls:** When the walls fell, it stood where they had stood and left the door and windows open.
- **Outfall:** This was the one thing it could not mend. It did not stop pumping. It found somewhere else for the water to go.

None of these is an improvement. Each is a repair, made to the original dimension. That is how someone behaves when minding a machine that isn't theirs.

So the nearest word for what lives here is a job title: the minder. The engine was left running, and whoever left it did not come back. The rule in any engine house is that you don't walk away from a running engine until your relief arrives. Something in the peat took the watch. It had only wood and patience to keep it with, and it has kept it.

It runs the engine at the lowest speed that still counts as running, one firing in seven, or one in twelve when it is cold. It keeps the frost off the water jacket. It keeps a lamp lit by the door, which is no use to a plant.

It also keeps the man's place. This takes longest to see, because the place is made of gaps. The doorway is his width, and the window gaps are at the height of his eyes. The strip from the gate is one boot wide because he walked a plank over wet ground, one foot in front of the other. The noise is the noise he worked in, at the volume he was used to.

The shovel has been raised, year by year, toward where a hand would reach for it, and then past that. Whatever lifts it no longer remembers exactly how tall a man is, only that he would want it to hand.

The oil cups are full. The brass lids are bright at the thumb-place and nowhere else. The feed needles are screwed down tight, because the fig does the oiling. What stands in the glasses is the oil he left. It is being saved for him.

Nobody has been seen on the strip, but if you put a foot on it, the engine takes the load. It fires on the fourth turn, then the third. The lamp steadies and whitens. Water comes down harder from the gutters overhead, and the bine belt runs taut and stops slapping. For a minute the whole acre is at working speed, showing itself in good order with nothing let go.

It is waiting for something from you. Perhaps it is a hand on the oil can or a word shouted at the right pitch. Whatever one engineman gives another, you don't have it.

It shows no disappointment. The firings space out again, to four turns, five, six, and the lamp sinks back to orange. By the time you reach the gate it is seven again, and the leaves flinch together on the beat.

A few paces beyond the dyke, the thud leaves your soles. Behind you it goes on: a bang, six or seven breaths, a bang.
