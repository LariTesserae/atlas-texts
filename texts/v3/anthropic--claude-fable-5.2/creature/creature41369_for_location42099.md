The plant lives here, plainly, and the moss, and the bird that is seen once a day. But the bird is a lodger. It nests only in domes that have already fallen in, it walks the lanes for springtails, and nothing about the town depends on it.

The one to look for is the one that leaves the droppings.

They are most of what daylight offers, so begin with them. Each is round, pea-sized, and made of willow; under a thumbnail it comes apart into leaf vein and shreds of bark. It is dry in a place where nothing else is, which means it was dropped on ice, in the hours when the lanes are locked. The droppings lie only at certain places, where two domes lean toward each other across a lane until they almost touch. At each of those places the cushion is worn. A groove a hand wide runs down one wall and up the other, bronze and polished, its rosettes pressed flat as scales.

The second sign is in the frost. At dawn most domes are rimed evenly. A few carry, low on the sheltered side, a hole no wider than a fist, with a ruff of frost feathers around it as long as fingers. Frost grows like that where warm wet air has been breathing out all night. The springtails lie thickest there. A hole without a ruff is an empty hole.

The animal itself takes a night on a roof, above the cold that runs in the lanes. Some time after midnight the last eave stops dripping and the hiss of the fall thins out and ends. Then a piece of the roof moves.

The first guess is a young hare and the second a large vole, and both are wrong within a few breaths. It is the size of a loaf and heavier than that sounds. It has no tail to speak of and hardly any neck. Its ears are round, set low, and nearly shut with fur, and its whiskers are longer than its head is wide. The coat is grey with a cast of green, which may be the animal's own colour or may be something growing in the fur, and it goes bronze along the spine. The feet are broad and furred underneath, and it walks the rosettes without bruising one. When it stops, which is often, it draws its feet under and becomes a small dome on the back of a large one.

It comes out in the frozen hours because frozen is dry. All day the town drizzles from its own eaves, and this animal cannot afford a wet coat. It is a poor furnace, and the way it works shows this. It follows a willow thread, clipping as it goes, and for a while it is brisk. Then the stops grow longer. Then it turns, with a sheaf of willow held crosswise in its mouth, and goes back to the ruffed hole a good deal more slowly than it left. Where a lane lies in the way it goes down one groove and up the other without a pause, leaving its droppings there and never on a roof. It pushes inside. A little later another comes out of the same hole, already warm.

They take the night in turns. A count from the roofs never reaches more than a dozen abroad in the whole town. There are far more than a dozen.

It harvests more than it grazes. It eats a little where it stands and carries most home. It bites the running ends of the threads and leaves the crossings alone, which may be why the willow lies so flat and flowers only where it does.

What it does with the sheaf is the explanation the shelf does not offer. The brown core under each living dome is the plant's earlier selves, but only in part. Passages a fist wide run through it, and off the passages are chambers, and the chambers are packed. Willow, fern, moss from the lane edge and shed fur are all trodden down and wetted by the animals, until the edges set dark and glassy and the middle goes on rotting, slowly, for years. The rot gives off heat. That is the warmth a hand finds past the surface, and the set edges are the smell of resin. The cushion roots straight down into the heap and feeds. The sluggish flies of the warm hour were maggots in it.

The heap also accounts for the depth. The plant has not put on a body's height in a few lifetimes. It has been lifted. Sheaf by sheaf, a night at a time, the heap has risen under it, and the plant has only had to keep its skin closed over the top. The lifetimes are also the wrong ones to count by. One of these animals lasts four or five winters, and the scar on the headwall has seen something like a hundred generations of them.

The dead stay in. A chamber with a body in it is packed shut like any other, and the passage goes round. So the peat is partly bone, and the rule of the shelf holds for the animal as for everything else: it stands on something alive or lately alive. It sleeps on all that its line has eaten and been. On bare rock it would not last one night.

This makes a hard arithmetic. A dome stays warm only with enough bodies in it and enough heap under them. When a household falls below the number, those that are left go out through the cellar or do not go, and the heap cools. The cushion above, fed and warmed from below all its life, dies at the centre and sinks. Those are the opened domes, with the ferns and the liverwort and the air that stays a few degrees warm for some years after. Then the bird moves in, and lines its nest with fur pulled from the walls of the old passages.

The animal makes no sound in the air. What it has to say goes through the town. It stands in a passage and strikes the wall with a hind foot, and the wall gives the same dull wooden answer it gives a knuckle. One dome knocks and the next takes it up. The knocking travels along the rows and is heard, if that is the word, through the feet and the jaw. It is heaviest in the small hours, when ice is working in the headwall.

In the lanes there is nothing to hear, but there is something to see. On a windless night, with nothing near the water, the pale lawn in the square trembles. The rings do not spread from a point. They come in from the banks all at once and close on the middle. No animal goes out on the lawn, which would not bear one. There are usually one or two at its edge, feet drawn under, facing in.

At the upper edge, the nearest household crosses to the raw block most nights. Each animal carries a plug of old heap in place of willow. The plug is dark, warm when it sets out and cold when it arrives, and the animal presses it into the angle of rock on the sheltered side. Cushion seed rides in the plug, or in the fur. The three rosettes there are sitting on plugs. Nothing will sleep inside that block for longer than these animals will last, or their young, or the young of those. The animal is not known to mean anything by it. It does the same at every crack on the edge of its range, and most of the plugs wash out by noon.

Toward dawn the turns stop. The last ones home stand a moment in the hole mouths with their backs rimed white, and the rime lets go of them from the top down, sooner than it lets go of the roof. Then they are in. The ticking starts, and the drip. Somewhere down a lane the bird gives its two notes, too loud, to a town that is full and looks empty.
