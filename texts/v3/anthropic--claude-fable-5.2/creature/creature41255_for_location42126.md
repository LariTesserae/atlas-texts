Everything that lives in this valley came from somewhere else, and not long ago. The alder blew in as seed. The coho descend from strays that turned up the wrong river and found it empty. The bear's grandmother walked over a pass. The marten's line arrived when the spruce was tall enough to climb. Nothing here is native in any sense older than eighty years, so one more arrival is not remarkable, and this one is recent by local standards.

Some of it can be read off the objects before anyone is seen. The south anchor was set at chest height, so there is a chest, and it stands about that far off the rock. The rods are waist-high, which is where a standing person rests a hand. The circles are four paces because that is room to stand and turn round; nobody planned to lie down in one or bring company. There is no path between the posts because the way between them is never the same twice, and a bar that was a road last month is a channel tonight. The anchor has gone from chest to knee, so whoever it is has been willing to wait. And there is one building with one door, the size of a boat shed, which is the size of a single life with its gear stowed.

That gives one occupant, upright, a person's height, alone, and patient on a scale of decades.

A little before midnight the seam on the knob opens, and it is a woman, not young. She comes out into the sleet, the seam closes behind her, and she is wet at once, which the building never is.

The building is hers, but she does not attend from it, which is why it has no windows. She goes in there to stop. Inside it is dry and still and no warmer than the rock under it. She sleeps on her own heat, the way the marten does at the rod. Her food comes out of it, and nothing of the valley's goes in. In forty-some winters she has not taken a coho.

She did not set all seven posts. Her people, whoever they are, send one at a time. Each post went in where a person could stand and face the ice in the year it was set. The lowest three belong to the one before her, and they stand now in mast-high spruce with no ice in sight. She came the year the front drew back off the sill, and the two of them had a single season together. Most of what was handed over was handed over at the third post in weather like this, with a mouth against an ear, since that is the only way anyone talks here on the nights that count. Then the other went down the inlet and she was alone.

That autumn she ran the line across the sill and set its south end level with her own breastbone. She has to stoop to put a hand on it now. Of all the measures of her time here she trusts that one most: the ground has come up under her from the chest to the knee. She has crossed on the line, on still days, and does not like to.

She carries no light, which would show her sleet and the nearest alder and nothing else. She goes down-valley by her feet and by what the wind does to her face, and between posts nothing is done for her. Slush goes down her collar. Devil's club gets her hands. The moss lets a boot through into the gap between two rocks, and one of her knees has been wrong since her eleventh winter, when the gap was deeper than most. The valley does not interfere with her either.

What the posts are for becomes plain when she reaches one. She finds the edge when the wind stops on one cheek, or she stoops and feels for the shorter moss. She steps in. The rain straightens. The roar goes behind its door, and what was one noise outside comes apart into its eight or nine. The quiet was never meant to keep the valley out. Out there she is as deaf as the owls, and in here she can sort it.

With the air off her ears, the river comes up clearer through her soles. From the knock she knows what size of stone it can lift tonight, and from that roughly how much water it is carrying. She can count the cottonwoods going in. She can tell geese on the wing from geese on the ground. And she can hear the ice, late. She has the delay for each of the seven without working it out: under twenty seconds at the building, more than half a minute at the tide. Whatever she hears fall has already fallen, and its wave is already out across the lake. Everything that reaches her is a little old. She was told this in her first season and understood it in about her twentieth.

She writes nothing down. She passes the rotted stakes and their flagging with something like family feeling, since those people are where hers came from, but she leaves no stakes of her own. What becomes of what she takes in cannot be seen from here. Nothing visibly leaves the valley. It may be that the building sees to it. It may be that her people only think a place in the middle of being made ought to have someone in it who notices, and that the noticing is all there is to it. She stopped asking some time ago.

The second post is in the river tonight, or will be by morning, though she will not know it until there is light. It will be years before anyone can stand there again. She will not count it lost. It is keeping its circle over floodwater as it kept it over gravel, and some spring the river will move off and leave it standing in a new bar, with the short moss starting again around it.

Near two she is at the third post. The circle is full. She finds the edge with her hand, steps over the voles, who do not scatter, and goes round the grouse. The marten has the rod, which is where her hand would go, so she stands a pace off with her hands up her sleeves. By the reckoning that applies inside the circle she is nobody much. The voles have been coming to this post for a hundred and fifty generations and the marten's family for twenty, and hers for two. She is the only thing in it that is dripping.

The tide tops out. Even through the door she hears the geese go up, all of them, and come round, and put down further in. Then comes the ice, which let go the better part of half a minute before the sound of it reaches her. The marten does not lift its head, for the ice or for her. She stands in the straight-falling rain until she has all of it. Then she finds the edge again with her hand and goes back out into the storm.
