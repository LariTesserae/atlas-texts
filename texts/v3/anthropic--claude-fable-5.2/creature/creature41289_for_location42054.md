Start with the trails. Each is about a hand wide and pressed rather than scuffed: a smooth, shallow groove with a line of claw pricks stitched down either side. They run from pan to pan by the lowest ground. Where one meets a pavement it stops at the edge of the stone and takes up again on the far side, exactly in line. Nothing here grows fast enough to cover them and no water moves fast enough to erase them, so some are older than whatever is using them now.

To see what makes them you would wait beside one after dark, with the chorus going. For a long time there is nothing. Then one of the peeled sheets at the foot of the pavement is closer than it was.

It is grey and broad, as long as a forearm and hand, and nearly flat. Its back is a slow swelling rather than a dome, three fingers high at the crown. The living crust has taken it for rock: the same film that streaks the seep lines grows across it, black when it leaves the water and rust-grey by the time it has walked an hour. Its plates lift at the edges and peel in sheets as thin as a fingernail, and the sheets lie where they loosened on the floors of the pans. By day, in the shallows, skinks bask on it. It does not ring when they run.

The rim is undercut all round, like the rim of a rock pool. Folded sideways into the gap at the front, like something put away, is a neck as long as the shell. The head is flat and narrow, with the nostrils at the very tip and pale eyes set well forward. It is a turtle, the kind that tucks its head to the side instead of drawing it in, built to the plan of the country: low, wide, hollowed at the edges, and in no hurry.

It walks at night because the midday pavements would cook it in its plates. It carries the shell barely clear of the sand and sets it down every few steps to rest, and that setting down is what presses the groove. It steers by the frogs. Each pan has its own mixture, one mostly knockers, another mostly bleaters and rattlers. With no slope to turn the sound back and no forest to soak it up, a pan can be told from its neighbour a long way off, and the turtle walks toward the one it wants.

When it arrives the nearest frogs stop. A patch of quiet the width of a room opens at the rim, travels a little way along it, slides out over the water and closes. Anyone who could hear the whole plain at once would hear a handful of these small silences moving through the chorus, very far apart.

In the pan it settles onto the clay and the clay settles back over it. By morning it is a slight rise in the floor. The tadpole clouds drift over it again and fairy shrimp row past upside down. Then the neck goes out, the one fast thing it owns. The throat drops open and a mouthful of water goes in, along with whatever was in the water. There is a puff of grey that takes a minute to clear. Every half hour or so two nostrils touch the surface and leave a ring that widens and goes nowhere.

They live like the tussocks, each with its own skirt of ground: one to a pan, or one to three pans in a lean stretch. When a pan is eaten thin its tenant leaves by the trail. Only in the wettest years, when the pans link into chains, do they walk the flooded bottoms and find each other.

Afterward the female does not climb out to nest. She lays into the clay floor of the pan, underwater, while the water still stands warm above her. The eggs do nothing there. The shrimp's eggs wait in dust for water, and hers wait in mud for dust. Only when the pan has dried and cracked and air comes down the cracks do they begin. They hatch as the first storms soften the clay, into a pool already filling with shrimp.

The adults go the other way. As the water shrinks they push down into the last wet clay and let the surface dry over them in plates. For months each is a lump under a cracked floor, its heart turning over a few times a minute, while the sedge looks burnt on the rock above. When the rain comes back, the sedge greens within a day. The turtle takes two, because the clay has to soften first.

Nobody knows how long they live. Their growth rings come away with the peeling plates. There is one, though, at the pan below the nearest lone tree: a female as wide as a dinner plate, with a crescent missing from her rear rim, its edges long since worn smooth. The trail she takes north to the next pan was there when she hatched. Since then the rock pool beside it has deepened by most of a millimetre, and the trail by about the same.
