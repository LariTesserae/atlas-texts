Past the last hung door, what lives among the stakes can be counted off roughly in order of number. Beetles come first, in the cork underfoot, more than anyone has tried to tally. Then moths, in their season, and lizards, about one to every thirty stakes, each with a stake it prefers. Then four callus-eaters, which is the wrong number, since a herd is a dozen and no herd comes out this far. And one woman, who is the reason for the four.

From the terrace she is hard to find. She wears the felt and bast everyone wears, and the meal has dusted her to the colour of the ground. What gives her away is her pace. Surveyors cross the plain in straight lines and stop dead, and trimmers go lamp to lamp at a working walk. She drifts, stands, and drifts again, at about the speed of something grazing.

Closer, she is around fifty, short, and narrow through the shoulder except for the right arm, which the saw has thickened. Bronze does not keep an edge in live wood. She dresses the blade on a slip of slate every twenty strokes, and has done it so long that her hand goes for the slate without consulting her. Her lips are dark with grease from the little wooden pot everyone here wears on a cord. She drinks as they do in the quarters, head tipped well back and the free hand cupped under the chin. Unlike them, she doesn't mind when a drop gets past. She watches the white fuzz come up for it.

Every morning she pats herself down: hips, breast, cuffs, the heels of both boots. In the quarters this is the check for anything that might strike a spark, and it is done at so many doorways a day that it has also become the way people say good morning. Out here there is no one to say it to and nothing on her that could strike. She does it anyway, facing south toward the terraces.

Her work is an argument the surveyors decided not to have themselves. Streets are laid along herd tracks, and north of the quarter there are no tracks, because there is no cut wood out here to scar over and so nothing to eat. Someone had to go ahead and persuade a herd. So at every tenth stake along the surveyed line she takes the dropper root that hangs above it and saws it off square, at about the height of her collarbone. That is the bait. In nine or ten days the face begins to skin over, pale and soft enough to dent with a thumb, and by then the four have worked their way up the line to it. They are culls, crowded off the town herds: two old sows, a barrow with a bad hip, and a young one that came along because the others did. They rear a little to reach the cut, lean on the stake until it creaks, and rasp. When they have finished she squares off what they leave ragged, and the lamp is good for another fortnight. Behind the five of them a line of small lights runs back to the quarter, every one still bright. Ahead there is the one she cut this week, and then nothing.

When the saw comes through, the dust that falls is damp for about as long as it takes to say so. She puts a pinch on her tongue. Wet food is the lamp-trimmers' vice, and in the quarters it is thought slightly low.

She has three names, like anyone. The spoken one is for indoors and arm's length. The tram crew, who leave a loaf and a full flask at the end of the rail every third day, are the only people who still use it. They get it slightly wrong, and she has not corrected them, in a voice that has worn down to a whisper with a crack in it. The hand-name is for across a street: two fingers tapped on the opposite wrist. The knock is for distance: one, a gap, two. From the last terrace it does not reach her, and hers does not reach back. She is, by now, well outside the range of the language.

But the four know the knock. She gives it on the stake when a cut has come ripe, and they lift their heads and start over in no hurry. So in the one place her name is still used, it means that the wood is open here and ready. She has thought about this and found nothing in it to object to.

With the lizards she is on terms. They drop at dusk when the moths go up, and their lines hang where they fell, sixty metres of amber thread as fine as a hair. In the slack between tides the lines hang dead plumb. She winds most of them in for binding buds, since they give as the bud swells and bast does not. She leaves one standing in each rank for the surveyors to sight along, when surveyors come.

The chalk is hers too, a stick of pressed meal. The tide rubs the numbers soft and she goes back over them. She does not need them. She knows the stakes as a person knows the stairs in their own house, and when she counts along a rank her fingers shape the figures unasked, the way someone else's lips might move. The numbers are for people who will come later and not know where they are.

She is asked, in the quarter, whether she thinks about those people, the ones who will live behind these walls. She turns her hand over, which means not much. A bud the size of a fist is a long way from a door. The first ones she bound, eleven years ago, stand in their jigs with pale wood just showing at the seams. She will be old when those have doors, and dead before these do.

At night she sleeps on a window. It is one of the bronze plates that will be left in a form to keep the wood out of an opening. She lays felt over it and lies on that, because anything that sleeps on bare cork wakes in a nap of root-hairs come up after its sweat. The animals do not care. In the morning there are four pale ovals on the ground where they lay and one clean rectangle, fringed white along the edge where her breath went over, thickest at the head. By noon the tide has combed it all flat.

Before she sleeps she watches the nearest cut go from white to yellow to nothing. She has never seen the sky. She knows it better than most people down here, having sat under so many small squares of it with no walls in the way. She can tell a flat day from a moving one, when the square on the cork dims and comes back and dims, as though something large were walking past the plant up there. She knows the yellow comes earlier for part of the year, and has no explanation. On perhaps a dozen nights the cut does not go out but holds a dull pewter until morning. On those nights the young callus-eater gets up and stands under it.

Once she walked on to the column itself, half a day out and half a day back. It has no mill and no turbine in its throats. It stands there the colour of the vault, as wide as a gasholder, doing what the others did before anyone hung machinery in them. She arrived at the turn of the tide and laid her hand flat on it. There was nothing to hear. Under her palm the whole mass drew breath, one long slow push with nothing in its way.

The surveyors come out twice a year to ask when the herd will hold the line by itself, since the mill cannot be sited until the street is. Each time she holds up one finger and turns it over: a season. She is not lying. The herd is slow, and she has found no reason to be faster than it.

The tide is going out now. The meal on the ground has started north, and the lizard on the next tenth stake has turned to meet it. She pockets the slate, gets the wind behind her, and walks up the rank with her fingers counting. Behind her the barrow with the bad hip lifts its head, though she has not knocked yet.
