The frogs are the easiest answer. There are three or four of them, each about the length of a thumb joint and the same grey-green as the tiles, and they live down in the seams where the root-mat stays wet all year. In cloud season they keep quiet until afternoon, since nothing that small can be heard over the morning. When the dripping thins they begin to tick, one at a time, each call placed in a gap. They lay in the drip-cups, one egg to a cup, and the tadpole grows up in a thimble of water on a tile that rings whenever the leaf above lets go. A dry cup is no use to them. When a leaf-tip creeps outward and starts a new cup, it takes years to wear deep enough to hold a body, and then a frog finds it.

The snails live here too. They spend the mornings folded into the liverworts and come out onto the bare stone once the dripping slows, to graze the film the water leaves behind. A drop from a body-length up is a hard blow to something that size, so their trails curve around every cup.

No bird nests on the shelf. Nothing small would choose a place where it couldn't hear a mate, or the hawk, before mid-morning.

A slower answer is the plants. The tree ferns and the basin-plants are older than anyone who has climbed up to look at them, and the rows of cups show their line was here long before that. They do all the playing, and they can't hear any of it. They have also grown off to either side of one tile near the center, and on that tile is a stone that no plant or frog put there.

So there is someone. It is tempting to give that person a shelter under the back wall and a life up here. But the bowl shows no hearth and no sleeping place, and for most of the year nobody could sleep through a night of it. Whoever belongs to the bowl lives in the valley and comes up one day a year, in the dry weeks.

It is a potter, and has been for as long as the cobble has been here. The cobble is an anvil stone, the kind a potter holds inside a wet jar while beating the outside to shape with a paddle. Every potter in the valley keeps several, picked out of the river for their curve. This one was carried up in a cloth, long ago, by someone who already knew what she wanted it for.

Potters knock things. A fired jar is balanced on the fingertips and rapped with a knuckle. If it rings, it is whole. If it clacks, there is a crack somewhere that the eye may find later or never. The dry weeks are the only time of year when a single tile can be heard by itself.

The one who comes now is a short, wide woman past fifty, with clay in the creases of her hands that washing doesn't shift. In the valley she is known for stopping mid-sentence when something rings, a dropped lid or a hoe on a stone, and not going on until it has finished. She has made the climb thirty-odd times. She takes her sandals off at the last bend of the trail, because her feet tell her as much as her ears do. She crosses the bowl on the tiles, stepping over the seams, and she never puts her weight on a green one. At the center she lifts the cobble out of its hollow. Then she goes to the back wall and begins.

There are twelve tiles, more or less, and she strikes each once. She uses the weight of the stone and a loose wrist, the same as for a jar, and hardly any arm. The pale patches are what generations of blows that light add up to. The high tiles by the wall are over almost as they start. As she works outward the notes lengthen, and she stands on each tile until it has gone still under her soles. The last is at the lip, on a column that stands half-free, and she waits there longest, with the drop in front of her. If a tile has gone dull since last year, she doesn't try it a second time. She finds a sound neighbor, tests it with a knuckle, and that one takes its place. By the next dry weeks the old one is green.

Once in her life she has been here in cloud season. When the work passes on, the old potter and the new climb together and sit through the dusks, taking turns on the dry tile with the cobble in a lap, until an evening comes when the drips fall into step. She waited nine evenings. It is the only time either of them hears what the twelve notes belong to. The rotted cord is from one of those vigils, when somebody tied back a frond that was dripping on the seat. That frond died long ago, and the fern has put out hundreds since without one coming back over the tile, so the cord doesn't explain the dryness. The cut is older. One of them started to mark a fern with the edge of her scraping-sherd and stopped after a single stroke. It is handed down as a mistake, which is why nothing since has been marked, stacked or cleared.

The potters' account of the silent tile is a potter's account. A jar rings everywhere except where it is held. The tile is where the bowl is held, and the anvil sits there as it would inside any pot still being beaten, against the wall and under the blows. By that reckoning the bowl isn't finished, and the water is still working on it. The account says nothing about the moss, and they don't pretend it does.

When she has knocked the last tile she carries the cobble back and turns it in its hollow until it seats. For the rest of the year it keeps her place. Anyone on the trail below would have heard twelve notes, well spaced, going from high to low, and then nothing. She puts her sandals on at the bend. Behind her the frogs have the bowl to themselves, ticking from the seams about once a breath, and she is the one visitor who has never taken them for water.
