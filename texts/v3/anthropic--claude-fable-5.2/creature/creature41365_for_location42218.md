Mostly the weed lives there. That has to be said first, because by weight it is nearly the whole answer: the kelp on the walls, the eelgrass in the plots, the red weeds, the pink crust on the stone. Under the muck in the drains, where there is no air, there are also the small things that turn fallen blade into something partway to peat. They are alive, slowly.

But a crop does not stand unbitten from the old shoreline to the shelf edge by itself. Fields are never left alone. Something comes for the seed, then the shoot, then the grain, and the people who heaped these beds knew that as well as they knew the fall of a drain. They would have set something to mind them.

They did. At some of the field corners, where a bank meets a lane, stands a post thicker than the stakes and sunk deeper. The top has been worked: two adzed slopes for a brow, a notch for a mouth, two sockets where pebbles of white quartz were set and in a few posts still are. The mud kept them as it kept the trackway, and the crust has started up them from the base. They were put there for the seasons when nobody could be spared to stand in a field. They don't live here. They only show that there was a job.

When somebody could be spared, somebody was sent. That one is still out.

It is small. Minding went to whoever was not yet any use with a spade, or no longer any use with one, and the cold has made it impossible to say which this was. It is dark, the same dark as the stake rows and for the same reason. When it lies up, it chooses a field drain where the drift of old blade is deepest and lets the muck close over, and the muck has tanned it. The skin is tight, with a dull shine. The creases over the knuckles are as sharp as the facets on the post tips. The hair has gone the red of the thin weeds on the cobbles. The chalky crust takes on it where it takes on anything that keeps still, at the heels, the nails, the points of the shoulders. Before setting out, it rubs the worst of this off against the rim of a quern.

It carries two stones. One is the upper stone of a quern, worn to the shape of a palm. The other is a cobble. That is a minder's whole kit. You walk the field and knock, so that whatever is thinking of coming in knows the field is watched. In water a knock travels much farther than it did in air. Not every small, dull knock out on the plain is a stone settling.

It walks when the blades lean seaward and lies up when they lean back. In this way it works out across the shelf, a few fields to a tide, and a long time passes between one visit to a lane and the next. It goes by the wall-tops, between the double rows of kelp, with a hand out to each side touching every stipe as it passes. The blades close over it and slide across its back with the same leathery sound they make on one another, so its passing sounds like the tide. It has no use for light. It knows where it is by the count of beds and by the places where the count goes wrong. Where one generation's surveying met another's, the lane bends, and the minder bends with it without slowing, as anyone turns a corner in their own house in the dark. When it steps down to cross a drain, the brine takes it at the shin or the knee with that sudden cold. By how high the cold comes and how hard it pulls, the minder knows which ditch this is and how far it has to go to the trunk canal.

What it does on the round is check. It runs a hand down a stipe to the holdfast and feels around the base for spines. It draws a blade through its fingers, all four or five meters, feeling for the scallop of a bite or the rasped track of a snail. At the eelgrass it kneels on the bank and combs the edge of the plot.

This used to turn things up. For a long time the round was slow work. Urchins were pulled from the holdfasts and snails picked from the blades, one at a time, into a basket. The fish were driven down the lanes by the knocking, toward the canals and out. The plain is wide and the minder is one, and for a long time that mattered. But an urchin covers a body's length in a day, and the minder had centuries. The things that crept were finished bed by bed. The things that swam learned what the knock meant, and then there were none left that needed to learn it.

The pickings went to the middens, because that is where shell goes. The top lens on each is different from the ones beneath. The shells in it are whole and unopened and were never near a fire. There are urchin tests among them, and there is no charcoal. It is the one layer on the whole shelf laid down by hand since the sea came up.

The minder still carries the basket. Nothing has gone into it since before the stone facings finished slumping.

It does not eat. The arrangement was that a minder in the far fields is fed from the house, and someone walks out along the causeway with something wrapped. The house is a swell in the plain a hundred paces across, and no one has walked out. The minder has not made up the lack from what stands in the plots. It was put here to see that nothing touched the crop, and it has not excepted itself. Nothing eats any of it.

In the weeks of grey-green dimness it can see the length of a lane, and it seems not to like this. It goes more slowly and stops often. Mostly it looks up. The light always meant the grain was heading, and that was the season for birds. It stands on a bank with a stone in each hand and watches the underside of the ice for anything crossing. Nothing crosses. It knocks anyway.

The far end of the round is the outfalls. The made ground stops there, and so does the job. The minder stands on the lip of a stone throat with the brine pouring past its ankles and over the edge. It faces down the slope, into water that was never anyone's field, and knocks. It stays there longer than anywhere else. It cannot know whether anything is down there to hear. The sound goes out and comes back off nothing, like the groan of the ice. Then it turns around and starts back in, counting beds.

It is waiting for the cutters. A minder minds until the harvest and then is fetched home with it. By every sign its hands can read, the crop has been ready for a very long time. It fills every plot to the edges and stands in rows along every wall. The minder feels the old blades come loose overhead and settle past it into the drains, and it knows waste when it feels it. But cutting is not a minder's work, and neither is deciding when.

So it goes on to the next field. Somewhere out on the plain, between one tide and the next, two stones meet once. Nothing answers. The minder takes that for the right answer.
