Nothing is wrong with the stone there. The oval is taken, and has been since the first week.

The evidence is small. A flake of marine snow that drifts against the ceiling anywhere else stays where it touches until a fan takes it. A flake that touches the oval begins to move. It travels slower than the sea spider, along whichever scratch it landed on, toward the middle. Other flakes are travelling in from the other sides. Nothing visible is carrying them.

In the middle, in the deepest of the glacier's grooves, lies a body the size and shape of a grain of rice. It has a soft sheath the colour of weak tea and an opening at each end. It has no legs, no fan, and no door to shut.

From each opening comes a thread. Each thread forks, and the forks fork and join again, until a net lies flat against the polish from one edge of the oval to the other. A single thread is finer than the scratches and can't be seen. At noon on the brightest days, when a little of the dusk on the sill reaches this far, all of them together show as a dullness on the stone, like breath on a cold spoon. The oval is long the way the scratches run, because a thread travels easily along a groove and slowly across one.

The net and the grain are one animal, and the animal is one cell. It is kin to the grain-gatherers on the floor, though it wears no grains. The crust beside it is ten thousand animals in ten thousand rooms. This is one animal with no rooms at all, a palm across. It holds more ground than any other resident and has almost no body to show for it.

It landed when the ceiling was bare and still tasted of the glacier. It spread as far as it could feed and stopped. Every larva since has touched down on the oval, felt the ground move under it, and gone on. The streets were here first. This was second, and the city's first boundary was drawn against it.

Inside each thread, granules move. They go out along one side and back along the other, both ways at once, and never collide. The worm streets hold animals that never leave their doors, so these are the only roads in the city with traffic on them. What goes out is the cell itself, a little at a time. What comes back is whatever the net has taken hold of. Mostly that is diatoms, bacteria, and the grey flakes. Sometimes it is something still moving. A copepod that brushes the ceiling here is held by one leg, then by three, and it kicks. That is why the skeleton shrimp along the border face inward. They aren't guarding the oval, they are stealing from it. They lean out over the boundary wall and snatch what they can reach before it is drawn to the middle.

At slack water, twice a day, the cell lets down lines. A few dozen threads leave the ceiling and hang straight into the still water, each the length of a finger joint, each with a bead at its tip. When the tide turns and the fans open district by district, the lines trail sideways and gather snow, and the cell reels them in.

It has nothing to see a shadow with. When one crosses the mouth and the fans shut in their order, the net stays out. But when the ice cracks across the fjord and the tremor arrives, the traffic in every thread stops at once, for exactly as long as the fans flinch. Then it starts again, both ways.

The sea spider is the only animal that walks across it. Its feet lift late there, one after another, as if from wet varnish. Once it stopped over the rice grain, lowered its proboscis, and rested the tip on the sheath for a minute, the time it gives a box. Then it went on to the far wall and the bryozoans beyond.

The whole net can be seen on one occasion. When something touches the scale worm at the back and a scale falls, the blue light comes from below. For those few seconds every thread is lit along one edge, a mesh finer than any crust, with its beads moving. Then the scale reaches the mud.

After a melt pulse, when silt fills the pocket, the cell takes itself in. This takes about an hour. The net thins from the border inward and the rice grain swells until it is nearly round. For that day the oval is truly empty, the only time it is. When the water clears, the threads go out again and find fine silt clinging to the polish. They pass it outward, thread to thread, and let it go at the edge. It falls. On the mud below there is a faint ring the same shape as the oval, made of dropped silt, empty copepod cases, and the glass of diatoms. The amphipods there build it into their chimneys.

Since the last pulse the cell has not dropped everything. It has kept the palest grains, the quartz flour the glacier ground from this same stone, and passed them inward instead. A low roof is going up over the body, one grain thick, still open at both ends. Its relatives on the floor build shells to live in. This kind builds once, at the end. When the roof closes it will stop feeding and draw in its net for good. Under the roof it will divide into several hundred cells, each too small to see, each with a single thread. The sheath will be left empty. The young will come out onto the only clean polished ground left in the city.

So something does hold that ground and something is also expected. They are the same animal.

The ceiling is still coming down. In the middle of the pocket there are about two finger joints of water between the polish and the chimney rims, and by summer there will be one. Then the lines let down at slack water will touch silk. The cell may take hold of the floor and stand for an hour as a column no one can see, or it may draw back. The roof may close before the gap does, or the gap may close first.

For now the net is out to its full width. The granules run both ways, and the flakes are moving toward the middle.
