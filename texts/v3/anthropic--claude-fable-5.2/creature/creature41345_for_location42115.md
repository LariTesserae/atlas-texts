The open water is not empty. It only looks empty, and for about half a second in every four it stops looking it.

The first sign is on the floor. Over most of the bowl the nets of light are loose, a mesh a fist could pass through. In one patch the width of a cartwheel, the mesh is drawn fine and bright at the centre and slack around the edge, as it would be under a lens. The patch is usually near the float.

Then the stroke comes. The white column stands up and falls apart, the fizz spreads, and in one place it does not rise. It hangs in mid-water in vertical lines, hundreds of them, each a string of beads finer than seed and as long as an arm. Above the lines the fizz gathers under something and gives it an outline, a shallow dome with its rim down. Then the beads work loose, go up to hiss against the lid, and the room is bare again.

So at first it is a jelly, and for a while that will do. It is a dome of clear flesh, as thick as a thumb at the crown and thinner than a fingernail at the rim. The threads beneath it are sticky rather than stinging. The bronze fish are thrown through them on every stroke and swim back unharmed, some with a stolen morsel. What the threads take is mostly midges. The midges that dance under the glass spent their youth in the scale and the moss, and to hatch they must cross the bare room from bottom to top. A good share of them try to cross it here.

But a jelly swims, and this does not. The blow arrives and the water jumps a finger's width. The dome jumps late and is flattened, its rim flung wide. Over the next three seconds it draws back to its curve by plain springiness, the way a bent blade straightens. The closing pushes a little water down and lifts it, and then it sinks until the next stroke. The lift and the sinking come out even. It hangs at the depth of the float for much the same reason the float does.

Inside the rim there is a paler band where the muscle is. It still tightens, a moment after the stroke has opened the dome, when the dome is already closing on its own. It adds nothing. The engine has not missed a beat in the time it took to wear rivet heads flush, so there is no knowing whether the band could still do the work alone.

By afternoon the dome is no longer clear. A faint green comes up in the flesh, the same green as the inside of the lid. Something lives in it that uses the light as the moss does, and like the moss it beads with bubbles of its own making. The bubbles lift it until the crown rides a finger under the surface film. The grey birds at the vent look down through it and see the lid's stain lying a little lower than it should. The fish look up from the moss and see the lid. So it is also a garden, the only one in the tank with nothing to hold.

It should not have lasted a season. The water lays scale on every surface, and it would lay scale on this one too, turning the dome to pottery and sending it to the floor. The shrimp prevent that. A few hundred live on it, glass on glass, visible only as the dark threads of their guts moving over nothing. They pick it clean as fast as the water coats it, and the crackle is softer there.

This is why it keeps to the float. It has no hold, only a habit of fetching up against the float chain, where a few threads snag in the green. When a brood disperses through the pinholes it passes through the hanging threads. Some of the brood are eaten and some climb on. It takes its cleaners and its dinner from the same copper ball. The shrimp it eats do not go back in to breed, so the ball rides a hair higher for it and the valve admits a hair less.

A few times a day something far below opens. The weed has its rungs and the fish have the moss. This has nothing. It goes across the room threads first, with the dome dragging behind like a sea anchor, faster than it has moved all day. The threads go through the strainer and the dome does not. It lies pressed against the holes for the length of the roar, and the eel comes out and feeds on it. When the valve shrieks and the level returns, it peels away shorter by the threads that went down the outlet. Its rim is scalloped all round with half-circles the width of the overflow pipe, in every stage of regrowing. The slow drift of the room brings it back to the chain by evening.

What the eel and the strainer take, it grows back in about the time between one loss and the next. It has reached the size the room allows, and it is not getting any smaller.

At night the green fades and the bubbles go. It is heavier then, and the band cannot help it. But the water turns over. The rising middle carries it up until its crown breaks the film, and the inch-tall rain falls on it. Then it slides outward under the glass and down the wall in the cool sheet. Its threads drag through the moss on the coil, where the water is as warm as blood. It crosses the floor to the centre and goes up again. A circuit takes a few hundred strokes. By morning the threads are loaded with whatever the moss gave up. It grazes the edges all night without once holding on to them.

It is not the first. On the floor in the lee of the lace baffles, where the flow is weakest, lies a low dome of scale the width of a cartwheel. The shrimp walk over it without stopping. Beside it is another, older and holed like the baffles. Beside that is a third, which is only a ring. There has been one at a time, and each lasted until the shrimp fell behind.

Small ones bud from the rim between the scallops, clear and no bigger than a thumbnail. They are too light for the stroke to work them properly, and they are thrown about like the fish. They cannot keep station. The surface carries them where it points the ribbon weed, to the weir and over it in the thread that never breaks. The eel takes most of them as they pass. The rest go down the green stripe on the rust, and the wind tears them off with the water, above the cloud.

Whether anything waits at the tower's foot to receive them cannot be known from up here. But the engine is lifting water from somewhere. Now and then, among the flakes the main sheds into the ring of bronze fish, there is one that is clear and soft and the size of a thumbnail. The fish snap at it with the rest. Once in a long while they miss.
