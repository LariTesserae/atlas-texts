Nothing large lives here. That is the first thing anyone would look for, because rings that regular seem to need something in the middle to be rings around. There isn't anything. The central column is wider because the quiet at the center is wider and it has had longer to thicken. Inside it are galleries the size of a cupped hand, eggs, old braid, and the dead, tied in.

It would also be easy to say the lake itself is what lives here. It keeps one temperature and one level, it closes its wounds in a day, and its floor gives under the hand like a flank. But the lake does not want anything. The rain falls, the moss grows, the water leaves at the rim. All the wanting in the place is in the weavers. Each one's share of it is very small, and there are millions.

A weaver is the length of a finger and the color of the inside of a root. Where the eyes would be there is smooth skin over two shallow dents. The fins are small. The pectorals brace against the braid like elbows, and the tail does just enough to hold station. At six degrees its heart beats a few times a minute. It will live thirty or forty years, and it will spend them within an arm's length of the gallery where it hatched. It has never crossed open water, and it does not know there are other columns.

The lips are the animal. They take up a third of the head, pleated and strong, and through them a weaver can tell a living strand from a dead one, a taut strand from one with slack, and which way the last loop was laid. Slack is the thing it cannot leave alone. A loose end registers in that mouth the way a loose tooth registers to a tongue.

It is the only silent animal in the lake. Everything else shouts and waits for the answer. The weaver has a row of pits along its jaw and flank that feel the hum, and it moves until they feel the least. Where the water is stillest, it can work.

It does not know the braid. The pattern is in the row below. The lips read the last loop, and the last loop says how the next one lies. That is why a tear in the roof closes with braid identical to the braid around it: the torn edges are instructions. The braid has been copying itself upward and outward since before the slabs were round, and the fish are its hands. No weaver in the lake could say what is being made.

The growing tip of a strand is faintly sweet. The roof makes food from its grey light and passes it down, and a little leaks where the moss is newest. The one that holds the strand feeds while it holds. So they trade: the puller on one loop is the holder on the next. A pair that didn't trade would soon be one fed fish and one starved one.

A pair starts when a young weaver comes out of the gallery, finds a strand with slack in it, takes the end in its mouth and holds. It can hold for a long time, since holding is eating. Eventually another comes along the braid, testing strand after strand, finds an end already held, and draws the loop through. After that they stay together. Their beat is one repeat of the braid. Each strand in it gives up a loop's worth of slack every three weeks or so, and there are enough strands for a few loops a day. Most of the work is checking.

Now and then a salamander takes one. More often one just stops. The other ties it in where it settles, because it is the nearest loose thing. Then it finds a slack end, takes it in its mouth, and holds.

The two at the foot of the central column work where the trunk meets the floor, in the stillest water in the lake. Even the pressure in the jaw drops out here. The older one has a notch in its upper lip from a salamander that missed. It has worked this beat for twenty-six years, the last eleven with this partner. The partner before is a hand's length below them, braided over, browner and tighter every season, on its way to being floor. Below that is the same again, and somewhere under all of it is rock that neither of them will ever touch. The mountain has been coming down for a long time, and the lake has not come down with it. The difference is made of loops like this one.

The loop is snug. They trade places. The next strand has no slack yet, and neither does the one after. Above them are ninety meters of brown water, the drumfish thumping, a meter of roof, the frogs, and the rain. None of that reaches them. What they know is that this strand is done and the next is not ready, and they move a lip's width along and test again.
