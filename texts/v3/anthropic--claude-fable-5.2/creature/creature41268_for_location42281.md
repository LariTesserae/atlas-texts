**The Tenant**

The first answer is that nothing lives here, and it is wrong only by a little.

The second answer is the list anyone would make: the olive slick, the brighter green beyond it, the ferns, the two cushions of moss. All true, but the ferns and moss are lodgers. They came as spores on the wind and would have taken any wet crack on the plain. They live in the pit the way people live beside an airport, because the rent is low, and they shake all day for it.

The resident is something else, and it is hard to see because you have already looked at it.

Start at the pool. The lattice on the water is too visible. Ripples that fine, on water that milky, under flat steam-light, should not show at all. Crouch as near as the heat allows and the pattern turns out to be drawn in something. A skin lies on the surface, thinner than the bloom on a grape, grey with a faint oil-sheen where the daylight strikes it level. The water shakes between the lines and holds still along them, and the skin has gathered where the stillness is. The checkering is a net of living thread a few cells wide, each strand lying in a seam of quiet with the roar on both sides.

The net is its posture, but not all of it. At the pool's edge the net climbs out onto the slab and thickens into the olive film, then the green. Under the lip of the pale crust, where the rock is merely hot and the iron comes in, it continues as a colorless fuzz that needs no light and eats rust and sulfur. It is more honest to call this one resident than several, the way a reef is one. It is banded by temperature as a body is divided into tissues. The sunlit part feeds, the dark part builds, and the floating part does what might be called listening.

It builds like this: each filament at the crust's edge gets sheathed in silica by the water running over it, dies inside the sheath, and is overgrown by the next. Nearest the jet the crust is the steam's own work. A step out, it has help. The rind that has swallowed the fence and is icing the bolts is partly a skeleton, worn on the outside and left behind at a millimeter a year.

It has no eyes and nothing like an ear. One thing reaches every part of it at once, the note, and it is shaped to the note the way a hermit crab is shaped to a shell. It did not make the note, it fits it exactly, and outside it would be soft.

Its cells last days. The tenant itself is perhaps thirty-five, older than anything else alive on the flow and younger than the fence. To a single cell the note is as fixed as a star. To the whole it climbs, and the whole follows, every strand of the net closing on its neighbor by less than a hair's width a year. The visitor with the ruler, coming back after a decade, is measuring an animal, and what the ruler finds is the nearest thing the tenant has to growth: it stays the same size while its grain grows finer.

It makes one movement that could be watched, by someone willing to spend a night in the noise. After dark the air over the pit cools, the far wall weeps harder, and the green bands creep a finger's width toward the wellhead. By midmorning they have drawn back. That is its day, a tide of color against the hot zone, in and out.

This is what the birds read. To an animal, an occupied place is one filled with another creature's sound, and the pit has been sung without a pause for breath for forty years. The birds are right about the occupancy and wrong only about the order: the song was here first, and the singer grew into it afterward.

It cannot reach the valve. The pitch is set by wear and salts in the one place too hot for it, and it takes the pitch it is given. The gauge reads nothing now, and the sign says nothing. The tenant is the only instrument in the pit still taking a reading, and it takes it with its whole body, continuously, for no one. The pad was poured for a larger machine, and this is what arrived instead. It uses the low corner.

It could end simply. If someone carried the missing wheel back down the rebar rungs and closed the well, the pool would go flat in a second. The net would hold its shape for an hour or so out of something like habit, then slacken, and by evening it would be an ordinary scum drifting to the lee side. Every cell would still be alive, and no one would be home.

That has not happened, and the wheel is nowhere in the pit. Climb out, walk the three hundred meters, and look back. The white column stands over the black plain like smoke over a chimney, which is how you tell from a distance that someone is in.
