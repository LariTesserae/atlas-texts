About two hundred people live in the forty-some houses. The number is loose because the houses are.

They are not a people of the dark. From thaw to first snow they live out on the open bog, a family to a hummock, with cooking fires and voices that carry across water. A town is something they make only for winter, and until this year they made it under dead wood, a tamarack log or a rotted stump, rooms that stayed where they were put. None of them has wintered under a living tree before, so about half of what they know applies. The knots came in with them, and so did the weave of the houses and the fire law. Where to hang things, what the lid does in wind, and how cold travels down a live root are this winter's knowledge, less than a hundred days old.

If there were light, you would see short coats of felted vole underfur, gathered where it sifts down with the frost when the voles pass overhead. Over the coat goes a cape pieced from springtail skins, dozens to a cape, because water will not stay on springtail skin and the walls weep. Their palms are black with pitch to the wrist. Since autumn their shoulders have thickened and their legs have thinned. They rest with a knee hooked over the line and carry loads slung beneath them on a toggle that rides the cord. A hand lifts over a mite's back without being told to.

They weigh so little that a fall hurts nobody. What matters is the landing. The floor closes over. The pool is worse, because at their size the surface of water takes hold of a body and gives it nothing to push against. The tassel is the first knot a child's hand is put on.

They have voices, and in summer they are loud. Here the voice is kept for inside the house, with the door plugged, and it is kept low even then. On the cords everything is said into the forearm with presses, strokes, and a pinch for a question. A name is a touch done on the wrist, different for each person, so nobody can be called from a distance. Nobody can be talked about, either, unless the speaker does that person's name on someone else's wrist, and for a moment the listener is being called by it. Gossip is slower here than it was in summer.

Nobody has seen anybody since the snow. People are known by the hand, and before the hand by the cord. Everyone has a way of coming along a line, a rhythm of reach and pull, and it arrives through the fibre well ahead of them. The faces from autumn are still in people's heads, but nothing corrects them now and they have been drifting. The four children born since the snow have no faces at all. They have a weight, a smell, and a grip.

Everyone is always a little cold. The resin workers are coldest, because a bead will not knead below body heat and has to be carried inside the coat against the belly until it gives. They can be smelled from two junctions off. The netters are the wettest. The granary keeper counts seed by touch, issues it by the handful, and has stopped being asked how much is left.

One household hangs from the largest root, above the tear, where the wood is still good. They live with the green of the fungus at the edge of their eyes, and with the rasp of the soft thing chewing, which comes down the root into the walls of the house. They sleep through it. When it stops, they wake.

Everyone counts, and nobody can help it. The leaf gets into the hands, so people reach along the cords in time with it and slow when it slows. The count that matters belongs to the counters, who sit by turns at the leaf with a knotted string. They pass one knot through the fingers for each drip and move a resin bead over for each string run through. When the last bead goes over, the counter pulls the centre line twice. The pull goes out along every street to every house wall, and that is the day.

The shrew does not keep the town's time. On a long cold day it comes nine times, and on a quick thaw day four. The older people have understood for a while what this says about the clock, and which of the two is steadier. Nobody wants days measured in that.

When it comes, stopping is the only skill. A cord in use is never quite still, so when the people on it freeze, the stillness itself travels from junction to junction. By the time the springtails go off, the whole town is hanging motionless from its hands. Then it is over. If a house is gone, its root swings bare and its cords hang straight down. Over the next days the town goes back through what that household did, whether a door left unplugged, or a child, or a voice. It can usually find something. The bark splice is left on the street a while, and hands go out from it into nothing. Then someone else hangs a house there, because sound roots are few.

At midday some of them go to the lip and stand their eyes in front of the patch that is less black. There is nothing to do there. They go so the eyes have somewhere to be. The babies are carried along and held facing it. They do not turn toward it, and they are held there anyway.

Then there is the frog, which the town counts, in its way, as a resident. The splice on its cord is a house splice, and nobody proposed another. It was there when they came in and still soft, and for the first days a hand pushed into its side left a dent. Then it went hard from the outside in, and they felt that happen. They know frogs. In summer a frog is what happens to a person who stands too long in the open. But none of them has spent a winter in a room with one, and nobody knows what the first sign will be. An old cord-twister says, into people's arms, that the frog is the only one in the room doing winter properly.

So one of them goes to it every day. The job did not exist before this winter. She was a netter until her shoulder went. She began going on her own, people began asking what she had found, and now the job is hers. Her name is two fingers laid inside the wrist and drawn slowly off. She climbs onto the flank behind the foreleg, lies down on the glaze with her arms spread, and stays for a full string of drips. Then she does the same at the throat. She is feeling for a thing she has never felt, in a body where it isn't yet. What she does feel every day is her own pulse coming back up out of the ice, and she has learned to subtract it.

On mild days, when the glaze goes dull and wet and everyone else takes the long way round, she stays longer.

She makes her report at the first junction, to whoever is waiting. She sets her closed fist in their open palm and leaves it there, not moving, for three drips. It means nothing has moved. It is passed hand to hand up the streets and is in every house before she is home.

The sign is new. They had no word for it in summer, because nothing in summer needed one. In the dark it has started to be used for other things: a child asleep at last, a net hanging full and quiet, a house found still on its root after a night of wind. And when the pressure comes into the air and the cords go dead, two people caught together on a line will hook their knees over. One finds the other's palm and sets a fist in it, and holds it there while the dry rain goes by underneath.
