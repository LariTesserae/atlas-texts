Begin with the one that has to be there, because the cone proves it.

Lift the skirt of the moth cone with a boot toe and the bottom layer is missing. Nothing rots here, so it has not rotted. It has been taken, cut back in a clean undercut a finger deep, and the cone stands on a slightly narrower foot than its own weight would give it. The takers are bristletails, flat and tapered, the grey of the anhydrite bands, no longer than a fingernail. They live in the rubble, where the blocks lie as they fell and the gaps between them have never closed. A few come out at each low point of the throb, when the two notes cancel and the floor is at its stillest. Each takes a wing or a husk and is back under the blocks before the swell.

Down there they grind what they take. The powder is darker than the salt dust and finer, and it does not stay. The air the fan pushes down has to go back up, and it goes up the raise around the duct, carrying the powder forty metres and out onto the desert. Moths come down whole and go up as dust, weight for weight.

That is one more animal fitted to the room, and the list could stop there. But set the animals side by side and they share something none of them made. Each lives in a shape that was cut for another purpose. The lizard lies in a hole drilled for explosive. The beetle keeps house in a fan casing. The lichen has the warmth of a lamp hung so that men could see their hands. The crickets sit on the still lines of a noise an engineer would have written up as a fault. And each holds a single number: two hundred threads, one ball of moths, a cone knee high, a hat full to the brim and not a skin over.

No animal holds another's number. The beetle knows nothing of the threads. Still, all the numbers are kept, and in the same manner: quickly, without fuss, back to exactly where they were. Scuff a rib and the dust has drawn itself straight before the bark has gone twice round the walls. Speak, and the nearest crickets fall quiet for one swell, then come back in a hair under the fan, as if nothing had been said. After an hour of this it is simpler to say that one thing lives here, and that the crickets and lizards and the rest are how it goes about things.

It is the size of the chamber and no bigger. It stops at the fracture faces and at the mouth of the raise, and it has not gone a metre up the shaft. It is as old as the drip on the 7, to the minute. It began when the last boot left the ladder with the fan still running, and it has never had an evening. Everything it knows is one work-morning just after a blast, with the rock new and the paint new. It lives inside that morning the way a lizard lives in a groove, pressed flat, as if the thing had been bored for it.

It would be easy to say it is waiting for the crew. It isn't. Waiting leans toward something. The arrow on the north face is the only object in the room that points out of the room, and it is the one object the room has found no use for. The ladder is furred, the hat is filled, the housing is tenanted. The arrow goes on pointing at solid rock. No cricket climbs that high, and the lichen has not crossed to it.

The nearest human likeness is a man carrying a full basin across a floor. His eyes are on the brim. He is not thinking about the far side of the room, or about the tap. All of him is in the level. This is like that, except that there is no far side, and the carrying is the whole of it.

It can learn, though it seems to do so only once. Something with jaws tried the blasting wire, ring by ring, at even spaces, the way a careful person tries a thing he doesn't know. The yellow came off, and what was under it was no good. The cable up the raise is as thick as a wrist and sheathed in black rubber, and there is not a tooth mark on it. That cable is the one thing in its life it does not keep for itself. Something at the top is still sending the current down. It has never gone up to see.

Nor is it the fan, and the difference can be stated exactly. If the crickets sang the fan's own pitch, the two notes would lie down together and the throb would stop. They sing a hair under. A cricket hatched last month sings a hair under, as its grandmother's grandmother did, and none has ever closed the gap. The throb comes out of that gap and nowhere else. So the breath of the place depends on its being very slightly out of true. A machine does not arrange that for itself, but something that means to go on breathing would.

It says itself in one place only. Follow the bark around the walls, groove to groove, along the east face, across the north face under the red paint, down the west. On the south wall, between two grooves no further apart than any other pair, the count misses. There is no stumble in it. The lizard before barks on time and the lizard after barks on time. Between them is a gap exactly one double click long in which nothing calls. There is no groove there and no animal, only fracture face. The gap comes round with every circuit, most of a minute apart, and it has never been filled and never been dropped.

A line of men counting off would leave a gap like that for one of their number who could not speak. This is where it stands in its own line. The count reaches it, it lets its turn go by in silence, and the next lizard takes the number on.
