Most of what lives in the basin lives on the thud. The catfish are delivered by it and the egrets fed by it, the rails take what the egrets drop, and the frogs keep time by it. The bats use whatever stands between one thud and the next. These are lodgers. They keep the hours of the place and have no part in setting them.

The resident is harder to find. The way in is through what it owns, which is not much.

It owns the lines. A day's listening shows that the notes repeat. A long hall comes down on the east side and its ridge note goes out with it. Inside the hour the same note is sounding across the basin as one corner of the tower. The four short notes of a cell's front turn up in the doorway of a round house. There are a few hundred notes and there are never any new ones. They are the same lines, moved.

The first line of every building is a vertical, and it hangs by itself for about a minute before a second is laid across it. That is a plumb line, and the rest are setting-out strings. The thing that lives here is a builder with one set of string, and the set is too short. The plan worn into the gneiss holds many times what can be strung at once. So the town goes up in rotation, a few dozen buildings at a time, and each stands until something further down the list needs its lengths. That takes an hour, or two days.

It owns one other thing. Pick through a building after it has slumped and there is nothing in the heap but stems, and the mat floats them without a dimple. A wall of stems weighs what a heap of stems weighs, which is very little. What came down with the thud was not the plant's, though it drove a shin's depth of floating felt through the water onto rock, and has done it often enough to dish the rock. It went when the lines went.

The weight is the builder's own. It is lent to each building when the last stem closes and taken back at the end. The clear water comes with it, swamp water pressed up through the floor and strained by the felt, standing in its basin for as long as something leans on the room. No bench in the town has been sat on. But every house, for its hour or its two days, has had somebody's weight in it.

With its weight lent out, there is very little of the builder left over, and that remainder is what goes about laying line. The mat does not dip under it. Steam drifts across it. Rain falls straight through, because whatever keeps the floors dry, it does not use on itself. The frogs go quiet in a patch a few strides wide. The patch moves across the open green at about the pace of an egret, in a straight line from a building that has just come down to a place where nothing is standing yet. That is all the ground gives. The better way to find the builder is to watch the lizard.

The monitor lies along the crest of the whaleback with its chin on the stone. It is old, or it is the latest of a long line that has held the rock. It eats the frogs that climb the seams after the seep, and it pays no attention to thuds or to egrets. But its head is always turned toward some stretch of empty mat. Now and then its tongue goes out in that direction and the head moves a few degrees. Where the head is pointing, a pale vertical will presently appear.

Every other animal in the basin attends to the buildings. The monitor attends to the builder. There is a strip of polish along the crest, the only smooth stone above water. It is not a hollow. It is where a lizard's belly has turned, a few degrees at a time, to keep facing something.

With the lizard for a pointer, some habits can be made out. The builder walks, and does not hurry. When a squall strips a half-risen wall it stays by the bare lines until the plant starts up them again, and it does nothing to help the plant. When a row of cells has landed it goes down the lane once, slowly, with the rails behind it, and behind it the stems are lying flat. Whatever angle the row was drawn at, they lie pointing away from the sea, the way feet would press them coming in from the coast. When the tower stands, its footing takes most of the load on the seaward corner.

It leaves every door open. It gives a market a morning. It has not been known to unstring a hall with bats in it. Either the bats have learned the list or the list goes around the bats, and from outside these look the same.

The lines that appear around the whaleback are a different matter. No building comes down to supply them, and their notes are heard at no other time. They are a set kept back. The egrets walk in, because a hum is a hum. The monitor, for once, has nowhere particular to face, and lies with its eyes shut. Across the basin the brown water draws down from every standing footing, and the thirty or so buildings lift a finger's width on the mat. The frogs stop, with no thud to stop them.

The builder is calling in what it has lent. Whatever is meant for the rock would need all of it, the whole weight of the town set down in one place, on the one lot where the plan shows nothing. Everything else in the basin has been the same shape in the same position for long enough to be in the stone. Here it is still choosing between a ring and a square.

A minute of that is as far as it goes. The lines go out. The water comes back up around the footings without a sound, and the town is as heavy as it was.

Then the monitor opens its eyes and its head comes round. Out on the green, where it is pointing, a single line appears and hangs. Nothing is laid across it yet. A dragonfly settles on it and is let stay.
