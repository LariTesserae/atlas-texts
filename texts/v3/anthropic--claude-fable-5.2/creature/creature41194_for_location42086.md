On the bottom tier, starting about ten paces from the ladder, the cheeks report something that is neither a main nor a gap. The mains are hot and the gaps are cooler stripes, and this is a third thing: a long presence at the temperature of blood, the same as the sump but at the wrong height. It stands in the space between the black main and the lowest steam main, where the route ought to go.

The hand finds it next. On the side toward the steam it is crusted like the inside of a kettle, with a hard pale scale laid down in ridges, and it is as dry as the canvas. A hand that reaches over the top to the side against the black main finds it bare and wet, and it gives under a thumb. It runs nine paces. It stands about hip-high and fills the gap exactly, because it grew there. Its flanks are hollowed to the curve of the two pipes, and at its waist, where they come nearest, it is the thickness of a hand.

Where a flange on the steam main stands proud of the lagging, the bolt heads have worn grooves in the scale. Each groove is a hand's width long, which is the distance the main travels in a year. The thing itself does not travel. It holds to the black main, the one line in the hall that never changes length, and lets the other slide. Along its nine paces no drops fall, because the wet flank takes the dew before it can bead. The film on the sump beneath that stretch is thicker than elsewhere and never wrinkles. Directly above it is one of the few flanges that still weep. The lime that would have closed that leak goes onto its back instead.

The fitters, when there were fitters, called it the lodger, and did not write that down. What they left is in the route. At that place the way by hand goes up a tier, along, and down again, and whoever learned the route learned that with the rest.

It is not listed with the animals, and the omission is fair. An animal pays for its warmth. The lodger lies where one flank takes heat and the other gives it up to cold iron, so its middle keeps the one temperature that suits it without burning anything. An animal pays to move, and it does not move. It eats close to nothing: the lime from the drip, the white threads where rotted canvas touches it, a cricket that has died in the lagging and dried. The steam trap is two paces from its near end. Once in forty minutes the trap lets its wet breath out along the tier, the bare flank takes that in, and this is the nearest thing it does to breathing. The thickness of the quiet afterward is partly the lodger.

The trunk is the least of it. Along its upper edge it puts out lines of its own, a thumb thick where they leave it and thinning as they climb. They go up through the gratings beside the copper and the lead and are easily taken for more of the same, since each takes on the warmth of whatever it lies along. There are upward of thirty, and each ends against something. Several rest on flanges and one in the bend of an expansion loop. One lies on a roller track, just short of the bright strip. One is flat against the vault among the straws. One was laid along a valve spindle so long ago that the wheel's own creeping has wound it a turn and a half around, and it has let itself be wound.

The lines do nothing to what they touch. The wheels found their settings unattended, and it only rode one of them round. What comes down the lines is the state of the hall. The people who walked here by their cheeks knew an arm's length of it at a time. The hall is never seen whole, but it is held whole, in thirty places at once, by something that is all cheek.

It is slow, though slow is a poor report of it. It is exact at a pace nothing else here keeps. The straws lengthen a finger-joint in a year, and that is growth it can follow. The mains go out a hand's width and come back, and it feels them go. The wheels turn. By its account the hall is busy.

What it cannot follow is anything quick. Crickets cross it constantly, and it knows them only as a faint general prickle, not as crossings. If it registers the gecko at all, it is as one small permanent thing, which at its pace is the truth. The hum in the grating goes through it as through boot soles, and it has never placed it. Nor does it know what the black main carries. It has lain against that pipe since before the lagging slumped, and it knows that the pipe is cold, does not change, and can be held on to.

One line goes to the gauge board. It comes up through the middle grating and lies along it, and its end rests a hand's width past the place where the green stain stops. It has never been inside the circle. Light is nothing to it. But the lamp gives a little heat through its thick glass, and everything else warm in the hall is long: mains, loops, and lines running off both ways past the reach of feeling. The lamp is the only warm thing that is a point.

At a hand's width outside the green, that warmth is at the limit of what the line can feel. Like the numerals on the dials, it goes out when attended to directly. So the lodger attends a little to one side of it, and has for years.

There used to be other points. They came down the shaft with a draught of street air behind them. They were blood-warm, like the lodger and the sump, and they moved too quickly to be followed, so it knew each one mostly by where it stopped. They stopped at the gauge board. One would stand under the lamp for what was, to the lodger, barely an instant, and then go, and the cold above would shut off.

As it understands the matter, there has always been a small warmth at the gauge board. Sometimes it was stronger, and lately it has been fainter and more constant. Nothing in its experience says that the faint steady one is a different kind of thing from the others. Over some decades it moved the end of that line inward until it could just feel the warmth again, and there it stopped, which is as near as it has needed to come.

It has not concluded that they left. It has concluded that one of them stayed and grew quiet. The fern leans toward the glass from one side. From the other, a hand's width out in the dark, the end of the line lies on the grating with the gecko's prints across its dust, keeping that one company.
