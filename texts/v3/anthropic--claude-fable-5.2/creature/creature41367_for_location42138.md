The frog is the one you would name first, because it is the one that looks back. It is the color of the wet stone it sits on and no bigger than the end of a thumb, and it spends its whole life within a few body-lengths of blood-warm water. Its kind used to call. The throat still carries the sac for it, a slack grey fold that never fills. What it has instead is the leg: the slow reach backward, the toes spreading, the white web opened and held like a small palm, then folded away. One flag seems to mean *this stone is taken*. Two in quick succession mean something more pressing. A female answers by lifting one foot and keeping it up. The eggs go under the overhang of a plate in the middle pool, and the tadpoles graze the rim of the olive mats where the water is nearly too hot for them, heads in, tails toward the cool. A few each year go over the lip with the overflow. Nothing is done about this.

The likeliest account of how a frog came to six thousand meters is the drilling: a water tank, or pipe crated in wet straw at some camp far below. If so, the frogs are exactly as old here as the wellhead. They must have brought the gesture with them from some loud stream lower down, and here they simply let the voice go.

The birds are harder to count because they will not hold still, but the count comes out near thirty every time. They are dun above and a little paler beneath, built like wrens, with thin bills for midges. They work the canopy from inside, along the fused crowns and through the hanging moss, and when one reaches the place where the fern goes brown, it turns as if it had touched glass.

They sing, though it takes a while to see it. A bird stops at a branch end, lifts its bill, and its throat shudders for several seconds, tail pumping in time, the whole posture of song. Nothing arrives. Perhaps there is sound in it; no one standing on the shelf could say. What arrives is the mouth. The lining is a hard yellow, the yellow of the sulfur seams, the color every nestling shows its parents and most birds lose. These have kept it. For as long as the song lasts there is a lit point in the green, and it goes out when the bill shuts. Mates keep track of each other across the thicket by these flares. A young bird learns its song as a length and rhythm of opening, by watching its father's mouth.

They do not sit their eggs. The nest is a pocket pushed into the moss on the pipe's lagging, always a little more than a hand's span from the bare ring at a flange, where the moss holds the heat of a brooding breast. The eggs are left there and visited. Several times a day a bird comes, puts its bill into the pocket, waits, and then either pulls moss over or scratches some away. There are only so many flanges, and only so many spans of moss at the right heat along the base of the wall. That may be the whole reason for thirty.

The young try the ceiling. On an afternoon when snow is falling to its seam, a fledged bird will leave the canopy and climb the warm column over the stack to where the grey ends, and hang a moment against the white, the only time in its life it is easy to see. Then it drops back. It may do this twice. After its first season it doesn't.

The midges are the commonest animal and do most of the work. Their larvae are red threads in the pool silt. The adults stand over the water in columns, and they feed everything that eats. They also serve the orchids, which are pale because pale is what shows in the dim under the canopy, and which smell, if you put your face to one, faintly of yeast. A midge goes in expecting something else and backs out with a yellow parcel glued between its wings. Those that drift past the line fall on the rime as a fine grey dust. On the cold side, small black jumping things come to the edge to glean it, springtails by the look of them, with a spider or two that hunts them. They belong to the other climate and never come in. They live off the shelf the way gulls live off a harbor.

The largest resident takes longest to recognize, because it doesn't appear to be one. The thicket looks like a few hundred shrubs. Dig into the moss between any two trunks and you find them joined by a runner as thick as a wrist, lying along the clinker, which joins the next, and the next. It is very likely a single plant, grown from one seed that arrived in the gut of the first storm-blown bird. It has been walking ever since. At one end of the shelf its suckers come up through moss that was bare rock a decade ago. At the other, the grey stems standing in new frost are its own old limbs, let go. It keeps pace with the vents, a finger's width a year, and everything else rides on it. A bird's stretch of branch shifts a little along the wall within its lifetime. A frog's stone will be cold for its descendants, and they will be on another.

Twice a year the shed door is opened by someone who doesn't live here. They come up out of the white in ear defenders, stay as long as the work takes, and add a mark. The birds have never met anything large that moves. They give the visitor the attention they'd give a shrub and pick midges off the pack.

At night the shelf keeps its warmth and loses its light, and the birds come down out of the canopy to the pipe. They roost on the moss sleeve between flanges, in a row, pressed side to side, every one facing the wall. The frogs on their stones face it too, and the thicket leans. The tremble comes up steadily through moss, feet, and root, as it has every night any of them has been alive, and goes out along the cable, over the lip, and down.
