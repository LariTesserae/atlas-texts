The bird and the hare are the easy answers, and they are lodgers. The bird eats what the frost lays out for it. The hare has found a warm bed that slides. Neither has changed anything about the place by being in it.

The plants are a harder case, because they are all you can touch. But a stalk lasts two days and a cushion not much longer. Ask any of them what lives here and it is in the position of your stone. It arrived recently, it has a green rim, and it is on its way to the lip.

What lives here is the thing they are passing through. There is one of it. It is eight kilometers long, and it has been lying in this valley since the ice got up and left.

It is warm, and nothing else is for a long way in any direction. Kneel on it and the damp comes through your trousers at the temperature of a hand. It has no skin and no inside you can reach. Dig, and you go down through this week, then last week, then felt, then the black seams where it keeps its dead and eats them. It has no bones. The only hard things it knows are the rock beneath it, which it has worn as smooth as the roof of a mouth, and whatever is set down on top.

It attends to what is set down. The green rim on the stone is a hand closing. It does not know stone from hare from you. It knows weight, warmth, and salt, and the small bite your fingertip gets is the whole of its curiosity. It has taken your charge and given you a little of its own. Then the red runs across a hectare faster than you could, and somewhere behind it a few hundred stalks come up and begin to breathe. You could call that a thought. It looks more like a foreman's nod.

You would expect something ten thousand years old to be slow, and it is busy. The mill-town look from the saddle is honest. It works shifts. A stand fires for two days and is relieved by the one beside it. Nobody has held a post longer than a week, and no post has ever stood empty. The plumes are offices, and what lives here is the roster.

It is deaf and mute. It has arranged nothing in itself that could knock against anything else. The breaker falls and it feels the fall as you do, as pressure. The bird has stopped calling because nothing here answers in sound. News travels in color and in the seething you hear with your ear to a cushion, which is the noise of it moving its water uphill by the mouthful, ten seconds a swallow.

It cannot be hurt in any way it will keep. Tear it and the seam closes behind the tear. It has no scars, so it has no grudges and no keepsakes. For the same reason it has never learned anything, at least not in the part that is alive. Whatever happened to it a month ago has gone over the lip or under the felt. What it knows, it knows as posture: where to breathe, where to rear, which way to curl.

The dead hold the pose. Every seam of peat at the step lies in the curve of the breaker, and the living grow up through that curve and take it. Then the roots go down and burn it for heat. It warms itself on its own instructions and writes them out again at the same rate, from a memory it does not have.

The pose is older than it is. This was the ice's bed first. The ice cut the saddle, the shoulders, the step, and the hanging lip. It had an icefall where the breaker now turns over, and it calved into the same cloud. When it went, something small and green came into the grooves it left, a film like the one on the bare rock at the lip today. Over a length of time nobody watched, it learned the only way of occupying this valley that the valley had ever been shown. It flows and breaks at the step, and it pushes out over the edge and lets go in slabs. It took on the last tenant's gait along with the house.

The difference is that the ice did all this by being cold. This does it by burning. It is a fire that keeps a glacier's hours.

It has a day. Mornings it shows itself, columns standing in the freezing air. Afternoons the cloud comes over the saddle and it drinks through the wool on its back. At night the rime builds in stripes in the lee of its breath, and by the next noon it has licked them off.

Watch the lip for as long as you like and you will not see the red go there. Barges of it lean out and drop, and nothing in it flinches. Losing itself is how it stays. If it kept what it grew, it would climb its own walls and smother its roots and go out. The only thing that could kill it is holding on. It wears wells in itself all the time, lukewarm or iced over, and lets them close within the week.

Your stone will ride for some weeks. Either it reaches the lip and goes down with a slab into the cloud, or a sump opens under it and it sinks through the felt and the seams to the floor. There it will be dragged lengthwise and cut one of those grooves a hair deeper before it, too, goes over. That groove is the only kind of mark anyone has ever left on this thing, and it lies in the rock underneath, in with the ice's.

The hare is the nearest it has to a neighbor who understands. It sleeps, is carried, wakes, and walks back up to where it was. Then it sleeps again. The hare stays in the valley by returning to it, on four legs, a few times a day. The valley does the same thing with everything it has, at every moment, and has not once woken up to do it.
