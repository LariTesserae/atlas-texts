Start with the small lives, because they are there and can be counted.

The pale mat on the spring-side stones is alive, and so are the colorless threads near the crack. Both feed on the part of the water that smells of boiled egg. The isopods graze the mat. The cricket takes what falls. All of them together would fit in two cupped hands, and for a while they seem to be the whole answer.

Then there are the pearls. Pearls left in a basin under a film of this water should be cemented down by now, like the lamp. These are loose. Each one turns in its socket under a fingertip. The two notches in the rimstone are still open, with edges cleaner than the footholds', though the same room has been working on both. And the palm ridges in the clay are sharp.

So something keeps the room. At first it is only a set of habits with no body: a turning of pearls, a thumbnail run through two notches. What it leaves alone says as much. It let the lamp be collared, the ladder crust over, and the pin bleed green. It keeps what belongs to water and to touch. It has given up light, climbing, and fastening.

The body is among the columns, in the close place where you have to go through sideways. It is the color of tallow and it stands as they stand, with water sheeting down it. It is narrow from front to back, as anything would be that had passed between those trunks for long enough. Its hair hangs limp and colorless and drips from the ends, like the threads near the crack. Its skin went through wrinkling long ago and came out the other side, smooth and faintly clear, so that darker shapes show under it as they do in the cricket. On the crown and shoulders, where the drips land when it is still, it wears a crust as thin as eggshell. When it moves, the crust cracks and slides off into the pool. That is the only sound in the room besides the ticking.

It moves seldom, and just enough. The bat on the ledge shows what stillness comes to here, and it knows the bat. It goes to the ledge now and then and runs a finger along the stone twigs.

Its eyes are open, the pupils as wide as they go, and they do not turn toward anything. They can still tell the gray wash from its absence, and that is all it asks of them. It follows the room by the ticking, which it knows drop by drop. A drop that lands on cloth or hair sounds different from one that lands on water, so a visitor is a patch of wrong notes moving in from the passage. It would be still for you. You would take it for a column. You might go through sideways with your chest against stone and your back against it, and feel only wetness, neither warm nor cold.

It eats the mat, scraped up with the side of a thumb, and sometimes an isopod, though it knows how few there are. It drinks in the lowest corner, lowering its face to the place where the surface is domed.

It cannot feel where it ends. The air and the water are the temperature of its skin, nothing is ever dry, and so its surface sends no report. It has no outline until something presses on it. That is why it stands where the columns are closest, with one against the chest and one against the back, the only edges the room offers. The clay is for the same thing.

At the clay it is easier to say she. The hands that go into the prints are small and short-fingered, with a crook in the left little finger that the bank has kept. She kneels in the lowest pool, sets both hands in with the fingers spread, and they fit. That is how she knows she is still the size she was. The prints are an hour old and they are older than the lamp.

She came in with the lamp. What she has left of that time is small. The lamp was lit, the shadows of the columns moved whenever the flame moved, the whole room seemed to lurch, and she did not like it. There were others. They deepened the basin. She was the lightest, so she was the one sent up the pole to the high recesses for the white paste. The pin held something closed at her shoulder until the cloth rotted off it. She does not remember deciding to stay. She remembers stepping into a pool on the way out and not being able to find her foot, and standing there waiting for it to report.

She did try to count. When the gray wash had faded three times she went to the bank and drew three fingers through the clay. Partway along she could not think what would make the fourth mark different from these. Her hand wavered and she stopped. It is the one mark in the room she never touches.

The pearls are the one thing she made rather than kept. She sorted them. The smallest have a flake of her shoulder crust at the core. The largest grew around a tooth. She does not think of them as a record. She likes that they get bigger toward one end.

She tried a word once, and it stopped a few steps from her mouth, as they do here. She has not needed another. The cricket makes no sound either, and they get on.

When the gray fades she goes to the basin and gives each pearl a quarter turn, smallest to largest. The dark is the only thing that happens in the room, and this is what she does about it. Then she goes back in among the columns and finds the two that fit her, and the water runs down her as it runs down them.
