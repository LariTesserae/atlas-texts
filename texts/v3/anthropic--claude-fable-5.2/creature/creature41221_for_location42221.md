Of the three pumps, the first two are furniture. The third is occupied.

You would not know it while the water runs. The third casing is the same cart-sized snail shell as the others, soft grey, with its maker's name gone to bumps. The differences are small and take several tides to add up. On the first two pumps the discharge mouth stands open, a round of black water as wide as a barrel head. On the third it is closed by what looks like a blanking plate, pitted and grey, crusted with keel worms, sitting a little proud of the rim.

The anemones on this casing are also wrong. They are not the white and orange kind from the crane, and they are not where the flow is best. They are squat, striped buff and brown, and they sit in a ring around one patch on the outer curve of the volute and nowhere else. Everything else in the hall grows where it happened to land, but these were put.

The plate is a claw. At slack water, when the drone dies and the flap valve hangs shut, it turns edge-on and folds inward, and something starts coming out of the open mouth.

The antennae come first, two whips each longer than an arm, the only fine things the animal owns. They go out over the silt and touch it at intervals, without hurry. Then come two eyes on stalks, small for the size of what follows. Then the legs, four that show, as thick as a wrist, set down one at a time on the pump's bearers.

Hermit crab is the nearest word, and it is close: armour in front, and behind it a soft body wound into a spiral that someone else made. But no whelk ever grew a shell this size, and this animal has not carried its house in a very long time. The casing is bolted down. It is a hermit that stays, and it comes out only as far as its hold allows, which is less than half its length. The rest remains in the dark of the volute.

What shows is the colour of the iron, and for the same reason. Years of leaning on the casing lip have worked graphite into the shell, and the edges that do the most work have the dull shine of a thumbed pencil drawing.

It did not start in the casing. Its earlier houses are still in the hall, lying in a rough line from the dock end, smallest first:

- **Grease cup:** Brass, thumb-sized, in the silt under the first engine bed.
- **Gauge case:** The width of a saucer, its bronze rim bright on the inside only.
- **Valve body:** Cast iron, shaped like a two-handled jug.
- **Pipe bend:** As wide as a bucket, resting against the foot of the second pump.

Each has its inside rubbed smooth and its mouth worn on one side. There is no shell among them, or none that has lasted. As far as the room can show, the animal has lived its whole life in things from one foundry.

The lobster under the first bedplate dug a hollow to its own measurements. This animal has done the same from inside, in iron, by leaning. The outside of the casing keeps every contour it was poured with. The inside has spent decades pressed against something that grows, and graphite gives.

On the outer curve the wall has gone through, leaving a hole the size of a palm. Something pale and creased lies across it and moves slightly every few seconds. The anemones are planted around that spot.

The movement is breathing. The animal fans water through the casing to wet its gills, in at the suction pipe and out past the top of the door. At slack you can see the outflow as a flicker in the falling silt. It comes to a few bucketfuls a minute, and it is the only pumping still done in the hall.

The conger in the third suction pipe lies in that draught. It is fatter than the other two, and when the small claw passes something inside, the grey head withdraws down the pipe toward the meal. Small hermits keep a worm in the shell on the same terms.

Inside, the soft body is wrapped around the impeller hub the way a hermit wraps the central pillar of a shell. The bronze vanes are polished where it lies, not where the flow would have rubbed them. When it shifts its grip the impeller moves on its bush and gives one low note, like a pan set down on a stone floor. You hear it only at slack, and most slacks you don't.

It spends its twenty minutes mostly on housekeeping. The flood drops things in the furrow where it passes the third pump: a dead pouting, a cast crab shell, a rag of kelp from the lantern lip. The animal picks these over and passes them in. Now and then it lifts an edible crab out of its pit, straight up, like a lid. The crab pits stop in a clean arc around the third pump at the reach of the antennae, and the pouting, when they spread across the floor, leave the same arc bare.

Most slacks it does one more thing. It leans out toward the second pump as far as its hold allows and stretches one leg and both antennae toward that open mouth, across water it cannot cross. It holds them there for a while. The second casing is the same pattern and the same bore, to the inch. A hermit measures every mouth it meets, and the habit has outlasted there being anything to measure.

Every few years a slack comes when the door does not open, and then several more. After that the mouth of the casing stands open for some tides with no door at all, while the new one hardens somewhere inside. Nothing in the hall goes near it then either.

Each time, the fit is tighter and the pale patch at the hole is a little larger. The iron has finished changing, but its tenant has not. It is the one thing in the room that has not reached its final arrangement.

When the seal drops through the slot, the door is shut before the pouting have finished balling. The seal passes the third pump on its circuit as it passes the others. Once it stopped and laid its whiskers against the plate for a moment, then went on.

The animal is the first thing in the room to know the tide has turned. The antennae come in. The legs fold back over the rim in the order they came out. The claw swings round and seats itself in the mouth without a sound, because iron that soft does not ring.

A few breaths later the flap valve lifts off its seat, the furrow begins to smoke, and every fish turns to face the harbour. The third pump looks like a pump with its outlet blanked off.
