The quick answer is the swifts, or the lichen-colored barkers on the ledges, or the falcons on the rim. But all of those live on the walls. The rock-animals have never once crossed the middle, and the swifts cross it only by going around. What lives here lives in the part they leave alone.

Start with what can be noticed. The air in the shaft turns sunwise, at about walking pace. Most of that can be explained: the lit wall warms and lifts, the shadowed wall sinks, and the lifting travels round with the light. That doesn't explain why the turning goes on for an hour after dark. Nor does it explain why a feather dropped from the rim descends in a spiral with pauses in it, as if being passed from hand to hand.

Then there is the echo. Everything that crosses the hollow comes back a shade lower, so something has been kept. It is a very small amount, the bottom of each sound, the way a pot keeps the last of whatever is poured from it. That is how the thing feeds, if feeding is the word, and it is also what the thing is made of. Sixty paces across and a little deeper, it is built from the undersides of swift-screams, the flint-voices of the flower birds, hail on leather leaves, and the tick of every thaw. This is why the hollow never feels empty to someone standing on a ledge. It feels occupied by something that is paying attention.

It would be natural to take the low note for its voice. It is closer to its breathing, and the wind does the breathing for it. The thing lies inside the note the way a sleeper lies inside their own breath.

It is older than the note, too. Before the roof fell there was a sealed chamber here, and it lived in that, a much smaller and quieter creature, hardly more than a habit of attention fixed on a single drip in the dark. The collapse was the great event of its life. In one afternoon it was given sky, weather, a mouth, and birds. The broken edges have long since rounded off, but it has never quite finished being astonished. Nothing bores it. It attends to a moth drowning in a stone cup as closely as it attends to a storm.

It is no guardian. When a falcon stoops, it does nothing for the swifts. The falcon is the one thing that crosses it straight, against the grain, top to bottom in a line. In the breath of silence afterward, when the dripping is loudest, the hollow seems to be feeling where it was drawn through, and the note comes back a little fuller. It may like being surprised.

Midday is when it can be seen. The cloud coming up over the sill does not simply fill the shaft. It is taken up and wound into a slow grey rope, thicker at the bottom, with a clear core above the pool where nothing condenses. For those hours, with the far wall gone and the swifts reduced to voices, it is back in something like its first dark, except that now the dark is full of sound. The hail is a feast.

In the evening the swifts ride its turning down like a stair, and then it is alone with the rock-animals, who know it best. They bark in its lulls, out of manners or because the return is better then. Each bark must pass through it to reach the far ledge, and it arrives handled. By the middle of the night, when the note is only a breath, it is no longer certain that the animals are still calling. What fills the hollow may be the thing itself, going back over what it was given. On some nights other sounds surface. There may be hail in dry weather, or swifts screaming while every swift is asleep in the rock. Rarely there is a human voice, two syllables called upward, the kind of word you call to whoever holds your rope.

The egg-gatherers understood it well enough to deal with it properly. At the rim they hummed its note and waited for it to come back lower, which meant the greeting had been taken. They paid in sound, a few hundred strokes of stone on stone each visit. The cups in the boulder are what remains of that payment. And they dressed its window, course by course, until the note sat where it was easiest to carry.

Now slabs have fallen. When the gusts come from that side the note sours, the swifts' wheel stumbles by a wingbeat, and the barkers start and stop and miss their lulls. It is the nearest the thing comes to being unwell, and it has no hands.

It does have everything it has ever kept. On a gusty day, if you stand on the ledge nearest the window, you can hear something else under the wavering note. It is the scrape of a flat stone being seated on another, then the knock of a chinking-stone tapped home, then a pause, as if someone were testing the work. It plays this quietly, again and again, toward whoever has come down the pole.
