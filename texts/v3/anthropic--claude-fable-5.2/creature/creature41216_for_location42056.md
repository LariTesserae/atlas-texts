They are white before they are anything else. From the bank you see them only as movement on the causeways, heads and loaded baskets crossing the planks, and every one is the colour of the ground. Chalk is in their hair and eyebrows, in the creases of their knuckles, and in the wool of cloaks that were brown once. From above, a child and an old man look the same age. When a shower passes they darken with the town and dry as it does, in patches, shoulders first.

Closer, they sort themselves out. There are several hundred, more young than old, because the town is young. They squint early and it stays. Their forearms are heavy and their hands split across the knuckles, and in the evenings they work tallow into the splits, so everyone smells faintly of lamp. The long-standing diggers have a dry cough they do not remark on. Fuel is carried in from the plain, so each courtyard keeps one fire and eats around it. A person asked where they belong names the court, then the wall they sleep against.

They speak low. A raised voice is taken for an accident, and heads come up to see who is hurt. They know one another largely through chalk: whose baby wakes, who talks asleep, who rises before light. Nobody mentions what comes through. That is the first courtesy, and the second is the knock. A rap before digging must be answered, whatever stands between the two households, and people who have not spoken in years still answer. Other knocks have grown up since. Two slow ones at night mean only *I am here*. The children have a set of their own, and the old quarter raps with the knuckle where the east uses the heel of the hand. All of this is recent enough that people can tell you who started which. The customs still show their tool marks, like the walls.

The arm's length is one particular arm. The woman who began the work measured her first partition against her own reach, and a cord knotted to that reach hangs in the room she cut. Every wall since has been checked against it or a copy. She was not tall, and long-armed diggers have to be reminded. Nobody agrees why she went down instead of up. Some say it was the wind and some the lack of timber, and one story says she was hiding somebody. Her granddaughter still sleeps in that room and is old herself, and tired of being asked.

Most of them dig or carry. Carrying falls to the young and to those whose shoulders have gone. The knappers are the exception and the only loud people in the town. They sit in the open yard with hide across their thighs and scars on their fingertips, and they talk at full voice over their own noise. The rest regard them as other places regard smiths. Their blades go out across the plain in bundles, and grain comes back.

The shepherds live out with the flocks and come in at dusk. They stand on the causeways with the dogs and would rather not go below. The townspeople are the same in reverse. On open grass they walk in single file, close together, as if the lanes still ran on either side of them.

The copper adze never spends two nights in one room. It is handed over at first light, and the household that holds it does nothing else that day. The neighbours bring the food. A squared door-frame means a room is finished and someone will be living behind it for good. Couples count their time together from their adze day.

The onions belong to a woman who married in from a river country. She moves the trough four times between morning and evening and has never been heard to say she misses soil. Her court thinks it a strange use of a strong back, but most of them find a reason to pass the trough.

They are careful with the things that arrive on their own. Nobody waters the fern or touches it, and the people filling jars beneath it look up each time to see that it is still there. The niche the jackdaws keep inspecting has been left empty all season by a household that could use it. The children know the snails apart by their bands.

One person goes down the shaft. She is narrow in the shoulder and was chosen for that. She is the only person in the town who is not white, since she comes up grey to the hair with chalk that clings to her as it clings to the basket. Every load she sends up is handled before it is tipped. Palms are pressed into it, and a pinch is rolled between finger and thumb. Nobody asks her anything. When she speaks of it at all she says it is colder. Meanwhile any of them can give the level of their cistern to a finger's width without looking.

Their dead go up the ramp the way the chalk goes. They are laid on the outer slope of the bank, and the season's baskets are tipped over them. The bank therefore holds them in order, the first digger deepest in and the rest a stride further out for each year. The nettles are thickest over the newest.

One of them lives nowhere yet. The six squares on the eastern grass were pegged with the cord, and the gouges in the opened one curve the left-hander's way. They are short and set close, the stroke of an arm not fully grown. Whoever made them has gone down to a fire to eat and will be back for the basket. For a season, until the next square is opened, this digger will be the only person in the town with no one to knock to.
