The easy answer is the grazer, because it has a face and somewhere to be.

Follow one. Her back comes to mid-thigh and her wool reaches the ground, so she seems to be drawn along her track more than to walk it. The fleece is grey-black and dry as paper. Charged, it stands off her body until she looks a third bigger than she is. Then she brushes down the margin of a cell, the needles tick, the blue threads jump, and she reaches the far corner sleek and narrow. By the time she has finished cropping, the live cushion has stood her wool out again, and she carries that to the next cell. Her teeth are green because the sap is full of copper. Her eyes are large and tuned to violet. The corona a visitor has to catch sideways is plain to her, so that working cells are lit rooms and the rest of the basin is wall.

She has never touched another adult of her kind. Her neighbours are a tick too far off to hear and a blue thread at the edge of sight, the same three or four threads all her life. One of them is her mother. She walked her first year in the mother's track with her nose in the wool, until the sweet frost began to be laid in two lines instead of one. The loop narrowed at its middle the way a drop does before it parts. At some junction the mother bore right and the young one left, and that was all the parting there was. What passes between grazers after that passes at the iron, or at one of the few junctions where two loops lie against each other without crossing. One animal leaves something on the polished face. Another, hours later, takes it up on her wool.

It is tempting to say she follows the shift, but the cell ahead of her has no seam and comes on because she touches it, and she goes to it because the frost is there. When a neighbour's thread stops appearing she holds her head that way for a while. Some shifts later the frost on that side is laid a hand's width further out than before, and she follows it. She is hired, and the starved cells have raised the wage.

The birds get about more. They are the only animals that pass from loop to loop, walking column to column with their heads down, so they alone could be said to live in the basin and not on a line through it. A bird that finds a cell beginning to swell puts up its crest, the tips spark, and birds two or three cells off turn and come. The grazers read this too. The birds do not brood. They lay in the thin edge of a cushion and walk away, and the egg is warmed for a few minutes each time that cell comes round, again and again, until it hatches into steam with the springtails already at its feet.

The springtails do not travel at all. They lie in the mat as frozen dust, and each shift thaws the ones beneath it. They feed, breed, drift about, and stop when the cell goes cold. A springtail's life is a string of warm minutes with nothing in between that it would know about.

Then there is the hunter. It is twice a grazer's length, flat as a hand, no thicker than the channel is deep, and the colour of the ice that skins the channel. It has no heat of its own. In the cold it is, for most purposes, part of the joint. When the cell beside it comes on, warmth soaks down the channel wall, and for those minutes it has a body it can use. Those are also the minutes the grazer comes, since the same warmth summons both. At a bend she slows and turns her flank to the joint. One long edge of the hunter lifts like a page and folds over her.

What follows is the odd part. The loop is now unwalked, so the cell that warmed the hunter drops out of the rotation. It has put out its own fire. It lies over its kill in a browning patch, fed and cold and unable to leave, until the neighbouring track has widened far enough, cell by cell, to bring the shift back to that joint. Then it warms and slides on up the seam. While it lay there the roots found it, as they find anything long and wet that keeps still. When it goes it tears free of a fine copper hair along its whole length and leaves a bright line in the channel.

So there is a carrier steered by frost, a bird hatched by the timetable, an insect switched on and off, and a predator that is wired into the joint it hunts from and must wait there to be reconnected. None of them fills the place. Each has its line or its point or its interval, and the dark between them is very wide. Only one thing here reaches from rim to rim, is present in every cell, and has been at work since the scree stopped moving. That is the rotation itself.

It has no edge but the tors, and no middle. The iron is not its heart, only something it found and has kept. Its body is the mat, the wire under the mat, and wool on legs for the gaps where wire will not go. In a fog-tide the fleece rimes exactly as the needles do, in feathers pointing into the flow, and by the ice alone there is no telling plant from animal. Its pulse is the shift, forty or fifty cells warm at a time and never the same ones. It feels weather, and the west runs hard when the west is lit from below. It heals, since a dead loop browns and is taken up. It remembers after a fashion in copper, because the wire runs to wherever a good conductor has ever rested. And it notices, with ten thousand needles leaning and one head held still.

More exactly, it is a discharge. The massif stands in the gap between the cloud floor and the upper air, and across that gap lies a pressure that anywhere else is let go in a white instant. Here it is let go slowly. The stroke comes down through every needle point as a violet too faint to look at directly. It crosses the summit at the pace of a walking animal, through sap, through root-drawn wire, through a fleece, with a pause to steam and a pause to graze. It was already arriving when the tors were still square. Everything alive in the basin is some part of the path it takes. The grazer is a length of it that can walk. The birds and the springtails warm themselves at it. The hunter interrupts it and is bypassed. Its ash is the clinker, and its smell is ozone and wet peat.

It is a calm sort of thing, with no use for speed. What it wants is better paths, and it is not particular about what they are made of. The iron fell out of the sky and was sunk, wired, and polished. Something upright that came over the rim and stood still would be the highest point in a day's walk, in a place where nothing else clears the knee, and the sky would start coming down through its shoulders before it had got its breath back. The cell under it would swell. Three hundred metres off, a head would turn. Frost would be laid toward it, a little further each shift. The wire would arrive in a season. In a few years a loop would bend to pass, and eventually there would be a band worn smooth at the height of a fleece.

Whether the newcomer was still breathing by then would make no difference to the basin, though for a while it would have been the loudest thing there.
