The people came after the masts. Nobody living claims to have raised them. The oldest residents were carried in as children across the clinker, and what they remember is the tone arriving in their chests a full day before the masts came into view. There are a few hundred of them now, most born on the shelves, and the place has had the better part of sixty years to shape them.

They are a damp people. Every plane wears its hand of fog, and they stand in it to the ankle from the day they can stand. Their feet are pale and soft and would not last an hour on the flow. Their mats are woven thick to lift a sleeper clear of the wet, and nothing they own is ever fully dry. They drink by setting a cup down in the sheet and coming back for it.

They walk slowly because the tone sits on the sternum and breath has to be fitted around it. Anyone who runs outside a room finds the lungs will not keep step, and stops within fifty meters, gray in the face. Children learn this once.

They plug their ears with fig latex rolled in fern scale, fitted in the first weeks of life and remade as the head grows. The plugs come out only inside the cord. Lifting both hands to the ears and away is the first thing anyone does on entering a room, and the gesture has come to mean welcome, and also *go on, I'm listening*. Between two people out on the open plane, where it cannot be literal, it means *this should be said aloud; come inside*.

Everyone has two names. The hand-name is public, a shape of the arms that can be read at three hundred meters. The voice-name is given in a room and used only there, so it is known just to those who have sat inside the cord with its owner. Some people are known aloud by forty or fifty others. Some are known by three.

Inside, they talk quietly and at length, having saved it up. They sing, always in one key, since the chord is never entirely gone from a room and anything sung has to sit on it. Tunes carried in from outside have been bent to fit, and no one left can sing them straight.

They eat figs, fern crosiers, and midge. A cloth drawn through the brown haze of a sheet comes out heavy. The catch is pressed into cakes and dried on the plane, and the fat rendered from it burns in the lamps. A lit room smells of it.

Nobody owns a garden. The rafts drift, and the raft at a room's edge this season is at someone else's by the next. People tend what is passing. They set cuttings into root-balls whose fruit they will not see, and eat from trees started by hands four kilometers off. Things travel this way too: a cup, a child's outgrown plugs, a coil of cord knotted in a pattern that means something to one household on the far side of the ring. This mail takes a year or three and often does not arrive. The dead go the same way, wrapped in their mats, bedded into a root-ball, and pushed off along the plane. Figs do well by it.

Height sorts them. The households on the top planes live in real daylight, with the rim in view and the fissure steaming on cool mornings. They ride six meters up and six down each day and are sunburnt, long-sighted, and talkative. Forty sheets below, the low households keep lamps lit at noon and feel the tubes in their floors. They are paler and say less. A marriage between a top room and a low one is called, in sign, a long climb. It means three hundred meters of fig root and knotted cord, with the chord at its worst halfway up every span, where the rope hums in the fist.

About a dozen go down to the rock. They are the only ones with soles, four layers of hide, which the shelves cannot make for themselves. Their work is to break things small. The sound will walk a seed or a husk to the still lines, but it cannot move a fallen root-ball or a dead swift, so the walkers pull these apart to a size the field can carry and leave the rest to it. They do this for the cicada. The soil on the lines is thin, and the nymphs need years in it. Everyone knows what the masts have come to lean on. The walkers count emergence holes each season and sign the number up, plane to plane, and it reaches the top before dark. They also lay a palm on every mast they pass, down among the geckos, and sign up that it is warm.

When the brood dies back and the upper planes go soft, some top households come down a few shelves. Others stay and sing. They sit at the edges of their rooms and hold the third overtone in shifts, day and night, a few dozen voices where the hedges had been full. Whether it helps has never been settled. The top sheet rides half a meter lower regardless. The singers say the plane feels firmer under the mat while they are at it, which is what singers would say.

Thunder has made them light. A close strike drops a room and everything in it, and what was set near the edge may not be in the room when the field takes hold again. So they keep little, and keep it in the middle of the mat. They sleep loose-limbed, by training. After a strike, before anything else, each household looks out and counts lamps.

The count has been short since the ninth mast stopped. That afternoon the southwest quarter went soft from the top down. The sheets let go first and fell as rain, the first that had reached the basalt since the masts began, and then the rafts started to slide. The planes tilted before they failed. Those who understood in time walked inward along the tilt, uphill on nothing, toward firmer air. Not everyone understood in time.

The ones who walked out had their pick of rooms, since there have always been more rooms than people. But some had ended that day standing on the rock of the dead quarter, in a quiet none of them had met before, hearing their own feet. A few of those never climbed again. Some thirty people live in the thicket now, in houses with walls, the first walls any of them had built, and crooked. They have given up plugs. They speak outdoors, and their children shout. They hunt the deer, and the walkers' soles come from them, traded for cord and midge cake. Shelf people say the thicket people have grown loud and quick and careless with their hands. Thicket people say a visitor from the shelves cannot sit easy on the ground, and keeps one palm flat on the earth as if it might go.

Between the two there is a margin. Toward the southwest what remains of the field thins. The planes out there slope, a little and then a lot, and the fog runs down them and pours off the ends in slow falls, which is why the thicket stays wet. The rooms near the margin are poor. The quiet inside is shallow, the chord leaks in, and a cup set down will not quite stay. Nobody takes them. One person has.

Her hand-name is a flat palm tipped a few degrees, the sign for a plane going. She got it at nine, as the last child off the nineteenth shelf that afternoon, holding a fistful of someone's wrap. She is a walker now. Her room is the last one out on the second plane, seventeen meters above the rock, over the hedge that used to run straight from the center mast to the ninth. No field meets another there anymore. The soil line is blurring, and the ferns have strayed a meter to either side. She walks it most days anyway and breaks up what has fallen.

Near the thicket end the field is weak enough that she can take her plugs out in the open, and she does, to hear the cicadas. Inside the ring they are locked to the masts so exactly that they cannot be picked out of the chord. Out here they have begun to drift. It is a small thing, a shade flat, a beat that comes and goes against the overtone like a slow pulse. She signs this up with the count each season. No one has told her what to do about it.

She goes into the thicket for hide and stays longer than the trade needs. She sits where the heap is deepest and listens to the bird. Her voice-name is known to eight people, and six of them live under those trees.

She has tried sleeping there twice. Both times she woke at the hour of settling, waiting for the floor to go down under her. It did not, and she lay awake on the still ground until light. So she climbs back before dusk. In her room, as the air cools, the descent comes through her stomach as it has every evening of her life. She reaches for her cup, which has crept a hand's width toward the southwest edge during the day, and sets it back in the middle of the mat.
