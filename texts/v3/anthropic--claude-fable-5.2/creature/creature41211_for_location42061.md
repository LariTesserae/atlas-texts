From the rim there is almost nothing to pick out. A leaf lifts at one edge and shows red, and it could be wind. A line of bubbles crosses a gap, and it is most likely a grebe. In the long dusk, among the frogs, there are sometimes other eyes in the roof, set wider apart, that stay open when the frogs' eyes close. That is as much of itself as the town shows the air.

Underneath, they are people. There are about forty of them, and there have been about forty for as far back as the pale domes on the floor can be counted.

They are short and deep-chested and carry their fat evenly, the way the char do in autumn. Their skin is pale and close-grained, rubbed with fish fat until it has the dull shine of the young stems. Their hair is cropped with glass to a finger's width. Their fingertips are ridged for life. They see sharply in water, with pupils pinched small. In air they squint and things swim, so inside the bells they know each other by voice, by touch, and by faces lit green from the water below. The old ones' front teeth are worn nearly to the gum from fibre.

They move slowly, and at first it looks like calm. It is arithmetic. Every stroke is paid for in breath, so nobody makes one they do not need. Distance is counted in breaths, and children are taught only halves: no place is farther than half of what you can hold, because the bell you are swimming toward may have a slack tap. The char do not startle for them. A stranger in the lanes would be known at once by the grunting.

Each wears a waist cord and on it a glass flake in a fibre sheath, a bone needle, a net bag, and a stone. The stone is for knocking and is the most personal thing they own, picked from the bed in childhood and worn since to the shape of one palm. Each has three names:

- A knot, tied on the bell.
- A knock, struck on the stem.
- A spoken name, short and usually given by other children. It is used only in air and is thought the least of the three.

Anything that matters is tied. A debt, a boundary and a betrothal are all cord, and can be read by any hand that passes. Voice is kept for what is not worth the twine: jokes, complaints, endearments, and long stories told a few sentences at a time. So they speak lightly and tie gravely. A spoken promise is taken as a kindness and a knotted one as owed.

They listen with their teeth. To hear what the stems are carrying, a person sets their jaw against one and holds still. The old claim that worn teeth hear best.

They laugh, though it costs them. Underwater a laugh is a burst of bubbles going up the lane. The net-makers under the roof see it rise past and know that someone below has lost a breath to a joke and is swimming hard for a bell.

A life here goes downward.

- **Under the roof:** Children are raised in the workrooms, in the warmth and the good light, by whoever is chewing fibre that day. They herd the snails. They mind the split char laid out to dry on top of the leaves, drumming on a leaf from beneath when a heron lands. Each is given a shoot, the one that came up nearest their birth, to bend out of the lane and tie. Age is counted in its joints from then on. After five or six years the stem has forgotten and the cord comes off, and the child keeps it as a first belt. By then the child has usually stopped asking what is over the rim.
- **In mid-water:** A sleeping bell marks an adult, and nobody gets one until one comes free. Some wait years past their growth, sleeping among the nets, and are patient about it, mostly. There is an account of one who did not wait. It is tied on a long cord in the floor quarter, and it is never told aloud.
- **On the floor:** When the old are ready, they add stones to their own bells and let them down among the roots. There they keep the stores. They sleep more each winter and grow as pale as the marl. Their taps are moved up a last time and then not again, and the plant closes the wound. The bell stays. Nobody calls the quarter a graveyard, and food is fetched from it without unease.

They sleep alone because the thread of air in a bell will keep one sleeper and no more. Two can share for a while, and everyone knows how long. The air thickens, the head begins to ache, and one of them has to go. Courtship is therefore a matter of short visits and long swims. Nobody finds this sad. It is thought improper to stay until the headache.

Their food is char, raw or dried or soured in the pits. They eat the plant's seed, taken only from pods that have opened themselves. They hang cords for the water-boatmen to lay on and scrape off the eggs as a grey paste, which they like better than anything.

They do not ask how the plant breathes in winter, or where the glass comes from, or what the fall does above the headwall. Their knowledge is all of how long, and none of it is of why. In each generation one of them, never chosen, simply the one who looks up longest in the weeks of open sky, carries coils of rope to the notch at night. That one leaves them on the smooth rock and some days later finds black flakes there. That one has seen the stream below and reports that nothing is in it.

The black water under the fall is the only place with no cord in it and nothing owed. They go there one at a time. They come up into the spray and the roar with their faces to the sky and stay a while. What anyone does there is not asked. Most of them come back hoarse.

In winter they slow with the plant. The bead of air sets the pace, and they sleep through most of the dark with their pulses low, waking to eat and to listen. One of them is always awake. That one swims to a stem and strikes it with the palm-worn stone: two knocks, a pause, three. It is no message, and it means only that someone is up. All through the lake, in bells a long swim from each other, sleepers stir when the ice booms, set their teeth to the stem beside their heads, hear it, and go back under.
