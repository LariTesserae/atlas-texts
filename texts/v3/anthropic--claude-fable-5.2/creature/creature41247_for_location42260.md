The simple answer is termites, and it is correct as far as it goes.

Break the tip off a young spire and they are at the breach within seconds. The workers come first, soft and pale as cooked rice, with no eyes and skin so thin that an hour of dry air would kill them. Each carries a pellet of wet clay in its jaws and presses it to the broken edge. The soldiers come behind them, darker, with amber heads as long as their bodies and jaws so overgrown they cannot feed themselves. A worker has to place food in a soldier's mouth. In return the soldier stands in gaps and bites, and beats its head against the wall when something is wrong.

But none of these lives here in the sense the question means. A worker lasts a few months and does not know the tableland exists. It knows wet from dry, its own clay from a neighbour's, the pull of a scent trail, and one particular smell that drifts through every gallery from a cell near the mound's base and tells it that things are in order.

The smell comes from the queen. Her chamber is hard clay, low-ceilinged, the size of a small loaf, with doorways far too narrow for her to leave by. The front of her is still a termite, with a small dark head and six legs that no longer reach the floor. The rest is abdomen, white and tight and as long as a finger, crossed by brown plates that once covered it and now sit far apart, like buttons on a shirt that will not close. It ripples without stopping. Every few seconds an egg appears and is carried away. Workers lick her, feed her and clean her. Somewhere under the slope of her side is the king, who is the size he always was.

She is the only one in the mound with a past. She had eyes once, and wings. On one wet night she came out through a slit in some other spire and for a few minutes saw whatever there was to see, a few green dashes over the pans and the shine of water. Then she came down and went under. Every termite in the mound descends from that night. Yet she decides nothing. She does not choose where a gallery goes or when the slits are cut. She is the place where the colony keeps its continuity, and she is kept there.

Above her are the combs, and on them the fungus. The workers cannot digest the grass they cut. They swallow it, pass it nearly unchanged, and build the paste into grey, folded masses like walnut meat. The fungus threads through these and puts out white nodules the size of pinheads, which the termites eat. The chimney, the porous skin, the cool flutes and the warmth under the hand are all the climate this one organism requires, held steady through every season. It grows nowhere on earth except inside these mounds. Every colony on the plateau farms the same stock, renewed each year from the caps that push up after the flight. Queens die and spires go vacant, but the fungus has not died since the first dome was raised. It is hard to say which party is the tenant.

Then there is everyone else, and they are all lodgers.

The long-clawed one comes up the lanes after midnight, alone. It is the size of a pig and built like nothing else, with a humped back, upright ears like a hare's, a tail as thick as a thigh at the root, and a blunt tube of a snout that swings a finger's width above the ground. When it settles on a mound it sits back on its haunches and digs with foreclaws as flat and heavy as spoons, its ears folded shut and its nostrils closed with bristle. Its tongue is as long as a forearm. It feeds for a few minutes and leaves before the soldiers can mass. It never empties a colony. It is the only animal here that lives by itself, and even it cannot manage to be private. It digs a new sleeping hole whenever it likes and abandons the old one, and so houses half the plateau by neglect.

The mongooses sleep in its holes in a single heap and move house every few days, carrying the young by the scruff. The porcupines back in tail-first, quills rattling. The pigs lie in a row, all facing the entrance.

In the thickets each weaver flask is the work of one male, a few hundred strips of grass torn green and knotted with the beak. He hangs upside down beneath it, fluttering, until a female comes to test the weave. If she refuses it he cuts it down and starts again from the same twig. The ground under every colony tree is littered with rejected houses.

The spiders in the silk tents are each no bigger than a lentil. They hunt by feeling the web shake and running at the tremor together. None ever leaves. The young are fed by their mothers and aunts, and then they eat them, and the tent passes to the next generation with the husks still in the walls.

The frogs are the size of a thumbnail. Each male clings to a grass stem above the water, his throat blown out into a bubble as big as himself, and times his note to fall in the gap left by the frog beside him. That is why no two pans keep the same beat. The hyrax in the fig is stout and tailless, something like a marmot and related to none. It calls from the same branch each night and knows each of its neighbours by the creak.

Lay these side by side and they look alike. Almost nothing on the tableland lives in the singular, and almost nothing lives outdoors. Each resident has made an inside of clay, grass, paper, silk or mud, or has moved into an inside something else made and left. The ones with no walls have the chorus. A frog calls so as to stand inside a thing made of other frogs. The hyrax's shriek goes out to the rim and comes back the way a hand goes out to find the wall of a dark room. Even the fireflies are answering one another. The cloud is a lid, and under it everything is at home in something made.

So the inhabitant of this place is always a household. The word for whoever lives here is *we*, said by a great many different mouths, most of them blind.

Only once does that narrow to something that can be pointed at. It happens on the night of the rain, after the roar of wings, when the frogs are too full to close their mouths and the ants are hauling off the fallen. Somewhere on the pale flour of a pan, among the shed wings, two termites are running. They are dark, hard-bodied and newly wingless, the stumps still showing at their shoulders. She goes first. He follows so closely that his antennae never leave the tip of her abdomen, and if he loses touch she stops and waits until she feels him again. They are the only things on the plateau tonight that are out in open air, on open ground, with nothing built around them, and they do not stay there long. At the foot of a slumped dome, where the old clay has gone soft, she begins to dig and he digs beside her. When the hole is as deep as they are long, they turn around and wall it shut from within, a mouthful at a time.

It is a cell the size of a pea in total darkness, with two animals in it who have seen the world for a few minutes and will not see it again. She will raise the first brood without eating, feeding them from the dissolving muscles that flew her here. This is the smallest household on the tableland, and the only time anything here is as few as two.

Everything else here, the spires and thickets, the weavers and bats, the din, follows from this cell.
