Count the living first, since it does not take long. A dozen threads have come up from the hessian, most likely oats from a feed sack that was cut up for curing blankets. A film of green has never ventured more than a hand's breadth from the bulb. Both are lodgers on the temporary works. The lamp hangs on a trailing lead and will go back up the ladder when the permanent lights are wired. The slab under the blankets will finish curing in a week and stop giving off its heat. The threads have spent everything that was in the seed and made no leaf. They are passing through, on warmth that is already listed for removal.

What lives here looks at first like frost, and the room encourages the mistake. The frost is only what it wears. The white is borrowed from above: fitters' breath, kettle steam and wet weather, carried down the hatch and laid on whatever is cold enough to hold it. On a dry day with the hatch shut, the band on the bare wall would still be there and would still take skin. There would only be nothing to see it by.

So it is tempting to call it a shape, and it has one. It is the shape of everything on the drawings that has not yet come down the ladder: the four-inch line, the pump, the headers that will one day bolt to the blank plates. Its edges are true to within the width of a chalk line, and where they were not, it was the chalk that moved. It is already at working pressure. The gauges on the blanked branches read it. Sometimes the knock in the main is followed by a second, smaller knock from beyond the plates, the way a sound travels on into the part of a system you cannot see.

But a shape would wait, and this does not behave like something waiting. It behaves like something early. Nothing in the room is old enough to have left a remnant. The concrete has not finished becoming stone, and nothing has happened here that could linger. Whatever this is, it comes from the other direction, and the thaw shows how far ahead it lies. When the mains chill again, the band on the wall whitens first. That pipe is cut and threaded and lying on trestles in the yard. The rectangle on the plinth follows a minute or two later. The pump is on a wagon somewhere. After those come fainter lines, in places where the fitters have not chalked anything yet.

The fitters do not have a name for it. They speak the way gardeners do: see what's come up. They leave the hatch open at the end of a shift so there will be damp enough to read by morning. When they offer a length of pipe up to its band, the iron goes white before the hangers are tight. After that it is only a pipe, and the thing is one run shorter. The tea can was set down for the length of a cigarette on a piece of floor already spoken for. There was no malice in it. The can was standing in something's place.

Every run they hang is a run of it gone into iron, and when the laggers follow with cork and canvas even the iron stops showing. On that account it is a creature with the lifespan of a building programme. It is at its fullest now, with half the mains still white, and it will be finished on the day the last blank plate comes off.

That account would do if every sign of it were cold.

There are two that are not. They show late in each cycle, when the frost has had hours to grow and everything bare is furred. One of the valve wheels sits on a main that no one has yet had cause to turn. The white takes its hub and spokes and leaves the rim black in two arcs, each a hand wide, on opposite sides. Beside the panel, at shoulder height, a patch of wall has dried pale ahead of the dark concrete around it. It is about where a man would lean to read the gauges, and it is a degree warmer than it has any reason to be. The dust on the panel is still unmarked. No one has chalked around either place.

So the room lacks more than iron, and all its lacks have the same habit. What lives here is the finished room, lodging whole inside the unfinished one. It is the pipes, the pump and the keeper who will come down the ladder every watch, put both hands to that wheel and rest a shoulder on that wall. The cold parts of it are close, a matter of weeks, and they come back sharp. The warm part is further off and has no drawing. It can only be seen as a place where the frost declines to settle.

Someone has laid a glove over one of the arcs on the wheel. Nobody says who, or whether it was meant to cover the place or to keep it. The other arc stays bare. After each thaw it is the last thing in the room to return. The white closes in from the spokes on either side and stops.
