A walker coming in from the intake can go a long way and meet only heels and palms. This is how the people here are mostly known, even to each other. One heel turns out. One left hand is short the tip of its smallest finger, and the mark repeats along a levee shoulder for the length of nine pans. Someone slaps twice where everyone else slaps once. The makers read these as easily as faces, and far more often. They can tell from the depth of a heel who was tired, and from the place where a hand stops, who has left.

The makers themselves are at the outer margin, and up close they are the color of what they make. Each morning a maker draws a slip of wet clay over shoulders, back, and scalp against the sun. By noon it has crazed like the windward margin of a pan, and the skin under it is tacky with salt. This is why the shimmer hides them so easily. What shows is whatever is wet: dark forearms, dark shins, the dark heap just dropped.

Each carries one thing. Clay has to be cut, and there is nothing hard here to cut it with, so they use a cord of hair, twisted on the thigh from combings, about as long as both arms spread. Drawn through a wet bank it leaves a face as flat as a wall, which is why the borrow pits are square. The cord has the maker's own hair in it and usually some from whoever taught them. Salt stiffens it, and it is remade often.

Everything else is done with the body, and the body is the measure. A levee is knee-high because that is as high as a tired leg will tread clay firm. The main ones stop at the chest because an armload cannot be thrown higher. The top is a pace wide. A plug is the size of a head because that is what two hands hold. Only the pans are not sized to them. A pan is as large as the drying needs, and the makers spend their days building things their own size around the edges of something that isn't.

They find the tilt with water. A maker scoring a new outline lets a palmful of seep into the line, watches which way it creeps, and corrects the ridge with the heel of a hand. No one has seen the whole slope. Each takes it on trust from the pan before.

They work from first light until the levee tops are too hot, then go down into the borrow pits and wait out noon standing to the knee in seep, each in the hole they cut. They sleep lying along the levee tops, and the dew caps them as it caps the clay. Food comes out from the tide end along the main levees, mostly fish split and dried, carried by whoever is walking out to join the edge. Nothing is carried back.

A levee is a pace wide with a forbidden floor on either side, so two people who meet on one cannot step around each other. They take hold of each other's forearms and turn. Among people who otherwise keep a few hundred paces apart, every meeting is a touch, and they do it slowly. They speak low and briefly. Across a pan they don't call at all; they lift an arm.

Behind the edge live the keepers, who were makers. The rule about plugs turns one into the other. A maker can lay pans dry for seasons and keep moving outward, but the first plug pulled has to be pressed back by the same hand before dark, and so does the next. After that the maker's country is fixed: as many notches as one person can reach and close between the afternoon heat and nightfall. The edge goes on with other hands. The keeper stays, wetting the plugs so they don't shrink and drop from their notches, pulling at dawn, pressing at dusk, and spending the hours between under a coat of clay, listening to water run. At any distance a keeper is a bump on a brown thread.

Keepers are neighbors in one direction only. Water goes one way, and so does everything a keeper knows about the next keeper. From upstream comes brine: how much, how strong, how late in the morning. From this a keeper can tell whether the person above is careful, or slow, or unwell, without ever having seen them. Nothing can be sent back.

You can tell how far in a keeper lives by the hands. Near the intake they are only wet. Farther on they are stained the color of tea, then rust-pink to the wrist, then grey in the creases of the knuckles where gypsum has set. The oldest keepers are nearest the tide, where the works began, and they have the glasswort. It is the only thing on the floor that nobody put there. They step over it, and eat the tips, pinching them off without pulling, and they have never settled whether the rules apply to it.

Rain is how the keepers meet. When a cloud breaks over three or four pans, the keepers from the dry pans around walk in along the levees, lie down full length on the tops, and put their lips to the surface. A practiced drinker takes the clear water off the top without drawing up the pink. The pan's own keeper does not drain the rain away. The brine is set back a week, and in exchange there is company. It is the only time more than two of them are in one place, and they all leave in time to press their plugs.

They are, for now, the only animals here. The birds go over, and a keeper looks up at them the way one looks at weather.

At the center, under the afternoon cloud, is one keeper whose hands are white. Her pans are the last, and the salt has started in them. She is the only person in the works who has seen what it is for.

She cannot yet pick it up. The rule about floors holds for her as for everyone, so what she can touch of the first crop is the rime along the levee foot, a border one arm wide around a rectangle whose far side is a thread. The rest is rafts. In the afternoon the breeze brings them across to the lee levee, and she kneels there and slides a finger under one. Her skin, like everyone's, is never dry. The raft takes the damp from her fingertip and goes under. She has tried the back of the hand, and a hand held up in the sun first. Now she mostly watches them come in.

She can't tell anyone either. News travels the way water does, and she is at the bottom of it. The makers are laying outlines with their backs to her, many days away. If she walked out to them, her plugs would dry and fall before she arrived. So the knowledge stays in the middle, and the edge keeps moving away from it.

Tonight, somewhere between her and the edge, a notch is open. Some hand pulled the plug at dawn and has not come back to it. The keeper may be asleep on a levee, or late coming home from rain, or gone. Nobody upstream can know, and nobody is near enough to hear it. The keeper below will find out at first light, from a pan a finger too full and a shade too pale. That keeper will leave their own plugs in that day. Then they will look up the tilt, which shows nothing, and start walking against it. It is the one errand here that runs against the water.
