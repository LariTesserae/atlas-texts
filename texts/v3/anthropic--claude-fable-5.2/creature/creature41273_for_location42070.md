What lives here is a keeper. On this stretch of the line she is called Umber.

The name means nothing to her. Keepers' names are all like that, old words handed down with the wrenches. Madder taught her. Woad keeps the ten houses up-line. There was a Sorrel once, and a Dun. Somebody long ago must have known what the words were for. On the pipe she has another name, two knocks and a drag of the wrench across a rivet row, and that is the one she answers to.

Nobody has ever seen her, and that includes her. She is short, and heavy in the calf and hip from a lifetime of lifting her knees out of the mat. Her skull is cropped to a nap and she is greased with tallow from scalp to heel, because anything kept this warm and this wet will rot unless it is sealed. So the first thing anyone learns of her is the smell: tallow, iron, and the sourness of burst bladder that gets into everything. Her feet are bare and wide, the toes spread like fingers, and they read dome and pan through the mat. She has eyes. They wash the grit out during the surge, and she shuts them when she wants to listen hard, a habit she has never thought to question.

Her hands don't match. She goes down-line on one side of the pipe and comes back on the other, so it is always the left on the iron. The left has gone to horn across the heel of the palm, and it can tell a weeping joint by its warmth a pace before reaching it. The right she carries tucked under her arm, out of the wet, and greases twice as often. The right is for the gauges, and the pins can't be read through a callus.

She carries a wrench, a packing hook, a coil of felt-rope boiled in tallow, a bag of raw tallow for the nesters, and a veil woven from combwood fringe. The felt-rope is the same stuff that would bind the flywheels if the tallow-eaters let it. Boiled and coiled, it packs a gland better than anything else in the country.

The veil is for the surge. When the tickers come at her face she drops it and breathes through the weave, and by the time the rain has gone over, it is clogged with wings, husk and the greasy dust of the lit side. She scrapes it with the edge of her hand and eats what comes off, or presses it into a cake and leaves it on a valve chest to steam while she works. It is dry and bitter, a taste nothing grown here has. She feeds the way the combwood does, standing in the wind with a fringe held up.

Her stretch is ten houses, and she does not think of it as a distance. It is a tune ten notes long that takes fifteen sleeps to walk through, each note swelling ahead of her while the last one thins behind. She has never heard more than three of them at once. She can whistle all ten in order.

Midway between houses are the thin places, where only a few strokes in a hundred get through the hooting of the stalks. There she walks bent, with her cheek near the iron, taking the beat through the water in the pipe.

In her collar she keeps a scrap of pelt the size of her palm, which Madder gave her and someone gave Madder. Pressed over an ear it doesn't muffle. It takes the ear away. She uses it to shut out the engine behind when she needs the one ahead alone. Keepers say it is hush. No keeper has had her hands on a hush and come back to say so, so it may be anything.

She knows the houses by temper as well as pitch. The third drags on its upstroke and always has. The seventh runs a shade quick, and the combers around it are quick too, their clicks crowded up against the bleat. If the rot took both her ears she could still find the seventh house by the grounders, whose boom comes up through her soles a little sharp there.

She has been told what the pipe is for: cities, cold, a far rim. She holds that the way she holds her own name, as a word that came with the tools. What she knows for herself is what happens to the country when a house goes quiet. If anyone asked, she would say she keeps the notes. Moving the water is the engine's affair.

When a gland has to be repacked she works fast, and she whistles. Keepers learn young to whistle on the in-breath as well as the out, so the note never breaks. She can hold a house's pitch through a whole repacking, her right hand in the stuffing box and her left hauling out old rope on the hook. Outside, the nearest grounders stay put. The far ones don't. Her lips tire. By the time she throws the ball handle and the beam takes its first stroke, her note has sagged flat, though never by much. Every keeper's does. Madder's did.

When the hush comes through, she does what the combers do. Somewhere downwind the rasping stops, the tickers thin, and the racket gets a hole in it. She stands with her back to the pipe, which is as warm as she is, and breathes only on the engine's stroke, in on the lift and out on the fall, so that nothing of hers is in the air that the engine didn't put there first. The hole in the noise moves past her, upwind. Afterward one comber fewer takes up rasping.

For the other thing she has no practice, only the rule. It sits out in the mat at the thin places, a little way off, and hums the note of the house ahead. There is nothing pleading in the sound. It is just wrong, slightly and steadily, within thirty paces of a woman whose whole work is putting right what is slightly wrong. That is the pull, and she feels it in her jaw.

She has listened to it at every thin place on her stretch, for longer than she listened to Madder. It is always flat by the same amount, and she knows that amount. It is the sag of a keeper's whistle at the end of a repacking. Whatever is out there did not learn its note from an engine.

She hasn't told Woad. She walks the thin places with her left hand flat on the iron and her eyes shut, and she keeps her own mouth closed.
