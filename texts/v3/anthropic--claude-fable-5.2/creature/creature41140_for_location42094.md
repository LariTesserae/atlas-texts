Someone tends the engines. The grease says so, and the full oil can, and the coat.

The coat is the best evidence. It is heavy canvas, soft with oil at the shoulders and long enough to reach the knee. The sleeves have been cut off above the wrist so that nothing lies between the hands and what they find. The collar is stiff with salt. The lining is warm the way a garment is warm when it came off a back a minute ago, and it is warm like that in every house, at whatever hour the door is opened, however quietly.

The pockets hold moths, a wad of cotton waste, a short spanner with its jaws worn slick, and a length of the jointed plant from the hollows, bitten off at one end. The moths do not leave when the pocket is opened. They walk over the knuckles and stay.

From these a person can be put together: tall, by the hook; careful, by the grease; hungry for salt. The needles are bent to meet a thumb that comes at them from a little below shoulder height and slightly to the left.

Then the count goes wrong, as it does here. The grease on the slide bars carries finger-tracks, still slumping closed. A hand drawn along them finds five. Drawn again, it finds nine, laid in the width of five, none crossing another. The wooden grip of the regulator is worn into a single hollow, and the hollow fits a right hand, and a left, and a hand narrower than either.

Nobody is met. A shout at the door comes back from nothing. One can sit on the bench through a whole stroke, the intake, the pause and the thud, and no one comes in for the coat.

The keeper is found by doing the work. Lift the oil can to a cup and the can is already tilting. Nothing has taken hold of it from outside. There is another hand in the place where one's own hand is, neither over nor under it, with a steadier wrist and its own opinion about how much oil. Sometimes there are two others. The thumb goes to the needle and the reading is in it before it has pressed. The knees know the step down to the crosshead. Near two hundred and ninety, in the long pause, the lips are found to be moving, and the breath is held until the thud comes up through the floor.

It does not push. It behaves as the crabs behave with a boot: it lifts, lets the hand down, and closes over it.

So the keeper is not one person and not a crew, in the way that what lies under a flat palm is not one crab and not four. It is whoever has hands on the engine, in whatever number that turns out to be. It has been that long enough to have habits. It favours the left foot, because the right boot was opened on a blister-lip at some point and the foot has not forgotten. It wipes its palms twice down the coat front before touching a door. It bites the salt plant during the intake. By the third house a visitor is doing all of this.

Something has to hold it. Everything here that is not touched comes apart, and the film reaches only to the ankles. The moths do the rest. Their soft collision is constant on the face and the backs of the hands, exactly the parts the coat leaves bare. A face touched all over, all the time, stays a face. That is the use of the cut sleeves and of the moths in the pockets, where a hand put away is still held.

When the warmth passes overhead and the moths rise, the face is clear for a while, and for those hours the keeper is loose. That is when the rail runs long. Eleven hundred sleepers is what it comes to when several are walking in the room of one and each is keeping count. Under the moths again it is four hundred. The keeper does not seem troubled by this, or by the doors. It greases the second 3 as carefully as the first and rests its thumb on the same dent each time, as one touches a doorpost at home.

It keeps the lamps burning because a turned cheek can find a door by them from thirty paces. It picks the scorched leaves off the glass and leaves the vine alone otherwise, perhaps because the vine is the only other thing on the plain that goes on reaching for a lamp.

One other lives here, and nothing holds it. It is the space in the air with no moths in it. It is the size of a cart. It is warm on its near side, and on a slow walk around it, at arm's length, it is warm on its near side the whole way round. It is turned toward whoever is there, from every direction at once. The warmth is the lamps' kind, from something switched on that gives off nothing else.

The crabs go flat under it. A palm laid on them then finds one back in the room of one. The hands in one's hands go still and drop to five fingers. For as long as it stands near, a person is one person, of one weight, in one place, and it is not comfortable. It is like the dry rises, where the edges are too sharp and too recent. Then it moves on along the rail at its own pace, the moths close in against the face, and the others settle back into the wrists.

It has never touched anyone. It may not need to. It may be the one the plain is kept for, going house to house and checking by its own means. It may be the part of the keeper that is in nobody's hands. The keeper does not say. The keeper only starts counting again, a little late, and makes it up before the thud.

Past the last house the engines are not the work. The keeper goes out onto the crackling rock ahead of the film, ahead of the crabs, and stands. That is the whole task. The left foot goes on one sleeper and the right on the next, in the hollows, which fit, limp included. It stands for as long as it takes to be the first thing touching that ground, until the rock under the boots decides to be rock. It waits there in the coat, which is also on its hook, with a hand in a pocket full of moths, until the first crabs come over the toes and the warmer thread finds an ankle.
