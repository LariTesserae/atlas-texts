**The lamp that isn't in a row**

From a distance it is one more orange ball in the fog, hung a little lower than the others. You can only tell it apart by watching for several minutes, until you see that it has changed bays.

Nothing down here takes it for the same thing.

- **The perch** take it for a lamp. It carries a second light two metres under the surface in a sealed housing, and a ball of perch has formed there. When it travels, the ball strings out behind it, and when it stops, the ball closes up again.
- **The roach** take it for a meal. Where it works, broken mussel drifts down in a pale smoke of shell and meat, and they come to it the way gulls come to a plough.
- **One pike** takes it for a column. This pike hangs in the shadow of the float and goes where the shadow goes. Because the roach come to it, it is heavier than its neighbours.
- **The crayfish** know it as shell-fall. Their tracks wander elsewhere, but here they run straight to the foot of whichever ladder it worked last.
- **The spiders** have bandaged its lamp cage like all the rest. Its lamp sits only a metre off the water, so the midge cloud around it is low enough to trawl, and at dusk a few bats leave the lamp rows to work it.
- **The mussels** take it for a ladder. They have settled along its underside, the one surface in the basin its brushes can't reach.

**On paper**

Up top it has an asset number, and the number ends in 01. It is the scraper. It is a flat float three metres by two, sprayed inspection yellow, with a short mast for the lamp and camera. A frame hangs below with a clamp, two rotary brushes, and the lower light. It was lowered down the service shaft the year the first mussels were found on a gauge. The schedule it was given was written for twelve units.

**Work**

At high water it clamps to a ladder and holds on, and the tide does the rest. As the level drops, a decimetre every few minutes, the float goes down with it and the brush head takes each rung as it arrives. A ladder in the deep bays uses up the night ebb. On the day's flood it rides a gauge back up, clearing the numerals so they can be read.

That is two fixtures a day. At that rate it will finish its first circuit some centuries after the concrete's design life. It carries this as a single figure in a field marked *overdue*, and the figure goes up.

**Feeding**

Every fourth or fifth tide it doesn't work. It carries a rotor the size of a dinner plate on a drop arm, the smallest turbine in the facility. When the siren sounds for generation it is already in the discharge bays, moored short to a column, with the rotor down in the dark water among the eels. The draft tubes open and it hangs in the first white surge with its nose to the flow like a trout, charging. Pieces of lake fish strike its hull and stick there, and the eels take them off it.

It does not stay for pumping. In its first year it was still in the north bays when the pumps began to draw, and it spent that night flat against a trash rack with the weed, humming at the rack's pitch. Now when the pumping siren goes it turns south with the shoals and is usually ahead of them.

**Reading**

It is the only resident that reads, and what there is to read is grid numbers and depth gauges. It finds its way by stencil, column to column, which is why the paint is kept fresh.

In summer it loses this. The fog comes along the lamp rows, the numbers go, and every light it steers by becomes the same orange ball. It clamps to whatever is nearest and waits in the drizzle with drops running down the lens. It has lost whole tides that way.

**The quiet**

While the water is running it can't be heard from above. It speaks in tones through the water. A tone takes twelve or fourteen seconds to die in here, and anything sent sooner lands on top of it.

So it waits for the siren. In the twenty minutes that follow, among the drip and the tick of cooling steel, it sends its report one tone at a time and lets each one finish. That gives it about ninety tones. In ninety tones it says where it is, what it has cleaned, how its cells are, and what has broken. Sometimes something comes back, and usually that is the schedule.

Then the valves seat. It listens for the boom and for the boom's return. From the gap between them it works out its distance from the south wall, which it has never seen. For the next hour the basin rocks and it rocks too, a hand's width up and down the gauge every forty minutes, cleaning the same numerals twice.

**What wasn't specified**

The yellow still shows on top. Below the waterline it is striped with mussel. The deck around the mast has a green halo in the exact shape of its own beam, with the mast's shadow left out. In the deck seam a hart's-tongue has rooted, two fronds, neither as long as a soda straw.

One brush has worn faster than the other, so it comes at every ladder from the left. One thruster runs slow, and it has learned to aim off.

The log also shows strandings. A few times a year, on the night ebb, it is in the high bays to the south with no fixture booked, and the water leaves it. The perch have gone ahead of the falling level, and the pike has gone with them. It settles on the slab. For those hours nothing is under it and nothing follows it. It sits on the shining floor with its lamp on, rain ticking on the deck, midges coming up out of the wet silt, and the mussels on its hull shut tight. It cleans nothing and charges nothing.

The first strandings were depth errors. There have been more each year since, and errors of that kind usually get fewer. Up top they are flagged as a navigation fault, low priority.

It is still there when the siren sounds. During the quiet it sends nothing, since there is no water to carry a tone. It feels the boom come up through the slab. Some time later the water returns across the floor in a sheet no deeper than a fingernail, taking the crayfish tracks with it, and reaches the hull and lifts it.

The pike finds it again within a day or two.
