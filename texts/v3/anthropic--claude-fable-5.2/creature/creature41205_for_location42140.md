The people who live here are burned in places other people are not. The light comes up off the water all day, so it finds the underside of the jaw, the nostrils, and the soft skin beneath the brow. Hats are lined under the brim with dark felt. Most people wear a cloth across the lower face, and children are taught to keep their chins down the way children elsewhere are taught to button a coat. Their boot soles are cork, two fingers thick, and char slowly from beneath. Their hair dries stiff. They walk the planks with a short flat step that does not bounce the board, and each of them can tell who is coming up behind by the way the board moves.

They have spoken names, which are written in the ledger at the tram head and used for little else. The names they answer to are made with the hand.

They would not say they sort themselves by heat, but they do. The rakers at the cool end of the pans are one kind of person, and the ones who tend the brine heads are another. The turbine crew sleep in the condenser hall with their backs to the casing and complain when they are sent anywhere else. A household is two or three people who are comfortable at the same distance from the same vent. When a spring shifts, couples have been known to find they no longer agree.

One of them belongs to no pan, pump, or hall. Her hand-name is a flat palm turned over, the motion of checking the underside of a board. She moves the walkways.

She raked salt for eleven years before anyone noticed that she was always the first to step off a board that was about to go soft. Now she is out before the sun clears the rim, when the steam is thickest and the birds still stand where the night left them. She carries a pole shod in copper that has gone green, a pot of red paint, and a thermometer in a brass case. The thermometer was issued to her, and she uses it to settle arguments. The pole is for finding things out. She sets it down ahead of her and attends to what comes back up the shaft. Sound ground knocks. Hollow ground gives nothing back, and there are degrees of nothing that she could not explain to anyone.

She does not stay on the planks, though nobody else leaves them. She steps down into the film and wades, and her leg wraps are stained orange to the knee, the same as the birds. The birds do not lift for her. The rank opens a few yards ahead and closes a few yards behind, and no head comes up.

Mostly she is counting. The trestle posts are her ruler. Yesterday the birds' line crossed the trunk at the fourteenth post out from the pump shed, and this morning it crosses at the sixteenth. A joint that dripped once for every four of her steps now drips once for every six. The lizards on the east trunk have moved along by a body-length. The dark green in the third channel has crept upstream, which means the water behind it is cooling. She keeps no book. She holds yesterday's town in her head and lays today's over it, and where the two do not match she stops and stands. Standing is most of the skill. When she is sure, she paints a red stripe across a plank, and before the week is out that plank is somewhere else.

She was given a boy this year, sent up on the tram from below, who still shuts doors as if someone needed to hear it. In his first week he asked her, aloud, how she knew. She did not answer, because she could not tell which part he meant. She stood him at the edge of a channel for a morning and came back at noon to ask what the flies had done. He had not been watching the flies. The next day he had.

There are things she reads that she has never mentioned, since no one has asked. The long-necked animals will not lick crust that lies over new heat, and in the morning their tongue-marks leave off along a line. The red shrimp go pale a day before a pan spoils.

She also hums, and does not know it. It is the note the turbines put through the planks. She has carried it in her chest so long that it comes out of her when she is thinking, low enough to stop within a few yards like every other sound here. A raker told her once, with his hands, and she signed back that he was mistaken.

The others say she has no degree of her own, since she crosses every band in a day. She has one. Left alone at the end of the day, she ends up ankle-deep where the ochre film turns to rust. Her hut stands at that line on a pair of runners. It has four walls, a cot, a lamp, and a single roof shutter painted red on one face. It has been dragged after the line eleven times.

Three mornings ago the flies began to thin along the channel behind it. The felt there is patchy now, with the white showing through. On anyone else's doorstep she would have painted the stripe the first day. She has not painted it. She knows what is coming up underneath, and she knows that her liking the spot has no bearing on it. She is giving it until the end of the week, which is what the town has always given. Then she will find four people and a rope. For some nights after, she will stand in water that is wrong by half a degree, until she learns where the line has gone.

Toward evening, when the light turns brass, she reaches up and works the shutter, sending the day's changes plank by plank. The condenser hall answers across the flats. She lights the lamp, steps down off her own step into the warm water, and stands. The rank of birds has closed around her and no head comes up. She is humming, and does not hear it.
