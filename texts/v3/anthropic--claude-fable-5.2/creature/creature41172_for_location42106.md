Something keeps the court. The frost is held to a line, the moss to a height, and the water to a few handfuls, and a place kept that closely has a keeper.

The first guess is a person. A town this old ought to have someone whose task it is: an old woman with a shell scraper, up before the lamps are lit, cutting the moss back from the sills and putting her thumb through the ice. There is no such person. The liming is done in a day by whoever's turn has come round, and for the rest of the year nobody takes a tool into the court except to work a skin or a flint. Ask in the passages who sees to it and you get the look you would get for asking who sees to the tide.

The second guess is something large. The quiet fills the court to its corners, so the mind supplies an animal of that size, asleep under the moss with the green growing on its back. But the things that are kept are small. The hole in the ice is the size of a thumb. The basin holds a few handfuls. The ferns grow from joints too narrow for a finger, and the groove that brings the water down was, in the masons' word for it, pecked. A large animal does not keep house in these measures. Whatever lives here could stand on the rim of a cup.

The crow has been working on the question longer than anyone. It is the only animal that looks at the court instead of living in it. It leaves the voles alone, though their ridges move in plain view below it. It stands among the sheep and turns its head to use one eye and then the other, the way a crow will watch men go into a boat shed and wait for as many to come out. It is counting. It has been counting since the oldest people in the town were small, and it has not reached a number it trusts.

What it counts is the wrens. There are a dozen or more, and that is the most exact figure available. People have sat on the bench with a pebble in the lap for each bird and come away with twelve. The next person gets thirteen, and the same person the following day gets fourteen and then twelve again. Wrens are hard to count anywhere, since they go in behind one fern and come out from under another. But a patient person can number the wrens in any other wall on this coast. Patient people have tried here, and have stopped.

The wrens explain part of it. A wren builds with moss, and the cock builds more nests than he will ever use, so a dozen of them stripping the sills each spring would hold the moss down. They explain the footprint by the limpet shell. They don't explain frost that stops at a row of pegs, or ice that leaves one hole. For that, one of the wrens has to be doing work the others aren't.

Nobody can say which. It is brown and barred, tail up, and it works the joints like the rest. It is seen every day by everyone who comes in. But everyone who comes in stands blind at the threshold for a few breaths, with a hand up against the light, and whatever was going on in the court when they arrived has that long to be a wren in a fern. The crow doesn't come in by a door and has never been dazzled. This may be why it stays.

It can be known a little by its habits:

- **Early:** Before the wall is lit, a wren goes along the antler pegs from one end to the other, stopping on each. A wren will do that anywhere there is a row of perches. Here, by the time the first person comes out of the passage, the frost stands exactly where the pegs are.

- **At the brightest hour:** When the wall begins to sweat, a wren stands on the ice of the basin, which bears it, since it weighs about what the limpet shell weighs. It drinks at the hole, and the hole is a wren wide. Whether the hole is there for the bird or because of it is not a question the town asks.

- **At dusk:** They all go into one joint in the east wall and pack in together for warmth, the way the snails do. Whoever is last on the bench can watch them go in and still not get the number, because by then the light has gone off the lime.

Then there is the quiet. A wren is the loudest thing for its size on this shore. One of them on a wall-end can be heard over surf. A dozen in a white stone box should ring like a struck pot. So the silence was strange about the wrens before it was strange about anything else, and the simplest account is that the one who lives here does not wish to be heard. It doesn't mind being seen, and it works in the strongest light in the town. But it has made some arrangement about sound, and an arrangement like that can't be made for one bird only. It takes in the other wrens, the doves' wings, the dropped pot, and the sea. Its reach is nine paces by six and a man's height deep. At the turf line, where the crow stands, you can hear the sheep tearing grass.

The same is probably true of time. A wren lives two winters, or three with luck. This one was here before the first lime went on, and staying as it is cannot be done for one bird alone either. So the ivy holds its thickness and the frost its line, and the basin neither fills nor fails. Whether these are the same doves in the niches is not something anyone can settle by looking. The crow, standing at the edge, has been caught up in it and may not know. People pass through too quickly to be held. They come out the age they would have been anyway, though those who sit longest on the bench say, once they are back in the passages, that the time doesn't seem to have come out of the day.

It also suggests how the court came to be. The builders left niches for the doves, and it is easy to think they left the court in the same spirit. They didn't choose to. The town was built wall to wall across the flat ground until it reached a place where a mason couldn't hear the man handing him stones, and there it went round.

Somebody knew all this, or enough of it. In one joint of the east wall, at the height a wren works, behind a fern where a shy thing can drink without being watched, somebody pressed a limpet shell into the wet lime, hollow side out. The basin is for people, who bring cups. The shell is a dish for something that doesn't. It holds one drop, a little green at the edges, as water gets when it has stood a long time. Whoever set it there left no other sign. There was no need to tell anyone, and in the court there would have been no way to.

There is a footprint in the moss beside the shell. Moss that deep doesn't keep a print. It takes a foot to the ankle and has closed again by the time the foot is lifted. This print is there every day, three toes forward and one back. So it is made every day, in the same spot, by something that stands to drink where it has always stood.
