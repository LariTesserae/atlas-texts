Break a brick from the top course of the wall; it is soft enough to break in the hands. In the grey there is a white grit, and where the lamplight catches a grain it turns pink, then green. It is mussel shell. The amphipods that work over the new bricks are after the last of the meat that went through the press with it. The grit is in every course, down to the rows where the words can be read, so whatever puts it there has been at it for longer than the wall takes to turn over once.

No mussels grow in the street. They grow in the intake tunnel, just inside the sluice, where the flow is strongest, and a mussel does not walk two hundred paces.

There are two more signs. In the forge shed, the face of the anvil is the only steel in town without rust on it, and in the lee of the anvil block lie a few half-shells the current missed, each cracked flat like a dropped saucer. And along the foot of the street's west wall, the cobbles are bare of silt in a track about a forearm wide.

What made them lives in the stone basement, in the old wheel pit under the turbine. It is as long as a man lying down, as thick as a thigh at the thickest, and flat. The head is the size and shape of a coal shovel. The skin is the colour of the wet brick, blotched darker, and too big for the body; it hangs along each flank in a loose frill from armpit to hip. The eyes sit on top of the head, low domes no larger than the rivet heads and about as useful. They can tell lamp from no lamp. The stain of daylight that comes down onto the chimney in high summer is too faint for them, and it has never known of it. There are four blunt fingers on each hand. The mouth runs the full width of the head and holds several hundred small teeth, good for gripping and no good for breaking anything.

It is some kind of salamander, grown past the size a salamander is supposed to reach by being left alone in cold water for a very long time. It was under the mill wheel when the wheel was wood and the stream ran in daylight. The brick went up over it, and then the steel. Then the lake came up, and that nearly finished it, because it breathes through the frill and cannot move water over itself. The water has to come to it. It is alive because the sluice rusted at the width it did. Twice a day the flow crosses the wheel pit and its flanks, and that has to last until the next one.

For this animal the town is not quiet. A line of pits runs along its jaw and down each side, and through them it gets everything the steel carries. The shaft starting is a long hum low in the belly. The press is a slow lean that goes on for most of an hour and then lets go. Each conveyor link is a tick, and one link in forty is a double tick where a pin has worn. The ice settling is a hand cupped over the whole body. It rests with its jaw against the first bearing block, where the wood turns without sound and it can feel every turn. It knows each machine and has no idea what any of them is for.

When the hum starts, it climbs out of the pit, takes a mussel off the tunnel wall, and carries it down the street in its mouth. It has one route and keeps to it: under the lowest pipe, around the foot of the hopper, and through the gap beside the forge on its edge, like a plate going into a rack. The burbot under the press bed gives it a body length as it passes, as the burbots before this one did.

In the forge it waits for the hammer to rise, sets the mussel on the anvil, backs off, and lays its chin on the floor. The hammer tips and comes down. The floor shifts, and inside that large shift there is a small one, the shell letting go. When the hammer lifts, it eats, and the current takes the shell toward the grate. It gets three mussels in a flow, or four if the flow is long.

Sometimes something comes down the tunnel and circles the lamps. The salamander holds still then and passes for fieldstone. Most visitors leave before slack water. Its mouth is wide enough for the ones that don't.

Mussels are not the only thing it has put on the anvil. A rust icicle came out as an orange cloud. A rivet came out a rivet. A moss ball came out as a disc the thickness of a thumb, and it carried the disc into the street and set it down among the others. When the flow came the disc could not roll. It tipped onto its edge, wobbled, and fell over. It took a season of flows to round it again. So among the drifts there are always a few lozenges, and it knows which ones they are. It finds them by feel, turns each over in its fingers, and puts it back where it was.

When the flow slackens it goes home north along the foot of the wall, on the bare track. Its tail is a third of its length and flat as an oar, and it sweeps from side to side as it walks, so that the moss balls lying against the wall roll ahead of it, a few paces at a time. It does not notice this. It has noticed that a disc left by the forge will turn up, weeks later, in a doorway at the north end. Some slack waters it lies along the wall foot with its whole flank attentive, waiting to feel the current that carries them back. The water there has always been still.
