Start with the crawler, because it is the only inhabitant that can be picked up.

It is oval and flat as a lentil, and it comes off the stone with a small reluctance, the way a damp label comes off glass. The back is a plate of sinter or iron varnish, whichever it was sitting on, built up at the leading edge and shed in flakes at the rear so that it never thickens. Underneath are a pale foot and a rasp. It has no eyes, and nothing that could be called a head except the end that goes first. Turned over, it does not curl or struggle. The foot goes on working at the air. Set down crosswise to the magnetite, it turns, taking a quarter of an hour over it, until it lies along the line again and faces the way the others face.

There are no small ones. Every crawler in the basin is the size of a thumbnail. There are no eggs under the stones, no pairs, and no empty shells. An animal that is never young and leaves no dead is hard to keep calling an animal. And when one is lifted, fine dark threads draw out from the cracks between the pavement stones, stretch, and part. That was the reluctance. The crawler was attached.

So look at the films. They seem the least of it, a coat of paint, orange on the side of a pool where the iron is rusting and grey-green on the side toward cooler water. Their edges are as crisp as the waterlines. They do not creep. Where a crawler has passed there is a clean stripe one thumbnail wide, and behind it the film closes at the pace the crawler opens it. The grazing is trimming. The film is kept at one coat.

The film is only the part that sits in the light. A pavement stone levered up (it takes a bar, the iron cement holds) is felted underneath with the same threads. They are dark, finer than hair, and have the dull shine of pencil lead. They lie parallel, and they run the way the magnetite runs. Each is a chain of cells set end to end in a single sheath, and the sheath conducts. In the joints they hang in the brine in skeins, combed downstream, and some of the depth of that blue-green is thread. They pass under the copper pans. They go up into the crust of every vent throat and through the foot of every terrace. In the end they all follow the fall of the floor to the sump. A weight lowered on a line into the tea-coloured brine comes up wound in black, heavy, as if dragged through wet wool. The bulk of it is down there under the smoke, lying against the wet end of the ore. Everything out on the flats is lead wire.

It is one thing, twenty kilometres across, and almost everywhere thin enough to scrape through with a fingernail.

It lives on the cell. Between the rusted ground above and the brine below there is a small, steady difference, and the thread stands across it and takes a toll on what passes. That is all its food, a fraction of a volt drawn without pause. It stores nothing and has no lean season to store against. Each cell lasts some days and is replaced by the division of its neighbour, the sheath unbroken, the way a cobble works loose from the bank into the pan. It holds as many cells as it ever held.

What it does with that living is the larger matter. A battery left to itself does not stay as this one stays. The ore armours itself with its own products. Paths clog, vent throats narrow under their crust, intervals lengthen, terraces advance, and pools drop and leave bands. None of that has happened here, and the thread is at every place where it would. Where sinter is laid down at the lip, the thread at the foot takes the same weight back into solution. The crust in a vent's throat is thickened by each stroke and thinned in each pause, so the bore is the same and the beat is the same. It is hard to say which came first, whether the basin found its balance and the thread moved into a place that suited it, or the thread is the balance. A before would have left a second waterline, and there is one.

It has one sense. It feels difference in potential, and it feels it everywhere at once. The prickle in a submerged hand is the hand being read. Salt and wet, a hand is an easier path than the water around it, and the current leans toward it. At the pool's edge the lines of magnetite swing a degree. When the hand is withdrawn they swing back.

The low tone is the grains themselves, which becomes plain with an ear close to the pavement. The thread passes its current in pulses, at one rate, through its whole extent together, and at each pulse every black grain tips and recovers. That makes twenty kilometres of grit shivering in step. The tone is the nearest thing it has to a heartbeat, and also the nearest thing to a memory. It holds one figure, and everything it does is a return to that figure.

To such a thing a walker is a discrepancy, though not a threat. A body at the temperature of the air adds nothing there. But boots skid the film, scuff the combed lines, and leave sweat and crumbs. A walker returning along the same track a day later finds the lines already re-laid across the heel marks. An outward morning turned up three or four crawlers, but the track back has one at nearly every scuff. Their crusts are still thin and pale as a fingernail. They work the torn margins of the film at their quarter-hour pace, each facing the usual way. They were not hatched; they were made at full size for the job. When it is done they stop. The foot withdraws into the cracks, and the plate stays where it is, one more stone shouldered into the pavement. Some part of that floor has been crawler.

A bird that came down would be a larger entry, though the cost would only be time. Nothing would happen to it that it would notice in a day. But the shore of the sump has no feather on it and no bone, and it cannot be that in all this time nothing has ever fallen. Whatever arrives is taken apart at the rate it arrives. The birds cross high and straight. Perhaps the sense that tells them north tells them something about the floor below, where every line is already drawn one way and none of it is drawn for them.

One account does not close. The heat comes up from underneath and costs nothing. The ore is another matter. Every pulse rusts a little more of the top and takes a little from the root. The basin's charge is weak because the thread lets through only what it needs. It has the cell throttled to the lowest draw that will carry twenty kilometres of housekeeping. That is thrift, the only kind open to it. At the sump, where it lies thickest, it lies against its capital, and it has no way of adding to it. It can hold everything else in the basin to the mark. The ore it can only spend slowly.

It gives no sign of this. A palm laid flat on the warm pavement at the shore feels the tone come up through the grains. Far off, the vents work through their order. When the sequence comes round to the nearest, the thread meets the stroke with the same pulse and the same figure, on the same beat, and draws the next from a root that is a little shorter.
