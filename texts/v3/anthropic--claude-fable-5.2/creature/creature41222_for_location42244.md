Nobody, to begin with. No coat hangs beside the mugs, nothing circles the lamps, and the dust upstairs is unmarked. What lives here is green and does not move, and all of it together would fit in a few buckets.

There are four kinds, and each has its own address.

The algae belong to the lamps. Every working bulb keeps one colony, a felt disc exactly as wide as its light. No colony touches another, because between any two lamps lies a stretch of dark that nothing can cross. Each disc carries the shadow of its bulb's cage, faint spokes where the wire has stood between filament and wall for years and the felt has grown thinner. The algae have never had a day. They have the rain, when the turbine's note lifts and the amber goes nearly yellow, and for eleven minutes they are wet and well lit at once. That is their noon. The rest of the time is a long dusk, and they wait it out.

The liverwort belongs to the faults. It lies flat and dark on every weeping joint, across the lagging, the banding wire and the brick beneath, and it stops where the seep stops. It is the one thing in the town that does well out of the decline, since every gasket that gives adds to its territory. Nobody reads the enamel tags now, but a list of everything that needs attention on Boiler Row could be copied straight off the liverwort.

The moss has the least. It is one thread under each downpipe bracket, the width of a bootlace, and it goes no further than the drip allows.

The ferns are the ones with posture. They root in the gutter silt, which is rock flakes, mineral scale and their own dead fronds, dried to paper and washed down by the rain. They lean from the eaves over the street, each toward its nearest bulb, pale enough to read a valve number through. They are the only things here that show a change of mind. Where a lamp has failed, the fern beside it still holds its older fronds toward the dead cage, while the newer ones have swung round to a bulb two landings off, dimmer and further away. A plant like that keeps both directions at once, and it records the failure as exactly as the chalk beside the jacks.

None of them is eaten, so nothing here is shaped by anything but light, water and heat. No edge is ragged, and a frond lasts until it dries. Whatever takes the dead ones apart in the silt is too small to leave a track.

The green does not keep the place going. The place is kept by the routine. Heat lifts the water, the damper swings on its thermostat, the rain comes down, the turbine takes it and the lamps come up. The relief valve breathes. The lift arrives on its timer. The routine has a pulse and regular habits, and it would be easy to call it the resident. But it cannot mend itself. It has lost a third of its lamps and replaced none, and each grey disc marks a tenant it failed. It is a set of instructions still being carried out, a shift that nobody came to relieve. The green lives in it the way the ferns live in the gutters, in something built for another purpose that happens to hold water.

The slowest tenant is not green at all. The mineral follows the water just as the plants do, and it needs no lamp. It is in the straws on the roof, each with its bead, in the crust that closes over a dead disc, and in the white lines on the brick, a hair higher after every rain. The chalk columns stopped, but this record is kept up to date. It will also outlast the others. Every bulb that goes takes a colony with it and turns a fern. When the last one goes the green goes too, and the rain will carry on without it for as long as the damper swings, and the white will keep climbing the brick. The green is the middle tenancy, after the people and before the stone.

The green got here by the lift. The gate opens on the empty landing and waits, and something does get off: a cageful of outside air, cooler than the street, and now and then a spore in it. Everything living here arrived that way, or on a boot before that. Once down, the spores go up with the vapour and come back with the rain, so the weather sows them everywhere, on plate and grating and hot rock alike. They take only where a drip and a lamp coincide.

One took at the far end, in the single course of brick under the last lamp on the line. There is no roof there, the rain falls straight through, and the rock is hotter than anywhere else in the town. The fern has three fronds, small and nearly clear, and at its centre a crozier wound as tight as a clock spring. The drill steel is still in its hole. The uprights hold up nothing. The fern is the only thing at the cut face still going forward, and the only thing in the town still under construction.

Overhead the layer has thickened and begun its slow roll. When it lets go, the lamp will yellow and the crozier will loosen by a little.
