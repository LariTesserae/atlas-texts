It is easiest to begin with the fish, because there are most of them.

One of them is the length of a hand, grey along the back and a paler grey beneath, with no bars or spots. It hangs a body-length off an intake slit with its nose in the flow and its mouth a little open, and it takes the fine pale fleck that grows in the sweet water and never grows thick enough to cloud it. Half a second before the stroke it goes sideways onto the bark between two slits. Half a second after the flaps clap it is back. Nothing warns it. The trunk gives no sign and the fish is not listening for one. It has the interval in its body, and it had it before it hatched, since the eggs are glued into creases in the bark and the thud goes through them from the first hour.

A fish swept clear of its trunk into open water goes on dodging every eleven seconds, away from nothing. If the flow brings it at last to another trunk, it arrives keeping the old beat, which the new trunk does not keep, and it is taken on the first stroke or the second. So the shoals do not mix. Every trunk has its own fish, and what divides one shoal from the next is timing, since the water between could be swum in a day. In a sea where no part is allowed to differ from any other, the fish at one trunk differ from the fish at the next only by when.

Each of them dodges nearly eight thousand strokes a day, close to three million a year, for four or five years. Then one day it is slow. Old age here is a quarter of a second, and it happens once.

The snails have settled the matter another way. They are horn-coloured and almost flat, like limpets that have been planed, and they live on the valve lips themselves. When the flaps come together, the seal closes over them. The shell is exactly as high as the gap the valve leaves, because any snail that grew a taller one was cracked and went up the bore. Between claps they rasp at the film on the lip, and at the clap they clamp down. They do not need to know when the stroke is coming, since they are already underneath it.

The shrimp are pale enough to see through, with the gut showing as a dark thread. They stand in the floor channels in single file, facing into the return flow, and walk. They walk at the speed of the water, so every leg in the file is moving and the file stays where it is, a few hundred metres of it, on wood their feet have helped to polish. Whatever the flow is carrying to the trunk comes down the line first, and each shrimp takes a pinch as it goes by. A dead fish that starts whole at the far end reaches the slits as a spine. A shrimp that stops walking is carried back past the others, who do not break step, and arrives soon after.

The eels do almost nothing. They lie along the rhizomes with their bellies pressed to the black skin where the prickle is strongest, and they can lie for days. Now and then one leans out over the nearest channel and takes a shrimp from the file, and the file closes up. When something touches an eel, another eel settling in beside it or a shrimp out of line, there is a tick of light on its flank, yellowish, the size of a fingernail. It is the only light in the sea that the plant did not make and the cloud did not let through.

Then there are the birds, which are warm. Everything else here is six degrees right through, the same as the water and the wood, but a bird is forty at the core. It is slate-dark and heavy and sits low in the water, with the fine rain beading on its back and the down underneath dry. It does not build, having nothing to build with. The egg rests on the bare leaf where two ribs meet and make a shallow cup, in the lee of the stack, and the bird stands over it and holds it on its feet under a fold of belly.

To feed, it goes down the trunk to the girdle of slits, takes hold of the bark between two of them, and waits. Every eleven seconds the fish come to it. It takes one, sometimes two. It does not chase them. Nothing in this sea chases anything.

There is no night, so the birds sleep when they are tired, each one separately, and at any hour the same share of them is asleep with the bill tucked back and the rain coming down. A bird coming up out of the water stops at the pad's edge and lifts a wing, and the white shows and shuts. The one on the egg shows white twice, and they change places. The chicks are in grey down with the patch already white, and they flash it before they can stand, at the parent, at the other chicks, at the stack.

That is the list, and the list is not quite the answer. The plant finished growing long ago. It does not flower or seed or lay down wood faster than the wood wears. But the pads go on drinking light and the line under the bark goes on running, and it makes more than a finished thing can use. The surplus of water goes over the rim. The surplus of the rest goes into the water as that faint sweetness. The sweetness becomes fleck and the fleck becomes fish. The fish become a shrimp's walking, an eel's tick and the forty degrees of a bird, which the rain takes away.

The animals are the other spillway. What lives here is the exact difference between what the plant makes and what it needs in order to stay the same, and it lives the way the curtain falls from the rim, always the same amount and never the same water.

From far enough off, that is true of the shoal, which is every size at once, and of the colony, which is always eggs and chicks and fledglings together. It is harder to believe of one bird.

She stands in the lee of the stack with the three notes going over her and the rain on her back. Every eleven seconds the thud comes up through the ribs of the pad into her feet. Just before it she shifts her weight, and she has never noticed that she does. The egg on her feet is nearly as warm as she is. Inside it a heart no bigger than a seed has started, and it goes four times a second. The stroke reaches it through the leaf and the shell every eleven seconds, and between one stroke and the next it has forty-four beats of its own. In there the stroke is the farther sound, and its own beat sits on top.
