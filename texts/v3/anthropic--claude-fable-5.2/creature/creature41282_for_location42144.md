The birds are the first answer, and for most of the gallery they are the only one you can think about.

Taken singly, a bird is not much at home here. On the ledge it squats instead of perching. Its feet are small and weak, and it rests on its breast like something set down. Its eyes are large and dark, made for moonlight under a canopy, and in the tube they do nothing past the brown dusk except shine red when a torch goes by. It finds the wall by clicking. It finds fruit by smell and by the whiskers around its bill, and it smells of fruit itself, sweet and slightly turned, under the ammonia. Each pair keeps a nest, a low drum of spat pulp and dung raised a finger's width a year, exactly one bill's reach from the neighbors on either side. On the fullest benches the drums already stand a hand high.

The bird's life is somewhere else. It is in the forests downslope at night, hovering at a palm crown and pulling the fruit off whole. The lava ran down to where those forests are now, and each night the forest is carried back up the same line, a cropful at a time. The tube is where the bird waits out the day and leaves its chick.

The chick is the one that stays. It sits on the drum for a third of a year and is fed until it weighs half again what either parent weighs, a bag of oil with a bill on it. Then the feeding slows, and it spends the fat on feathers and comes down to flying weight. On the floor below, the seedlings are doing the same thing with what the seed gave them. The difference is that the chick gets to leave.

Under the ledges, the floor animals live as if beneath a sky, and none of them looks up. The roach is the brown of leaf litter and still runs from a light it will rarely meet. The rat has good eyes and trusts its whiskers. The crabs came down the drip line from some seep under the turf and found the pools; they are pale because they are that kind of crab, and the cave has had no hand in it. The whip spider is the same flat, patient animal it would be under bark. Each of them is living a forest-floor life with the forest removed to a distance and delivered. They don't know the difference, and so far their bodies don't either.

The snake at the bat passage may be the most settled creature in the tube. It has one ledge at the one place where the passage narrows, and the bats, commuters like the birds, come through its reach twice a day like water through a weir. It needs nothing else the gallery has. Even so it is a tree snake, hanging from stone the way it would from a branch.

Then there are the people, who don't live here at all and have been coming longer than any of them could remember. The soot fan says so. It marks where a torch is held at arm's length against the wall while someone else works the pole, the same spot each time, one thin coat a visit. They come when the chicks are heaviest and still in down. They can't talk in here, so the work has been reduced to gestures everyone already knows. They cut with the tube's own glass and drop the flake when it dulls. They take what a three-man pole can reach. The benches run higher than that, and above the pole's reach no ledge has ever been touched, which is why the low ones are full again each year. Whether that is a rule they keep or only the length cane grows to, it works the same. Leaving the pole means they will be back and that there is nobody else to take it. They are the only ones who carry anything away.

So the tally is odd. The birds sleep here and live elsewhere. The people visit. The seedlings arrive and fail. The floor lives here entirely, in bodies made for another place, on food it doesn't make. For somewhere this loud, the tube has no one who belongs to it. It is a lodging, full to the ledges, and the lodgers turn over while the number holds.

That leaves the cricket on the last lobe.

It is the same kind that stands on every block upstream: hump-backed, wingless, brown with banded hind legs, its eyes dark and whole. Its antennae are twice its own length. It had those before it ever came underground; they were for bark and wet leaves at night. Up in the gallery they are a convenience. Down here, past the last skylight, they are all it has, and it carries them wide and forward, lifting one and then the other.

There is nothing for it here. No guano has fallen this far in, and the rustle stops well back. There is the white crust in the seams and whatever film grows on damp glass. Most crickets that walk this far are simply lost. But the air at the pinch is the stillest and wettest in the tube, and a cricket loses less of itself to it. A hungry animal that dries slowly can last a long time on very little.

If the tube ever has an animal of its own, it will start like this, with one of the borrowed ones that went past the food and the light and found it could stand it. Its far descendants would be paler and longer in the leg, slow and sparing, with eyes that had quietly stopped being worth building. That animal is not here. It is a long way off as crickets count time, and no distance at all as rock does.

What is here is its ancestor, or more likely a cousin of its ancestor: brown, sighted, a little thin. It stands where the lava stopped and tastes the air beyond.
