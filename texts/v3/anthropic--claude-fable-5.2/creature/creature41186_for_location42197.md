Begin with the grazer with the torn ear, since it is the one nearest to hand.

It stands chest-high to a person. Its coat is felted into plates, dun along the back and paler under the belly. When a palm is laid on it, the wool is neither warm nor cold but exactly the temperature of the palm. It has no smell. The split lip is dry, and so is the eye, which is amber, with the pupil lying across it like a slot for a coin. The left ear is torn from the edge inward, a short straight tear, long healed, with a hard pale rim. It is the kind of mark a person makes on purpose, with a point, to tell one animal from the next.

Under the palm the ribs rise and settle once every four seconds. Ten paces away, the ribs of the roofed windbreak do the same. The hide pegged out by the hearth is dun, felted in plates, and the right size.

It would be easy to say that a herd lives here. The builder said so first, in pebbles. But a herd has a front and a back. It has calves, and an old one with worn teeth who lags. It leaves dung and trampled ground, and eventually bones. What is here is one grazer. It has stood through a very long afternoon, and its afternoons have been set down side by side on the pavement instead of one after another, flank to flank, each under its own shadow. None is young and none is old because it is the same age in all of them. Every total is right because nobody has ever counted animals here. They have counted how much of one animal's afternoon can be seen from where they stood. From the hearth that is one amount, and from the windbreak another.

The same holds for the rest. There is one hare. There is one lean dog, lying next to the hare it has never chased, because a chase needs a before and an after, and here there is only beside. There is one grey bird, in rows, and one moth. One beetle scales the lee of every stone, one fly hangs at head height across the plain, and one raptor is pinned against the indigo at many heights.

That is closer, but it is still a tally, and the place does not hold a tally. Lift the left ear of the hare crouched between the grazer's forelegs. A hare would not allow it, but this one only follows the hand with its eye. The ear is split from the edge inward, short and straight. The dog's left ear is the same. The grey bird on the grazer's back has no ear to tear, but one small feather on the left side of its head is broken, and the break lies at the same angle. Walk a hundred paces through the gap and look again. The animals left behind are whole, and the ones now within reach are marked.

So it is not one of each. It is one. The mark was put on a single living thing, and that thing wears it in whichever body is closest to the person looking. The dun backs running out to the rim, the rows of birds and the blur of wings are the remainder of it, standing behind itself.

It does not eat. The lichen rings have spread wider than a reach without a tooth laid to them. It does not sleep, since no eye on the tableland is closed. It is not hungry, not afraid, and not tame. What it does is breathe and attend. The heads stay still and the eyes follow.

It also makes room. The gap opens to the exact width of the walker's shoulders before the walker has chosen a direction. This is neither courtesy nor retreat. The dust in the hollows has never taken a print, and the gap is the one print the place accepts. It holds a person's shape for as long as the person is in it, then goes smooth.

It was killed once. The ribs say so, and the hide and the scraper. It went on standing beside the hearth with its ear torn, breathing, and the ribs arched over the windbreak went on breathing with it. Nothing has ever been subtracted here. The mountains were worn away grain by grain and the stone came no lower. The fire burned and the ash kept its heap. The counting probably began after that.

That leaves the builder. There is no grave, no second hearth and no path out, in a place where nothing is subtracted. The pebble rows run to hundreds, far more tallies than one person lays in an afternoon. But they could be one person's afternoons, set side by side, each kneeling behind the hearth with a handful of pebbles, each reaching a different total, each right. The windbreaks are all curved by the same pair of hands. There is one awl. There is one hollow in the knapping floor, the shape of someone sitting.

The hollow is not empty. It is only one afternoon over, the way each shadow is. The scraper lies with its edge toward the hide because it was put down a moment ago, for a moment. The fly hangs a hand's width above the awl because that is the thickness of a hand closing on an awl, and flies settle on skin.

The grazer keeps one eye on the walker, as every animal here does. The other is on the fly, and that is the only eye on the tableland turned elsewhere. It is looking at whoever is nearest to it, as it has for the whole of the afternoon: someone seated, a tool half lifted, looking back.

Two live here, and each is the nearest thing to the other. One has been counting and has not finished. The other has been counted, and has held still for it.
