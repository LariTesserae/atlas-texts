Everything that lives on the shelf lives on what the station lets go.

The algae eat the warmth and the minerals in the outfall, the flies eat the algae, and the tilapia and the herons follow. The geckos live on the lamps by way of the moths. The swifts live on the lagging, the dogs on the buried lines, the moss on the drift. None of them was here before the pipes, because sixty years ago there was no here, and none would last a month if the fans stopped.

The same is true of the people, and they know it, though they are paid and the herons are not. There are about four hundred of them. They wash in water that came up a well and was on its way to the sea. They dry their clothes in a shed built over a bare length of steam main, since nothing dries anywhere else, and they are damp again before they reach their own doors. At one of the wellheads a bleed valve has had a steel box welded round it, and at any hour somebody's pot is sitting in the box with a stone on the lid.

You can tell who was born here by the knees. Every child falls on the clinker, the clinker goes in, and the skin closes over whatever the nurse could not pick out. It leaves a scatter of blue-black flecks under the skin of the knees, the palms, sometimes the chin. People who came from elsewhere, for the drilling or the construction or a marriage, have clean knees and never quite stop mentioning where they came from.

Born here or not, everyone has the boots. The soles are cut from truck tyre in the pipe shop and riveted on, and they last as long as anything lasts on this ground. With no seasons to count by, people count in soles. A baby arrived two soles after the canteen roof was finished. For longer spans there are the shrubs outside the administration block, so a thing happened in the time of the second hedge, or the fourth.

They touch things with the backs of their fingers first. The habit begins with handrails and spreads to cups, door handles, a child's forehead, the arm of someone they have just met. A burnt knuckle can still work a spanner.

At the seaward end of the outfall, where the channel has cooled to something a body can stand, they get in after shift and sit to the neck in the blue water among the egrets. The silica dries in their hair and leaves it stiff as a brush. The men crop theirs and the women oil it, and the oil is the one thing on the supply truck people will argue over.

Outdoors they talk with their hands. The signs came from the plant and have wandered a long way from it. A wrist turned as if opening a valve means yes, go on, come in, have some more. Turned the other way it means no, enough, goodnight. A flat palm held level and still means the needle is on its mark, and it is the answer to how are you, how is your mother, how was the shift. The same palm tilting means something is off. Nobody asks further in the open, because the rest is said with the mouth at the ear.

Quarrels are therefore silent and can be read from across the football pitch. Tenderness and a request to pass the salt look the same from any distance, two heads together. Visitors think the whole town is telling secrets. The old go deaf in the pitch of the fans first and lose nothing by it. A fitter in the pipe shop has never heard anything in his life, and a visiting inspector once took two days to notice.

They do not hear the station. They hear any change in it. If the note from the turbine hall drops by a hair, the third of the town that is asleep turns over. The handful of times a unit has tripped, the off-shifts were standing in the street before the horn went, looking at each other in the quiet with their hands hanging.

There are really three towns, the six o'clock, the two o'clock and the ten o'clock, living in the same houses and meeting on the pipe road. People stay on a shift for years and children keep their parents' hours. Football is played at three in the morning under the plume, which gives about as much light as the afternoon. The six o'clock people have the swifts and the kites. The two o'clock people have the bats. The ten o'clock people have the moths and the night herons, and consider themselves the quiet ones, though nothing about their hours is quieter.

No child here has seen a shadow with an edge on it, or a star, or a morning that looked different from the one before. What changes on the shelf is the people. The needles hold, and the rock keeps its edges where it is left alone. Along the pipe road, though, three shifts a day have walked for sixty years, and the clinker there has gone dull and small. It is the only ground on the shelf that will not cut a hand. The boots lose every time, and the road is slightly softer for it. The ferns are thickest along its edges, where the grit gets kicked.

There is no cemetery. The rock cannot be dug, and where it is cracked the water is a forearm down and warm. So far it has not mattered. Everyone who has died here was from somewhere, and went back there on the supply truck.

The oldest person born on the shelf is fifty-eight. She arrived in the drilling camp when the rock was two years old, in a tent pitched on a slab the dogs would have liked. Her name in the hands is one finger laid across the palm, which means "first". She has a spoken name too, but it has been years since anyone had cause to use it outdoors.

She walks the steam traps on the ten o'clock shift. There are three hundred and some, one or more at every loop. She carries a steel rod as long as her arm. She sets one end on the body of the trap and the other against the bone behind her ear, and hears through her skull what nobody could hear through the air. A good trap clicks, spits, and clicks. A bad one hisses without stopping, or says nothing and is cold to the back of her fingers. She has done this on something over ten thousand nights. She knows which traps the geckos prefer and which heron stands where. She notices a missing heron sooner than a missing person, because people have days off.

Nobody born here has yet grown old here, so the town does not know what it makes when it is given a whole life to make it. She is as far as anyone has got. The flecks in her knees have blurred and spread with age into a grey-blue stain. Everyone younger looks at them at the outfall and learns what their own will do. Her hair has gone white, and the silica stands it out from her head like wire. She has lost the fans entirely and kept the low note, though she says it has moved, and that she used to feel it in her chest and now feels it in her teeth. The control room has green marks painted on the glass to say where things belong. Nobody has painted a mark for her, and people watch her more closely because of that.

She went away once, at twenty, for six months' training in a city. If asked, she reports three things. The ground was brown and soft, and people walked on it without looking where they put their feet. They stood far apart and shouted, and she took every conversation for a fight. At night it went dark and quiet at the same time, and she lay awake waiting for someone to come and fix it. She does not tell this as a joke.

She is the first person the shelf will have to keep, and it has nowhere to put her. She has been asked about it, in the roundabout way things are asked here, with a tilted palm and a glance at the ground. She answers with the flat hand. She has also noticed that at the edge of the road, by the third loop, the grit is now deep enough to take a finger to the second knuckle. She checks it now and then, as she would a trap.

At ten the horn goes. She comes down the pipe road against the outgoing shift, and each of them, passing, shows her a level palm. She shows it back. Then she takes the rod from its hook, walks out under the first lamp, puts the steel to the bone behind her ear, and listens.
