Nothing with legs lives here. The eye keeps looking for it anyway: a track in the grit, a hole chewed in a leaf, something lifting off the pipes at evening. There is none of that and never has been. What lives on the tableland is the grove.

At first that seems to mean many groves, each a thicket on its own crossing with kilometers of nothing between. Each does look like an individual. One sits on a crossing of two seams and is squarish. Another sits where five seams meet and has grown round and heavy. Each has its own facing, since every blade lies along the field of its own crossing and no two crossings agree. A grove has no front or back and no shaded side. Its facing is the nearest thing it has to a temperament.

But a grove is less a thicket than a ring. The living part is the few meters of green at the rim, and everything inside is what the ring has left behind. It moves outward the way a fire-front moves through grass, though it has never moved fast enough for anything to see. What it leaves is metal: tube and lace, gray-rose, standing exactly where it grew. Because nothing here falls or rots or blows away, the whole history of the grove is still in it, in order from the inside out. The oldest stems are at the center, and they are thin. At the very middle of every core stands the first stem that grove put up, a single tube no wider than a straw, upright and whole.

The ring does not live only on the seams. In the quarters between the arms of the crossing, the ribbon roots plate themselves onto the feet of the dead pipes, and the pipes, being copper, carry the current out to them. So the grove feeds through its own remains. The core is the crossing made wider, a disc of worked metal built by the plant so that it can stand farther out.

It has no defenses. It has no thorns or bark, no bitterness in the oil, and no trick of closing a wound quickly, because nothing has ever wounded it. A broken leaf simply bleeds, slowly, until the oil films over with bright metal. It keeps no reserve against drought or cold, having never met either. It has never been wet. At night nothing in it changes. The warmth comes up the same and the hum holds its note. The stars come down to the horizon without dimming, and the foils hold them faintly, each grove at its own angle.

When a leaf has finished, the oil draws back into the stem, and what remains is the foil, which lets go with a tick and slides down through the still air. It lies where it lands. Under the living ring the rock is shingled with these foils, thousands deep toward the inner edge, and none has ever been shifted. The grove stands in its own leaf-fall as it stands in its own skeleton. It has never lost anything.

Then there is the runner, which changes the answer again. The green wire that goes out along the seam does not break off behind. Its tip is a soft bead of bronze-green, oily, about the size of a seed, and it feels its way along the copper by the current. Behind the bead the wire stays alive across all eleven or twelve kilometers of bare rock, a thread of the same oil under the same thin skin. When the bead reaches a crossing it stops and thickens and puts up a stem. The first leaf opens and lies down along the line of the new field, which is not the line the parent holds. This is the only moment in the life of the plateau when something turns.

So the smudges on the black are not separate. The wire runs from rim to rim. The same oil stands in all of it, creeping, carrying its small freight of metal. The same note sounds at every crossing, and whether the note belongs to the plant or to the seam under it, nothing here can say. It is more accurate to say that one thing lives on the tableland. It is shaped like the seams and it faces a different way at every node. Its body is forty kilometers across and, at most points, the width of a stalk of grass.

Somewhere on the plateau is its first crossing. That core is the broadest and the pipes at its heart stand highest, though the straw at the center is as thin as any other. From up there the plan can be seen whole. The black is ruled with bright lines, and some of those lines are edged in green where a wire has gone down them. The dark nodes, seen from that height, are plainly rings, each with a pale center. The centers are the finished part. The plateau is being made, and the making is all the creature does. It takes a seam, which is metal poured blind into a crack, and stands it up in the light as tube and lace, a leaf-thickness at a time. The living part is only the thin edge between unworked copper and worked copper. It is never larger than it was, and it is always a little farther out.

It does not hurry, since nothing here would give it a reason to, and it does not rest, since nothing here marks a time for that either. It has no seasons. Its only clock is its own width.

But one part of it is going somewhere. Out on the open pavement, on a seam a hand's width across, a green wire comes to an end in a bead the size of a seed. The nearest grove is nine kilometers behind it. The crossing ahead is a bright X on the black, about three kilometers off, with nothing on it yet. The bead is a finger's width nearer to it than it was yesterday.
