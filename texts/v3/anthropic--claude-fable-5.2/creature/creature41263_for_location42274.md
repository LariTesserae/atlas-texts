The people in the collar rooms would say that they do. They are a slow, deep-chested lot, brown in the face and pale everywhere else. They come down the rungs with a spanner or a mantle hook, wearing wide oilcloth hats that they put on when they step into a shed and take off when they step out. They do the one thing they came for, then climb back to rooms with windows, dry bedding and a view. They live over the city the way a crew lives over an engine room.

The birds have a slightly better claim, since they leave their young here, but they leave them the way you leave a pot on a stove. The things in the settling tanks do live here, and that is all anyone can say about them.

Then there is the chair.

It stands at the corner of Seventy Street and Sixty-Two Cross, on open grating, a few paces from the lamp. It is iron, and its legs and back are as fat with sinter as everything else, but the seat is chipped clean. Near it are a cot strung with rope, a bin with a lid that fits, and a can on a long wire hooked over the rail. There is no roof, because a roof here is a thing that rains on you. There are no walls, because there is no wind and no cold to keep out. The room is the lamp's halo, round and twenty metres across, and its walls are where the light gives out. Every other structure on the shelf has a function. This is the only address.

The occupant is called Downstairs. It began as a location, as in "leave it for downstairs", and hardened into a name, and she answers to it. She had another. It is still on a painted tag at the head of the Seventies ladder, on the board where each person's tag is turned over when they go down, so that nobody is forgotten below. Hers has hung on the down side for nineteen years, in full sun, and the sun has taken the letters off.

She is small and wide in the ribs, and she has not been properly dry since she was twenty. Her hair lies flat and her fingertips are permanently creased. She wears nailed wooden soles like everyone, and the people upstairs know her step when they feel it through the grating. It has no more direction than anyone's, but every other step in the city is on an errand. It goes out steadily and comes back steadily. Hers goes a little way, stops for a long time, and goes on. It is the step of someone looking at things.

Mostly she looks at the colour in the channels. She owns no thermometer and would not trust one made somewhere else. She lies on the grating and reads the skins instead, flesh-pink, ochre and bottle-green, and from where the bands have crept since last time she knows what the ground is doing. Then she takes a nail and scratches the true figure into the sinter on the pier beneath the street plate. The plate on her own corner says 70, and the scratch under it this season says 48. People coming down read the plate to learn where they are and the scratch to learn what it is like there. The scratch fills with new deposit in about the time it takes the heat to move again, so no correction outlives its truth by much.

If you chip back the crust under any plate, you find older numerals beneath hers, a seven with a bar through its stem and a four left open at the top. Neither is her hand. The cot was already strung when she came down. Nobody appoints a Downstairs. The crust thickens and flakes, and the shape stays about the same.

Asked why she stays, she says the rooms up there are too small. Asked again, she says the sky is too big. She sees no contradiction. A collar room is four paces round a pipe, outside its window there are eighty kilometres of air, and nothing up there comes in any size between the two. Down here the world is as wide as a lamp reaches, and when she walks it lets her go and takes her up again at the next corner.

She counts in strokes of the beam engine, which she feels as everyone does, as a lean in the air. A can of meal lowered through the lifted panel at Fifty-Five Street, which is the hottest place this year, is cooked in sixty strokes. Her corner to the rail at the lip is four hundred. Once a week there is a sack hung on the bottom rung of the Seventies ladder, with meal, salt and spare mantles, and usually a biscuit baked that morning in dry air. She eats the biscuit on the spot, standing. It is the only thing she does quickly, because in a quarter of an hour it would be as soft as everything else.

The eggs were nobody's job, and she made them hers. When a main is to be shut for work, the pipe will cool, so she walks its length first. She tests each flange with the inside of her wrist and lifts off whatever pair is lying there. She carries them inside her shirt, under the arm against the skin, across three blocks of grating at the pace the air allows, to iron she knows is right. It is the only brooding they ever get. If nobody has warned her, she knots a strip of grey rag round the valve wheel, which means there is a pair on that line and the wheel is to be left alone until the rag is gone. Nobody upstairs agreed to this, but they observe it.

Everyone knows the rings in the settling tanks come at the hour the lamps are trimmed. She noticed that on days when the trimmers were late, the rings were late too. One noon she went to the tank on the Sixties and walked up and down beside it in her nailed soles, and the rings came. So it is not the hour or the light. Whatever is down there rises to footsteps. She has told no one, because she thinks they would only net harder. On evenings when no trimmer has come down, she goes over and walks for them.

At night, when the ceiling sinks to the third storey, the climb through it is short enough for her. She takes it ten rungs and a rest at a time, three times over, and her head comes out into cold, stars and a floor of cloud as level as a table. The stacks stand out of it like piles in a harbour, each with its lit room. Along the rims of the stacks the adult birds stand in rows, long wings folded, facing the wind. She has carried perhaps two hundred of their eggs and wanted to see what she had been carrying. She looks for a while and goes down. The people upstairs know about this. From a window, on a clear night, there is sometimes a head on the cloud near the Eighties standpipe, turning slowly, and then there is not. Nobody calls out.

The holes in the ceiling find her only by accident. A shaft that opens four blocks off has closed before anyone here could walk to it, and she doesn't try. In nineteen years she has been standing in the right place three times. Each time, she says, she did not look up. The plates dried around her feet and her clothes smoked. On the grating in front of her lay a shadow with edges, her own height, its hands the size of her hands. On every other day her shadow is a hall of darkness leaning away into the fog, the largest thing on the street. She looked at the small one until it went. Then the drizzle started again in the sheds, and she stayed dry for about as long as a biscuit does.
