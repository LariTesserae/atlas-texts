From the gallery there is almost nothing to see of her. Once in several weeks something the colour of tallow crosses the low edge of the lamp's cone, close against the wall, and is gone before the eye has settled on how long it was. The rest has to be worked out from the room.

She is a female, as the big ones always are. She is a little over a metre long and as thick as a forearm, with a blunt head and small eyes she has not much use for. In a river her back would be olive, nearly black. Here it has stayed the grey-yellow of an old candle, shading to a white belly. The last two centimetres of her tail are missing, taken by the middle pump when she was the length of a bootlace and still swam in open water.

The pipe stub runs back an arm's length and a half to a blank flange. She lies in it doubled, tail beside her face, chin on the lip. Ninety years of current have polished everything else in the well. The inside of the pipe is polished too, burnished black, and that she did herself, by turning round.

She has never been in silence and has no way to miss it. The drone, the hum and the frying gravel are a condition of the water, like its warmth. What she attends to is change. Along each flank runs a line of pits that reads the push of water, and with her chin on the lip she can feel the room going past the mouth of the pipe. Each pump pulls across it differently. She knows which are running the way a person in bed knows which door in the house has opened.

The hammer she cannot prepare for. It comes through the iron at her back a moment before it comes through the water, and for some minutes afterwards she lies slack.

Her calendar is the city's, reversed. In a cold January all three pumps run day and night for weeks, and she does not come out at all. She can go a winter without eating. In summer the streets want little, one pump idles, and the well turns slow and heavy for hours at a time. Then she is out most nights, between the midnight slump and the third pump at six.

She never crosses the middle. Open water leads upward to the bells. She travels with her belly on the standing aggregate, which grips like cobbles, taking the north wall, the west wall behind the ladder and the floor's edge. She goes around the three dishes as a walker goes around wells.

Sound has no direction in here, and the light reaches two metres. Smell is different. It rides the water, and the water, however it thrashes, comes from somewhere. The wandering seam is a border to her, and she knows both countries. The culvert side tastes of brass tube and of water that has been round before. The north side tastes of outside. It carries the tide twice a day as a faint salting, rain a few hours after it falls on the streets, and leaf-rot in November.

She knows a stickleback is in the room before it has finished its first circle, and she has until the end of the second. She goes out along the wall and takes it where its circuit passes the ladder. If she misjudges, the pumps have it. They are the other hunter in the room, and the quicker.

In May the screens let through elvers, glass-clear, a thread each, as she was. She eats those too. That is why there is one eel.

There is so little in the well that she has had all of it in her mouth at one time or another: the rust knuckles, the orange fleece that comes apart into nothing, the valve spindle, the bottom rung. She tried the spanner too, in a year when it still had jaws. It moved, so she took it, held it and let it go on. It passes under her pipe every few hours now, and she does not stir.

On October nights the river comes down in flood, and the north stream runs cold and thick with the taste of it. On those nights she leaves the pipe with all three pumps running, which she does at no other time. She goes along the wall to the inlet and holds there in the cold with her nose to the flow. Eels her age in the river are turning silver that week and going down to the sea. Something in her begins it. Her eyes widen a little and her gut tightens. But the screens are spaced a finger apart, and she is the width of a wrist. After some hours she goes back along the wall. By the first frost it has stopped.

Everything else in the well is finished or is being taken away, a little from the rungs, a little from the floor. She is the one thing in it still being added to. Behind her eyes are two ear-stones the size of rice grains, and they have laid down a ring for every winter of three pumps. They are the only record of years in the room, and nobody will ever count them.
