Only the plant lives here. I looked for something else, because a place this large usually has a tenant somewhere: a grazer in the felt, a mould on the fallen plates, a borer in the trunks. There is nothing. The felt takes the old plates back without help, and nothing rots, because rot is someone else's meal. There is not even a second kind of plant. So the question is what sort of thing the forest is, and that takes a few tries.

The first try is the obvious one, a great many trees. But a tree has interests. It wants its neighbour's water and grows thicker than the tree beside it if it can. These have one girth. No trunk has a crown of its own, because the roof is common, or a root of its own, because the felt is one sheet. The current that climbs one trunk leaves along the runners to six others. A trunk here is a part, like a rib, and it is swapped out in sleeves from the inside while it goes on bearing its load.

The second try is one body, but it has no front, no middle, and no part that is more itself than another. Cut out a hectare anywhere, on a ridge or on the raft over deep water, and you have all of it, lacking nothing. It is large the way a crystal is large, by repetition.

Nor does the material stay. Plate, runner, valve, and trunk all go down into the felt and come up again. Given long enough, not a fibre is the one that was there. What stays is four metres, three tiers, a plate as broad as a table, and a knuckle that will seat in any collar on the plain. What lives here is closer to a set of measurements than a substance. The wood passes through the measurements as the water passes through the wood.

The third try comes from asking where it measures from. The trunk in the fallen tube is longer by exactly the depth of the hole, so the forest is built down from the roof. Over deep water it lets go of the rock altogether and stays flat. So it is a level, a dark lid the size of a continent, with legs let down to wherever the warmth is. The ground is optional and the level is not. That explains its manners toward the rock. It grips the glass skin without cracking it and takes nothing but heat.

It has one sense, if that is the word: the difference between its underside and its top. Each valve opens when the push from below is stronger than the push from above, and shuts when it is not. That is all of its behaviour, the same decision made billions of times.

The tone has no ear to reach, but it is felt in bark, and the only thing touching the bark is more of the forest. I think, and cannot prove, that the hum is how it stays the same. A valve can hardly drift off a tempo it is standing in. The current is similar. The lattice has all the wiring a mind could want and nothing to have a mind about. A mind is for news, and no news has arrived since the roof closed. What the lattice carries is agreement, which is why a runner from the west fits in the east.

The arcs are the nearest thing it has to events. By my count there are over a million knuckles in a square kilometre. If one arcs there in an hour, any given knuckle waits more than a million hours for its turn. I take each arc for a small failure, a junction near the end of its service. Yet across the whole plain there must be thousands every second. The creature is never entirely dark, though no part of it can see.

It cannot always have been like this. It reached the rock before weather could, so it must once have run. There was a front, younger at the edge than behind, and a first plate. Then the edge went out over the sea, the roof closed, and the drizzle began. What lives here is what remains after arriving. It is not asleep, since every part is at full work, and not waiting, which needs something to wait for. It is not lonely either, having never had an other. The fog band, the cloud, and the rain are its own doing, so it lives inside its own breath.

The last version is a thing that lives in a gap. Below it is warm rock, which is not it, and above it is cold sky, which is not it. Everything between is: wood, water, fog, cloud, and the tone. It is the shape a difference in temperature takes when something alive holds it open and keeps it level.

It can replace every part of itself. The warmth is the one thing it uses that it does not make. It has no way to tell if that warmth changed, because it measures only against itself, and all of itself is the same age. The heat comes up as evenly as ever, and the valves tick.
