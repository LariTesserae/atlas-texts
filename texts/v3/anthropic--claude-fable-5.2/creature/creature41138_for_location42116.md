Not the crew, though they are the first you'd think of. They come up the red road in the morning and go down again before the cloud arrives, and all they have here is a station with a kettle in it. The plateau has worked on them all the same. They watch each other's faces and hands more than people usually do. They are quiet in the truck going down, like people who have stopped expecting speech to carry. Every one of them has, once, pressed a palm into the moss at the road's edge and watched the water come up. None of them steps off the scoria. They are well-behaved guests, and guests are what they are.

The ravens are nearer the mark. One pair holds the whole plateau. Their nest is a heap of sedge stalks and scavenged cable ties in the steelwork of the first pylon, right at the rim, where the hiss is thinnest. They work the road because it is the one surface on which a thing can be found. A moth knocked down by a windscreen, a crust, or a beetle crossing red gravel shows up there as it never would in the green. Inside the yellow posts they have mostly given up calling. Instead one lands on a well dome, where it can be seen for a kilometre, and bows, or holds something up in its beak, and the other comes. In June they quarter the moss on foot, light enough to leave no mark, looking for the plover's eggs.

The plover stands on a dome with its beak working because it is calling to a mate who cannot hear it. It has learned, or its line has, that on this ground you stand high and get seen. Its nest is a shallow cup pressed into a cushion by its own breast and lined with flakes of pale lichen. A cup pressed into this moss lasts as a footprint lasts, so the birds come back to the same ones. Some of the cups are older than the birds sitting in them.

Count those and you have perhaps a dozen warm bodies in a day's walk. But the place is full. Its population is in the water that came up around your fingers.

A cushion is built like a forest with the years stacked in it. The top two fingers' width is green and growing. Under that the stems are brown, last decade's and the decade's before, still upright and still holding water, down to a black paste on the clinker that is the nearest thing here to soil. Every leaf carries a film of water, and the film is the inhabited part. There are worms in it finer than a hair, lashing from leaf to leaf. There are rotifers, glass-clear, which anchor by a foot and spin two wheels of cilia to pull food in. Every one is female, and they have been, mother to daughter, for some tens of millions of years. There are amoebae that build themselves flasks to live in, grain by glued grain, and here the grains are volcanic glass.

And there are water bears. A water bear is a third of a millimetre long, a plump segmented sack on eight legs with hooked claws on each foot. It doesn't swim. It climbs through the leaves slowly, hand over hand, pierces a moss cell with a pair of needles in its snout, and drinks it. It has nothing to hear with. Of everything on the plateau, it is the one creature the roar has never reached. A single cushion holds as many of them as the towns below the rim hold people. For the whole field the number isn't worth writing down.

Nothing walked here. The lava came out sterile. The moss and everything in it arrived dry, as dust on the wind, and waited for cloud. That is still how they live. Mid-morning, when the light goes hard, the film on the top leaves thins. The water bear draws in its legs, folds its segments into one another like a telescope closing, and lets the water leave. What remains is a wrinkled grain with almost nothing wet in it and nothing going on in it. It isn't asleep, because sleep has a pulse. It has stopped the way a clock stops, and it can stay stopped for an afternoon or for ten years. The rotifers do the same. The worms coil and do the same.

So the slow silvering you watch cross the field, from the tips down, is the moss closing its leaves, and it is also the population leaving off, cushion by cushion, the shallow ones first. When the first drizzle turns the field yellow-green again, they are up and walking within the half hour, as if nothing had happened, because for them nothing did. A water bear has a few months of living in it, and only wet hours count. Here it gets the nights and the drizzle, and those months can take years to spend. On a bright afternoon the wind lifts some of the stopped ones off the top and carries them over the rim. When the cloud is heavy others are washed down through the cushion and the clinker into the tubes, and they come out days later at a spring far below.

The wells have changed one thing for them. In the long green tongues downwind, the warm rain never lets up and the moss never silvers. The animals there don't stop. They live straight through, day and night, eating and moulting and laying their eggs in the skin they've just climbed out of, and they are done in a summer. Then winter turns the whole tongue to rime and stops them harder than the sun ever does. From the road it is a strip of deeper green. For the residents it is another country, where time runs without gaps and runs out sooner.

Then there are the bootprints. Each one is a hole where a heel and sole crushed the stems, and crushed stems don't spring back. They mat, go dark, and hold water like a cup. When the field around has dried to silver, the prints are still wet at the bottom, shaded by their own walls, so in each one the afternoon comes late. The animals in a print get an hour or two more than their neighbours a hand's breadth away, on every clear day since whoever it was walked out from the third dome. New stems lean in from the rim a centimetre a year, and one day they will roof it over. Until then there is a line of small dark wells a stride apart, each sunk by one person's weight and each holding a town that keeps later hours.

At four on a bright afternoon the plateau is grey from rim to rim and stopped, with the column roaring over it. At the bottom of the farthest print a water bear is still climbing, one clawed foot after another, through a leaf's worth of water.
