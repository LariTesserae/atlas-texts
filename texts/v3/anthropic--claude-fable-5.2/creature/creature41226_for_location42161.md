You look for citizens first. The streets are the right width for something smaller than a flea, and for the first hour at the rim you keep expecting one to cross a bridge. None does. The ramps aren't scuffed, and the openings that look like doorways lead only into tubes.

Then you take the water for the people. The slugs queue and give way. A milky length will stand at a corner while silt goes by, and it is hard not to read patience into that. But follow one. It leaves the stockyards loaded, climbs eleven tiers, stops under a hanging rank of grains, and comes away clear. Two crossings later it is divided in three, and the thirds go separate ways. The slugs aren't anybody. The manners belong to the crossing, the way right of way belongs to a road.

So you listen instead. Heard long enough, the chord comes apart into voices, usually between nine and fourteen. Each stands over one unfinished block and holds its grains there, and each has its own edge, reedier or rounder, so that after a day you could tell them apart with your eyes shut. A new voice comes in thin, wavers for a minute or two, and steadies. It is the only unsure thing in the pool. When its block is done the voice doesn't fade. It stops, cut as square as the wall it made, and no voice returns to a place it has built. Nobody moves in.

That is the first true thing about what lives here. It doesn't live in the town. It lives only where the town is unfinished. A pool four paces by three was built full long ago, so the oldest quarter comes down on every ebb. Nothing there is worn. It is finished, and finished ground is no use to its builder.

For a while you count the voices as a population, a dozen at a time, each lasting an hour or an afternoon. But they don't behave like separate creatures. When one stops, the others shift at once to close around the gap. No two ever take the same pitch. They are more like the fingers of one hand.

The hand belongs to the bass. The bass builds nothing and never drops out. At high tide, when everything sinks an octave, it goes below hearing and you have it only in the shins and the back teeth. Those are the hours when the sea is running over the lid, and the lid holds. Put your hand in and the pressure at the wrist has the bass in it, buzzing in the small bones. The bass keeps the water domed and cold, and it keeps the noon. If one thing lives here, this is it, and the upper voices are what it works with.

It can be known only by its habits. It is exact. It is not hostile, since it handles a sandhopper more carefully than you would, and it tells a sandhopper from a pebble faster than you can. It takes apart only what will sort cleanly into piles, and whatever is alive it hands back whole and dry. You can't tell whether that is courtesy or distaste.

It gives you the same treatment and nothing more. Your shadow stops at the surface, because the pool has its own light and doesn't admit yours. You can shout, or sit at the rim all night, and the chord won't alter by a hair.

It doesn't rest. The noon is no display. Nothing in the pool has ever needed dark, so the light stays at the hour that suits the work. It takes its time from the tide, which brings it sand, and takes none from the sun.

And it keeps nothing. Nothing material in the pool is older than a few weeks, so the keeper is older than its entire body. Only the chord and the rules carry over from month to month. The quarter that goes up is never quite the one that came down. A ramp has shifted, or a tube has been doubled. It is revising. The town is a draft of something it hasn't worked out, and the harmony never resolves. By its own rule a finished thing goes silent, so it may be taking care never to finish.

For all that, it can't close its accounts. Sand has to come in, the grey plume has to go out, and the heat has to go somewhere. It has no rule for the warm thread once it leaves the landward notch. The slime there is the only resident a biologist would count. It lives on the keeper's waste, outside every ruled line, and the keeper pays it no attention. Sandhoppers set down on the rim sometimes find their way to it.

Three paces on, the small hollow has had that bass coming up through its floor for as long as the pool has been singing, and it has begun to answer. The answer is a fifth above, the first note a string gives that isn't itself again. Its one voice wavers the way new voices in the big pool waver, and it hasn't steadied. It has no bass yet that you can feel. At night the hollow is as dark as the other ten, but if you cup your hands around it, the tower shows a grey edge, like the light a few minutes before dawn.

You can't tell whether the keeper hears it. During the tide hours, when the big chord is down an octave and the ticking has stopped, one of the upper voices will sometimes sit on that same note for a while before moving on. The harmony shifts all day, so it may mean nothing.

Between the two pools the green stripe runs down the rock, warmed by one and in earshot of both. It is a hand wider than it was in spring.
