In the east the powder keeps a record, and most of what it records is one set of feet. The print has three toes, spread wide, and a round heel, and the powder takes it so exactly that the creases of the pad show. The prints do not run in lines, as they would behind something passing through. They go from wall to wall. In front of certain walls they pile into a trodden ring a pace across, where something waited a long time and never once stood still.

The animal that makes them is knee-high at the shoulder and a little longer than that, deep in the chest, on four thin legs. Its coat is short and the color of the sky here, old paper, which in the light of the eastern rooms is no color. It has a neck, a long face, and ears as long as the face. Everything else alive in the town would pass through a seam a hand wide: the grazers are flat, the hunters narrow, and the crust and the stalks arrive as dust. This one would not pass. It came in at the other end, off the plain, following the wobble of the overflow up to the notch and over the sill. There is one of it.

It lives on what the walls give back. The stalks are the staple. Every stalk stands on a roof that will go grey, and when the slab thins far enough the stalk comes down through it, roots first, and lies whole in the powder of the room below. The animal knows which ceilings still have threads hanging from them. It walks under each one every mild period, looks up, and moves on. When a stalk is down, it eats it from the root end, a joint at a time, shifting its feet as it chews.

Then there is what the walls themselves hold. A grazer that stopped is sealed a finger deep and stays sealed while its wall goes straw, rose, blue. In the grey the wall thins until the next room shows through, and then the grazer shows too, a dark patch the size of a palm with light around it. The animal finds these and visits them daily, treading its ring in front of each. When the wall is thinner than a shed plate, it puts its nose through. What comes out is dry and complete, with the legs folded. It eats all of it except the plate on the back. That plate was closed in before it could finish tarnishing, so it is still straw, or still rose, and for a few days it lies in the grey powder as the only color in that end of town.

Hunters come to it the same way, but seldom. Most pilasters in a greying corner are casts, open down one side, and it has learned to look for the opening and walk past. A pilaster with no opening has a hunter in it that stayed too long. Now and then the hunter has only cut it fine. Then the two of them wait on either side of a failing crust, one lifting its feet in turn and one not moving at all, until the crust splits down an edge or doesn't.

The rule applies to it as it does to a stone. It has no plate to shed, so it does the work by hand: it licks down everything its tongue can reach, and it drags its flanks along the free-standing corners, which keep their edges to the end. Neither reaches a strip between the shoulders. The first cold period it spent here, it spent lying in a room, and it woke crusted. It got all of that off except a ridge on the strip, a hand long and a finger wide, squared along the top like everything the vapor makes. The ridge was white then and is blue now, so the animal has been here about as long as one wall lasts. When the ridge greys, the next wind will take it, and after that nothing on the animal will say how long it has stayed.

It has not slept in a room since. Rooms are eddies, and a lane that ran as current yesterday may be slack by the time a sleeper wakes. The notch does not shift. When the cold comes on and the seams breathe harder, the pool rises to the sill and the whole town's air pours out through that one break in the rim, fast and silent. The animal lies down in the middle of it. One patch of the broken rock there has lost its fresh color and gone dull and slightly dark, the shape of a curled body. It sleeps with its back to the basin and its face to the plain, and the stream runs over it all through the cold and leaves nothing on it.

It seldom goes west of the blue. Young walls keep what they hold, and a doorway there is narrower each time it is passed. In the east the walls only subtract, so every day there are more ways out of a room than there were the day before.

Its ears were made for open ground and have no work here. A stalk drops into powder three rooms away without a sound, and its own feet are just as silent. The ears turn anyway, both together, toward doorways and toward the crisp holes in the walls, and then the eyes follow and do the finding.

The dry wind is the one weather it knew before it came. When the wind drags the vapor out in rags, the rule goes out with it, and for an afternoon nothing in the basin settles on anything. The grey quarter comes apart around the animal all at once. Everything those walls still held drops to the floor, more than it could eat in days. It does not eat yet. It walks to the middle of the main way, where there is suddenly room, turns its face and both ears into the wind, and puts all four feet down. This is the only time it stands still. From the rim the whole plan of the town is drawn in violet along its upper edges, and at the east end, where the lines break off into nothing, there is one more line, a hand long, at knee height.

When the wind drops and the seams refill, it starts on the floor. After the glut comes the lean stretch, when no wall in town is grey and it walks the edge of the blue waiting for the first to turn.

At the start of each cold period it leaves whatever wall it has been watching. For once its prints run straight, down the last of the main way, past the stubs, to the rim. It steps up onto the dull patch and lies down in the gap where the air goes through.
