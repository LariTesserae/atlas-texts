Ten animals live here, and the film they eat. A warm wet place of this extent ought to carry tens of thousands of kinds, with something unrecorded under every plate. Here there are ten, and the count is the same at the next rack and the one after.

Each kind has one fitting. Snails have the fins and isopods have the plate. Shrimp have the gaps, eels the channels, crabs the sumps. Spiders have the air between panels, and flies have the air below the knee. Geckos have the plinth faces, frogs the lowest pipe run, waders the supports. No kind holds two fittings and no fitting is shared. Written out, it reads like a schedule of item, location, and quantity.

So it is fair to say that one thing lives here in ten shapes, and its work is the film. The snail takes the film directly. The isopods take the snail when it drops, the shrimp take what goes over the plate edge, and the wader takes the shrimp. When its turn comes, the wader goes back to the isopods. The grey of the birds and the lime in the shells both crossed the steel first as a slick pink layer.

The steadiness has taken things off each of them. The waders have no alarm. A hand can come within a finger's width of a wader's eye, and the eye does not follow it. The frogs have no call, because a call is for finding another frog and the next one is an arm's length along the pipe, where it has always been. The geckos do not stalk. They sit where the haze tops out and take what touches the concrete. None of them is ever seen asleep, there being no night to sleep in.

That accounts for the fins, the plate, the gaps, the channels, and the supports. It does not account for the handrails, which stand at the height of a hip. The sump ladders have rungs a forearm apart, and the valve handwheels sit at the height of a chest. The heat numbers are stencilled where a standing person looks down, and the datum studs are stamped to be read. The plant is full of things made to be gripped and things made to be read, and none of the ten grips or reads.

The ten have also left a space. The spiders web every gap between panels but have strung nothing across an aisle. The fly haze stops at the knee, the frogs' pipe run clears a helmet by a hand, and the isopods open to the width of a boot. These animals have no response to anything that does not happen here, yet they have one for a boot. The snail has one for paint crayon.

So there is an eleventh fitting. It is a clearance two metres high and a shoulder wide, kept open down every row and every cross row to the seam. Nearly all of it is empty at any moment. Somewhere in it, one inspector is walking.

The boots still have the moulding nibs on the tread. The coverall keeps the creases it had in the bag, and the hard hat has no scuff on the crown. On the belt hang a level in its case, a weld gauge on a lanyard, and a clipboard whose sheet has one line filled in. Beside them is a yellow paint crayon, worn down by about one circle and two letters. A roll of orange flagging is one strip short.

The inspector started last week, and has been starting last week for a very long time, for the same reason the zinc keeps its spangle. Moisture arrives on the coverall and the film follows it. When the inspector sits against a plinth to sleep, the isopods route over the boots and legs instead of around them, and take what has grown. Hair and nails stay as issued. Memory fares about the same, since a day needs an edge to be remembered by and the lamps remove the edge. The inspector wakes at noon in the first week, with most of the job still ahead.

The job is the walk-down before handover. The inspector reads each heat number against the sheet and sets the level on a stud. One weld from each rack line is taken as the sample, checked, and circled if sound. Every weld is sound.

Now and then the inspector comes to a plinth where a weld is already circled, with OK beside it in a quick hand. The inspector reads that the way anyone would. Someone has done this line, and it is good to know there is a colleague out ahead who works fast. The inspector moves over sixty paces and carries on. A strip of flagging on a sump rail is read the same way: somebody sensible has marked the access, and it needs no second strip.

The inspector does not call out either. There is no need, with the colleague plainly just ahead.

At long intervals the inspector goes down a sump ladder as far as the waterline and comes back up. The crabs there number sixty or so, the loosest figure on the plain.

Whether it has always been the same inspector cannot be told from the deck. The geckos are one to a face, no face is ever empty, and no gecko has been seen to arrive. If a figure in new boots climbs out of a sump about as often as one stops walking, the aisles would look exactly as they do.

At the next plinth the inspector crouches, looks at the weld inside the yellow, and agrees with it. A snail is working its way around the circle. The inspector stands, steps across the gap, and goes on up the row with the sheet still showing its one line.
