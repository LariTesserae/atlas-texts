About three thousand people live in the town, and out of doors they are hard to tell apart. They go about in grey felt made from hair gathered off the ridges, hooded, with the face greased against the dry and the ears stopped with wax and moth-down. Each trails a cord of braided gut from the belt. They are short and deep in the chest, and they walk as the grazers do, flat-footed on wide soles, without hurry, like everything at this height.

They keep to the streets. From the rim what shows is a few figures in single file on a tan line, well spaced, each glancing up every few steps. A shout carries nowhere here, and a person can only be hailed when they happen to look, so everyone looks, often. They go on doing it indoors, lifting their eyes from their food in a quiet room. The palms of their mitts are faced with pale hide. Outdoor signing is done from the shoulder, large and slow, made to be read at two hundred metres under a brown noon, and the palms are what is read.

They do not drink either. The moths that fall before dawn are on the streets by midday, the streets being still lines like any other, and each household rakes its own length, presses the catch into grey cakes and eats it with rendered fat. They make their water as the herds do. A horn hood over each lamp collects a spoonful or so in an evening, which tastes of smoke and goes to infants and the sick. An adult may go a season without swallowing anything liquid. Their lips do not crack, though a visitor's will by the second day.

At the door of a shell they stop, coil the cord, take the plugs out, and start talking. They do not ease into it. The rooms are full of talk, three or four conversations laid across one another, low and quick, kept up through the meal and after it. The voices are small, because no one on the plateau has ever had a reason to raise one. The lamps throw shadows up the ribs, which nobody has outdoors, and overhead the birds sleep through all of it.

Each of them has two names. One is spoken and was given indoors at birth. The other is made with the hands and was given later, on a street, by whoever first needed to call them from a distance. People from different shells who have raked adjoining lengths for thirty years may know only each other's hand-names and have never heard each other speak. Those who seldom go out, the lamp tenders and the ones who mind children, sign small and close to the body, and the street workers tease them for it.

The sounder gives nothing to the air. Its note is in the wood, and a child is taught it by setting the jaw against a rib of the wall until the teeth pick it up. Everyone knows their own shell this way and can tell a strange one through the soles of the feet. Each shell's sign is made at the jaw for that reason. Children also discover that if they hum one steady pitch with the jaw pressed there, and come back to the same spot for some weeks, the wall thickens under it. They are told to stop. At child height along the inner ribs there are rows of smooth wooden knuckles where someone did not. The songs they sing as adults slide from pitch to pitch and never rest on one.

No one living has made a sounder or knows how it was done. In each shell, whoever wakes first puts a palm on theirs. Nothing could be done if it were still, and they check anyway. The account of the western shell is that the drone came back into the room during a meal, all at once, and that everyone was outside before they had thought to stop their ears.

The other shells took in as many as they had sleeping places for. A sleeping place is a hollow in an inner wall, the length of a body, and it is inherited. There are about three thousand of them, which is why the town is the size it is and has been for as long as there has been a count.

The rest went out onto the plateau, and their descendants are still there. The town reckons them at a thousand. They go with the herds, at the herds' pace and in the herds' direction, taking the lame and the old, gathering shed hair from the ridges, and watching the loud ground, since the grazers trust their ears and something has to look. They go thin-soled and can find a still line in the dark by the feel of it, before any dust has gathered to show where it is. In the hours after dusk, when the mat cools and the ridges begin to travel, the herd gets to its feet and travels with them. The people go too, half asleep, and lie down again where the line comes to rest. They sleep facing outward. One who wants to be found at dusk lifts the cord clear of the ground and holds up a hand, and after a moment the fingertips show violet. Nobody does this on a windy evening.

They have no speech. Their plugs go in at birth and are changed as the ear grows, and what is behind the plugs by the age of forty is not worth protecting. Their hand language is wider than the town's and faster, and townspeople follow about half of it. An infant's crying cannot be heard out there by anyone, including the infant, so the young are carried on the chest, skin to skin, where it can be felt.

That is why the women come in. A birth on the open mat cannot be listened to, and no one can tell whether the child has breathed. So when her time is near, a woman of the herds walks to the town with one or two others, crossing the band at the limits in daylight because of what waits beyond it, and comes up the western street. A shell on that side keeps a room for the purpose and is paid in felt. The child is born into silence. The midwife hears it breathe, and it hears itself.

Then the mother gives it a spoken name. The herder women keep a few dozen of these and pass them along in that room, and they are close to all the speech the herders have left. She says it once, in a voice she may not have used since her last child. Then the ears are stopped and the child is carried out. A daughter may hear voices again when she comes in for her own. A son most likely will not. Every one of them has a spoken name, heard once, and most do not know it. The women of that shell could recite the names of half the herders on the plateau.

Their hand-names all begin with two fingers laid along the jawbone. In the town that is the sign of the western shell, and nobody else uses it. The herders attach no meaning to it. It is how a name starts. They go home by the street they came in on, which runs west and stops at the swell, and they walk up one side and down the other without altering pace. Asked what it is, they make the sign for ground.
