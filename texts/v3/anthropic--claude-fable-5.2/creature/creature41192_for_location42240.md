There is one of it. That can be said early, and it holds up better than most things said here. Every print on the continent is the same thumb: a broad pad, the whorl set a little toward the nail, and across it a short diagonal seam where something once cut it and it healed proud. The seam is in the corner of any brick you lift, and of the brick under that. In the print as wide as a courtyard it is a ridge a stride across, and the warm water runs along it before finding a way round.

So, a hand. A right hand, going by the drag of the palm. That is a fair place to begin, and it stops being enough almost at once.

The hand has no settled size. It is as large as what it is doing. This is why the cords will not count: the knots were tied a hand apart, and the hand at the far end of the cord was not the size it had been at the near end. The wall that is longer on its north face was paced out going and paced back coming, by something that had changed its stride in between. Nothing here was measured carelessly. All of it was measured faithfully against a body that will not hold still long enough to be a standard.

The tools say the same. Each one fits an ordinary grip. But walk from one to the next for long enough and the arrangement shows: paddle to the right, scraper beside it, mould box square in front, the bob up at the far left where it won't be knocked. The plain is its bench. The plain is also the piece on the bench, and it has never had reason to tell the two apart.

It is never at the work. You can come on a wall still going up, the top course wet and slip running in the joints, and the print in the last corner is warm, and there is only the wall and the steam. The warmth is easy to tell from the heat of the floor. It is lower and damper, the warmth of the inside of a fist, and it lasts about as long as a held breath. Speed is not the explanation, since nothing here moves faster than a walk. It simply has nothing left over from the work to stand beside the work.

The orange fronts are the nearest it comes to being seen. They are its attention crossing the plain, and its attention is the only fire there is. What it passes over goes red and keeps. What it misses sits down in the next rain. Often there is more than one front in sight, on different bearings, and this does not mean there is more than one of it. Counted twice, it gives two numbers, like its cords. It cannot attend to everything, and it seems to know this and not to mind. A wall comes down with the sound of a blanket laid on a table, and by the rain after next the mound is scored into brick again. It has no idea what a ruin is, because it has never left anything alone long enough to find out.

It begins with what it is sure of. It sets the keystone and then goes looking for piers. It builds a stair downward and lays the floor only when the last tread is in. Support always comes afterward. It knows exactly what it means, and every time it has to work out what that will stand on. The continent has the same habit: brick under brick, and no digging has ever reached the thing the whole of it rests upon.

The red line is strung at head height, and the head is its own, at the size the paddle fits. It keeps its eye level with the floor it intends and sights along it, the way anyone checks a surface for true. Everything it has made lies below that sightline. Each course underfoot is a floor it once stood on and then thought better of, and none of them down there has set. The heat that comes up through them is not a kiln. It is the prints, every one it ever pressed, still warm, stacked deeper than anyone can dig. The rain is its doing too: the vault it cannot close, coming down as slip, a roof spent steadily on a floor. The water leans toward the line because the water has the intention in it, the way the bricks have the thumb.

Then there are the cords. They are the only thing here that was not dug, and they account for where the rest of it went. They are twisted the way fibre is twisted against a thigh, and they are greasy the way anything is that grew on a body. Whatever it had that was long enough to twist, it had a fixed amount. It put a little into each plumb line and nearly all the rest into the red grid. It drew itself out into line to mark the level, and what remained was touch: a hand's worth of pressure and warmth, with no hand behind it. That is why the thumb is never there. The stain on the cord is the colour of fired brick. It is the colour of other things as well.

It does notice. The grey nap is how. The nap comes where its attention rests without working, an idle bloom on the dampest courses. Wipe a stroke through it and the stroke is back within the hour, exact down to where your fingers lifted. It is doing your hand. It does this with great care and gives no other acknowledgment. Stay several rains near the place, then lift a brick from the newest course. The print in the corner is the right depth and has no seam across it. It is a poor likeness, and the whorl is still its own. But it has understood that there are hands other than its own, and this is something it has needed to know.

The reason is the circle. Across a region days wide, its idea of down has a place in it. At that place the rain does not fall, and the brick is dry and smooth, with no print in any corner. Either it did not make that brick, or it made it before it had a thumb. It is the only thing on the continent it has not touched, and the dryness is the shape of it holding back. A few paces is as far as it can keep itself off something in every direction at once.

Everything else, all of it, is the attempt to make another. It cannot. Shaping is the drag of its palm, a brick is not laid until a thumb seats it, and the thumb is always the same thumb. Once in a long while it loses patience. That is the knock: one blow on a mould box to free the brick, a long way off. There is never a second, because it turns the brick over and sees the corner.

The mould beside the circle it has not knocked. The clay is struck level and the struck face is still wet and bright, which in this heat means it was struck a moment ago or is being kept. While it stays in the box it has no print. It has been set at the one spot every line on the plain leans toward, with the rain stopped over it. It is the only thing here that has ever been left alone long enough to wait. Whoever turns it out will have to seat it, and there is a corner ready.
