Nothing here walked in. The walls are sheer and three winters is not long, so everything in the hollow came by wing, by spore, or by falling. Most of the frogs fell. They were crossing the carr on wet February nights, as frogs do, and the ground stopped under them or a cascade took them over. An adult frog is too heavy to climb moss, so whatever goes over the rim stays.

The walls belong to small things. The liverwort on the seep-lines is the kind patterned like snakeskin, and it smells sharp, close to turpentine, where a jackdaw's foot has bruised it. Springtails graze the film of algae that runs ahead of the moss, and mites follow the springtails. The golden saxifrage is in flower, flat and greenish, with only gnats to visit it. Its seeds sit in open cups, and drips from the ledge above knock them out and down, so each mat sows the ledge beneath it.

On the bare stone between the wet lines there are orb webs, and they are not spread evenly. They hang an arm's length apart, in the spots where the hum nearly vanishes. Where the hum swells, a web would shake all day as if something were caught in it, and a spider cannot hunt by that. In fog the webs bead white and mark the quiet places on the wall like chalk.

A cock wren has started three nests in the alder roots that dangle under the turf, each a ball of moss stripped from the nearest seep. His song is pitched so far above the drone that he is the one animal here that doesn't have to shout. The jackdaws are paired and building in the open joints of the mudstone. They build by dropping sticks into a crack until one jams, and most of the sticks do not jam. They fall the whole height of the column and land in the pool.

The crack has its own residents, arranged by how much dark they can stand. In the last of the daylight, just inside the lips, herald moths sit out the winter with their wings folded, rust-orange and so covered in condensation that they look like wet leaves. Further in are the cave spiders, big and glossy, the colour of strong tea. Their egg sacs hang from the roof on single threads, white and the size of a thumb joint. The sacs lean outward in the breath, a whole row at the same angle. When the breath reverses they swing through the vertical and lean in.

The wrens that raid the gnat-smoke by day come back at dusk to sleep, a dozen or more packed into one pocket of rock at the lip where the mild air touches them. The bats pass close over them on the way out. Some are the small quick kind that go straight over the rim. Others stay in the hollow for the first half hour, flying low circuits a hand's breadth above the pool and lifting gnats off the surface film with their feet. No one has seen where they hang, and the fissure narrows past anything but a bat.

The pool filled the same way the walls did. The diving beetles flew in at night, taking the black water for sky. Caddis larvae on the rubble have built their cases from flakes of the mudstone, so each one drags a blue-grey tube with edges as sharp as the walls. The snails that still have colour in their shells are the ones that crawl downward when the light goes. Newts fold their eggs one at a time into sodden alder leaves below the low-water line, where the heron's bill reaches but the air does not.

The heron is always the same bird, with a broken plume. It treats the pool as a very small estuary. It knows nothing about pumps and only knows that this water ebbs before dawn, which is all it needs.

In the last puddles at low water, among the stranded beetles, there is sometimes something that came from the other direction. It is a shrimp the length of a fingernail, white as candle wax and without eyes. These lived in the flooded joints of the rock before there was a hollow or a mine. The falling tide draws them up out of the rubble and leaves them in daylight, which they cannot detect. The wrens take them first because they show up so well on the mud.

Every forty minutes the shudder comes up through the stone and the frogs stop for a breath. Earthworms answer it too. They read a tremor in the ground as a mole and rise away from it, and a minute later the till band and the crust of the rim are threaded with them. A hen blackbird has worked this out. She waits on the leaning alder among the siskins, which hang upside down on its cones and take no notice of her. When the pebbles tick down she drops to the turf and begins. She does it at the same intervals all day, and she is fatter than any blackbird in the carr.

Then there are the frogs, who hold the centre of the place. The raft that carries their spawn was not an accident of the collapse. It is jackdaw work, three springs of failed sticks dropped from the ledges, and it has drifted in and felted together around the island. The alders shed onto it, and it rides the tide. The frogs did not learn to use it. The ones that laid at the high line left nothing behind, the ones that laid among the twigs left froglets, and now those froglets are grown.

The smallest males on the raft this March are two years old. They hatched here. They have never been in water that did not hum and drop away at night, or heard their own voices without the rock handing them back. They grip the twigs and purr slightly under the pitch of the fans, so that the two sounds beat against each other in slow pulses. The females, coming up from the bottom, swim toward that.

By June the tadpoles from this year's clumps will have their legs. Then, on some warm wet evening, the green stripes on the walls will start to move. A froglet weighs almost nothing, and wet moss will hold it. They go up the seep-lines by the hundred, past the saxifrage and the webs and the jackdaw ledges. Most of them lose their hold at the overhang and fall back the full two seconds into the pool. Some get through the dangling roots and over the crust. They scatter into the carr and live there on ground that keeps still.

In two years or three, on a wet night in February, the survivors will turn and walk back toward the place where the trees stop. They will reach the edge and go over it.
