They are a small, close-grained people, and the room has worn them as it has worn the crawlway.

**The bodies**

From the lip they are first a movement on the ledges, unhurried and continuous, like the beads running on the walls. Up close they are compact, heavy in the calf and forearm, and they shine. They grease their skin with the same fat that feeds the lamps, and the damp stands on them in droplets. Where hide covers them they are pale as the moonmilk, and where it doesn't they are smoke-tanned amber, like the lit stone.

The left foot is broader than the right, the big toe splayed. Every climb begins on the left. No one has an account of why. A child who starts on the right is lifted off the wall and set down to begin again.

Their hide shirts are worn glossy at one shoulder, the one that takes the corners. The sleeves stop at the elbow. Their hair is cropped raggedly and at different lengths, because hair is what the wicks are twisted from, and a head grown long is a head the lamps will soon need. The pad of every right thumb is black.

**The talk**

Two of them talking stand side by side, each with a hand on the other's bare forearm, looking at nothing. Both speak at once. Touch needs no turns, so a conversation here is something two people do together, like carrying a load.

A line drawn from elbow to wrist, the way the gutter runs, means yes, or it continues. The same line drawn from wrist to elbow means the crawlway. The flat palm over the ear means the fall, and also here, and always, and, since they have no other sign for it, us.

Lying is hard with a pulse under the fingertips. Quarrels are conducted at arm's length, gripping, and cannot be walked away from without ending them. The old, whose skin has gone loose, must be pressed harder. People thinking tap their own thighs, so thought is visible, and a knapper stuck on a difficult core will be seen arguing with his own knee.

The flicker across the chamber is a coarse tongue with few words: come, food, born, dying. Children who play at it are cuffed.

They do have voices. A laugh is a shaking of the shoulders, and a sob on a wet face in a wet room goes unseen. But a mother and child, or two who share a hide, will press brow to brow and hum. The sound travels through the bone, under the fall. It is the only use they have for the throat, and it is not for saying anything.

**The hearing**

They are not deaf. Their ears are narrow and exact. Any of them can pull one note from sixty.

When a ringing-stone cracks, the household stands lost on its own ledge. A knapper is brought. He thins a new blade flake by flake while someone holds it under the spout, and he watches the people rather than the stone. When their shoulders come down, he stops.

The very old lose the high notes first. They go home by trailing a hand along the wall and counting jambs, or a grandchild walks ahead of them.

No one lingers in the quiet cell, but most have at some time leaned their head through its doorway to hear themselves swallow. They come away careful, as if carrying a full basin.

**The sleeps**

There are three sleeps, and each person belongs to one. Each sleeping-hide has three owners and is never cold. A person lies down in the hollow and the warmth of the one before, and is woken by the hand of the one after.

These are particular kin. The one before is someone a person has seen all their life mostly as a turned back, a breathing shape, a warmth left behind. The two may never have had a long talk. The hand on the shoulder is the same hand for forty years.

When a sleeper dies, the hide has a cold turn. That is what grief is here: lying down and finding the hollow unwarmed. It lasts until the household shifts someone into the gap, usually a child old enough to sleep alone. The child is told on the arm, firmly, whose warmth this was.

**The offices**

There are none. The lamp is fed by whoever is passing. The narrowing door is chipped by whoever notices, and the black drop is thumbed away by whoever is nearest. Nobody could say who last fed a given lamp, and the question would not make sense to them.

The households on good chert have followed their seams back into the rock. They sleep in rooms shaped like the seam and hand out blades to whoever comes to the door. The crickets are left alone in the same way that the lamps are left where they sit.

When children are lowered over the sump to touch an olm, it is the one time in their lives that no hand is on their arm and nothing is being said to them. The olm does not move. They come up counted. Adults at rest rub forefinger against thumb without knowing it, the way one worries at a word.

**The count**

There are perhaps two hundred of them, as there are three olms. No one can say whether the olms are the same three.

When a hide has a fourth and no turn to give them, the fourth goes up. It is usually someone young, and it is done without ceremony. The line is drawn wrist to elbow on the arm of each person in the household. At the lip the flat palm is laid over the ear. Then they go into the tube, on belly and knees.

The bundles that come down are found at the last bend, and no one in the town sees the hands that leave them. The gut cord on them is tied in the town's own knots.

So the first rule holds for more than the lamps. The town is fed where it sits, by its own people going past above it.

Those who go say nothing afterward, since no one hears from them. But the hiss and the bass must fall away behind them in the tube, bend by bend. The ringing would not. It would stay with them in whatever silence is up there, thin and continuous, and by it a person could tell which way was down.

Below, on the third tier, a knapper sits in the doorway where a knapper has always sat. He is lit from the side on which the knapper there has always been lit, and the water strikes the same note beside him. On the ledge behind him a hand comes down on a shoulder. One person rises and another lies down in the hollow, which is still warm.
