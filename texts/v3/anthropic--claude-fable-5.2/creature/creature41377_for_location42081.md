The first answer is the plant. There is one of it. Every stem in the six streets comes off the same rhizome, so the terraces are a single body standing up in a few hundred places, and the kerbs are the same body lying down. It keeps hours. It presses in the cold and slackens after, and at the peak it sweats from every collar. It has no head end. It has made glass and never used it to look at anything. If the question is who lives here, the plant has the oldest claim, and the snails, shrews, bats, owls, moths and eels lodge with it.

But the plant builds to measure, and it takes the measure from whatever is already waiting. That leaves a few things in the town unaccounted for.

Every ground-floor chamber has one opening, a split between two flutes, about five feet high and eighteen inches wide, with the edges turned outward. No owl needed that. At the works the spindles stand in their row at the height of a waist, with a gangway beside them one body wide. The press bed is thirty inches off the floor. When the press is up, the gap takes a hand and a forearm, and the stroke is set for someone who can get the hand in and out in eleven seconds. Nothing in the town can do that. An owl needs minutes to fall its own length.

In some of the ground-floor chambers, on every street, the floor is not level. There is a shallow place in it as long as a body, wider at the shoulder and the hip, where the plant came up around something that was lying on the open silt and closed its walls afterwards. The silica in the hollow is smooth. A palm dragged across it comes away unmarked. The split is always in the wall nearest the hollow, and it looks as though each was made from inside, by something leaving while the wall was still soft.

From each split a line of footprints goes out and turns down the street toward the works. The silt has kept every one. They are a stride apart, and each holds a bead of water from the last rain. There are forty-three lines, and none has reached the works yet.

As far as can be judged, they set out when the wheel first turned. They take one step per rain. When the bulbs go from orange to yellow each line gains a print, and when the bulbs sink to brown the walkers stand, and go on standing until the pressure comes round again. On the plan a street is short. Inside, there is a great deal of it. The works has been running without load for the whole of the commute, and the press comes down on the empty bed to keep the place open.

The walkers can't be seen, since no lamp lights anything. They are known the way the moths are, except that a moth blinks a lamp and one of these puts it out. Seen from the low end of a street, a few of the orange points are missing, low down. They are the same few for a whole cycle. After the rain it is the next few along.

In the rain they are plainer. The drops come off the collars floor by floor, and at the head of each line there is an oval the rain does not reach, two feet by one, dry while the silt around it beads. That is how the count of forty-three is made.

Now and then a snail leaves the wall, crosses the silt, and goes up one of them. The trail climbs through the air in a slow turn to the height of a shoulder and stops there, and the rasping starts. After so much standing there is evidently something on them worth grazing.

Everything in the town crosses the remove at its own speed, so a walker does not arrive all at once. The weight is at the front, where the newest print is. The warmth comes a few yards behind, a length of street where the air is not quite so cold in the teeth. Behind that is the smell, wet wool and tallow, lying over the cucumber and slate. The sound of the step comes last. It reaches a listener two doors off several rains later, a soft knock with the edges gone, when the foot that made it has long since moved on. Gathered up, each of them would be about the size of a person. At present each is about forty feet long.

The works would call them hands. They are alone in the usual amount of room, and no line comes within reach of another. Once in a few hundred prints there is one set crosswise, where a walker has turned to look back up the street. What it sees is what anyone sees there: orange points in rows, with some of them out. The ones that are out are the others. Then the next print points at the works again.

One line is ahead of the rest. It starts from the end house of the north terrace, where the split in the wall is only four feet ten, and it is now eleven prints from the works door. The silt at the low end is wetter, from the wheel, and takes a sharper print, so this is the walker about whom most can be said. The prints are short and narrow. They were made by wooden soles shod with iron, a curved iron at the toe and another at the heel, real iron like the dynamo housing. The left has seven nail heads. The right has six and a hole. The right foot comes down lighter than the left.

Beside every fourth standing place there is a ring in the silt, four inches across, where a can with a rolled rim has been set down and picked up again. The ring is a little shallower each time. Whatever is in the can has been rationed for the distance, and going by the last ring there is about enough left to reach the door.

Twenty prints back, pressed flat into the silt, is a hairpin of actual drawn brass wire. Nothing says where the brass came from. Three prints after it there is a crosswise print with the toe dug in. She looked back for it. The print after that points at the works.

Where she has stood facing the door, rain after rain, the remove has given a little. Up the street the press is thinner than a watch under a pillow. At her newest print the tick of it arrives with its edges on, and the gap between one stroke and the next can be counted. The last three prints are deepest at the toe. Between her and the door there are eleven still to make, and it is the first short distance the town has.
