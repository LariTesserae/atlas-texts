**What lives in the basin**

Most of what lives here is cliff bird, by sheer count. A cliff bird is a dark, narrow thing that spends its whole life at an address. Its ledge is so many sockets up from a certain ring of lichen and so many flutes in from a certain crack, and it knows this the way a hand knows the way to its mouth. It does not know its mate's voice, because nothing here has a voice in any useful sense. It knows that the bird standing in that place is the one. Two ledges over, the same bird would be a stranger. They scream all the same, the way a body shivers.

Because no season comes, nothing on the wall happens all at once. On any day there are eggs, naked chicks, fledglings stepping off into the wind for the first time, and old birds that do not come back to their places. The wall is never fuller or emptier. It keeps a single line, like the lake.

The waders are pale all the way round. The ground lights them from below, so they have no dark side and look cut out and set upright in the brine. Each wears a white cuff of mineral on its legs, at the same height on every bird.

The rodents have kept the whistle and lost the use of it. The whistle has become something to look at. An animal reared on a block with its chest out and its head tipped back is a shape that means *above*, and the others go under the stones without having heard a thing.

The grazers walk the same trails every time. Each step rings a plate, the note comes up through the leg bone, and a herd crossing its usual ground plays its usual phrase without knowing it. I think a wrong note underfoot would turn them back.

The scavengers are to the colony what the dry air is to the lake. They take the difference between what hatches and what the ledges can hold, and they are the only ones that cross the middle, high above it.

The cushions are the slowest residents. One of them widens by a finger's breadth while generations of grazers are born and worn out beside it.

**What they have in common**

Every one of them has given something up. The birds have given up being recognized, the rodents being heard, and the grazers calling to their young across open ground. This happened long ago, the way the stones fell long ago. Any animal that needed to be heard has already left.

And all of them keep to the edges. At first that looks like a matter of food and water, since the seeps, the brine, and the ledges are all at the margins. But it also looks like the behavior of animals in a room where something very large is lying in the middle.

**The note**

The middle is occupied. What lives there is the sound.

It is older than the colony, as old as the first flute the wind cut. The notch feeds it without pause, so it has never once stopped. The bowl is its body. The standing ripples on the lake are the one place it touches down and leaves a print. Every scream, bray, and clink goes into it, and it hands them back as one thing that belongs to none of them.

It is tempting to call it a beast, but it isn't one. It wants nothing and hunts nothing. It is closer to a held note, every key down at once, which is to say a sound with no before or after in it. That is the real inhabitant of the basin. The tail-flashes, the postures, and the ledge-counting are the shapes living things take when they live beside it. It did not drive them to the edges. It is simply already everywhere else.

**The six paces**

There is one spot where the note cannot feel itself, and that spot is the only place in the basin where one thing comes after another.

A grazer goes there to give birth. For as long as she lies on the stained stone, she can hear her own breath and then a second, smaller breath. The young one hears it too. If it is male, it will never hear breathing again. If it is female, it will hear it again each time she comes back to give birth.

The people come to the same spot, rarely. They arrive from below, breathing on purpose. They carry their own pale stone for edges, because the dark plates here are good only for ringing. They burn dung and dead grass. The cushions near the fire ring, hard as wood, bear no cuts. Outside the circle the people are like everything else in the basin, all hands and posture.

Inside it they do the things that need an ear. Knapping is one, because a worker listens to the stone to know where it will break. The row of plates is another. The wall plays every note at once and forever. The plates play one note, then the next, going down, and then they are finished. I think that is what the people climb here for. In the low country, I imagine, quiet is ordinary and goes unnoticed. Here it has walls, and a small sound made inside it has a beginning and an end.

They do not stay. The plates stay, propped and tuned, waiting in the only place where there would be anyone to hear them.

So the basin holds one long note and everything that has learned to live beside it. In the six paces near the lake, a wet calf is listening to its mother breathe. In a little while it will stand and walk out into the roar. The first plate it steps on will ring. It will feel that in its leg and take it for the ground.
