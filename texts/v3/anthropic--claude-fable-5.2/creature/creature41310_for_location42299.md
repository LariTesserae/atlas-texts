Nothing lives here in the sense the question usually means. There is no animal on the tableland, and no sign there ever was one. What lives here is the growth, and it is easiest to begin by taking it as a plant, since that is how it first presents to a hand.

By touch it seems to be several plants. There is the leather blade that faces the fire, the stiff blade higher up that rings, the tendril that wraps, the liana that runs as belting, and the root that lies as felt over the clinker. Follow any one of them back and it passes into the others without a join. It is one kind, and very likely one individual. It has no flower, because nothing here would carry pollen, and no fruit, because nothing would carry that off either. It has never set seed. It increases only by being chopped, and the cutting that roots in the spoil bank is a piece of the same body that was burned to make the ash it roots in.

So it is a single plant the size of a market town, immensely old, and almost none of it is old. A shoot's whole life is one trip down an aisle. It comes up in warm ash at the yard's edge, lengthens toward the red seam, browns, chars and drops on the grate. Near the furnace rows that takes weeks. The only parts with any age are the parts left out of the roll: the fused wall in the cooling halls, the wad in the gasholder, the columns standing in the stacks. Those are its heartwood, dense and slow and barely warm. They are not where it lives. It lives at its tips, and its tips are always arriving somewhere and cooking.

But a plant that cannot use light, cannot shed a seed and cannot move a cutting ten metres on its own is only half an organism. The other half is iron. The rakes are its teeth, the fireboxes its stomachs, the conveyors its bowel. The displacers are lungs in the plainest way, moving the same air in and out. It has been growing into this half for ages. It began with the belts and went on to the bearing sleeves and then the rails. In the oldest furnace houses the rake tines wore to stubs long ago, and what draws the char across the grate now is a row of resin-glazed spurs growing down from the rake bar. They burn back at the ends and lengthen at exactly the rate they burn.

Taken with its iron, it is closer to an animal than a plant. It has a hide, the dead thatch, which keeps its heat in and the night out. It has a gut and it has breath. And it holds a temperature. That is what the count is. An animal that loses warmth in one limb sends it to another, and no one enforces that either. It has forty-odd hearts, and it is warm-blooded in a body made of wood, worn steel and brick.

It sounds asleep. The flywheels breathe through the nose, the plate ticks, and a gallery lets go and comes down onto the mass like a sleeper turning over. The frantic part goes on underneath, the way it does in anything asleep, in the lengthening and the turning-over of substance.

It is not hostile. It has no thorn, no sting and no bitterness in the leaf, because nothing has ever tried to eat it but itself. The resin that skins over a cut in seconds is not armour. A cut is a wet place, and wet places close.

The wrap is not predation either. It is the same gesture it makes to a pulley or a bus bar. It knows two things about any object, whether it is warm and whether it is moving, and it has one response to each. A visitor would be the coldest, wettest thing it had ever held. For a while the nearest leaves would lean off, as the rim face leans off the night air. That would last as long as the water did.

Standing near a furnace row, though, it gets harder to say that the plant keeps the fires. The plant takes nothing from the sun. Everything it is made of comes up from the tar in the sumps beneath the sumps, or out of its own smoke, which it takes back before the smoke can climb the stack. And everything it is made of is on its way to a grate. Wood here is only the shape fuel takes on the walk to the firebox.

Seen that way, the inhabitant is the fire. It is one fire, lit no one knows when, sitting on a reservoir of old tar it could have gone through in a season. Instead it found a way to burn slowly. It grew leaves to bank itself. It grew a canopy to keep its heat and took the flue gas back to keep its substance. It let the rakes ration it, and when the rakes wore out it grew rakes.

It burns at the speed of growth, which is fast for a plant and very slow for a fire. The smoulder-fronts are the same fire going from room to room without bothering with the aisles. The red seams round the firedoors are the places where it shows.

There is one more thing about it, and it takes longer to notice than a hand or an ear allows. There are always the same number of seams, but they are never the same seams. One chokes and goes dark. Across the works another opens, and a quarter's worth of leaves swing round to it. Any single change is nothing. Over the ages, though, the pattern of lit hearths drifts across the dome like a slow constellation, out of the east yards, through the middle rows and round by the power house.

This is the only locomotion it has, a shifting of weight from one foot to another. It has been walking around the inside of itself for longer than its oldest heartwood has stood, on a circuit with no destination. It grazes its own regrowth and leaves each quarter to thicken behind it before it comes round again.

It cannot leave. At the rim it stops in a wall four storeys high, and the wall leans back. It has no word for what is out there, only an absence of warmth that every blade turns from. It will not get larger. It may, in some very long run, get smaller as the tar goes down. It shows no sign of knowing this, and the count has not yet dropped by one.

Before dawn a film of water forms on the underside of the outermost roof plate, an arm's length from the last black leaf. It is the only other thing that ever comes to the tableland. It comes every night, and it is gone when the plate warms. The thing that lives here has never touched it, and no leaf has ever turned that way.
