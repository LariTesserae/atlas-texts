Start with one of them, because one at a time is how they come along the wall.

A worker is about as long as a grain of rice and nearly the same colour, except for the head, which is hard and the yellow of weak tea. The rest is skin so thin that the last thing swallowed shows through it: grey for clay, brown for grass, almost clear for water. There are no eyes. Some workers are male and some female, and it makes no difference, since none of them will breed. What a worker knows of the world is a strip a few body-lengths wide: the wall under one antenna, the taste of the floor, and above all the wetness of the air against a skin that dries like cut fruit. Alone in the open it would walk in loops for an hour or two and then stop.

It is hard to call this a someone. It has no cell of its own, no rest that anyone has noticed, and a life of a few months. In place of a self it has a short list of strong preferences: toward damp, away from moving air, toward the taste of its own kind, against any gap. It also cannot keep anything. Whatever it swallows it will give up to the next mouth that asks, and that mouth to the next, so that a drink taken at the bottom of the shaft is, by the following night, a little way inside everyone. Where one of them ends is not a question they could make sense of.

They do differ. The youngest have never left the gardens. They eat the raw grass and build the comb, and they are kept in the middle, where it is warm and nothing can reach them. The oldest go out at night to the stems. That is the work that gets a worker killed, and it falls to those with the least time left.

The soldiers are another thing again. Each has a rust-red head as large as the rest of the body, jaws like a pair of curved thorns, and almost nothing behind that. Those jaws cannot chew. A soldier stands with its mouth raised until a worker stops and fills it. It is a door with legs, fed until the day it is needed.

The two in the low cell are unlike all the rest. They have eyes, dark and faceted and of no further use. On their shoulders are four small triangular scales where wings broke off. For one damp evening at the end of the last rains they flew in the open, and they are the only things in the cone that have ever felt weather. Nothing about them now suggests they recall it.

The king will stay as he is. The queen will not. Over the coming years she will swell to the length of a finger, unable to turn round, and the cell will be torn out and rebuilt around her again and again. She may live twenty years. Neither of them gives an order. They are fed, cleaned and licked without pause. What is licked from her travels outward with the water, and by it a worker in the furthest empty gallery is kept informed that she is alive.

The grey-white nap in the gardens has to be counted as living here as much as anything does. It arrived as spores in a gut. It cannot survive outside these rooms, and the insects cannot survive without it, since they cannot digest what they gather and it cannot gather. It is tempting to call it a crop. But the gardens are the warm, crowded, defended centre of the place, and the comb, built at the top, is eaten away from the bottom only once the fungus has finished with it. Most of the stale breath that seeps out through the shell each morning is the fungus's and not the insects'. A good share of the building is done to hold it at the wetness and warmth it prefers. It would be as fair to say that the fungus keeps a few thousand pairs of jaws.

It has also never yet been all that it can be. Somewhere in that nap is the ability to drive a stalk up through a forearm's depth of hard clay and open a pale cap in the air. That takes rain.

So the description keeps sliding off the parts. Put them together and what lives here is one animal, not yet a year old. All its insects gathered into a cupped hand would weigh about what a hen's egg weighs. It has spread that weight through a body shin-high and deeper than that below ground, most of which is not flesh. Its skin is clay of its own making, and the skin heals. Its lung is the cone. Its stomach is another species, kept in rooms. Its throat is the width of a grass stem and runs straight down to what is left of last year's stream. Every night parts of it leave through the soil to feed, and most of them come back. Every few minutes it gains an egg. Some afternoons it loses a few hundred of itself, carried off crosswise in black jaws, and it shuts the wound and does not count.

Nothing in it knows that it has three spires. It has no place to stand and look from. What it remembers, it remembers in its walls, as a taste that says *here, again*. If it has a temperament, it is fastidious and slightly anxious, as suits something whose real enemies are dry air and openings. It cannot leave a hole alone. It drinks its own dew. It has no opinion about the light that gets into it.

For something so careful, it is wildly hopeful. It builds ten rooms for every one it can fill. The empty halls are the most accurate portrait of it there is: the outline of the animal it expects to become, built first and occupied later.

It already has company, as any body does. The silverfish and the beetle have learned its taste well enough to stand in the circulation of food and be fed as if they were part of it. Mites ride it, and springtails graze its inner skin. A skink has borrowed its warmth for four eggs. A bush keeps feeling toward its breath with white roots. Each dusk a gecko settles on the outside and waits for it to open somewhere. At well under a year it is already a country, with residents who have never heard of each other.

It is still unfinished in the way that matters most to its own kind. Every one of its thousands is a dead end. It will be some years before it is large enough to grow members with eyes and wings and let them go. Until then it is all body and no offspring. It has never fruited and never flown.

It has never been rained on, and it has no means of expecting rain. Only two of its members ever met any, and they are in the dark at the bottom, being fed. When the air over the streambed thickens, it registers a change in dampness at the spire tips. It answers that the way it answers everything, with a little more clay.
