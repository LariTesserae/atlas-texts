Several thousand people live here, and the first thing to know about them is that they are quiet. They have never raised their voices because it has never done anything. An argument in a lane looks like two people working very fast at something small held between them.

You would know them anywhere by their shins and palms. Everyone fell on the rock as a child, and everyone carries the fine pale scars, a mesh across the knees and the heel of each hand. Their hair is cropped to the scalp with a glass blade because it would never dry. Their skin is pale and soft, like something kept in water, and they rub it with bird oil against the wet, so they smell like their lamps. The boat crews are the exception. They go out past the edge of the steam and come back brown to the elbow. They are the only ones who have seen their own shadows, and they have a sign for the moon that the town regards as boat-talk.

Most people are half deaf by the time they are grown. Nobody notices it happening, and it costs them nothing.

Because the hands are the voice, some things follow.

- **Carrying:** A person carrying a load is mute, or mumbles with chin and eyebrows.
- **Injury:** A knapper who has lost a fingertip has an accent for life, and his household learns it.
- **Age:** The old slur.
- **Light:** You turn so the white wall is behind your hands. It is rude to speak with your back to a dark doorway.
- **Privacy:** Anything said outdoors can be read from four roofs away. Nothing is overheard, but everything is seen.

Privacy is the voice, indoors, mouth against ear. It is used for the name and the few things that go with the name. Most people's voices have been heard by five or six others in their lives. The voices are hoarse and unpracticed, and nobody knows whether theirs is a good one.

They reckon by the tide. At the flood they stand in the low lanes in warm brackish water and scrub the droppings off. At the ebb the knappers and kelp-cutters walk out to the margin. Age is not counted in anything that passes. The sign is one flat hand with the other hand's knuckles stacked on its back, one knuckle for each roof built above your own. A white-haired man with nothing on his roof is signed as young, and he is treated as young.

Take one stack, four rooms deep, known across the lanes by its knock: two quick, one held.

The top room is the whitest, because its plaster is new. Terns distrust fresh ledges, so it is also the poorest. A boatman lives there with a woman from another stack, whose hand-name is two fingers drawn across the opposite wrist. They live on fish and on what comes up from below. Over the door he keeps a straight piece of driftwood the length of a forearm, which came from his family. It is used for nothing, and it is the only straight line in the room. Their infant has a hand-name, a fist opened against the cheek, and no other name yet. It watches hands all day. It cries like any infant, and nobody has ever heard it.

They built on the roof of a woman whose hand-name is a pinch at the earlobe, and she aged by one knuckle the day their slabs went up. She is the stack's egger. Her ledges are old and crusted, and the birds trust them. She climbs the outside of the house step by step, past beaks that stab at her ankles, and she knows every nest by its pair. Since there is no season, each nest is its own calendar, and she keeps the count on the shells. She takes the first egg and dabs the second with orange slime from the pool rim, which means it is to be left alone. Her forearms are white to the elbow and her thumbs are orange. The top room owes her a share of eggs from the first tern that settles on it, and will owe it as long as the walls stand.

She built on a knapper, whose hand-name is a knuckle rapped twice on the breastbone. He is short two fingertips on the left hand, and strangers ask him to repeat himself. He leaves at the ebb with a sealskin of hammerstones and sits at the seam, striking blades he feels and does not hear. He comes home with new cuts over the old ones. He has two knuckles of age, and he is paid in eggs from both rooms above him. He is the one who cuts the stack's hair.

He built on a woman whose hand-name is the thumb drawn once down the side of the nose. Whether he is her son is not something the hands record. There is one sign for a child you bore and a person who built on you.

She built on rock. Her room is the bottom of the list, and she pried its slabs from the flow herself with bone wedges. It has been plastered so many times that the corners have gone round and the door has closed in. She made it wide enough to carry a seal quarter through, and now she turns sideways. Hers is the only worn floor in the stack, dulled grey in the shape of where people have slept. She no longer has sandals made.

The fumarole is hers, and so is the cistern. Eggs come down to her and go back up steamed in gut bags, and the sulfur water goes up with them. At high tide the lane comes to her sill, and she lifts the lamp bowl onto its shelf without looking. Crabs walk through the room and she lets them. Nobody here eats crab, because everybody knows what the crabs eat.

Her eyes have gone milky and her hearing went long ago, so people speak into her hands. They put their fingers inside hers and sign there, and she answers slowly with knuckles that will not close. The stack says she mumbles. But she still has her voice, and at the bottom of a stack that is what matters, because the spoken names come up from the bottom.

When a child is old enough to climb to the ledges, it is brought down to her room. She puts her mouth against its ear and says a name she cannot hear herself say. If the child is deaf, she lays its fingers on her throat. She has done this for every person above her. Each of them knows three or four of those names. She knows them all.

Her own name was given to her in another stack by a woman long since lowered through the skylight. The man who used it went the same way. No one living has said it. She is called by the thumb down the nose forty times a day, and by a hand on the shoulder more often than that, and by the knock through her wall whenever a load of eggs is coming down. She feels all of it.

Under her floor is the tube. After dark the petrels come in beneath her and churr in the rock, and she lies on the warm glass with it in her hip and her shoulder, and sleeps.
