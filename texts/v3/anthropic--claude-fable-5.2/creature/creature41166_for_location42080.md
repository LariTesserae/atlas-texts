Most of what lives in the basin is passing through it, even the things that never leave. The pale fish go where the water column leans. The amphipods come up when the cold comes down. The snails hold on, which is a way of being moved more slowly. Only the carriers are anywhere on purpose, so if the basin has an inhabitant it is the carrier, and the rest is weather.

From above, which is how the diving birds see one, a carrier is a cobble slightly out of line. It is the grey-brown of the paving, with a net of fine dark lines over the back where the joints would be, and it lies with its head into whatever the water is doing at that moment. It is as long as a forearm and deeper than you expect. The skin is thick and loose, with no scales you could find with a fingernail. The eyes are small and set high, and probably not much use, since nothing here holds still long enough to be looked at. The fins that matter are the pair behind the head, broad and stiff, with a few rays much heavier than the others. The fish stands on them. It plants them in two joints of the paving and leans forward, the way a person leans on a table.

It is heavy for a fish. The bones are dense, the swim bladder has shrunk to almost nothing, and a carrier that stops swimming goes down like a dropped tool. But it is not heavy enough. Anything lighter than a fist moves, and a carrier, weighed in water, comes in just under. Empty-mouthed, it is shoved along the floor with everything else, bracing and losing ground. With a cobble between its lips it stays where it stands. This takes a while to see, because the eye wants the stone to be a burden. It is closer to an anchor.

So a carrier holds a stone nearly all its life. It rests with one, wedged in the lee of a wall-stump, and the mouth is built for this. The lips are the only pale thing on the animal, two thick pads callused to something like horn. There are no teeth in front. The corners of the mouth do not close, and the fish breathes through them, drawing water in past the stone on either side. In an old carrier the jaw no longer shuts at all. It has set, over decades, around an absence the size of a cobble.

The curve of that absence is worth attention. Every stone in the basin is worn to the same soft curve, and it is the curve of the roof of a carrier's mouth. Each cobble has been lifted more times than could be counted, and each time it lost a little to the rasp. Whether the stones were worn to fit the mouth, or the mouth has grown over generations to fit the stones, cannot be determined from here. They fit.

What a carrier cannot do with a stone in its mouth is eat. The food is in the joints: amphipods, snails, the grey slick on the lee side of things. To get at it the fish has to put its stone down, and a stone put down on open floor is gone before the fish has turned around. A stone put into a socket stays, at least for the length of a meal. So the carrier feeds where there is a gap. It sets the cobble and works along the nearby joints with its fins dug in, slipping backward the whole time. When it can hold no longer, it takes up the nearest stone that will come. This is never the one it just set, which is seated. The nearest loose one lies down-current, where the water has been working, and the fish swims it back up because that is the only direction in which it can still steer.

Seen this way, there is no building at all. There is an animal that needs ballast and needs to eat and cannot do both at once, and the floor is what that looks like after long enough. The account may even stretch to the walls. Lee gathers food, a stone set against a standing stone makes more lee, and a habit like that will eventually draw lines. It does not stretch to the angles, or the terraces, or the rim.

It also does not cover the turn. After the stone is in, the fish turns it once with its snout. This costs time it does not have, with an empty mouth and the water pushing. What it is doing, as far as can be told, is listening.

A loose cobble ticks in its socket. A displaced one rolls and knocks. The carrier is built to receive these sounds. Its head is pitted all over with open pores, lines of them running back along the jaw and flank, and a stone shifting forty meters off is as plain to it as a touch. That is how it finds the loose ones. It only appears to be nosing along the paving, when in fact it is going to the tick. When it has set a stone, it turns it until the ticking stops, and only then does it leave.

It would be easy to say it wants the quiet. Nobody knows what a fish wants. But the basin is quieter than its violence would suggest, and this is some of the reason.

The same hearing keeps them apart. A stone going home makes a short dull knock that carries a long way along the floor, and a carrier that hears one close by moves off. Each works inside a circle of its own knocking. There are a few hundred in the whole basin, spread more evenly than chance would spread them, each out of sight of any other.

The exception is the mound. A carrier's ordinary knocks wander, one here and the next thirty meters on. Once a year, when the meltwater ropes at the rim are thickest, one fish begins bringing every stone to the same place. The knocks come from a single spot, at a steady interval, for days. Another carrier, hearing that, comes toward it instead of going away.

They do not touch. She leaves her eggs among the heaped cobbles, a few to each stone, each egg heavy and sticky as a drop of pitch. He passes over them and she goes. He keeps adding stones until a surge spreads the heap across fifty meters of floor. This looks like failure, and it is how the eggs get anywhere. Each scattered cobble carries two or three glued to its underside. The carriers that find those cobbles ticking will seat them in the floor and the walls, eggs and all.

The young hatch pale, no longer than an eyelash and light as silt, and the water takes them at once. For years they live as something else. They travel in the bands of pale fish, poured around the wall-stumps with the rest and taken by the birds with the rest. Nothing marks them except that when a band slows, a few of its members sink slightly. Then one season a fish is too heavy to be carried. It falls out of the band, lands on the paving, is rolled, and closes its mouth on the first thing that will hold it down. Usually this is a pebble the size of a knuckle. The small ones go about with pebbles for some years, and they are the ones who pack the joints.

They grow slowly in this cold and live a long time. Nobody has counted, but a carrier the length of a forearm has been at it for decades. The lips go first, worn through to a hard pale rim. The jaw stiffens. An old one feeds badly, because putting a stone down and taking another up has become slow, so it sets its stone less often and holds it longer. In the end it does not set it down. It wedges itself into the angle where two walls meet, fins planted, head into the current, and stays.

The amphipods come up out of the joints for it, as they come for anything the storm delivers. In a week there is a cobble sitting in a basket of bone. In a month there is only the cobble, seated in the angle and silent. It is the one stone in a carrier's life that it places without turning.

There is one resting now in the lee of a wall, heavy by exactly one stone. Overhead a wave breaks. The thud passes down through the water and through the fish, and every cobble in the basin lifts a hair in its socket and comes down again. Nearly all of them come down true. Somewhere up-current one does not, and begins to tick. The carrier shifts its fins.
