You will not see it whole. The hall allows an arm and a half, and it stands taller than that, so it arrives in parts, and each part can be taken for something else. A shoulder is a machine casing under its finger of moss. A forearm is ribbon-weed hung over a pipe. The feet are two clumps of cryptocoryne in the silt between the beds, liver-red like all the others, until one of them lifts, trails a little smoke of silt, and sets itself down a pace further on.

It is easier to begin with where it has been. The iron is smooth as a gun barrel wherever anything rubs it, and the smooth places are not scattered. There is the top of the guard rail round each flywheel, at the height of a hand. There is one corner of the big planer's table, at the height of a hip, where a body would cut in close to save a step. There is the lid of the brass oil cup on every bearing pedestal along both line shafts, some sixty lids, each one clean of film with its hinge worn loose. Join them and they make a circuit: out of the well-house, down the east side bearing by bearing, round the south engine, and back up the west. Anyone who worked in a mill could name it. It is the oiler's round, walked before the engines were given steam.

So you look for a man, and for a while a man is what you find. It has a man's height and a man's stoop, and a man's habit of putting a hand to each column before stepping round it. There is a cap. There is a row of buttons down the front. He was the engine tenter, which is what a mill called the one who minded its engines, and he was the last. Either he was in his chair by the south engine when the water came up, or he came back down to it afterward. The slate on the wall there might have said which, but chalk was the first thing the water took.

Look closer, and it is made the way the belts are made. The weed rooted in him first, and when he went it kept the shape, strand for strand. Ribbon-weed runs the long way down the limbs where the muscle ran, bound across by its own runners. Water-moss lies a finger deep on what was level: the shoulders, the crown of the cap. The beard is hornwort, which has no roots anywhere and holds on out of habit. The boots lasted longest, being leather and dubbin, and by the time they failed they were packed with silt and the cryptocorynes had them, so it carries its own ground about with it. Under the green it is packed and peat-brown, like the bobbins.

Of the man there is only what the water had no use for:

- **Buttons:** Seven brass buttons run down the front, held where they were sewn. One is worn bright where the left arm crosses it in walking.
- **Hobnails:** They are still in their rows under each foot, black-skinned, set in the root-mat like pips.
- **A watch case:** It is closed and grown over, low on the left where the waistcoat pocket was.

The rest went the way of the loose lime in the brick, in the first years.

The belts still carry a little power. This carries something too, and what it carries is the round. It goes a pace to each turn of the shaft. At every pedestal it lifts the lid of the oil cup and lets it fall. There is nothing in the cups but water and the film that serves better than oil, and it lifts them anyway. When the governor on the south engine ticks, it stops where it stands for one full turn, the way a tenter stops when the note of his engine changes. Nothing follows the tick, and it goes on. At the slate it draws two fingers down the stone. Everything else in the hall that keeps still is furred or filmed, but the slate has one clean stripe.

That is the part of it that is his. It is the smaller part, and it is getting smaller.

Any weed long enough to touch a shaft is taken up, and it is weed. Over the years it has given the shafts most of its fingers, a sleeve's length of one arm, and the peak of the cap twice. What is taken grows back about as fast, but it grows back the way weed grows, toward the glass, and not the way he was. None of the strands that rooted in him are still in it. It has been replaced right through, more than once, and each replacement was a little more leaf and a little less likeness. He stooped. It has begun to lean, and the lean is upward.

It keeps hours he never kept. In the morning, when the squares brighten and the curtains of ribbon-weed tilt toward them, it tilts with them, and under each panel it stands longer than the round requires. A tenter had no reason to stand under the pavement lights. It has one, because it is feeding. The ramshorn snails come down off the glass onto its shoulders and work there, so it goes about written over in the same loops as the light.

By afternoon it is beaded like every other lit leaf, silver all over, and the beads lift it. First the hobnails stop marking the silt. Then it leaves the floor altogether and goes up slowly, feet last, until the moss of the cap meets the air lens in the crown of the vault. It hangs there for an hour or two with its face in the only air in the hall, and has no use for it.

In the evening the beads let go, the water takes the gas back, and it comes down as slowly as it rose. The hall's one current has carried it a few paces south in the meantime, and it settles wherever that leaves it, across a machine bed or along a lathe, and lies there until the squares brighten. The moss holds a pressed shape for months. The beds and tables are printed with them, a man's length each, the old ones fainter, some lying across the catfish's. This is why the round, which took him the first half-hour of a shift, takes it nine or ten days.

Once in each round it comes to whichever machine the catfish is under, and stops. The barbels go over the cryptocorynes, the hobnails, the silt between. The catfish is old enough to have lain under these machines when the boots had feet in them. It makes the same inspection every time, and what it concludes it keeps to itself.

Then there are the shadows. When a foot covers two prisms, then the next two, it turns its front toward the glass and goes after it for a pace or two, already left behind, as far as the next column. There it stops, puts a hand to the iron, and takes up the round again. A plant will follow light. Nothing in the weed accounts for following the lack of it, and nothing in the round does either, since the tenter never left his engines to walk along under strangers. This belongs to neither of them. It may be the first thing that is only its own.

The second is at the south end. After dark one panel stays lit a weak yellow from the lamp-post above, and on the evenings it comes down within a few paces of that panel, it does not lie down. It stands in the tall weed under the glass with one arm raised. At an arm and a half you cannot tell which strands are the bed's and which are the arm, only that the tallest of them nearly reach the shaft. That arm has had light when nothing else in the hall had any. It is longer than the other by a forearm, with fingers past anything he owned.

If it touches, it will be taken up, a hand-width an hour. It might be wound in whole and go south in rags to the bank at the grating. Or it might grow about as fast as it is wound, as the weed here generally does. Then it would stand under the lamp for good, paying itself out to the shaft, a belt from the floor to the line, carrying a little power. That would be a settlement of the usual kind. Or it is only reaching for the lamp, and the shaft happens to lie between.

It has not touched yet. Each night gains it less than you could read off a rule, and it has about two fingers left to go.
