About a hundred and eighty people live inside the kerb. They were delivered with the town, on the same morning, at the ages the town needed: a harbourmaster of sixty, two midwives, a net-mender, a boy of nine to run messages along a quay. They woke knowing the lanes. Each knew which door was theirs and which neighbour they did not speak to, though not yet why.

Their years are coming in the way the ground is, from the bottom up. Everyone has the last four, which were got at the ordinary rate and are their own. Under those is a stretch like the top of the cores, grey, with nothing sorted in it. Under that, the earliest things have begun to show. A woman of fifty has her mother's kitchen and does not yet have her own wedding. At first the kitchen was a table, a window, and someone at the stove with no face. The face came in the second year. This spring the apron got its pattern. She mentions these arrivals in the lane the way other people would mention letters. The boy is thirteen now. Four of his years are his and nine are on the way.

The middle is still owed. There are couples here who have been married twenty-six years and have had four of them. They were given the ease first. They pass the salt unasked and know each other's breathing in the dark. The courtship is expected. In the meantime they are slightly formal, and avoid asking one another anything the years are going to answer.

Bodies were finished ahead of everything. The net-mender's thumbs are split from brine, and no one here has touched salt water. A man in the long row limps. The fall is to be put in at about his thirty-first year, and he already dislikes the roof it will turn out to have been. The coats came darned at the elbow. There is a man everyone calls Twice, and nobody, including him, has been told what happened twice.

Speech came the same way. They say a thing is as late as the Marren boat, and laugh. There is no boat and no one called Marren. The joke was supplied and the occasion is to follow.

They have the trades of a coast. The harbourmaster walks to the middle bridge twice a day, fifty minutes later each day, and rests his hands where the rail will be until something he feels in the empty channel has turned. His hands come away wet. Then he goes home.

What marks them most is their care about debt. They live in a result, they know it, and they are scrupulous. When a lane gives back a cough no one made, whoever is nearest stops, puts down what they are carrying, and coughs. It is done without embarrassment, like picking up a dropped glove. They talk low and keep their sentences short, since nothing here will carry a long one. One woman on the south side has owed a dropped pan since the first autumn. She carries her pans in both hands. Nothing is said to her, but she is not often handed things.

They are short of room as well, and the shortfall is held in each other. Two kitchens in the long row share a hand's width of shelf, and the women on either side have drunk from the same cup for four years. Each washes it. A man who sleeps against a party wall sleeps, by that hand's width, next door, and both households know his shoulder. The lanes give a finger a year. When the strip finally parts there will be two walls and one cup, and that has not been discussed.

Not everyone on the roll has been delivered. By the east bridge lives a widow. She had the grief from the first morning, complete and already some years worn, and a man's coat on the peg with the right cuff rubbed pale. He is to be put in at about her twenty-fourth year and taken out at her fifty-first. In time she will remember him well. She will not meet him. From the cuff she has worked out that he wrote a great deal. So far that is most of what she has.

Eleven children have been born since the town came. The eldest is three and a half. They are exactly as old as they are, like the sedge. When one of them falls there is the fall, then the graze, then the crying, in that order, and grown people have been seen to stop in the lane and watch it through to the end. The children learned the plover's call from the plovers. They pipe it up and down the lanes, and it is the only sound in the town copied from something that had already happened. They step over the kerb without noticing it is there, and are fetched back. They are measured against the door frames more often than the lanes are measured with the stick.

The hare is old now for a hare. It has gone grey round the mouth at the rate hares do. Its form is in the channel bed under the middle bridge, below the stain, and someone has thought about that.

The someone is the man at the north-east corner. He was delivered like everyone else, on the same morning, about fifty-five by the look of him, with that door for his own. He has a name and it is used. Nothing has come in under him. The first year he waited, as they all did. Then the others began to report kitchens and dogs and the colour of a first boat, and he had the morning of delivery and what came after it. His hands arrived with nothing on them, so no one could say what his trade was to have been. No echo has ever come back off his wall. His steps are flat because four years of one man's boots do nothing to that material.

He is the one who drills the cores. He made the drill himself, and the first cores are crooked. The later ones are straight. He is the only grown person here who has been seen to get better at something. He keeps the cores in his front room, laid in rows by hole and date, and people come to look at their own ground, at the bedding firming up and the ribs arriving on the shells. He shows them, civilly. They thank him and stand a little way in from the wall.

Once a year he drills his own lot. The core comes up paste from top to bottom, as far as the drill will reach. He labels it and lays it beside the last.

He owes nothing here, and it has not made him easy. He could read the other lanes and take his washing in when they go dark. He does not. Some in the town think his gauge will tick one day. Others think he will be collected. No one has asked him which he expects. He looked about fifty-five on the first morning and looks fifty-nine now, and that is all there is to add up.

The children sit on his steps, which are the only sharp ones. He has scraped a second form for the hare in the thick sedge by his apron, above the height of the stain. The hare has not moved into it. It will not act on water that has not come.

It does come up at dusk to feed there. On afternoons when the lanes go dark, the town's washing goes in behind him, line by line, and he stays on his top step, which is dry. The hare crops along the edge of the apron. Then the rain arrives, and it falls on the two of them when it falls on the plain.
