They are a dozen or so, and they are not far. The knot says as much, and so does the floor, which would have begun to cushion over within a month of being left. A house here is a patch of fibre kept flat by feet, under trees kept bent by knots, and both jobs need doing again every season. Nobody who began this dome ever slept in it. The band that sleeps in it now keeps a ring of such places, each within a season's walk of the next, and must keep arriving at all of them or lose them. Home is the going round.

Begin with the feet, because they do. The feet are broad and splayed, stained brown to the ankle bone by the water. They come down heel first when the walker wants to be heard and on the outer edge when not. Above the feet the people are narrow and long in the shin, and pale, as things are that have never thrown a shadow. Their eyes are large and not much consulted. The cold begins at the knee, so they rest squatting, down in the warm layer. Children live in that layer entirely and then grow up out of it. Adults are slightly colder than children all their lives and think nothing of it.

They have no blade, since nothing up here will take an edge, and no fire, since nothing is dry and the ground already does that work. Food that wants cooking is pushed an arm's length into the ferment and left until the band comes round again, when it is dug out sour and warm. They drink from heel prints. Whoever walks ahead treads deep on purpose.

They have the one knot. A knot is the only shape on the plateau that growth does not arrive at by itself, so it means that someone was here and meant it. It has never needed to mean more. The stems would straighten, given time, so each arch is retied when it slackens, always above the last, and the mat comes up around the old work. For these people the past is a direction. It is down, and it is where the heat comes from. Every knot is tied right over left, as far as an arm can reach. But under one arch, about elbow deep, three in a row go left over right, and then never again.

They are not silent, though the air would say so. Each has a name, and the name is how they walk: the weight, the interval, the small drag of one foot. It cannot be withheld. It goes out ahead of them through the mat, so that everyone arrives already announced, and it changes when they do. A limp is a new name. A child has none until it walks, and then has the one it stumbles into. A woman carrying a child is known to be carrying by every band within a day of her, usually before she has decided to say so. Two people who keep falling into step are teased for it, because the ground hears them as one heavier person. After enough years the ground is right.

Since a word travels at about a walk, talk has habits here that it has nowhere else. A band on the move hears mostly what is said ahead of it. What was said behind comes in after they stop, so every evening opens with the day's replies arriving through the soles, one after another. It is possible to walk away from something said to you and not have it catch up until you lie down. And a runner gets there before their own name. This is how children ambush grandparents, and it is not otherwise done.

Out on the cushions, speech is heels. On a trodden floor a toe will carry, so the dome is where the small things get said. A dozen people lie in a heap in the dark, and the talk goes on in the feet at the edges. Laughter is the heap shaking. A good joke can be felt outside, and the amphibians answer it.

The dome is also the only place they meet the thing in the crowns. It never touches the ground, so it has no gait and no name, and outdoors it barely exists for them. But the walls of the dome are trunks. A few nights a year something long goes over the roof, and the drag of it comes down through the wood into the floor, under their backs. They know its weight very exactly. Nobody goes out to look.

Stillness is plant for them as for anything. It starts between the shoulder blades, the one place a person's own hand cannot reach. The young are scraped clean there by whoever is nearest, in passing, without comment. At some age the scraping gets less thorough. The old carry a green patch, then a green back. They walk at the rear, slower each year, their names getting longer between the beats. Nobody here dies sharply. They slow below a certain rate.

When one stops, the others wait for dark, lay the body on the cushions and tread, around and around, close in. The mat lets it down into the warm, where everyone else is. Because broken surface glows, the place shows blue-green from edge to edge, the brightest thing any of them ever makes, and it lasts about an hour. Then the band leaves, all of them walking in the gait of the one they left. They keep it up for a day. Anyone within reach feels a single name said by eleven pairs of feet, and knows what it means. The hollow takes a year to rise and comes up a hummock like any other.

Once in a life, each of them goes the other way. When they have finished growing they walk to the rim alone. It takes weeks. After the first day their steps are out of the band's hearing and the band's are out of theirs, which has not happened to them before and will not again. At the rim there is noise in the air, from the falls. There is a lip to lie on and nothing in front of it. They go over, down the hanging roots, down forty metres of brown fibre to where plant stops and the black begins, and they put a foot on the rock. It does not give and it does not answer. It is the first ground they have ever stood on that was not listening.

In the seam there are pale pebbles. These are what is left of the mountains, though no one taking one has any way to know that. They take one, climb with it held in the mouth, and carry it back in a fist. Where the line of stones begins they set it down, a few paces short of the last, and stamp once beside it. It is the only thing they will ever put down that stays where it was put and stays itself. It is allowed the stillness they are not. Then it starts to sink like everything else.

So the line is added to at the rimward end and swallowed at the other, at about the same rate. It has been the same length for as long as there have been knots. It is only nearer the edge than it was. A band passing walks along it and touches each stone with a foot, knowing whose it is. Then they touch the dents the same way. Then they go on a little past the last dent, stepping at the right intervals over nothing, out of manners.

The pale knot above the door was tied yesterday by the smallest of them, lifted up to reach. It is a little loose.

They are half a day inward now, standing in the cushions with their weight back on their heels. They left a day sooner than they would have chosen. Since the day before yesterday there has been a walk in the ground that belongs to none of the bands: heavy, uneven, with long stops in it. They felt it stop at the stones. They felt it at the arch, lopsided, the way a body is when it kneels and puts one arm down. This afternoon they felt it stand on their own floor, where even a toe carries.

The small one has been trying it out, a few steps at a time, heel down hard, getting the stops in the right places. No one has told her not to. It is the first new name any of them has had to learn, and you have been saying it over and over since the rim.
