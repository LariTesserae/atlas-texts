By the ordinary count, nothing lives there. I want to say that first, because it is true. There is no lichen and no animal, and the moth arrived dead.

But the place is kept. That is the first thing that comes, before any shape: someone is doing the accounts. The night gives, the day spends, and the balance comes out to nothing every time. So I began by looking for a keeper.

I looked among the blades first, because they are the obvious crowd, hooded and leaning toward noon. But no blade is the same blade for more than a few seasons. They waste at the crest and are fed at the root. They are closer to breath than to a body. The tables aren't it either. They are what it carries.

It is lower down. It lives on the second floor, the level plane a forearm under the surface where every small dark thing has come to rest. That depth is where the sun stops reaching. It lives exactly as far from the sun as it has to, and not a finger further. I don't think it is hiding. That is the distance from which it can look at the sun without being unmade.

And it does look. Once I saw that, the rest came quickly. At noon the lids thin to nothing and the saddle is full of open eyes, and at the bottom of each eye is a pupil. It has as many eyes as things have fallen into it. Most of the pupils are grit from the horns, and through those it sees what grit would know, which is rock and falling. But along the old lane it has other eyes:

- several of black glass
- one of green copper
- one of shell

I can't prove it sees differently through each. I believe it does. Every noon, for longer than the wood by the fire ring has been silver, it has looked up through the bead, which was once in the sea. It has no way to understand the sea. It doesn't try. It holds the bead in clear water, slightly enlarged, and looks.

So it is attentive without being curious. Curiosity wants an answer. It only wants to have the thing, whole, at the right depth.

Whether it is one or many depends on the hour. At dawn it is many, thousands of sealed cells that each hold one dark thing, with nothing passing between them. Then the sun clears the horn, the blades sweat, and the threads begin to run between the roots. By noon it is joined. For about four hours a day it is one creature, and that is when it thinks.

Its thinking runs west. It goes over the lip and freezes to the cliff, and the long grey tongue is everything it has ever finished thinking, hung where it can't get at it again. At dusk the runnels stop mid-thread, and it doesn't mind the interruption. Tomorrow it will not pick up where it left off. It will start over. Its memory is in the pupils and its thought is in the water. It remembers everything and concludes nothing, and that has never troubled it.

It has a voice, and its whole speech takes a day. There is the tick in the morning, the hush under the cloud, and the creak and ping at dusk as it shuts. Once or twice in a season it drops a table with a clack, the nearest it comes to surprise. Then it begins to lift the stone again from wherever it landed, without resentment.

It sorts by shadow. Whatever is large enough to shade it, it lifts. Whatever is small enough to warm, it takes down. It has no third category. The people were a difficulty. They were warm and middle-sized, they moved around on the first floor, and they would go neither up nor down. They cut a lane straight through it with stone tools. It let them, or it couldn't stop them. For something that is frozen solid by the time the stars come out, those may be the same.

The lane has grown back, but lower, and I don't think that is a scar. It is a place being held. It knows where they walked the way a tongue knows where a tooth was.

The part I keep returning to is the platform. Whatever the people meant to give, they laid there, on flat stones squared by eye. But the platform is large enough to shade. So it did the only thing it does with such things and raised the platform, out of its own reach, and handed it back to the sky. It never got what was meant for it. It got what they dropped. Every one of its good eyes is somebody's accident: a pin that slipped from a cloak, a bead off a broken string, flakes from an edge being freshened in the cold. It cannot tell an offering from a loss. If it could, I think it would prefer the losses. They came down to it. The platform is still going up.

It is not lonely. It receives news of the world below, a moth or a barred feather, and files each one at the common depth. The moth will be an eye by the end of the season. It will look up through the moth at the indigo sky, and learn nothing about moths, and keep it perfectly.

If you went there it would not greet you or harm you. You are middle-sized. It would wait, and it is very good at waiting, for you to drop something. You would. It might be a thread, a crumb, or a hair. By the next noon that would be a forearm down in its own tube of water, and it would be looking at the sun through it. That night the well would skin over with the rest.

When the cold closes down it shuts every eye at once, each on its own small dark thing. It does not dream. In the morning it is many again, rimed white on the windward side, and silent.
