Start with the makers, because everything else in the town is a lodger.

A maker is the length of a thumb joint and the color of candle tallow, and at first it looks unfinished. It has no eyes to speak of, only two dark flecks that may tell day from night. The head is broad and flat in front, and the jaws are short and thick, less like pincers than like the two halves of a pestle. They have to be, since a maker spends its working life chewing glass. The body is waxed as heavily as the rosette leaves, and its joints close so exactly that the animal looks cast in one piece. Ash clings to the wax, so most makers go about grey. You see the real color only when one stops to clean another, which they do often and thoroughly, swallowing what they clean off.

Their slowness is not temperament. A running insect breathes hard, and breath is water. A maker keeps the small ports along its sides shut and opens them a few times an hour, briefly, like someone lifting a lid to check a pot. Between times it walks on what it has.

When two makers meet in a file, they stop and touch faces. Sometimes one brings up a bead of syrup and holds it between its jaws, and the other takes it. The bead passes from mouth to mouth without touching anything else. This is most of what passes for speech in the town, and it is also the water supply. A maker that has just fed shows a little amber through the wax at its hind end, like a lamp turned low. A hungry one is opaque.

They do not defend themselves. A gecko takes one out of a file with a single movement of its head, and the file closes over the gap and goes on. There is no alarm, and the same wall is used the next day. This makes sense once you notice that the gecko never leaves either. Whatever was in the maker is in the gecko now, and within a day or two it will be on a roof, where makers will wall it in. By the town's ledger, a gecko is just a slow way of getting to the garden.

Inside, a tower is not hollow the way a chimney is. The makers have filled the flue with thin paper vanes set at angles, so the rock's breath has to wander on its way up and cannot leave without touching a root. The roots hang through the vanes in white skeins, and makers go up and down them all day, grooming the root hairs clear of ash. Midway up, where the warmth holds steadiest, are the nurseries. These are flat combs of capped paper cells, each holding one grub sealed in with its own breath. Nobody opens a cell until the grub is ready, because opening one early would cost a mouthful.

In the towers taller than a person there is something else. Under the high galleries, in the dark, makers hang by their claws in rows. They have been fed until their hind ends are the size of grapes. The plates of the back sit far apart, like scattered tiles on a dome of stretched skin, and the syrup shows through, clear dark gold. They do not move, and they may hang there for years. Walkers come up, touch faces, and give or take a bead according to how the season is going. The canopy, the moss and the traffic on the ramps are water in circulation. The hanging ones are water at rest. When the seam's breath thins or the moths fail, the row is drawn down from one end. A hanging one that has been emptied lets go, drops to the gallery, and walks off wrinkled to carry ash like anybody else.

Makers dry out as they age. No seal is perfect, and each one loses a little more than it takes in. The old ones are noticeably light, with jaws worn to stubs that can no longer work glass. These are the ones that go out. Nothing sends them, as far as you can tell. But it is the old, dry ones you see past the boundary at night, a body-length or two onto the bare flow, raking glass-hair out of the hollows. It is also the old ones who work the western end, where the rock is fiercest, because they have the least in them to lose. One of these laid the paper ring around the hairline crack, a few courses a night. One of these carried the lentil-sized rosette the length of the main street in its jaws and set it in place. An old maker does not generally see the tower it starts reach knee height. When one feels itself finishing, it comes back in. You find them on the ramps in the early morning, facing uphill, stopped. The flat beetles do the rest.

There is a mother. She is not in the tallest tower or the newest. She is in a low, plain one a little west of the middle, where the seam is neither failing nor fierce, and you could watch that tower for a month and not pick it out. She is hardly longer than a walker, narrower if anything, and darker, the color of old piano keys. She does not lie swollen in a chamber. An egg is a drop of water with a skin on it, and the town has only so many drops, so she lays to the count. When the beetles carry a dead maker up to the roofs, word of it reaches her somehow through the syrup, and a day or two later there is one new capped cell in the comb. In a fat year there are a few more, and in a thin year none. The town does not grow. It has been four hundred towers, more or less, for as long as it has been anything, shedding one in the east for each it starts in the west.

Every few years, when her tower has drifted too far toward the cool end, she walks. She does it at night, along the main street, on her own legs, with perhaps a dozen walkers around her. By morning she is three or four towers west, and the traffic has rearranged itself around the new address. On her back, behind the head, are four short ragged stubs, and nothing in the town is old enough to say how long she has had them.

The stubs are explained perhaps once in twenty years. After a long run of good moth seasons, when every hanging one is full and there is nowhere left to put syrup, the western nurseries cap a few cells larger than the rest. What comes out has real eyes and narrow fringed wings like the moths'. They are fed for weeks. Each is given a full crop, a plug of resin, and a rosette offset no bigger than a lentil, carried in a pouch under the jaw with a crumb of moss around it. Then on a still evening they climb to the rim of the last tall tower and go.

It is the only time the town lets go of anything. The finches get some of them within the first few yards, which the town can count as a refund. The rest go out low over the clinker and are not seen again. The files on the walls do not break step.

Most of them must end on the flow, dry as chaff. But the mountain has other seams. And the ring at the western end, a finger high around its crack with one green lentil in it, is worth a second look, because the whole town looked like that once. Somewhere east of the eastern ruins, where the board has long since gone back to ash, there was a single paper ring over a single warm crack. In it was one rosette, and one insect with her wings bitten off at the root, lying with her face to the rock and waiting for it to breathe.
