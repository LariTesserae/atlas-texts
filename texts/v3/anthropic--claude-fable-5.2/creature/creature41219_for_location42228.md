For a while the answer seems to be nobody in particular. The frogs live here, and the snake, and the guppies, and all of them have been counted. But the count comes out slightly wrong, and the error keeps falling in the same places.

**The discrepancies**

- The guppies never go under the lip of the mat, though there is shade there and the kingfisher cannot follow.
- There are tadpoles of every size, but fewer of the largest than there should be.
- The snails keep the leaf drift from growing. Something keeps the snails from growing in number too.
- When the terrapin passes below, one fern nods. Some evenings three nod, one after another, in a line that crosses the floor from the reed wall to the pool. Whatever does this is slower than a terrapin and much longer.

Then, at no particular hour, the crosshatch on the pool parts around something that is not a leaf and did not fall. It is blunt, olive, and two fingers wide, with a pair of small tube nostrils at the front. The wavelets reassemble around it exactly as they would around a leaf. It stays for about the length of a breath and then is gone. There is no ripple, because the ripples here are already spoken for.

**What she is**

She is an eel, and female, as the big ones that settle far up in quiet water always are. She is well over a metre long and as thick as a forearm behind the head. Her back is the brown of the pool. Her belly is the yellow of a willow leaf the day before it drops. Her eyes are small.

She does not live in the pool, which is only her window. She lives in the floor. Her passing has worn galleries through the two metres of root, each exactly her width and as smooth as the inside of a cane. They run under the stump, under each fern, out to the foot of the reed walls, and down to the warm water and the flour-fine silt, where she lies by day with only her head showing.

In the wet root she can breathe through her skin. She can go anywhere beneath the room that a visitor could walk above it. The visitor would feel only the floor give slightly and give back.

**How she came**

She came in by the water underneath, most of the station's sixty years ago. She was the length of a finger then and hardly yet coloured, one of thousands working upriver in a spring she has no reason to remember. The others went on past the weir. She turned aside into the warmth, under a mat that was thinner then and a willow still deciding whether to live.

She grew fast, because there was no winter to stop her feeding. Then she slowed. Now she takes a guppy from the edge of the shade, a snail from the drift, a tadpole of the largest size, or a frog that misjudged the lip. The mat has thickened to the weight the water will carry, and she has grown to the size the room will feed.

**The note**

Nothing here has lived inside the hum as long as she has. It reaches her through the line of sense along each flank more than through anything like an ear. To her it is not a sound but a property of water, like wetness.

She hunts by the gaps in it. A frog sitting on the rim is a small soft shadow in the tremor, and she knows the terrapin by its shape in the note long before it reaches her. Her resting places lie where the shaking of the mat cancels itself and goes quiet. If her galleries could be seen from above, they would look something like the pattern on the pool.

**What has not happened**

An eel like her is halfway through a journey, and yellow is not her last colour. Folded inside her is a second animal with a silver belly, a black back, eyes twice the size, and a gut that has closed for good. It is built for one swim down the river and across an ocean to the weed-choked sea where she was spawned. It is not built to come back.

Particular things let that animal out:

- **Cold:** The water has to cool. Here the cold stops at a line forty metres off.
- **Flood:** The first autumn spate has to turn the river brown. It goes by on the far side of the reeds, and the mat rides up a little and settles.
- **Darkness:** There has to be a night without a moon. Night here comes amber.

So the signal has been sent every autumn for decades and has never got in.

Everything else here has finished arriving. She is the one thing that has not, because she was only ever stopping on the way. The room holds her pause open the same way the warmth holds the pool open, by constantly trimming back what would otherwise close it.

She does not know this, any more than the room knows about frost. But on the dampest nights, when the lines above the roof sizzle hardest, she comes up through the pool further than she needs to. A third of her length lies out on the mat, with her head toward the kingfisher gap and the roar of the weir beyond it. The nearest frogs stop calling. Behind her the crosshatch closes over the place she left.

She lies there a while. Then she slides back tail first, and the groove she pressed stands full of water for a minute before the mat drinks it back.
