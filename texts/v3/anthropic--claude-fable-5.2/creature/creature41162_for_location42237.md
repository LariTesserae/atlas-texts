Something does the counting, and the count leaves it out.

You come on the evidence first. Somebody stood the mug under the drip. Somebody laid the level on the sill and has not had cause to move it. The splice in the exciter belt is laced with tie wire, and so are the nests, and one of those was the pattern for the other. On the shuttering, below the carpenter's dimensions, there is a second hand, smaller and pressed harder. It has written a single row of figures, and beside them a check mark gone over so many times that it has cut through the face ply. Above everything, underlined once, is the word HOLD. It was written for the wall.

Then there is the lamp by the penstock. Eleven lamps have algae and one of them has moss as well. That lamp is the only one with somebody breathing beside it.

And there are the choughs. Every bill faces the machine, and a turbine is of no interest to a chough. Follow the bills.

She stands at the nozzle end of the casing, in the middle slab, where the roar is worst and nothing else wanted the floor. The midge sheet hangs in front of her and the flange fog behind. The lamp reaches as far as her right shoulder and gives out. For the first minute she is a donkey jacket hung on a scaffold tube. Then the jacket has a hand, and the hand is on a wheel.

You take her next for one of the crew, the one who stayed. That lasts until you are close. The jacket is somebody else's, with the cuffs turned back twice. Under the wool cap the face is nineteen or so, wide across the cheekbones, with fog beaded in the eyebrows. Each rubber boot has a grey collar of springtails at the waterline, and they gather only on what holds still. An apprentice, then, and near the end of her time, because she does everything the right way and nothing the quick way.

That is nearer, and still not it.

Look at the casing. Beside the nozzle are two bosses cast for a governor linkage, drilled and not tapped. No governor was ever fitted, yet the set has held one speed for as long as there has been a room around it. The wheel under her hand works the spear valve. She is the governor.

No panel was fitted either, so she reads the room. The lattice on the floor is her frequency meter. She knows its mesh, and a finer or coarser one would mean the speed had wandered. The edges of the four black stripes are her second check and her back teeth are the third. The mug gives her the seepage, and the seepage gives her the head. The level tells her the crest has not moved. The flakes at the sleeve tell her the air is about to cool. That matters, because the speed she holds is not quite fifty. It is whatever fits the room four times, and that shifts with the air by about a tenth of a cycle to the degree. She trims for it. The quiet stays where the salamanders lie because she keeps it there.

You will not see her do it. The spear thread has a sixteenth of a turn of slack in it, and she holds the wheel with the slack taken up, so the next pressure of her palm moves the needle and not only the wheel. She makes each correction ahead of the drift that would call for it, and by less than an eye can follow. That is the whole of her skill. No drift in the room has ever got far enough to be seen. Where the rim has worn through its primer, the steel is bare and wet and has not rusted.

At intervals she leaves the wheel and makes a round: bracket, sill, mug, sleeve and back. She goes sideways through the tubes where you went sideways and steps the stripes without looking down. Her lips move the whole way. They are saying numbers, the only words in the room. The valve accounts for the hum and the stripes. It does not account for the eggs or the wall. She counts those too, and they come out.

If you meet her in the tubes, she puts her back to the pipe run and you put yours to the cable tray, and you pass chest to chest. She smells of wet wool and lime. She looks at your hands before your face, for a paper, the release for the hold. She was never told how far the word reached, and has taken the safe reading. You have no paper, so she goes on, and the choughs that shuffled aside for her shuffle back.

If you stop by the generator she will take your wrist and lay your palm on the exciter bracket, and watch your face until the tick arrives in it. Her fingertips are cold and split from the lime, clean splits that neither heal nor deepen. When you nod she lets go. That is all the conversation there is.

The slack taken up is how she stands in general. Her weight is on the front foot. Her mouth is a little open, like someone about to speak, and that is her face at rest. It would be easy to call this waiting, but waiting has slack in it and she has none.

On the casing flange by her left hand is a snap tin with a wire catch, and her thumb is under the catch. Every chough in the room has seen what is in the tin, and the whistle they keep up is the one they use at hut doors. You cannot tell from her face whether she knows what the thumb does to the birds. You think she knows, and that it is the one thing in the room she holds for her own sake.

By the smell of the lime it is still the morning the mixer left, and by her it is ten minutes to the break.
