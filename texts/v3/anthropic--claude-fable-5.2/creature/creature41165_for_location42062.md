About nine thousand people live on the shelf, and you could pick one out of a crowd anywhere else. They stand with their knees a little unlocked and their teeth a little apart, the way anyone stands on a moving tram, except that they do it on solid ground and have done since they could stand at all. They look round often. Nobody here can be called to, so each of them keeps a loose watch on the whole half mile around, and no one comes up on another person from behind. You walk round into view first, then closer. The herons get the same courtesy, for the same reason.

Most are hard of hearing by thirty and do not know it, having had no occasion to find out.

They have two kinds of signing. Long sign uses the whole arm and is made to be read across a slag flat. It has perhaps forty words: come, wait, the tram is late, the shift has changed, someone has died. Short sign is made at the chest and is a whole language, and children are quick in it before they can write. The slate is for figures, for strangers, and for anything that has to keep until morning. Its back is painted white, for waving.

There is a third way that is not much discussed. You put your jaw against someone's temple and speak low, and the bone carries what the air won't. Mothers do it with infants. Couples do it in bed. Most people therefore know two or three voices intimately, as a buzz behind the ear, and have never heard them cross a room.

Everyone has a written name for the pay envelope and a name-sign, given in childhood for some habit and kept for life. The spoken name is used least. At about a year old a child is carried to a still and held up between the white posts while someone with a strong chest says its name at it. Plenty of people have heard their own name aloud fewer times than they have had birthdays.

They take the hour from the tide, and it shows. A person standing motionless in the middle of a street with head slightly bowed is not praying. Shifts change on the crest.

They sleep well. Infants sleep through everything and wake screaming if the nearest battery stops for re-shoeing. Anyone sent down the incline for their health is back within the week, grey from lying awake listening to nothing.

A few households keep a fern or a fig seedling indoors, in a pot slung from the rafters in a net of cord, so that it hangs clear of the shaking and can keep hold of its soil. Neighbours notice these and sign nothing about them either way.

That is the surface. A third of them work under it.

Below about forty fathoms the stamping thins to a tremor in the rock, and there is air a voice can use. So the miners talk. They talk the whole shift through, over the drip and the knock of the pump rods. They argue, tell long stories, sing badly, and come up hoarse. For some minutes after the cage their lips go on moving, the way a sailor's legs go on rolling. Their short sign is clumsy and is said to have an accent. They marry battery hands and tram drivers like anyone, and they are the ones who talk most at the temple.

They also say the town's name a dozen times a day. It is the mine's name, it is stencilled on the tubs, and below ground it is an ordinary word. They do not see why the surface makes anything of it.

And they are the ones who know the water. Everyone on the shelf understands that the engines hold it down. The miners work in the space the engines hold open. When they pass a puddle they do not look at its figure, only at its level.

Between the two walk the tighteners, fourteen at any time, belonging to no engine house and no row. They lodge wherever the circuit has reached, and by custom the nearest household makes room. They test a joint with the flat of the hand: sound iron hums along with its wall, and a loose nut chatters against it. Each nut drawn up and re-pinned gets a dab of paint. The colour changes with each circuit, so the oldest trestles carry seven or eight colours down in the angles of the ironwork. A tightener who starts at fifteen will lay a hand on any given bolt four times, perhaps five, and knows which time this is. Households count by them. A child was born the year the tighteners did the row, and a grandmother has seen them six times and does not expect a seventh. They are the only clock on the shelf that runs slower than the tide.

One woman keeps a slower one still. She is the warden of the stills, about fifty, and her name-sign is a flat hand lowered an inch, as onto a table. She carries a tin dish, a can of water, and a pot of whitewash. At each still she sets the dish on the ground and fills it. Outside the posts the water stands up in its small squares. As she slides the dish inward the figure slackens, and where it lies nearly flat is the centre. She measures from last year's mark. When the centre has gone a foot she has the bench unbolted and moved, and leaves the old holes to rust. She does not scrape away the dying moss behind.

Her ledger goes back before her grandmother's hand, and from it she knows things nobody asks her. One still will walk into a pipe trestle in forty years and be lost. Two others are slowly closing on each other. Another has spent three generations creeping through the front wall of a house in the row by Number Nineteen. The family there now has a room where an old man can be heard without shouting, fern coming up out of the skirting, and callers most evenings who have something they need to say aloud.

She is the one person on the shelf whose work requires her to think of the town as something that moves. Everyone else is employed to keep it where it is.

Her ledger has no pages for the north-east. Nothing there needs finding.

That quarter has one resident, in the end house of the last row before the sedge. He is an old miner who worked two of the three shafts before they were given up, and like many who spent their years below he has kept most of his hearing. His name-sign is a hand cupped behind the ear. Nobody else on the shelf has a use for the gesture, and it was given him half as a joke.

He reads the gauge. It is a line of brass pins set a finger apart up the wall of the nearest dead engine house. Once a quarter he walks out along a plank, sees which pin is wet, writes it on a slate, and sends the slate in by tram. Everyone knows the figure because of him, and people treat him accordingly: politely, and not for long.

He hears rain coming across the sedge before it reaches him. He hears heron wings. He knows that the frogs, which have no call, make a small tick going into the water, and that the swifts scream, thinly, a long way up. He has written this on his slate for visitors, who are doubtful. And he hears the town. To everyone else the noise has no source, since it is simply the condition of things. From his door it is an object off to the south-west with an edge and a direction, and on calm nights he can hear the tide cross it like weather.

He is not at peace out there, and is less easy than anyone. He is the only one who knew quiet beforehand. Underground, quiet is what happens when a pump rod stops, and you put down what you are holding and go up. He has been living inside that moment for some twenty years, with a kettle.

On rest days people walk out to talk. He hears their boots first, and is at his door before they have seen him, which they do not like. They sit on the engine-house steps among the fig roots and shout at one another, hoarse and pleased, about nothing. He answers in an ordinary voice, which is too quiet for them. After half an hour their throats are sore and they go back to their hands. So on a rest day, in the one place on the shelf where talk is possible, a dozen people sit in the fern, signing. Along the margin of the pool the pale frogs do the same, each lifting one hind foot and spreading the toes.

Toward dark the visitors walk home. Somewhere on the way the sedge gives out and the slag begins, and the beat comes up through their boots again. You can see it reach them, because their knees unlock. He watches them as far as the first lamp mast. Then he goes in and lays his palm flat on the floor to find out the hour.
