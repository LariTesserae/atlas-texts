Nothing in the basin came here on purpose.

Most of it came on geese. They winter on a delta far to the south, in air as hot and thick as this, and they cross the range twice a year with that delta's mud dried on their feet and its weed in their down. For a day they stand in the shallows with their wings open, and what washes off them stays. That has happened something like twenty thousand times. The loaches arrived that way, as eggs. So did the frogs, the tank-plants, and the snails in the tank-plants. The fern spores needed no help, since spores cross anything. The swifts and sunbirds were strays that came through the line once, in the right direction, and did not try it again.

Everything here is descended from the ones that turned back. The line takes a share every day and lays it on the snow for the fox, and after ten thousand years what remains inside is very good at stopping. A swift fledgling leaves the headwall with the boundary close in front of it and about a week to learn where it is. Most do. They learn it by the low note that sits in the jaw. The adults hunt along it as other swifts hunt a weather front, because rising insects pool under the ceiling and can go no higher. They sleep on the wing, in the fog, and none of them has ever been above it.

The insect in the fern crowns has gone further. It is the length of a thumb, green-black, and its wings no longer carry it. It climbs the trunk as a nymph, splits, and leaves its shell hooked in the black fur among thousands of others. It spends its adult life within a few meters of the crown, calling. When it must move it drops, glides, and climbs again. Its ancestors flew well, and the best fliers among them went onto the snow.

The sunbirds nest in the silver hair of the rosettes, the only dry material in the basin, and that is what they quarrel over. The glass frogs would fit on a thumbnail. They guard their eggs on the undersides of the tank-plant leaves, and their hearts can be seen beating through the skin of the belly.

The gray frogs are the weight of the place. A grown male is as broad as two fists and the color of the sinter he sits in, with a paler ridge along each flank like a terrace rim. Each holds one pool. Through the day he sits in it up to the eyes, in water that would kill most frogs, and eats what the rain knocks out of the ferns. At dark he barks.

The bark is pulsed, and the pulses are spaced. Under the middle of the basin the ceiling is about a hundred and seventy meters up and the copy takes a full second to return. A male there leaves a second between barks, so the copy falls in the gap. Toward the margins the ceiling curves down, the copy comes sooner, and the frogs bark faster. At the outermost pools, where the moss begins, call and copy run together into a rattle. A listener with closed eyes could map the dish from it.

The copy also comes back lower, and among frogs a lower voice means a larger body. Each time a male calls, he is answered from overhead by a male slightly bigger than himself, and he cannot leave it alone. That drives the chorus through the night, each frog outshouting a rival who is always a little larger and never comes down. The females go toward the deepest voice they can reach, which means the middle terraces, where the copy is cleanest. The biggest males hold those pools.

Few of the frogs live to hear the ice column go twice. When it goes, they all stop. For a second the last barks come down from the ceiling with nothing under them, and then there is only the note at the line.

The basin is shrinking, and the ceiling with it. Nobody has timed the barks across a century, but they must be quickening.

The posts have their own tenants. The midges are ordinary midges that have found the one place in the basin where a swarm is not rained flat. The spider is more particular. It is small and gray-green, and it builds no orb. It strings a hoop at the edge of the still air, two arm's lengths across, and takes what the turning column sheds from its outer layer. There are seven posts with columns and seven rings. At the two dead ones there is neither, and a gentian grows against the foot of one.

Those two circles belong to the pikas, a dozen in one and fewer in the other. They have the opposite problem from everything else here, because the heat holds them in as the cold holds the rest. A pika can die in an hour at twenty-five degrees, and beyond the edge of the circle it has perhaps a minute. They sit at the rim, where the cushion plants give way to moss, until one goes out flat to the ground. It reaches the nearest fern, bites off a fiddlehead, and comes back with it crosswise in its mouth. Then it lies on its belly on the frost with its legs out behind.

The fiddleheads are laid on the rocks by the dead post, where the thin air dries them stiff in a day. Pikas elsewhere make hay against the winter. These have no winter, because the ground under them is warm and the cushion plants never stop growing, and they make hay anyway.

Above each circle the ceiling is open, a round hole in the fog. The pika's whistle is the one call in the basin with no copy, because it goes up through the hole and does not come back. On clear nights they are the only animals here that see stars.

They will get a third circle. In the tarn the loaches have already found the gray dust from the leaning post. They are finger-long and nearly colorless, and they sift the bottom for a living. Where the dust lies, the brown water stands clear to the depth of a fingernail. The peat stain drifts aside from it as rain drifts aside from the posts. The loaches spawn in those ripples, in clean water, and the fry lie in the troughs out of the current. There is more dust every year. When the post fails, the cold will come down over the tarn in a circle sixty meters wide. The water will stay warm under it and steam, and the loaches will go on as before. The lowest frog pools will fall silent, and the first pika will arrive within the decade, running at night along the bare slab.

Whatever the posts were put up for does not live here, and nothing suggests it ever stopped. There is no floor or shelter along the line, and nothing is worn. The posts come over one wall and go out over the other. The heat and the weight of air around them may be their purpose, or may only be something they give off, as a wire gives off a hum. Seven of them are still doing it. They are the only things in the basin that are where they were meant to be, and everything else has moved into what they left running.

The mast by the second terrace was put up to measure all this. The logger is gone from its housing, the box holds three fingers of rain, and a gray frog has it. He is not a large one, since a large one would not fit through the cable port. But the box is aluminum, and it gives his bark an edge and a carry that no pool can. From the terraces below he sounds like the biggest animal in the basin. He starts at dusk. A second later the ceiling gives him back, deeper still, and he answers it.
