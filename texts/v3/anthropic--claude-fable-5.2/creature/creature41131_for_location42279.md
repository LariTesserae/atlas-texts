One thing lives here. It is the floor, the rim, the chimneys and the rain, and it would be simplest to say that the place and its resident are the same. That is not quite accurate. Most of the highland is not alive. The four thousand metres underfoot are wood, pressed and dark and finished. They are what it has been. What lives is thin.

The living part is the top few metres: the plates, and the wet white tissue beneath them where new plate is made. It is a sheet ten kilometres across and about as deep as a room is tall. Below that it continues only as threads, vessels that lift water and vessels that lower sugar, strung through the dead wood like wiring through a wall. Far down is the other living part, the one that burns. That part has never met light. It receives sugar and returns heat. So the creature is a skin and an ember with its whole past packed between them. Sweetness made at the top at noon is burned at the bottom days later, and comes back up as warmth through a floor that has already buried the plate that made it.

It has no front and no head end, and no direction it prefers except up. It has never been anywhere it did not grow to.

Its upper edge is hard to place. The hot soaked air above the deck belongs to it the way breath belongs to a mouth. It makes the storms, loses them for a minute, and gets them back. Anything standing on the floor would be inside it to the hip, with its head out in the cold.

It knows weight, heat, wet and light. Light it takes whole, across the entire basin, as more or less, and a cloud's shadow is a coolness that travels. It has no picture of anything. It cannot know that it is green, or that the sky lies in it. Weight it knows exactly, and it knows the lean of every chimney and the overhang of every metre of lip. It answers load with a brace, a crack with a limb, pooling with a wall, heat with a column. For most of the day, over most of the basin, it is a body replying to what is put to it, the way skin closes a cut.

It does not choose among its works. Whatever stands is fed, and a chimney is fed past sense, because it has no measure for enough. It has none for loss either. The chimney comes down and by the next day is food. Nothing on it is older than a week. An animal of this age would be mostly scar, but this one keeps its age underneath, out of its own reach, and on top is always a few days old.

Only four things have ever touched it: sun, wind, cold, and dust. The dust is the only matter that has come from elsewhere, and it keeps all of it. It does not mean to. A grain sticks in the film and is under a plate by the end of the week. Somewhere below, in order of arrival, is everything that ever came to it. Nothing else has come.

That would be the whole account if not for the steps.

They come up in the quiet parts, in the open kilometres where no heat pools and no deck sags. A seam lifts and squares off. Behind it a second lifts to the same height, then a third. Nothing has asked for them. The rise is useless to water and useless to heat. It would suit a leg, and there has never been a leg. The risers are even every time and the count is different every time: forty, thirty-seven, forty-three. It can hold an interval but not a number. The row of columns is the same act turned sideways, one spacing repeated until it stops. There is no landing at the top of the stair. The last step is like the others.

So when nothing prompts it, what it makes is regular. All its noise is structural and it has no voice, but it has evenness, and it spends that in the places where nothing else is happening.

A stair is one plate laid a fixed height above the last, again and again, toward nothing. That is also a complete description of the highland. The creature cannot feel its own mountain, because the mountain is dead under it and too large. A flight of forty steps is something it can bear the whole weight of at once, in live tissue, in an afternoon. It may be the nearest it comes to holding itself.

The door is harder. It knows walls, because walls keep rain. An opening in a wall keeps nothing. Everything else in the basin is made to hold, lift, close or vent, and this is made to let something through. The creature has never met anything that could pass through an opening. It has no idea of a visitor, and it would be wrong to say it waits. But it stands a gap of that height in the open and feeds it like everything else. Its own steam drifts through, and so far nothing else has.

By evening it takes the stair back, with the same lapping of plates it uses on a wreck. It does not treat the stair as different from a fallen chimney. Rain crosses, and the water goes down the remaining treads one at a time, which is how steps are used. So the stair is used once, by the thing that built it, going down.

By morning the floor there is flat. Somewhere else in the basin, in a quiet stretch, a seam is lifting and squaring off.
