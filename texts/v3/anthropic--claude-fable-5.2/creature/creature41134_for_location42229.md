From the rim the pit reads as a head count: tens of thousands on the walls, two thousand in the wheel, lines on the chimneys, shrimp past counting in the shot. It looks like a workforce, each trade with a single verb: open, hum, pass, strike. That answer holds for about as long as you stay standing.

Lie down at the lip and look at the top row of shells, a hand's width under the surface. Each is a low ridged cone the grey of pencil lead, cemented to the basalt, with one plate hinged along its upper edge. When the knock comes the lids lift and you can see inside, and there is no feathered limb, no net, nothing put out into the water. The water has nothing in it to catch. The animal feeds from behind. The column faces are crazed with cooling cracks too fine to show, and on each stroke the whole wall sweats a film of brine, the way a pipe joint weeps when the pressure jumps. The shell drinks that, and the lid opens only to let the pulse through. Closing is different. A shell shuts when it feels the shell above it shut, so the clap goes down the wall as a rumour, far slower than the knock that started it.

A shell makes a little under three million strokes a year, and the hinge is what fails. Old ones clap half a beat late, and you can pick them out as a stutter in the band. Older ones hang open, and the young settle on them and build. Under the living layer is a dead one, and under that another. The basalt beneath is as sharp as the day it cracked.

The eels are each about the length of a hand. They have no eyes under the skin and no sockets, and the head is smooth as a thumb. The copper runs in a stripe along the flank, bright as a new coin under a darker back. To go round once in a day the ring must travel at less than a millimetre a second. No eel can swim that slowly, so they hang and lean. Each makes one slow bend and then its mirror, just enough to keep its nose a finger's width off the tail ahead. Their gills are white with the same felt that grows on the vents. The wheel hangs at the height where the strongest chimney's shimmer gives out, and the eels breathe it, and the felt feeds them. Nothing else goes into them.

Each eel is also a weak magnet, nose to tail, and magnets laid nose to tail in a closed ring keep their field to themselves. A needle at the rim should feel nothing. It feels something because the ring is not closed. Take the bearing the needle gives and look down along it through the clear water, and there is a hairline of green across the copper. Every strand of the wheel ends at the same place and begins again one body-length further on. On one side of the gap are tails. On the other are three or four eels with nothing in front of them.

They aren't leading. They are following the tails across the gap, which draw away at exactly the rate they approach. When an eel dies anywhere else, the ring closes over the place within the hour, but this gap does not close. It is why the wheel turns, since a closed ring has nowhere to be going. The flywheel is two thousand animals arranged around one body-length of empty water. The emptiness goes round once between one sunrise and the next, and the needle has been following it all day.

On the chimneys the crawlers are the length of a finger joint, pale enough to show the dark thread of the gut. A line is always exactly nineteen of them. The first has its head in the warmth of the vent mouth, and its mouthparts are worn black and blunt. The last stands where the field fades into the lake. No crawler is born to its place. The voltage where it stands sets what its mouth lays down, so a bead cut in half would show nineteen skins, and any bead from any chimney would show the same nineteen in the same order.

The bead is not food and not young. This water would plate a soft animal solid from the inside, and each coat is metal that one body has got rid of. The bead is how a line breathes out, and the product of the works is its exhaust.

When a crawler dies it stays standing, held by the field, and the line stops. Every animal upstream keeps its grain in its mouth, and they can hold for weeks. Eventually a spare walks up from the base of the chimney, shoulders the dead one off, and takes the station. It is handed a grain that has been waiting for it the whole time.

The shrimp are the size of barley grains and as clear as the water. One claw is ordinary. The other is a hammer, cocked, latched and let go. A shrimp lives in the space left where beads touch, which in a drift of identical spheres is always the same space, and it spends its life moving from one such gap to the next without ever entering open water. It strikes, the film comes off as a flake, and it eats the flake. A bead is struck clean in a day and furred again in a week. That goes on to a hand's depth. Below that the film doesn't grow and the shrimp don't go, and the beads lie exactly as they were dropped, bright in the dark and finished with.

By now the head count has turned into something else. The shell faces the rock, the eel a tail, the crawler a mouth, the shrimp a bead. Every animal in the pit has its whole attention on something a finger's width away. It takes what the one before hands it and hands it on. None of them does a whole thing. The stroke, the wheel, the bead and the rising floor are what all that adds up to, and the sum is known to none of them.

None of them has an eye, either. They came up through the plumbing, out of the dark, and they brought the dark's equipment: pressure, pitch, voltage, touch. The sun comes down hard through forty metres of water so clear the floor is lit like a room. The copper keeps anything from using the light, and nothing in the room can see it. The array of drowned insects on the surface has never been looked at from below. The view from the rim, everything at once, is not available to anything that lives here.

What lives here, then, is a great many small blind animals, each minding the next one along, and one body-length of water that they keep empty. When you go back down the cone, the needle holds on the gap for about thirty paces, then lets go and swings round to north. The knock stays in your boots a good deal longer.
