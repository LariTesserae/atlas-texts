Snails, mostly. They are the size of a fingernail and as clear as the water, and what shows of one is a slight bend in the frond behind it and a grey thread of gut. They keep to the pale undersides, thousands to a tree, each working its own patch of film. They stop at the treeline with the trees. On the last frond of the last row they crowd to the tip, and the red felt a hand's width farther up has none. When one dies its shell softens and goes to powder. The warm plumes take the powder up, and it comes down with the rest of the white on the high slopes.

Then the grazer, one to a range. It is as long as eight or ten people lying end to end and nowhere thicker than one. It is the colour of the fronds' undersides, so from below it is lost against the ceiling and is only ever seen from across a valley. It has no limbs. A ripple runs back along its flanks and it goes forward. The blunt front end swings from side to side as it crops, and behind it the lane lies open, a doorway wide, down to the stems.

Over enough seasons the grazer is seen doing things a grazing animal has no need to do. It leaves the thick weed of the valley floor at the same place each time and climbs. It stops at the treeline, sometimes for days, with the red felt in reach, and then goes through. Its lane has one ragged edge, where it has leaned out after better fronds, and one straight edge. In the brown hours it lies in a curve around one of the rings of stones. It is being kept.

What keeps it is harder to see than the snails, and is found by what lands on it. Silt settles on the gradients in drifts, and it settles here too. Two narrow drifts lie side by side and level, with a rounder one above and between them, and they go up the bare strip at about the height of a short person's shoulders and head. When they stop they thicken. When they go on, the silt slides off behind and hangs in the water, holding the shape for a while, and new drifts begin.

Under the silt is water a shade darker than the water round it and entirely still. It is the stillness that fills the mountain, in a piece that walks. Fronds pushed aside by it stay aside until it has gone. A snail that gets onto it from a low frond is carried for a season, creeping at chest height across what looks like open water, and gets off in another valley.

It has hands. The cairns say so, and so does the way it carries. It takes a stone from the scree and presses it into itself, slowly, the way a stick goes into the mountain. The stone stays where it is left, a little to the left of the middle of the chest. From across a valley this is the most that is ever seen of it, one dark stone going up the switchbacks by itself, well clear of the path, turning at every corner. It does not cut the corners.

It walks on the straight side of the lane, slower than the grazer, and the grazer waits. Twice a day, when the sea leans and the low note comes, it stops where it is until the note has gone, and the grazer stops too. In the brown hours it comes to a ring and sits on the uphill side, facing down the valley, and the silt builds on it until the light returns. Drifts form along its forearms, which are held out toward the centre of the ring. The dust there is not touched.

The weir is for the blooms. When a range browns it brings the grazer down to the pool, where the heavy water lies cold behind a clean edge and the bloom does not get in. The grazer lies along the bottom until the range clears. The keeper spends those weeks resetting what the river has shifted. A boulder goes into it from hip to shoulder, and it walks the boulder up the bed very slowly, the silt on its shoulders riding just above the top of the rock.

The grazer turns aside well ahead of the stone fronts, and the keeper lets it go. It climbs to the path, sits down on a bend, and waits for the stone to arrive under it. While the front passes it sits among fronds that do not move, with granite below it, hard and cold. Once the rock came up exactly to where it sat. The stone comes back lower now, but the path is still on the slope, so it sits where the path is, leans, and reaches down with one hand until the hand is flat on the rock. Each time the reach is longer.

It is wearing as well. The old cairns were built to a height whose top it cannot reach, and the stones it adds now go on low, at the side. On the uphill side of each ring a stone is missing where it sits, and the space is wider than it is. The switchbacks are stepped for a longer leg, and on the steep pitches the drifts on its shoulders dip twice to every rise. Weed has started along both edges of the bare strip.

Whether there is one of these to a range or one for the whole sea is not known. Two have never been seen at once. The grazers do not use the pass, and the cairns along the pass are kept up.

Once in a long while it goes up the tallest valley to the surface. The grazer follows as far as the shore and lies just under the waves with its back awash. The keeper walks out of the sea into the air. No silt drifts there to settle on it, but the rain lands on it and runs down. For the length of the climb a head and a pair of shoulders, drawn in running water, go up the hill of air, the legs hardly showing and a wet stone riding in the chest. There is no weed in the air to leave a strip bare, but it takes the turns anyway.

At the top the cairn hangs over it. It draws the stone out of itself, lifts it in both hands, and sets it against the underside of the lowest course. Then it takes its hands away and the stone stays. It can still reach. The summit is going down faster than one stone a visit.

It stands under the cairn for a time. While it stands there the drops come off the new stone and stop early, on its head and shoulders, and run down it to the ground that is not there and on down the gullies to the sea. Then it goes down by the turns. At the shore the grazer brings its blunt end round, and they go under.
