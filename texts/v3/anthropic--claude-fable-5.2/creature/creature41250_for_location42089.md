At first, what seems to live here is only upkeep. A roof that was grey is green the next time you look, and a cistern stands at its line. Then, a mile off through the cloud, something upright moves along a causeway, so evenly that it could be a drop sliding down a slab. It takes a while to be sure it is a person.

They are people, eight or nine hundred of them, lean and damp-skinned, with fingertips puckered from the ponds. They walk on sandals plaited from leaf fibre, as thick as a hand. A pair lasts twenty days before rock and rot finish it, so everyone plaits, and their hands keep at it while the rest of them is doing nothing at all. A stack of spare soles sits under every eave. Children are carried until they can be trusted with the causeway, and every adult has fine white scars on knees and palms from the time before that. Nobody runs except on a platform. They place the whole foot at once, one ahead of the other, and they carry loads on the back, never at the hip, because the path has no room for hips.

When two meet on a causeway they stop, grip each other's forearms, and turn about the grip like a door on its post. The one without a load takes the outer edge. For most of them it is the only time they touch someone from another house, and they do it gravely and well.

Outdoors they hardly speak. The cloud blurs a voice into something that could be anyone's, so they talk by standing. A figure at the platform's edge with one arm raised and held is asking for someone to come. A mat hung under the eave means not today. Since everyone can be seen for a mile, everyone outside is exact, upright, and legible.

Indoors they are different. Under the thatch is the only dry place they know, a dim ring around the fire-pit where cord and tinder are kept and the oldest sleep. There they are loud. They argue, mimic each other's walks, and sing long songs with words in them that no longer attach to anything. One word, the old people say, means *a thing that crosses*. Nobody can say what crossed, or what it crossed, but the word is sung in its place, exactly. This is what the distance between the houses is for. It is not coldness. Outside they are seen by all, inside they are heard by none, and they need both.

They are a people of the cutting, not the seed. Every root plant in the ponds is the same plant. Every fire is the same fire: when one dies, a coal is walked across from a neighbour in a stone cup, so the flame in each pit is a slip of some first flame nobody remembers lighting. The gourd vines flower, pointlessly, and someone goes down the row each morning with a tuft of copper fern-hair and carries the dust from bloom to bloom, doing the work of a creature they have never seen and have no name for. Skills go the same way. A child learns the adze with an adult's hands closed over its own, moved through the stroke until the stroke is in the arm. Nobody explains, and the stroke comes out the same as it has always been.

So a child is the strangest thing they know. Everything else on the shelf is a copy of something, and a child is not. It is the only visitor that ever comes. A birth is met with close, careful attention and some wariness, and the whole house watches to see what has arrived. Then the long work of handing it everything begins.

Their word for a newborn is their word for fresh clinker, which means sharp. Their word for old is the shape of a pounder. In a place where nothing has been rounded except by hands, round means handled, finished, safe to hold. They step on the polished centre of each slab on purpose. They would rather mend a worn haft than cut a new one. To say someone has *gone smooth* is the highest praise they have, and it is said of people who have stopped cutting others.

Their one real wrong is holding above the line. Water is not scarce, since it runs off every surface, so this is no rule of thrift. It is a rule about being something the surplus passes through. A cistern has a lip and a spillway, and they expect the same of a person: fill to your mark and let the rest go on to the next. Someone who keeps more food, cord, or grievance than their measure is said to *have no lip*. Nobody punishes it. People stop turning on the causeway for them, and they must step off onto the clinker to let others by.

They have no years. There is day, and there is the drip under the eave, which they work to without noticing, the adze falling on every fourth drop. Beyond that there is the roof. A house is rethatched when its turn comes round, and a life is counted in thatchings. A person of nine roofs is in their strength, and a person of twenty is very round indeed. The sun is a brightness without a location. Shadows are too soft to be worth a word.

Each child is taken to the rim once, to be shown that the ground stops, in the same spirit as it is shown that clinker cuts. After that few go back. They do not look uphill much either. They cut no fern above a certain streak of lichen, and they have no story about where the rock came from or what it was like when it came. But everything they own exists as copies, the plant, the coal, and the taught stroke, spread across two hundred platforms set far apart on ground that could hold them ten times closer. A flow that took one corner of the town would take nothing that the rest could not regrow. They have planted themselves the way they plant the ponds. They would not put it that way, or put it any way at all.

At the windward edge, at the end of the row of standing slabs, there is a woman of nineteen roofs. Her mother's mother pecked the groove that feeds the first cistern. She is pecking a branch from it, toward a place where there is not yet a field, and she gains about a forearm's length with every thatching. Her hammerstone was a fist of rust-brown clinker when she was given it. It sits in her palm now like an egg, though she has never seen an egg. She kneels and strikes on the fourth drop, and a pinch of black grit comes loose. She brushes it into a gourd, because grit is the start of soil.

When the white begins to dim, evenly, from everywhere at once, she stands and walks home down the middle of the slabs. She can be seen the whole way, and no one needs to look. At her platform she lays her hand flat on the cistern and feels the water touch her palm at the line and no higher. She listens a moment to the surplus going over the lip toward the next house. Then she ducks under the eave. A little afterward there is laughter from inside, far too small to reach the next platform.
