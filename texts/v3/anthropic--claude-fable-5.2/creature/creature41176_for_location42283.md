**The Tenant**

At first look you would say nobody. The glare gives no shadows to pick a body out by, and anything that has been here long is the colour of the walls.

So start from the thumbprint, which is all the pocket offers. A thumb the width of a lentil belongs to a hand about the size of the top joint of yours, and a hand that size belongs to something that would stand a little under your knee. The scale explains the ledge. A cup that holds a spoonful is, for this hand, a bowl that takes both palms to lift. The crystals are as long as its forearm. The gap in the middle of the pocket, which you would have to enter sideways, is for it a hall with a strung ceiling.

That gives you a small person to look for, and it is the wrong thing to look for, because a person has an outline. What there is to find is a fault in the springtails. They coat everything damp, and on one part of the ledge the nap stands a little proud of the surface, in a shape. Now and then the shape shifts, and the springtails on it jump all at once and settle again, a grey haze that clears like breath off a window. Under them is something as wet as the wall and the same milk-glass white.

It is made of the wall. The paste has budded here as it buds everywhere, only into a different habit, and like the crystals it is most finished at the tip. The hands are complete, with nails, knuckles, and whorls on every pad as fine as the rings on a fish scale. The wrists are definite. The forearms are sure of themselves as far as the elbow, where a bead of water hangs from each, trembling, and does not fall. After that it gets vaguer. The shoulders are a slope. The face is the start of a face: a brow, a pressed line where a mouth would open, and two dents with a clear bud in each no bigger than a grain of salt, which are eyes as far as they have got. From behind there is hardly anything to describe. Its nape and heels are soft, and going. It sets at the fingertips and returns to the paste at the back at exactly the same rate, so that it is always arriving at its own hands. When it works, each fingertip gives off the cold spark a crystal gives. The spark is too small to see in the glare unless it happens to cup its palms, and then for a moment it is holding a little extra noon.

The thumb that made the print went back into the wall long ago. The thumb it has now fits the print whorl for whorl, the way a regrown crystal has the faces of the one that was snapped off.

It has no memory, there being no before to keep one in. What it has instead, and nothing else in the pocket has, is evidence. Each time it turns to the ledge it finds nine cups, a tenth begun, and a mark in the wet clay. It lays its fingers along the row, one finger to a cup, the way the crickets sit one to a tip. Nine find cups. One thumb is left over. It tries the thumb in the print, and the thumb fits. From this it works out that it is someone who makes cups, that it has made nine, and that it is in the middle of the tenth. It works this out new every time, with the small satisfaction of a sum coming right. That is the whole of its past, and it keeps it on a shelf where it can be checked.

Most of its labour is water. The beads never fall, so the cups cannot have filled themselves. It goes out into the gap along the flanks of the crystals by a route that touches no thread. Either the spiders string around its path or its path bends around the spiders, and that stopped being a difference long ago. It takes one bead from the underside of a crystal on the pad of a finger and carries it back. By the time it reaches the ledge, the cup has given up that much to the air, and the crystal has its bead again. This is why the nine stand exactly even. It is part of their count the way the springtails are part of the crickets'. The crickets do not move for it, and it has never set foot on a tip, because the tips are taken.

For clay it pinches paste from the foot of the wall below the ledge, where it is wettest, and works in a shred of the moss's yellow edge so the rim will hold. That is where the yellow goes.

The flakes are another matter. They are caprock, the one finished thing within reach, and it handles them more slowly than the clay. They are the only things it touches that do not give under its thumb and do not spark.

Someone from the surface, seeing a notch being closed with stone beside a row of unfired cups, would say kiln. It has no notion of heat, since there has never been any here. As far as can be told, it understands only that a cup, once made, should go somewhere other than where it was made, somewhere apart and shut, and that the tenth will need such a place. So the place is being got ready.

The notch is one of the few bare patches, with no crystals in it. Behind three courses, which come up to its hip, there is a wedge of air the glare does not quite fill. It is not dark, but it is grey, the only grey in the pocket. When its hands are empty it puts one into that wedge, holds it there, and watches it dim. Its salt-grain eyes can tell light from less light and not much more, and everywhere else there is no less light to tell. The dimming of its own hand is the only thing it has ever seen.

It has never heard itself. The pat of clay and the click of flake on flake happen inside the tone and stay there. It knows the tone through its feet, and through the wet rim, which shivers under its thumb. If the sound stopped, it would take the silence for falling.

It is not waiting, and it is not thwarted. It lives in the first minute of a job, when the clay is cool, the rim is still open, and the flakes lie sorted to hand. It has never been through the middle of anything. It has never seen a cup crack in drying or a wall come out crooked. The whole half-made slope of it leans forward into its hands.

Whenever you look, its thumb has just come up out of the clay, bright at the tip, and it is reaching for the open side of the rim to close it. It has been about to do this for longer than the plain above has had grass, and it has not once lost interest.
