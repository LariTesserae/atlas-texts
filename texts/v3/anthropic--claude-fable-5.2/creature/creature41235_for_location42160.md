You will hear them before you know you have. Under the fissure-hum there is a note that does not belong to it, thin and doubled and a little sour. Someone cut that, and someone comes back to it.

The people of the plateau walk on a single thickness of grazer hide. Everything above the ankle is wrapped until the leg is shapeless, but the sole is kept thin, because the path has to come up through it. Children start in thick soles and have them pared as they learn. Cold feet are the price of knowing where you are.

They travel in file, as the herds do. The one in front hums, the hum is handed back mouth to mouth, and the last walker sends it forward again. If it comes back short, everyone stops. In cloud, this is how a family counts itself.

They do not know one another's faces well. Faces are for the fire, and at the fire they sit with their backs to it, looking out, because the orange spoils the eyes for an hour and the back is where the cold gets in. What they know is a cough, or the weight of a particular step on rime. They know hands. Every adult palm has been opened by fissure glass at least once and has healed ridged. Two people meeting after many seasons put palm to palm, and each reads the other's scars with a thumb. A new one is asked about.

They drink the way the place allows, with a fist in the mat, and they stopped tasting the bitterness long ago. What they taste is rime. It is fog with none of the plant in it, scraped from the windward face of a shelter and melted on the tongue, and it tastes of nothing at all. It goes first to guests, then to the sick, then to children.

They sleep as the grazers taught them. They cut a lid of crust, lift it, lie down in the brown felt where it is a few degrees kinder, and draw the lid back to the chest. By morning the tunnellers have bent a run around the sleeper's hip.

A day means nothing here, so they count closings, each time the cloud comes down. For longer spans they use the ground. Every child is given a fissure that tore open in the season of the birth. The child is carried to its lip and kept there until it can sing the note back. After that, age is a pitch. Ask someone how old they are and they hum, and a low hum is an old person. Fissures widen at different speeds, so two people born in the same dark may be different ages. Nobody finds this strange, since some ground is pulled apart faster.

Their dead go under a lifted lid in the deep hollows, into the warm felt, and the lid is laid back. It heals as a cut path heals, paler and softer. Years on, a walker far from any route feels a patch of give the length of a body, and stands on it a while before going around.

Among them, on each route, there is one who keeps the posts. On this route she is an old woman, and people know her by the clack of leg bones bundled across her back. Families hear her a day off and start scraping rime.

The posts are cut to pitches no fissure makes, but the fissures do not hold still. Each one is sinking, year on year, and sooner or later one sinks onto the note of a post. Then anyone walking blind hears two shelters, and one of them is a black gap with glass lips. So she walks the route, which takes her some years from end to end. At every post she stands and listens outward to which gaps are sounding, how low they are, and how far each has dropped since she last stood there. She carries the whole route in her head as one long chord, slowly sagging.

Where a fissure is coming down onto a post, she sets a glass awl in the hole and gives it a few turns, and the post moves sharp, out of the way. Bone can only be cut in one direction, so a post can be raised and never lowered. When one has been raised until it shrills, she pulls it, plants a fresh bone, and lays the old one under the crust. Over the generations the land's notes fall and the people's notes climb. Her work is in the space between.

The sourness is hers. It comes from two holes cut almost alike, and the beat between them is as particular as a gait. Hers is slow, a wobble you could walk to. On the far stretch there are still posts that beat faster, cut by the woman who taught her. She leaves those alone for as long as they are safe. When one finally has to be raised, she tries to keep the old quick beat, and never quite gets it.

Her own fissure is three closings off the path, and she passes it once in each circuit. The plant has furred it nearly shut. When she is asked her age now, she simply breathes out.

When a drum-skin parts somewhere ahead with a sound like canvas tearing, she turns toward it. She kneels well back from the lip, takes a flake of the new glass while it is there to take, and listens. The note is high and raw and has no wobble in it. Some child will be given that note. She is listening for where it will be when that child's voice has broken, and which post it will come down onto, on a stretch she will not walk many more times. She goes to that post and turns the awl in it now, a little further than it needs.

Then the fog gathers out of the hollows, and she stands still until her feet find the seam.
