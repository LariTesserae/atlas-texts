The jackdaws would tell you it is theirs, and for most of the day you would believe them. There are dozens of pairs, grey-naped and pale-eyed, and each pair holds one hollow for life. They build by dropping sticks down a rotten trunk until enough of them jam, then lining the jam with sheep's wool pulled off the field fence and coarse hair from the badger path. Nearly everything they say concerns standing. The long row at dusk is the flock restating who ranks above whom, perch by perch, and it comes out the same most nights. Their call was always short and hard, so it needed no adjusting to carry over the crusher.

But the jackdaws only have the dead parts. The living parts belong to lighter things. Goldcrests sling a cup of moss and spider silk under the end of a bough, where the needles hang over it like thatch, and the bird that sits in it weighs about as much as a small coin. Their song is pitched so high that the quarry has nothing up there to set against it. Plenty of people past middle age cannot hear it at all, so a walker of sixty and a walker of twenty stand in two differently populated groves. The wren keeps to the fluting of the trunks. The robin holds the moss patch all year, which is why it sings in January as it does in June. That patch is the one piece of floor with daylight on it, and the robin does not share it.

The floor belongs to the voles, to a forearm's depth. Their runs go through the litter like seams. They open every seed the same way, with a neat hole gnawed in the hard coat and the kernel taken, and the seed that would stop a horse's heart seems not to trouble them. They feel the hopper train before anything hears it. The owl in the hollow bole lives on them, and under its hole lie grey pellets of fur and small skulls, losing their shape about as slowly as the needles do.

The badger does not live here, because nothing can dig where the rock lies so close under the roots. It comes through because the grove is on its way to somewhere else. In autumn it stops to eat fallen arils, then carries the seeds out in its gut to a dung pit under some hedge. The thrushes do the same from the fence wire. That is as far as the grove's offspring get. Whatever comes up out there comes up in a ploughed field.

Smaller tenants live in the tea-brown pools in the forks. These are the larvae of gnats and flies that breed in tree-holes, and their whole world is a few cupfuls that a jackdaw drinks from. And in late summer, on some of the trunks, shelves of sulphur-yellow bracket push out through the bark, soft as cheese, and fade to chalk by winter. They are the fruit of something that lives in the heartwood, the oldest lodger of all. It made the hollows and it ate the parent tree, stump and all. The jackdaws live in rooms it cleared.

Everything on that list is a lodger, and they all lodge in one body. The floor, the pillars, the walls and the roof are a single yew, grown from one seed that landed on a hump of rock the hills left behind. It is the one inhabitant here, and the longer you look, the stranger it gets.

It has no oldest part. Cut any trunk and count the rings, and you would get the age of that trunk, which is a fraction of the age of the tree. The part that could have told you is the part the fungus finished. Nothing in it is as old as it is.

It moves in the only way it knows. A bough gets heavy and lies down, the part that lies down takes root, and the tip turns up to become a trunk. That is one step, and it takes several human lifetimes. The tree has taken two or three of them, in every direction at once, walking outward from itself and leaving nothing in the middle but light.

It eats itself. Step into one of the hollow trunks and look up. A root as thick as a wrist comes down the inside from where a limb joins, through the tree's own crumbled red heart, and into the litter. It is feeding on what the fungus has made of it. It also drinks from its own roof, combing the mist and dripping it to its own feet.

It is poison in needle, bark, wood and seed. The exception is the red cup around the seed, which is sweet and which it makes for the thrushes and the badger. The field fence stands a stone's throw back, and that gap is a farmer's estimate of how far a bullock can reach.

Most of the ring is male and smokes in February. One arc went over to fruit at some point and has stayed that way. Yews do this now and then, and nobody knows why. So on a dry day in late winter, the pollen drifts across the open middle, over the moss where the parent stood, and settles on the tree's own far side. In a small way, it is company for itself.

It hears nothing of the crusher, the screens, the power line's one note or the jackdaws. What reaches it is the tremble. Its roots are locked onto the rock, and the same rock runs unbroken under the fields to the quarry face, so when the crusher runs, the yew is holding the other end of what is being broken. This makes no difference to it that anyone could measure. The quarry has been there for less time than it takes a bough to finish lying down.

So there is one resident, hollow in the middle, deaf, poisonous, hospitable, of no knowable age, and at present mid-stride. Somewhere under the skirt, a bough that has rested on the grass since before the branch line was laid has put down roots as fine as hair. Its tip has begun, by a few degrees, to turn upward. Nobody has recorded it. Above it the jackdaws finish their argument and settle. The plant across the fields runs on through the night, and so does this one.
