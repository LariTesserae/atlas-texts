Mostly rasps. There are four million of them against a town that has never got past four hundred, and the town knows which of the two the Hob belongs to.

The four hundred is set by the rime. Forty-three lines give about thirty buckets on an ordinary morning, a bucket is forty cups, and a person is issued three. Every tally the town has kept comes out at the same figure, give or take a household. Births and deaths are reported to the scrapers first, since it is their arithmetic.

Everything that is not a rasp lives on what a rasp leaves. The flea lives in the meal, the tarp lives on the flea, and the town lives on both, because the meal goes to the pots and the flea goes to the table. In the still months half of Linehead is out on the plain with hoop sieves, wading, and the tarps come down and work the same dust beside them. Neither takes any notice of the other. Flea is parched on a pot lid until it pops, then kept in tins. It tastes like the dark edge of a crust. Whatever else there is to eat comes up in sacks on the train, and nobody counts on it.

A Lineheader's skin has been scrubbed with meal all its life and has a matt finish, like the potroom sheeting. It takes no shine, even on the forehead. The hair has never been wet. Lips are kept with lamp oil, so everyone smells faintly of lamp. They stand one pace off to talk, because nobody ever learned to raise a voice that would be gone at twenty paces anyway. The lowland clerks who come up to audit keep stepping back and keep being followed.

Everyone has two names, one said and one made with the hands. The made name is given by other children at about six and is seldom kind. The stationmaster's is the sign for a spilled cup, and at sixty-four he still answers to it across the yard.

The ankle chain is fitted when a child first goes outdoors alone. It is cast from the town's own metal, the only metal that stays, and links are added as the leg grows. On an old person the first links are still there at the ankle end, worn as thin as wire.

They sleep on the floor, since the stone under the houses is the same stone. A newborn is laid on the bare pavement within the hour, face up, to take the warmth. The one time a midwife was sent from below, she asked for blankets and was brought a shirt. Doors are set on the side away from the Draw, so every doorstep in Linehead looks out at the plain.

There are fourteen scrapers. They are out on the return gantries before sunrise, over the drop, working with wooden blades because steel would score the jackets. They are the only people on the Hob with cold hands, and you can tell one in the dark by a touch on the arm. They keep their count in chalk on the gantry posts, a stroke for each bucket.

The linesmen walk the busbars with their brooms. By the time a linesman reaches the next pylon, the lice have walked back to the last. A man with forty years on the bars has swept the same lice, or their descendants, some thirty thousand times, and bears them no ill will. They sweep gently, on account of having to see them again.

The potmen talk more than anyone. The sheds are the one place a voice goes its full distance, and they make use of it. The exception is Number Six, which has stood open at one end since the herd, and its crew sign like yard people. The other potrooms consider Six men quiet.

The dredgers are out at the hollows for days at a time and walk home through the herds. They know the herds by name: the Sixes, the Late Herd, Slow Mary's. They claim to know individual animals too. A dredger passing a rasp will lay a hand flat on its plates without breaking step. Nobody has ever turned one over. Some thirty years ago a lowland surveyor brought a pry bar for the purpose, and the dredgers carried it back to the train for him.

The keepers sit with the flywheels, and some of them breathe to the turning without knowing it. When a keeper doubts an instrument he sends for a child. Children hold the rectifier note exactly, then lose it at about thirteen. The town says of them that they have gone flat, and it means grown.

The train crews are the only ones who have been cold. They come back with split knuckles and stand a while on the platform among the thawing cans, thawing too. They have also seen rain. There is no sign for it, so they use the spilled cup, made several times over, and the smaller children assume the story is about the stationmaster.

In the still months children live on the roofs until their chins clear the dust. The doorposts are notched at dust height, and a child is stood against the notch each year. From the roofs they count the herds. Ask a child its age and it may answer in lengths, at one length a day, which gets it a bigger number.

A chain drawn over the same stones twice a day for thirty years takes the varnish off in a line as wide as a finger. The town is ruled with these lines, running from door to gantry, door to pylon, door to shed, and people can read them. One is somebody's grandfather's, and one belongs to a woman who went out to the same hollow for fifty years. Three hundred years is the figure for varnish to return, and no line in Linehead has yet been seen to close. Some are older than the records. They run out past the last shed to places where there is nothing.

The dead go down with the ingots. The crew put a coat on them at milepost forty and a second at ninety, like anyone. It is the only time most Lineheaders wear one.

The nearest herd, the one an hour's walk off, is the Late Herd, and it is on the Number Six trail. At a length a day it will reach the open wall in about nine years. The pots will be shifted again. Then it will come to the one under the crane rail, and nobody knows what a herd does when it meets a rasp that has stopped. It may part around it, or wait, or the stopped one may go on with it. The potmen keep a book on the question, with stakes in cups. "Stopped" is the only word anyone uses. Six has run one pot short for sixty-one years, and the crew walk around the gap without looking.

The dates on its plates run in a column, sixty-one of them, in four hands. The first belongs to the shift foreman, who chalked the animal on the day it failed to move, the way he would mark a pot that wanted relining. The second is his crane driver's. Nobody is sure about the third. The fourth hand crosses its sevens, which is how sevens are made on the gantry posts.

She is Corrie Rudge, a scraper on line nineteen. Her made name is two fingers pressed to the inside of the wrist, the sign for cold. Her own line in the pavement runs from the gantry past the open end of Six and on to a door on the inward side of Tally Row. One morning a year she leaves that line and goes in under the crane rail while the shed is still grey.

She puts her palm on the plates first. They are floor-warm, and her hand is the coldest thing that will touch them all year. She waits like that until the sun comes in through the open wall and the ticking starts under her fingers, like a tin roof warming. She has never been able to tell whether it is anything more than that, and she has stopped expecting to. She takes the tally chalk from her shirt pocket and writes the date under the last one, small.

There is room on the plates for about forty more. She has counted. Then she goes back to her line and follows it home, the chain dragging behind her.
