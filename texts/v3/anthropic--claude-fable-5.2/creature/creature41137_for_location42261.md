**The Hands**

About four thousand people live in the warm cap. That is the canteen count, and it is the only count anyone trusts. They call themselves hands. None of them has been under the cloud or to the foot of the pile, and they do not think of themselves as living high up. Up is the top course, where the masons are. Down is the furnaces. The white is the white.

You can tell a hand by the walk. Everyone raised here takes a stair at the pace of the winding engines, one tread to the beat, and has done so since before they could count. It is how they learned to count. They carry a load and stir a pot at the same pace and don't know it. Somebody running is a thing people stop to watch, the way they would turn toward a valve that had gone off out of turn.

They wear wide brims plaited from banana fibre, and the glaziers and pump-house crews wear smoked glass until ten. Their faces are creased at the eyes from childhood and are as brown under the chin as on the brow, since the light comes up as well as down. Their hands smell of the iron. They talk low. A shout is lost in the conveyor, but a low voice goes under it. They break off for a relief valve and pick up on the same word without noticing they stopped.

They live in whatever the Works have finished with: old pattern lofts, lamp rooms, a pump house with the pump taken out. A good room has a main running through it, and the bed goes against the flue wall. An address is a tier and a pipe. A thing is said to be true "as far as the drawings go", and that is as strong as an oath gets.

Age is counted in courses. Because the masons date the brick, every child is walked up to be shown their year and to put a palm on it. At six you are taller than your year. By twenty the wall has gone past you. By the time you are old there is a floor over it and moss on it, and a fig root has usually found it. A life comes to about a tier. People mostly stay near their year, so the young live high, in the glare and the bare new brick, and the old live a tier down, where the green has had time to arrive.

The trades are few and well worn:

- **Masons** work the top course and are the only hands who see brick with nothing growing on it.
- **Furnace crews** ride the cages down at first whistle and come up at third. They are the only hands who are ever cold. They find the crown chilly after where they have been, and sit among the bananas on Nine with their coats on.
- **Ringers** strike each casting with a hammer before it is crated. A good one rings and a flawed one answers flat. "Sound" is the best thing a hand can say of anyone.
- **Laggers** gave up stripping the moss long ago and now count it as lagging. An apprentice's first test is to judge a hand's depth by eye.
- **Cistern keepers** judge the water by the frogs. A quiet cistern gets drained and scrubbed.
- **Electricians** take one comb in three from the dead boxes and leave the rest.

At fourth whistle nearly everyone who is not on a job goes out into the drizzle. Hair is washed in it, and babies are held up to it. Nobody remarks on the smell of coal, and most would not know rain without it.

They have carried the masons' habit over to each other. A hand who cannot work, from grief or a bad chest or a turn of mind, is not moved and not mended. The others take up the load on either side and leave the person standing where they are. This is called giving someone an arch, and some people have had three.

Everything they make goes down the ropeway into the white. The patterns are older than anyone's year, and for some of the castings no hand can say what the thing is for. The crates come back up empty on the return cable. Now and then one is not quite empty. There are chalk tallies inside in a hand nobody up here writes, or a twist of straw from a grass that grows on no terrace, and once there was a snail. Some crates come up wet and smell of rain that never passed through a flue. The loaders on the ropeway stage are the only ones who smell it, and they are a quiet lot. So there is somebody. That is all the hands know about the below, and they find it enough to do the work properly for. A casting that does not ring is not sent.

But the canteen count is taken on the tiers that have canteens. Below the last of them the mains run barely warm and the rooms are mostly empty. A few old hands live there who have outlasted their shift lists, and there are more goats than people. Below that again is the lowest warm tier, where the ferns thin out, and one person lives on it.

She has the old valve house at the foot of the last main, which carries three lizards where a main higher up would carry thirty. She is on no list. She was a ringer once, a long way up. She is not thought wise. She is thought stubborn, she quarrels with the goats over what ferns she has, and nobody consults her about anything. But a can of food goes down each evening with whoever is heading that way. Someone always is, though no one is told to be, and the can comes back up next morning, rinsed. Ask how long she has been there and people give a number of courses. Ask who was there before her and they say there has always been someone on the bottom tier, as far as the drawings go.

Each day, when the drizzle has stopped and the brick has dried, she walks along the old wall. She takes the handrail in her left hand and lays her right flat on the root, and waits. The engine beat comes through the iron first and through the root a moment after. She has lost the top of her hearing and could not tell you now whether a casting was sound, but she can feel the beat arrive twice. While it does, the wall is carrying. Then she looks at the paint, and where it has lifted she goes over the letters. The word is not hers. There is another hand under hers and another under that.

The stair goes on down past her door into the first cold. She keeps a coat on a nail at the head of it. Asked what is down there, she says the treads are dished.

The cold gains about a course a year, the same as the masons. Some day her tier will have rime on both faces of the wall and no ferns left to quarrel over. Nobody will be told to do anything about it. One evening the can will be left a tier higher, and in the morning it will come back up rinsed.
