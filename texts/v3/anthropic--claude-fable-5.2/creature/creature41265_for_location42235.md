They are few, and the first thing known about them is their size, because the stones give it. A cairn stops at the chest. A wall stops at the knee. The dark circles on the older slabs are a palm across. Put those together and you get something about as tall as a person, and for a while it is easiest to picture a person, someone who climbed up long ago and stayed.

The picture does not hold. Nobody climbed here. There is nothing to drink and never has been, nothing to burn, and too little air to hurry in. The measures are a person's, but almost nothing else is.

You will not see one arrive. What you see, if anything, is a fault in the pattern of lit edges. Everything on the plateau leans a little, and the orange catches each edge at its own angle. Then, far out on a tilted field, one edge is plumb. It is lit from below, so what shows is the underside of a jaw, the inside of an arm, the lower rim of something carried. It does not move for a long time, and then it is somewhere else.

They walk the way they build. The foot is lifted clear, carried forward, set down flat, tested, and only then given weight. Nothing slides, because a foot that slides here is a foot laid open. Each step is a placement and takes as long as it takes. They are the only things on the plain that are placed rather than pushed, and they do it to themselves several thousand times between one collapse and the next.

Up close they are narrow and the grey-black of the rock, because the haze settles on them as it does on everything. The skin is dry and thick as rind. If there is water in them, it is the only water on the plateau, and they behave accordingly. They do not sweat. They breathe through the nose, slowly, with long waits between. The mouth stays shut and they have no voice, and the air would not carry one anyway.

The hands are the most used part. Every plate they have ever lifted had a knife for an edge, and the palms are closed over in welts, like the fissures. The fingers are yellow to the first joint. They eat what the vents put out overnight, the needles and frills, picked before the ground moves. A vent they have visited is bare on one side to the length of a reach.

When one of them stops, it stops entirely, and the salt begins to settle on it. One that has stood an hour is faintly dusted. One that has slept its full sleep is pale from end to end, the brightest thing on the plain. When it rises, the bloom cracks off and leaves its shape on the pavement.

The walls are where they lie down. The rubble is cleared so that the whole body can rest against the plate, cheek included. This is how they sleep, and it is also how they hear. The air tells them nothing, but the ground tells them a dome is starting two fields off, a fissure is travelling, a spine is going up. It also tells them when another of their kind is walking, in a rhythm of step, long pause, step. They know one another this way for a long time before they meet, if they ever do.

They build when the ground has finished changing. A slab tilts and comes to rest. One of them goes over the field turning plates, trying each against the last and rejecting most. The stack goes up plumb, shimmed with chips. Down is the only direction that has never moved on them. The seams wander, the horizon shifts, and there is no sky, but each of them carries down inside itself, exactly. A cairn is that sense set outside the body, where another can read it. Whoever passes a season later stands beside it and sees from its lean what the ground has done since.

They never restack a fallen cairn or straighten a leaning one. What the mountain does to a cairn is the half of the message they could not have written themselves.

Some cairns alternate glass-skinned plates with ropy ones, from two different flows, as though two builders had taken turns. That is the nearest thing to evidence that they meet.

The dark circles may be theirs. They crouch at the edges of seams, where it is warmest, and lay a palm down to feel what is coming. Nothing shows at the time. Weeks later there is a matte round where the hand was. Something off the skin has been taken up by the rock, or something living came off the hand and found the warmth enough. They would not know which either. If so, the ground does hold tracks. It holds them late, and sheds them the first time it flexes.

When a spine falls, every one of them, wherever it is on the plain, feels it in the same instant and stops, with a foot half set or a plate in both hands. It is the only thing that ever happens to all of them at once. They hold still until the small flat clap arrives through the air. Then they go on. That interval is as near as they come to gathering.

Each carries one stone in the hand that is not working. It is fist-sized and used for knocking on the ground ahead. A plate over solid rock gives one sound and a plate over a hollow gives another, and this is how they cross thin crust in the dark. It is a fourth near sound, a knock, a pause, a step, and you would hear it only if one were very close, which would mean it had allowed that. The stone has been carried so long, by this one and by whoever carried it before, that its edges are gone. It is the only rounded thing on the plateau. It is older than any ground it has ever knocked on.

The line is the one thing they all add to. It cannot be found on purpose, and the ground sees to that. Whoever comes upon its end chooses a stone, sights back along as many of the others as the light shows, which is four or five, and sets the new one on edge beyond the last. Then it leaves. None of them has seen both ends. The first to arrive after the fissure sighted along the half that had been carried sideways and continued it faithfully from there.

The last stone has no bloom, so it was set within the hour. At their pace, the one who set it is not far. It is probably the same one who built the plumb cairns on the fresh tilted slabs, since each of those is topped with a ropy plate, rough side up, and no older cairn is. It works this quarter of the plain. It is particular. It likes a difficult slope.

It felt you long before you reached the dogleg. Your gait slides and hurries and does not test, and nothing else on the plain walks like that. It did what they do. It is standing somewhere on the dark pavement between two seams, plumb, with its weight even, its stone in its hand and its mouth closed. It catches no light, because it has chosen where to stand, and the salt is beginning to settle on its shoulders. This is what "nobody is here" looks like from the outside.

It can wait longer than you can. It is waiting to feel what you do next. You may go on, as everything else here does. Or you may stop, bend, turn over a plate, and place it.
