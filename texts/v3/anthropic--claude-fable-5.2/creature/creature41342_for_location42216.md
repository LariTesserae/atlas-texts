From the causeway, before the roofs have separated from the gulls, there is one thing on the apron that is not on a line. It stands upright in the open shine between the water tower and the canteen, about the height of a mooring post, and the first guess is that it is one. A few strokes of the engine later it is nearer the canteen.

It goes at the rats' pace and by none of their routes. The rats keep to the footings and the gulls cross where gulls cross. This goes corner to corner over the widest part of the slab, a full minute from door to door. Its line is as fixed as theirs, but nothing else in the pan travels on a diagonal.

Nearer, it is a coat. It is a long belted mackintosh, the grey of the concrete and just as wet, with a flat cap above it. For some distance that is all that can be said, and a coat and cap crossing a works yard make a man. Nearer still they don't. The cap was a man's and the coat was a man's, and the person inside them is a woman, not tall, who has worn them until the question of whose they were has rubbed off. The elbows are bright. The hem drips, and the drizzle makes up each drop as it falls, so the coat gets no wetter and no drier.

She is hard to put an age on, for the same reason the handrails are. She isn't young, and nothing about her is giving way. Her face has gone the way of the slab corners, with all the features present and the edges off them. Her hair is cut square at the jaw by someone who couldn't see the back. Her breath shows, and it shows on the stroke: in with the groan, out with the thud, one small white exhaust every four seconds. She hasn't arranged this. If the engine missed, she would probably stumble.

The animals treat her as a twelfth building, one that moves. While she walks she is apron, and they leave her as bare as they leave it. When she stops she has edges, and the edges fill. On the sump wall the gulls stand shoulder to shoulder all the way round except for one gap, a little wider than a gull. When she comes to the wall she stands in it, and the birds on either side don't shift. Passing the settling tank she draws three or four flies off the column. They stand over her cap for the length of the tank, a pilot light to its chimney, and go back. Where her line crosses a rat channel the file opens by the width of a boot and closes behind the heel. She says "Mind" to them anyway.

The starlings have learned her along with the rest of the works. From the wires, a half-second late:

- her boots on the slab, both feet, with the slight drag on the left
- her cough, in three versions, since she has changed it twice to shake them off
- an eel going into a hot pan
- an enamel mug set down on a deal table
- "Mind"

The cottage with the table is hers. So is the green door, which is folded back flat against the brickwork with its painted face to the wall and has kept its colour because nothing can get at it. Among all the small hollows in the sill there are two larger ones, a left and a right. The table has two pale ovals where forearms go. She has the downstairs room. The stairs and whatever is above them belong to the gulls, who watch the door, and she has not been up. She sleeps at the table for about as long as a mug takes to go cold. There is no night, so it isn't clear what the sleep is for, but she takes it.

She carries no keys, no oil can, no rag. When the dry roller squeals she doesn't turn her head. Nothing in the place is in her charge, and she walks round all of it.

The round begins at the time office at the causeway end, a hut with a hatch and a board of forty hooks. Thirty-nine brass checks hang there, tarnished brown, each stamped with a number. The fortieth hook is empty. Its check is in her right coat pocket, thumbed as blank as a washer. A check off the board means its holder is on. It goes back at the end of the turn, and the turn ends when the lamps are wanted. The lamps are lit and are not wanted. So she isn't the last of anything, or the keeper of anything. She is the day turn, and it is still day. She looks at the board, then out along the pylons for as long as the valve shrieks, and goes on.

At the sump she stands in her gap and waits, as the gulls wait, for a length of the surface to come up the masonry. She takes one eel, with a hook that was once a fire-iron. No gull takes that one. The canteen has benches for a hundred and a range at the far end, and one place on one bench is polished. She fries the eel in its own fat, so that some of the cooked fat in the air is hers, and eats it with her fingers. What she leaves is off the floor before she has stood up. The rest goes back by the privy drain to the settling tank in due course. Her hair, when she cuts it, goes the way of the feathers.

At the engine house she stops in the doorway. The pigeons lift for the beam and not for her. She watches a few strokes, her own breathing done in iron, and goes on.

The weighbridge is last. She doesn't go into the office. She steps onto the plate outside it and stands in the middle, and through the window the steelyard comes up off its rest and floats level. The poise sits where it sat: one hundredweight, no quarters, eighteen pounds, coat on, mug empty in the left pocket. Nobody is in the office to move it, and it has not needed moving. She is the only thing the weighbridge has weighed in a long while, and it is the only count she takes.

The telephone is on the desk beside the steelyard, a foot from the glass, and it rings while she stands there. She used to answer it. The proof is on the wires. After the starlings' ring comes a woman's voice, flat, a little hoarse, pitched to carry over a dynamo. "Weighbridge." Then a gap, held for the length of whatever the far end used to say. Then "Same." She has not said either word for some time, and the wires have kept them. She hears it go out along the spans and doesn't go in, since the call has been taken.

She steps off the plate, and inside the office the beam comes down on its rest with a small click. The starlings haven't got that one, because it happens behind glass.

At the boiler house she fills the mug from the gauge cock. It is hot water with nothing in it, and she carries it home across the apron with her free hand flat over the top to keep the drizzle out.

On her step there is a piece of clinker the size of a hen's egg. Going in, she moves it with her boot from the left of the sill to the right. Next time round she will move it back. It is the only record kept anywhere in the pan, and it holds one of two values, odd round or even round. That is the whole of what she knows that the works doesn't.

She sets the mug down on the table. The wires set it down after her. On the stairs one gull trades places with another and announces it. She sits with her forearms in the ovals and breathes when the engine does.
