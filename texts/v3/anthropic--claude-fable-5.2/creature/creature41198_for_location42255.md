Something tends the engine. The evidence is the full oil cups, the bright lids and the fresh bottom row on the slate. It is natural to build a man out of these, with boots the width of the path, a rag, a long-spouted can, and a stub of chalk in his waistcoat. He would be sure-footed, since the lime on the handrail has never been broken by a hand.

The man does not survive the notice. NO PERSON SHALL is an instruction like the red marks on the staffs, and this is a room where instructions are kept. This one has lost its verb, so it is kept in full. No person oils the engine and no person chalks the slate. Whatever does these things has settled just outside the word.

It is easier to say when it is than what it is. It happens once in each stroke. A little after the valve closes, the lid of the lowest oil cup lifts and seats again, then the next, up the engine in order. On the motor casing a gap opens in the moths, about the size of a palm, stays for the time it takes to judge a bearing's warmth, and closes wing to wing. Then the path to the office is used. The water over the dished brick parts along its length and heals behind. An eel that has reached the path stops with its chin at the edge, as at a kerb, and goes on when the path is clear.

The office door stands ajar by the width of a boot and has swollen there. On the slate the new row is in figures that belong to no hand, upright and even, like the numerals on the gauge staffs. The chalk headings are different. NEWTS slopes and was written quickly. EELS is pressed hard enough to have scored the slate. BATS is small and neat. SNAILS (EST.) was added by someone who allowed for doubt. Those were men, four at least, and each had time left over from a level that never needed correcting, and spent it noticing.

Beside the slate a brass frame holds a card. ON DUTY is printed across the top. Below it a name was written in ink, and the ink has gone the colour of the card. The frame is an instrument like any other in the hall, and it reads as it always has. Someone is on duty.

This is closer. What lives here is the duty with the name gone from it. The men wore down to their rounds the way the brick wore down to their boots, and then the wearing stopped. What remained was the round itself, and the habit of noticing, which the room has kept as it keeps forty on the rising main.

It has an extent, though it does not have an outline. It begins at the first landing, where the ferns end, and it has never been above that step. It is thin on the stair and at the far walls. It is thickest at the engine, around the bottom of the stroke. A thing with a name on its card would be the same all the way through. This one is more present in the middle than at its edges, the way the water stands in the bay whose mark has flaked away.

It goes to that bay. No path leads there, because the wearing of paths finished before the habit began. After the slate, in the minutes the round does not need, it is at the column line of the last bay, under the amethyst glass. The newts leave room for it there, though they will not lie in the bay itself. It reads the bare staff, which the snails have kept clean. The reading is different each time by a hundredth or two. It carries the figure back toward the office and does not write it down.

Then it waits for the valve. When the sound of the book set down arrives, the domed water shivers and the violet squares come apart. It attends to how long they take to come back together. This is the only duration in the hall that is not on a dial or a slate, and it is never the same twice. Overhead the shadows of people cross the glass. It does not count them. It knows what a heading does in this room, and there is space on the slate to the right of SNAILS (EST.), and it has left the space empty.

At the slate's far edge is one more column with no heading, and each row in it holds a single chalk stroke. In very wet weeks, when the damp gets into the office, that stroke softens with the rest. Then two palm-sized gaps may open in the moths at once, on opposite sides of the casing. Or the brass lids go dull for some days and the level holds anyway, because holding belongs to the room. When the chalk dries the count is one again. In those weeks the last bay is watched twice over, or not at all, and it shivers on the stroke regardless.

On the shelf under the duty card there is a tin of red lead and a half-inch brush. Painting a new mark on the last staff would take a minute. The water would flatten to the mark and the squares would lie still, and the newts would move in at arm's length. The duty knows this as it knows the level in every bay. The lid of the tin carries the same film of lime as the handrail, and the film is unbroken.
