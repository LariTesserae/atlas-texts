Four kinds of plant live in the drum, along with one thing that isn't on the plate.

The plate by the door lists the four by drawing number. Between them they fill a little over four thousand baskets, and all of them went into the water within the same few weeks, so nothing here is much older than the yellow paint. There are no seedlings and no old growth. Below the six-meter line, every plant still has every leaf it ever made.

The mat is the lowest of them and has gone furthest from the drawing. It was set as a plug in the middle of each basket, and it has since run over the rims and across the mesh until it met itself coming from the next basket. From the gallery the grid can no longer be made out. It is one sheet now, and its tags are somewhere underneath it. The light that reaches it has already passed through nine meters of leaf. It grows slowly on that and holds each small leaf level.

The plumes stand in the mat to about twice the height of a person. This is a kind of plant that folds its top whorls shut when the light goes. The light has never gone, so the plumes have stood open since the day they were unwrapped. Where two stands meet, their leaves have grown through each other so finely that the stems can't be told apart without following each one down to its tag. Most of the bubble threads start here, one from the tip of each leaflet.

The climber is the only one of the four still on revision A. It goes by touch. Each growing tip swings a slow circle in the water, a full turn every couple of hours, and when the circle meets something the tip closes on it and the plant goes up. That is how it took the nutrient headers and the sensor stalks. The probes at the top of those stalks now sit inside a sleeve of leaf, and the numbers they send down the conduit describe the water as the climber has left it. On the service ladder, the leading tip has been circling just above the second rung for some days. The third rung is thirty centimeters up and a little outside the circle. The tip will reach it once the stem has grown longer.

The haze on the glass has no drawing number. It was not installed. It came up the risers in the cloud water and settled on the first lit surface it found, and it is the only resident that arrived on its own. It lies in an even coat because nothing has ever disturbed it. It grows on the gallery windows, on the wet face of the lifted corner of blue film, and on the roof cone out to where the gas keeps the glass dry for part of each cycle. That dry patch gives it an edge, a faint ring on the roof at the float's radius.

Then there are the ribbons, which make up most of what can be seen. Each one is a plant of two ages. Below six meters the strap is as old as the drum. It is dark, thick enough to hold a crease, and wider than when it was planted. Above six meters it is never more than forty days old, and it is paler and thin enough for lamp-light to come through. Each strap has a seam where the two parts join. The bar has passed twice at exactly the same height, so there is only the one seam.

A ribbon's life has very little in it. There is no night to stop for, nothing bites it, and no current is strong enough to lean on. It grows, and once every forty days the bar goes through it. The cut piece tips over into the intake slots and goes down the column. The rest of the ribbon starts again from the seam.

The ribbons are getting better at this. On the first turn the regrowth did not reach the roof before the bar came round. On the second turn it touched the glass. Now the tallest growth bends along the glass with length to spare. The old part below the line is where the gain is kept. It has had a hundred days of unbroken morning and has lost nothing, and it puts more into each regrowth than it did into the last.

None of the four keeps a rhythm of its own. The gas comes off them at the same rate every hour, and the only periods in the drum belong to the machinery. The tooth seats every minute and a half. The valve opens every nine hours and twenty minutes, and its hiss is the thicket's one long breath going out through the top of the column. The arm makes the only season.

No one else lives here. Whoever carries the paint marker comes up, makes a tick, and goes back down the mountain of roofs. The binder box is still sealed.

The newest thing in the drum is out near the float, just ahead of the bar, where the gas at its widest is only a finger deep. A few ribbons there have more length than there is water. When the mirror reaches them, their tips come through it and lie flat on the surface with their upper faces in the gas and nothing between them and the lamps but glass. This is the first surface any leaf in the drum has ever lain on. For an hour or two the top side of each tip dries. Then the valve opens and the water comes back up over them. They go under wearing a skin of silver that clings for a few minutes and breaks off as a string of bubbles much larger than the ones the leaves make.

Afterwards those tips look like every other tip, green with whole margins. The bar is a few days away from them. It will take them at the seam as it takes everything, and the lengths that lay in the gas will go down the column with the rest. On the next turn the ribbons will get there sooner, and there will be more of them.
