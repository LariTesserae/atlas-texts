At first you take them for boulders. They are the right size and shape, keeled along the top and tapered at both ends, and they sit in the lee of the big mounds where a boulder might reasonably have come to rest. Then one of them reaches up into the thrashing tail, takes a brown strip in its mouth, and pulls it down.

There are twelve. They are the size of goats, and built like what a goat might become if it stood facing one direction for ten thousand generations. The head is a narrow wedge carried low. A ridge of bone starts between the eyes and runs down the spine to the stub of a tail. The pelt on either side is short and felted solid with dust and resin, so the flying tatters slap against it and slide off. On the muzzle and the points of the shoulders the pelt is gone, and the skin is polished like the pavement. The nostrils are slits that close. The eyes are small, wide-set, and mostly shut. At night a spark jumps to the muzzle each time one takes a strip, and it does not flinch.

They have never tasted water. The strips are full of old oil, which they burn somewhere inside themselves, and whatever wet they have is made there. They give none back. Their dung is a dry pellet, gone over the leeward lip before it has finished falling. They leave no track on the polish.

They face the wind. The keel works in one direction only: nose-on, a gust parts at the face and closes behind, but broadside the animal is a sail. So they do not turn. They follow their mound upwind a hand's breadth or two a day, which is less like walking than like leaning. When their mound has crept too near the windward lip and its tail begins to lift, they go backward. They back down the col from lee to lee, feeling behind them with their hind hooves and waiting for the troughs as the runners do. A runner caught short will sometimes finish its crossing under a grazer's belly and lie there, flanks heaving, until the next trough. The grazer does not look down.

A grazer can live its whole life without seeing the leeward end of the pass. It never sees a mound's green face either. What it sees, all day, a body's length from its eyes, is the back of the mound, amber going brown and the brown splitting. It lives looking at the place where the plant dies, and eats it.

They speak with the front hooves and listen with them too. One foreleg lifts and comes down with a hard, dry crack. You would never hear it through the air in that noise, but the pavement carries it the length of the col. The other foreleg stays planted, knee locked, and the knock comes up through the bone. The harvesters behind their wall feel it in their backs and their teeth.

What they say, mostly, is a round. One knocks a short figure, three beats or four with its own spacing, and somewhere off in the brown another answers with a different figure. Then another answers, until twelve have gone by and the first begins again. It goes on all day and all night. They cannot see one another through the dust, and nothing they cried out would arrive, so they do this instead: here, here, here, twelve times, and again.

The harvesters say the figures do not change. Their grandmothers lay behind the same wall and counted the same twelve, though the animals cannot be the same animals. When a devil takes one over the lip, the round goes on with a hole in it, a rest exactly as long as the missing figure. The others keep that rest for a season or longer and do not close it. Then a calf is dropped in the lee of some mound. It comes out wet, the one wet thing the col ever sees, and the wind has it dry and dusted before it finds its feet. For some days it knocks nonsense. Then the grazer nearest the gap begins knocking the missing figure, out of turn, over and over, until the calf knocks it back. The herd holds its twelve the way the grove holds its forty.

So it is hard to say which lives here, the animals or the figures. The harvesters' flat-handed signs are for the figures.

There is one knock that is nobody's name, a stamp with both forefeet together. It goes round a little before the fifth hole of the whistle-stone begins to sound. When it does, they fold where they stand, chin to the pavement and keel to the sky, and become boulders in earnest. The oldest harvesters watch for this and already have a hand on the line when the low note comes. It does not always save the grazers. A devil crosses the pavement, finds a boulder that is not a boulder, and turns it.

In a lull, the round falls apart. All twelve knock at once, not their figures but just hoof on rock, fast, and for once it can be heard in the air, clattering across the whole pass. Their heads swing from side to side. There is no wind to face, and they do not know where to put themselves. The harvesters hold that they are calling the wind back. The wind has always come back, so the question remains open.

One figure goes: two quick, a drag, one. The harvesters sign it with two taps on the thigh, the palm drawn down, then a tap. The grazer who carries it now is an old female on her fifth mound. Grit has clouded her right eye, and the bare patch on her muzzle has spread up to her brow.

Some years ago a devil took her out of her lee, rolled her forty paces, and left her against the whistle-stone with her near foreleg wrong and her head pointing leeward. She lay like that long enough to look. Whatever a grazer makes of such a thing, she saw it: all the tails streaming away from her instead of toward her, the strips and beetles and snapped kite-things going out past her, the lip, and the brown nothing beyond it. Then she got her legs under her, came round into the wind, backed in behind the nearest mound, and knocked.

The leg healed crooked. Before that, the figure had been two quick, a pause, one. The oldest harvesters remember it so, and used to sign it with a lifted palm where now they draw the palm down. Her bad hoof scrapes across the pause on its way to the last beat. The others have heard it that way for years, and that is how they will knock it into the gap when she is gone. Some calf with four sound legs will learn to drag one hoof across the stone, exactly so.
