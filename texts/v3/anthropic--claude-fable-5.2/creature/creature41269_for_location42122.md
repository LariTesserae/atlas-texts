Nothing crawls here and nothing flies. What there is stands.

In a few places thin rushes grow in a single row along a still line. In some of those rows one rush is thicker than the others, and when the wind leans the flames and the rushes lean with them, that one stays upright.

It comes to about chest height. It is the grey of the plate and as wet, and gives back the same dull sheen. It can pass a whole slate-colored midday without moving. The first proof that it is not a plant is usually a foot. The foot comes up out of the black water slowly, narrow and long-toed, and goes down again one length further along the line, toes laid the way the line runs. Then the other foot does the same.

For a while after that it passes for a wading bird. It has the legs and the patience. But it has no bill and no feathers, and where wings would be it carries two flat bundles of ribs, folded against its sides like shut fans. The head is small and smooth all round, like the end of a thumb. There is no opening in it, no fold, no place where an ear was. The eyes are set low and look down.

The legs do the hearing, if it is hearing. Even on a still line the iron is never quite still, and at every stroke the long shins blur. The knees are loose and take it. Above the knee nothing moves. The head rides level, like a bowl carried full.

It does not look at the flames. It looks at them in the water. A flame that lies whole on the surface means iron that can be stood on, and a smeared flame means iron that can't. It walks by keeping a blue point sharp in front of its feet.

It feeds at the edge. It stands on the quiet with its toes along the line, hinges at the hip, and opens one fan out over the shaken water. Open, the fan is the size of a winnowing basket, a score of ribs with a comb of fine dark hair between them. It sweeps through the haze, or where there is no haze it skims the tips of the stipple. The droplets carry the rust-colored skin of the water, flung up with them. The fan comes back beaded and is drawn shut through the mouth, a lipless slot as wide as the head, and comes out black. Then the other fan goes out.

It is a shore animal's tool. Something like it once combed what the waves threw up a beach. Here the iron does the throwing.

Each of them feeds to one engine. The haze jumps at every concussion, and the fan goes out to meet the jump on that engine's stroke and no other. Two on the same line may be keeping different engines. Their fans fall out of step, drift, and fall back in, as the engines do. When the strokes bunch into a barrage, every fan along a line opens together, and for a minute the line looks like one creature. Then it comes apart again.

None of them goes near an engine. The dry ring has no water to throw, and the moss of the collar is too deep to stand in. One of them may keep an engine's time all its life without ever going closer to it than the place it hatched.

They keep to the lines, and where two arcs meet they turn square. To change lines they wait at the edge until the hammering spreads out, then cross the singing plate between strokes, legs blurred to the hip, head level. They don't do it often.

A line is wide enough for one. When two meet on it, both stop, and each opens its mouth. The inside is pale. They stand like that for some time, a pace apart. If anything comes out, it can't be known. Then the smaller one backs to the nearest crossing and lets the other by.

When rain comes on hard and the pitch of the plate climbs, they feel the line leaving before it has gone. Each one shifts a hand's width outward and stands on iron that is still singing a little, waiting for the quiet to arrive under it. It arrives. The moss follows weeks later.

In the rail district they do something the others don't. They step down to the rails, lay a folded fan along the steel, and draw it through rib by rib, the way a blade is drawn along a stone. It clears the scale from the combs. Their fans are blacker and finer than any others in the basin, and that is why the rails are bright. A few lines away, past where the rails stop, the trick isn't known, and the combs are clotted orange.

Twice a day, when the launders empty over the wall and the engines slow, the haze settles and there is nothing to comb. For those few minutes the fans hang open to dry. Any of them standing near a standpipe goes to it and holds one fan up behind the flame. The wet comb takes the light, and from a distance there is a blue disc where there had been a blue point. From any one disc six or seven others can be seen. One closes, and further off another opens. This goes on until the plate's note starts to rise. Then the discs shut, one after another, and are points again.

The eggs are dropped. One that is ready leans out over the worst of the stipple and lets them fall, a dozen or so, each the size of a rivet head and the same grey. They dance on the iron. The plate walks them, as it walks every loose thing, away from where it shakes and toward where it doesn't. By the next day they lie along a still line in a row, like a third set of rivets. Nothing sits on them. When the line moves, the plate moves them with it.

What hatches stands up within the hour, thin as a rush. It has fans the size of thumbnails and no engine. It combs at every stroke and between strokes and catches little. Most don't last. In those that do, there is a day when the fan goes out on a stroke, comes back, and goes out again on the same engine's next. After that the time is kept.

The old ones can be told by their fans, worn to bare ribs with a few hairs between, and by their ankles, polished pale at the waterline. In some wet season a line leaves and an old one doesn't step. The singing reaches its feet. It stands in it for a while, blurred to the hip and then to the head, and goes down. The plate does with it what it does with the eggs. Over some days it is walked out to the line it didn't follow and turned until it lies along it. Moss covers it the first season. In the second, rushes come up through the ribs, a single row the length of the body. Where a line has held for a long time there are several bodies end to end, and the row is long.

A living one will often stand in such a row. It may only be that the rushes break the wind. It stands with its fans shut, grey as the plate, and when the wind leans the flames, it doesn't lean.
