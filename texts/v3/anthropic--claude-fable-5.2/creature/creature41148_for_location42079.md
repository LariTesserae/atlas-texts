**The Tenter**

At first look, nothing here is larger than a swift. The evidence against that is small and takes a while to add up.

One of the sodium lamps is yellow. The others shine brown through their crust of moths, but on any given night one has been scraped back to the glass, and it is never the same lamp two nights running. There are forty lamps, and the clean one travels the ring like the hand of a slow clock, once around in forty nights. The moss on the gallery floor has a path worn into it, one foot wide, keeping well back from the edge. Beside each green band, the plate carries short level scratches, hundreds of them, ranked like the marks on a tide gauge.

Then something crosses a bare band. It goes up a cable tray hand over hand, quickly, reaches the green above, and stops, and is gone. She is the colour of the still levels and she keeps to them. She is seen only in the gaps, for a few seconds at a time.

She is the tenter, the old works word for whoever minds a machine. It is stencilled on the breast of a boiler suit that is now more patch than suit, the patches cut from dead moss felt and sewn on with fig root. There was a name under the stencil once. She is small and grey, with hair cut short by tin snips, and she goes barefoot because the plate tells her things. A fan bearing going dry, eight kilometres down, arrives in her soles as a flutter a week before it shows on any dial.

She does not know whether she is deaf, and there has never been a way to find out. Even on the outer gallery, where the air is too thin to carry much, the note comes up through the frost into her feet.

She has learned that still is not the same as quiet. In the green bands the air does not move, but the note squeezes hardest there, a little under sixty times a second, in the ears and the sinuses and the chest. In the bare bands the pressure eases and everything shakes instead. She has chosen the squeezing, as the moss has. Her bed is a cable tray in the fourth band, lined with felt taken from the dead margin and never from the living. It hooks into a slotted upright, with the slots a hand apart. Every few seasons she moves it one slot up or down.

She finds the still level with a swift's feather on a thread. Held out at arm's length, it stands up from her fingers like everything else here. In the bare bands it is a blur. As she raises it, the blur narrows, and at one height the edge of the vane comes sharp. She scratches the plate there, and those are the tide marks.

What she keeps:

- **A wrench:** It is on a lanyard, because she lost three before she understood that here you do not know you have dropped something until you reach for it.
- **The feather and the scriber:** These are for finding the still level and marking it.
- **The snips:** They cut felt, fig root, and her hair.
- **Moth cake:** It is pressed from each night's scraping and dried on the warm stretch of the bus bars. She eats it with figs and drinks from the tank-plants, whose water tastes of coin like all the rest.

Her work is mostly reading. In the morning, when the daylight comes level through the louvres, she looks across the throat at the shelves of mist. Flat shelves mean the fans are in step. Then she walks to the gauge board. Two of the dials are behind the fern now, and for those she writes *fern*.

Under each louvre actuator is a handwheel for the days the actuator sticks, and she gives the blades the degree they have missed. Closing them flattens the note and opening them sharpens it, as with the cap of any organ pipe. It is the one thing she does that the whole room feels, and she does it by quarter-turns, with the feather out in her other hand.

The log is scratched through the paint of the warm housings, the only plate in the room that is both dry and still. The geckos lie on it and have to be moved. It is in five hands. Each begins as a careful copy of the one before, down to the crossed sevens, and loosens over the years into itself. Hers has leaned left for a long time now. The entries use words she has never heard said, as she has never heard any word said: *steady*, *trim*, *fern*. Nothing records how one hand gives way to the next. There is a day of readings in the old hand, and the next day there are readings in the new.

There is a second record, and she does not keep it. The swifts build from whatever the updraught carries, which is moss crumb, fig down, moth wing and hair. The newest layer of every nest between the blades is bound with grey. Under that is the same hair, darker. Deeper, in nests older than her first entry, there is hair that was never hers, a coarse black and below it a red.

When she rests, she sits at the gallery edge with the roots streaming up past her knees, raises one hand, and spreads the fingers toward the far wall. Some of the frogs over there show their pale feet. They may be answering, or they may have been signalling anyway.

And she hums, though she could not say why she began. She sets her breastbone against a stiffening ring and holds a note, and where it is wrong she feels a slow beat in her chest like a hand opening and closing. She slides the pitch until the beat lengthens and stops, and then for as long as the breath lasts there is nothing to tell her from the wall. Over the years her note has sunk with the flue's by a little less than a semitone. She has not noticed, any more than the moss has.
