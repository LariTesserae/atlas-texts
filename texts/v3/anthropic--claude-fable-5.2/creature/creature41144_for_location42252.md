On paper the Box belongs to a contractor. By number it belongs to the cockroaches, or to the mites, if anyone could count mites. By weight, noise and heat it belongs to the bats. Since everything else down here is eating what falls from them, or eating something that does, it is simplest to say the bats live here and the rest lodge with them.

From the platform they are a texture. The crown looks upholstered in something dark and badly fitted, and the surface crawls a little, as the guano does below. From the upper landings of the scaffold stair it comes apart into animals. Each is the size of a thumb and weighs about what three sheets of paper weigh. The fur is the brown of strong tea. The ears are wide and leathery and nearly meet over the forehead, the upper lip is pleated like the neck of a drawstring bag, and a thin bare tail pokes out past the membrane. They hang by their hind claws from the mesh, which happens to have been welded at a gauge that suits them, and they hang touching, packed so tightly that the steel can't be seen.

The static comes apart too. Most of it is argument over room. One bat shifts and the four it touches object. Some of it is song. Here and there a male holds a patch of mesh, often the square around a single bolt plate, and defends it with a short rattling phrase repeated for hours while a handful of females hang inside the boundary. Through ear defenders none of this is anything but hiss.

The heat is on purpose. On the stillest stretch of the crown, where no draught reaches from the shaft or the tunnel mouths, the mesh in season is pink instead of brown. This is the crèche. Several thousand pups hang there, naked and blind, each born at a quarter of its mother's weight and parked among strangers within hours. They can't yet hold their own temperature, so the colony holds it for them. That is what the packing is for, and it is probably why the first bats stayed, when they followed warm air down a shaft and found a ceiling nobody was using.

Some of those first ones may still be up there. A free-tail that avoids the snake can last ten years, and the oldest lime straw on the crown records fewer than that. But most of the roost was born here and has never hung from limestone. A cave, to them, is a place with a hum in it and a pool of orange light at one end, where the ceiling comes with steel squares at regular intervals. They steer by the plates, the dead rig, the cabin roof and the hard clean echo of the one finished platform edge.

Everything below is arranged by what drops. The roaches eat the guano. The mites and crickets eat what the roaches leave, and the mould that grows on it. The centipedes eat the crickets. The rats come down off the cable trays for whatever is largest. A pup that loses its grip, as some do every day, lands on the drift alive and does not stay that way long. Within a couple of days the hide beetles have it down to a skull smaller than a fingernail, and the drifts are seeded with them all the way through.

The lamps run a second, smaller economy, and it is losing. Each working bulb is an estate, with its green halo, its ferns and moths, and three or four geckos with fixed stations and a strict order among them. When a bulb fails the moss browns within the month and the geckos walk along the wall to the next lamp, where they are not welcome. Nobody replaces bulbs. Each year the electric part of the Box gets a little darker and the bat part a little deeper.

Exactly one animal down here has a name, and the name is a gesture, because the crew can't hear each other. It is one forearm hung from the other hand, wrist cocked. They have seen her twice by day, asleep along the yellow duct with a bulge a third of the way down. Mostly they find her skins on the ladder cage, each longer than the last, the latest a little over two metres. At dusk she anchors her back half in the cage, holds the rest out into the column, and strikes at whatever touches her. She misses most of them. She needs two or three, and out of tens of thousands that is only a toll.

The fruit bats keep their corner by the shaft. There are a few dozen of them, twice the size of the others, with dog faces and open brown eyes that follow a torch. They don't join the column. They go up after it, singly, and come back with the city's figs in them. One of them, or one like them, presumably planted the tree at the shaft head whose roots are now on their way down.

At dusk the colony becomes one thing. It loosens along the crown, drops, and forms a slow wheel the length of the vault, turning over the pond. Then one edge of the wheel finds the shaft and the whole of it pays out upward like thread off a reel. Above, it spreads across the city and eats, each animal taking about a third of its own weight in moths and planthoppers before morning. This is how the Box is fed. Apart from a few figs and the moths that wander down the draught, the only food that enters it is well over a hundred kilos of insects a night, carried down the shaft already ground fine.

They come back before dawn as single animals, folding their wings and falling down the shaft like thrown stones. A nursing female lands in roughly the right square metre of crèche and calls. Several hundred pups answer. She walks over them on wrists and hind feet, calling, until one answering voice and one smell are the right ones. Each pup's call is its own and stays fixed for as long as it is needed. This is the loudest hour in the Box, and the crew have never heard it, because by Thursday mid-morning it has settled back to static.

The station has no name. For a few weeks each year there are thousands of names under its roof, each used by exactly one other animal. Then a pup lets go of the mesh one evening, drops a metre, and finds that its wings work. It flies its first two hundred metres under bolt plates, by the light of one bulb in three. After that it meets the shaft and the snake, and comes out into a sky it had no reason to expect.
