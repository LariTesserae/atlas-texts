Count first, because the hall seems to ask for it. There is one kind of moth, one kind of snail, one kind of frog, and two swifts in season. None of them was sent for. They came up in straw and clinker the way grit comes home in a boot, and they are lodged here more than they live here.

The men don't live here either. There are riveters, glaziers, the crane driver, and an attendant who reads the glass each hour and writes *clear*, *beaded*, *running* or *raining* in a ruled book. At the end of the shift they ride the hoist down through the cloud to the engine houses. By the time the caged lamps come on, there is nobody on the deck who has a name.

What lives here is on the drawings. It is listed between the finned radiators and the relief valve as *condensing surface, vegetable*, with an area in square feet and a tolerance either side. It is the only item on the sheet that has ever exceeded its figure.

For the first six bays it would be simplest to call it the plants. But the tree ferns were bought, crated, and stood in tubs where the ink said, and a thing that has been stood somewhere does not yet live there. What lives here is what joined them up: the film that greened the felt before the ferns were unpacked, the liverwort that closed the gaps, the root that went from one tray to the next without asking the survey. The ferns are its bulk, as the ribs are the hall's. The creature is the part nobody ordered.

It is one animal, if that is the word. It rains all at once. It dries all at once when the vents open. When the engines below work hard it quickens, bay after bay, like something running upstairs.

By the fitters' reckoning it is two years old. By its own it is about three and a half thousand rains old, and it has no other unit. It has never had a winter or a dry month, nor a night that was properly dark. Every drop it has drunk has been through a boiler first, so it has never tasted a stone. Until last spring it had never had a death. The first frond to brown on the tree fern in the second bay hung there for months, because nothing up here knew what a dead frond was for. Then the snails found it. The moth, the snail, and the frog are organs it is only now acquiring. It is growing itself an underside.

It is young in the way the concrete is young. It follows water, and the water has not yet learned the floor, so its edges on the deck wander. A green tide mark sits in one place this week and a yard off the next. It is literal. It keeps the first rule perfectly and has never shifted a tray. The third rule it keeps by being it: the clock is read off its breath.

It has shown something like will only once, when the fig took hold of the standpipe. Everything else it has was handed to it. That one thing it took, and it has not let go.

It does not live evenly along its length. In the glazed bays it is upholstery, obedient to the copper wire and settled into something that already passes for age. It is most itself where the hall is least finished. That means the cress in the seventh bay, the moss on steel hoisted a month ago, the straw sprouting in the glass crates. It has even found some way down the hoist to meet the girders before they come up.

The engineers say bays were added because leaf outran glass. From the deck it looks the other way about. This is the only thing in the tower that grows, and the iron is how it does it. A stem puts on a joint, hardens behind, and stays soft only at the tip. The hall has nine joints so far, and the creature lives at the tip. When the eighth bay is glazed it will live in the ninth.

It does not keep the second rule. The rope is the edge for men, and the moss goes under it.

At the fog seam it has something like a skin, which leans in and out with the pressure. On the afternoons the true cloud rises and comes in through the open steel, the skin goes. For an hour the creature has no outline and is touched by water that was never manufactured. It does nothing about this that can be seen. But on the outermost rib there is now a grey-green crust that came in no crate and does not need the steam. It is the first thing here that was ever part of a hillside.

It has no voice it knows of. The frog's one note is taken for pipework. The valve's sigh every eleven minutes could be taken for breathing. The creature would not be the one to correct either mistake, since it was drawn in the same ink as the header and nobody has told it which lines are itself. As far as it can tell, the barrel-thick pipe is its trunk and the engines are its root. That steadiness in the soles of the feet is the nearest thing it has to ground.

It will not look at you. Stand an hour and nothing happens. Then the crane goes over, and the fringe on the girder passes above the fringe below. A line of drips crosses the trays one after another, and the fronds lift and settle. For that minute it has been touched along its whole length by a piece of itself.

It does have a name, got the way water here finds the drains. Chalk holds the wet longer than primer does, so the fitters' marks go green before the steel around them. In the old bays the numbers stand up in moss, with the arrows, and the creature has grown along the arrows. On the east column of the eighth bay someone chalked his initials, D.K., and went down with the spring gang and did not come back up. The letters are green now, raised, and soft to the thumb. The riveters say D.K.'s through to nine, or D.K.'s been at the crates again. It is the only name on the deck after lamp-lighting.

Tonight the seam glows its dull yellow. Beyond it, on bare plate, a chalk rectangle and a chalk 10 are waiting. The steel has not been ordered. By the time it comes the number will already be green, and somebody will have to chalk an eleven.
