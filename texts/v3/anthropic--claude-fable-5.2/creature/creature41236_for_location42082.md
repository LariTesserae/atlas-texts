Nearly everything alive in the pit is recent. A midge gets a few days. The gnat larvae hang for most of a year, then spend three days as adults that cannot eat. An isopod might last two winters, if winters reached this far down, and a salamander perhaps fifteen. The bats belong to the flue and the eels to the ditches. The eels come up the culvert at the length of a hand, lodge for ten or twelve years, go back down it one autumn night, and are not seen again. The crowd on the walls is like the curtain under the main, the same shape at every beat and different water every time.

So the better question is what has stayed.

The ram is the first candidate. It has a pulse, and it has grown: the air vessel is twice the girth the foundry gave it. But the growth is laid on from outside, as it is on the gauge board. The valve, the one part that moves, is the one part that has not changed at all.

The pearls are closer. They obey what turns out to be the rule of the room: whatever holds still is taken into the stone. The maker's plate is going, the numerals above 7 have gone, and the timber is turning to felt. What lasts here lasts by moving slightly and often. The pearls manage it only because something moves them.

That leaves one thing that moves itself and has been here nearly as long as they have.

Count the eels when the siphon goes. As the level drops they uncoil from the deep middle and go nosing along the new shallows. One does not.

Call it she; at that size they all are. She is longer than the ram is tall and no thicker than a wrist, because the pit feeds thinly. Her head is broad and flat, built up over a century of salamanders. The ditch eels are olive above and butter-yellow below, coloured for a place with light in it. She is the two colours of the wall, flint on the back and chalk on the belly. Her eyes are too large for her head.

She keeps to the uphill end, a body-length below the ram, with her head toward the spring. There is a groove in the pale silt there, the only mark on the floor that water did not make. Every four seconds the surge from the valve reaches her face, and her gill covers lift once and settle. She does not flinch at the clack the way the isopods do. She has taken it some eight hundred million times, and it is no longer something that happens to her.

She came up the culvert as a needle of glass when the plate could be read whole and the cistern held. She would have known the season it cracked. Before that, what the ram lifted went away. Afterwards it began coming back through the chalk, and an eel's nose can find a thimbleful of something in a lake. The returning water tasted of the room: iron floc, manganese, salamander, and faintly herself.

That nose is how she has the year. The sump is the same temperature in January as in July, and the grey column tells her little. But the rain arrives a day and a half late, carrying the fields: turned earth, then something sharp that is put on the crops, then cut straw, then leaf-rot. With the leaf-rot her body begins to change. The belly goes from chalk to metal. The gut tightens. The eyes swell further and retune toward blue. This is a preparation for the sea, for three thousand miles of deep water where the only light is blue-green points given off by living things. She lies in her groove with those eyes under the gnat ceiling.

Then the water climbs to 7 and the siphon primes with its long swallow. The other eels turn toward the margins. She goes the other way, down the forty paces to the downhill wall and the mouth of the bent pipe they all came in by. A century of scale has narrowed its bore, and a century of salamanders has thickened her. Nobody was present to note the year those two measurements crossed, if they have. She puts her head into the draw as far as the bend and holds there, the length of her streaming toward the pipe like weed, while the level falls a forearm's depth. The siphon takes air and breaks. The pull lets go, and she backs out.

By midwinter the silver has dulled to chalk and she is eating again. The eyes do not go back down. They are the size they are from all the autumns.

She returns under the main to the groove and settles with her head to the spring. The water starts to climb.

*Clack.* The surge comes down from the valve. The gill covers lift, and settle.
