You will count them wrong the first time, because they are never all on the shelf. Nine are here on any day. Two more are on the steps at the leeward end, where the columns break down toward the lower country in six-sided treads, each a stride across. Those two are going down with slabs of the ringing stone lashed to back-frames, or coming up with grain, salt, lamp fat and hammerstones. Everyone takes the steps in turn except the old man and the girl, so the nine are seldom the same nine.

They are broad through the back and thick in the forearm, and they go barefoot on wide pale soles. Moss has stained them green to the knee and the elbow, and it does not wash out. They wear capes of plaited fibre that shed the cloud in drips from the hem, padded at the shoulders with wool stripped from the rosette leaves. Each carries a pebble of the dark rock on a cord at the wrist. That pebble is the voice they use outdoors.

A knock has a rhythm, which says who, and a number, which says what. The where comes free, since every block in every wall has its own pitch and they know them all. Very little can be said this way: come, lift, stop, look, cloud. For anything more, one of them walks the distance and speaks into the other's ear with a hand cupped round it. They keep the habit even in the huts, where the turf takes the top off the chord. So nothing here is ever said to more than one person at a time. No one addresses the others, and no one is overheard. What all of them need to know travels down the streets from hand to hand, like the flat palm.

They do not hear the chord. They hear what rides on it and how it moves. It goes flat before any cloud shows at the edge, because the cloud is in the tubes first, and that is their signal to finish setting wedges. Dry wooden wedges go into a line of sockets in the morning, and the cloud comes through and swells them. Some time in the afternoon the slab lets go along the line with one clear note, and whoever set it looks up from wherever they are.

They listen with their mouths open. The sound sits in the jaw, and changing the shape of the mouth changes which part of the chord stands up in the skull. Walking a lane, they look like people about to speak. The girl does it most. She was born on the shelf and is the only one who has never been down the steps. She is the one who will take a course off a wall, and if asked why, she points to the plumb bob, which does show a lean. The bobs usually do.

Most of the work is not building. The ticking puts the walls out, so they take down and relay and recut the marks. They clear the drains with an antler tine, since moss will close a hand-wide drain in a season. They dress blocks for courses that cannot be laid yet and stack them. When one of them needs a block's bedmates, they strike it and walk toward the answer.

One of the women cuts the notch. She has cut twenty-six. At the turn of the year she stands with her heels in two scratched marks and raises her right hand, and someone marks the wall at her fingertips with charcoal. Then she cuts. Ask whether the wall is going up or the floor going down and she shrugs. Either way, every notch she has made except the newest is above her hand, and she could not touch her own first year without a block to stand on. Above hers the line steps a hand's width to the left and goes on at a taller person's reach, then steps again. There are three steps. No one alive knew the hand that began it.

The old man walks the bowls. His hands will no longer close on a hammerstone, but they can tie a knot. At each doorway a cord hangs from a peg in the jamb, with a knot in it for every day the water has kept still. He looks at the bowl, ties the knot and goes on. He lifts the snails off the rims. They are small and pale, with shells thin enough to show the dark body coiled inside, there being little lime up here to build with. They climb to drink, and a snail drinking sets rings going on the water. He knows rings from a shiver, because rings start somewhere and a shiver is everywhere at once. He moves the snails anyway.

The moths he finds already drowned. They do not live here. The wind carries them up the mountain with seed-fluff, spiders on their threads and small flies from fields two days below, and lets them fall. That fall of bodies is what the pair of dark birds works at the edge all day. He tucks each moth under the moss at the foot of the wall, where everything else here gets its living.

When a bowl shivers, the cord is cut at the peg and a new one hung. Fibre is scarce on the shelf, so the cut cords are plaited into the hauling ropes. Every sledge here is drawn on counts that had to start over.

A shiver counts only if someone sees it, and nine people cannot watch two hundred bowls. A room out on the terraces could pass its year simply by not being looked at. They know this, so the longer a cord gets, the more that room is watched.

The longest cord anyone remembers hung at a small room on the lowest terrace, two plots from the edge. It reached three hundred and forty-one knots. For the last forty days they took turns sitting in that doorway, at night with a fat lamp on the sill so that its flame lay in the bowl. Two of the men cut rafters from fallen fern trunks and stacked them against the outer wall. The click came on a clear afternoon, when the woman who cuts the notch was sitting. She held up the flat hand, though the street behind her was empty, and then she cut the cord.

The rafters are still stacked there, with filmy ferns growing along them. No one has moved them, and no one has cut any since. That room holds the highest note on the terraces, and under a roof it would lose it. If any of them has said so, it was into one ear.

The two hundred plots are for the people below, who have been about to come up since before the first notch. They hang the slabs in their doorways and strike them for births and deaths. They ask the carriers how the town is coming. The carriers hold out a level hand and tilt it slightly, and this is taken to mean soon. The carriers sleep badly down there. They gesture instead of speaking and knock on tables, and are thought to be mutes.

Each builder has a plot picked out too, and will show you which. The old man's is beside the cistern stairs, for his knees. The notch-cutter's is against the headwall, in the drip but out of the wind. These are sensible choices, and they are not the rooms the builders go to when the work is put down.

The old man lies on his back in the big room on the second lane, the one that booms, with his hands flat on his chest. He says it gets into the knuckles and loosens them. The girl stands in a narrow room on the fifth lane with her mouth open, turning her head a little at a time. The two just up from the steps go, before food or fire, into the nearest room that keeps the bottom note, and stand there until their chests have it back. The notch-cutter sits in the far corner of the room that seems silent.

They take nothing in and leave nothing. A room is not to be lived in until it is roofed, and by their reckoning sitting is not living. But moss a forearm deep keeps a shape. In the booming room there is a dent the length of a man, with two shallower places for the heels. In the narrow room there are two footprints side by side. In the corner of the quiet room there is a hollow, and the moss in the wall joints behind it is pressed flat at the height of a pair of shoulders.

By the time the next cloud has gone through, the moss has drunk and risen. The old man, coming round with his knots, finds every floor level and every room holding nothing.
