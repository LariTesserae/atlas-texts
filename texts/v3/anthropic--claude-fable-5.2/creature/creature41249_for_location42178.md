The first answer is a list, and the list is true: frogs, crabs, midges, geckos, rails, swifts, bats, spiders, snails. But start lower, with what is missing. The basin has no soil, yet everything a soil does gets done. The breaking down, the holding of water, and the turning of dead leaf into something a root can drink all happen at speed, inside bodies. The soil here has legs. It is red and the size of a thumbnail, or grey and armored and rolls shut when touched, or it carries a shell the color of wet bark and leaves a rasped track across a fallen frond. It covers the rock the way earth covers rock elsewhere, but it is never more than an hour deep, and it does not lie down.

Nothing these animals leave stays. A crab's leg tips are ground blunt on the lava ropes, come back sharp at the molt, and the crab eats the shell it cast. When a bat drops from the sheet in a tube mouth, snails rasp its bones away for the lime, and later other snails take the shells of those snails. There is no bone anywhere on the floor, no feather, no empty shell, no shed skin. The rock keeps all its corners and the animals keep none. The only round things in the basin are alive, or are water: an egg on the felt, a gecko's eye, a fiddlehead, a bead of sweat about to join another.

Because there is no night to hide stillness in, and because the eaters work by one rule, that what does not move is food, nothing here stops altogether. A rail stands on a root with one eye shut and half its brain dark, and after a while it changes eyes. Wakefulness crosses the bats like a hand passing over fur. Anything that holds too still is tested. A millipede walks over it. A crab plucks at it. So dying here is not an event with a body at the end. An old frog slows, the slowing is noticed, and by the time there would have been a corpse there are only crabs, then droppings on a root, then the green edge of a new frond. No animal in the basin has ever come upon a dead one of its kind.

Take the smallest pan, the one the size of a cupped hand. It holds two frogs. One calls while the other sits, and then they trade. There are always eggs on the rim felt, and the water will carry two tadpoles through to the end and no more. When those two climb out with their tails still on, there are four frogs at a pan for two. A short while later there are two again, and which two makes no difference to the pan. They call at the note the hollow gives them, because the rock is part of the voice, so the note has not changed. A few steps away a pan the size of a table holds eleven frogs and a lower chord. The sound that seems a single fabric is thousands of fixed points, each sounding the pitch it had when the fabric began. Something with a good enough ear could cross the basin blind by it, and perhaps the bats do.

A midge lives a few hours. The column over the pan has stood since the pan first held water. The gecko at its edge sits on the crest where a gecko has always sat.

So the frog is a poor answer to the question of who lives here. The frog has been here a little while. The place for two frogs at the cupped pan has been here since the forest took hold. The same goes for the gecko's station, the column, the sheet of bats, and the fern that is the same size it was. These are the residents: shapes that hold while what fills them changes, like the pans at their one level, or the threads of steam, which are steady and never the same steam.

The shapes are not separate. Each is fed by the ones beside it and eaten by them, and when they are counted together they come out as one inhabitant.

It is ten kilometers across and one tree thick. It has a dark green pelt. It sweats. It is warm the way a gecko on a hot stone is warm, with heat it never had to make, and the stone has never cooled. It has no heart and nothing like a vein, because nothing in it flows farther than the nearest hollow. Every handbreadth of it feeds itself from the film on the rock and from its neighbors. It eats only what it sheds, and eats it within the hour, so it has never gained and never lost. It has one voice, and it has never drawn a breath in the middle of it. It gives off a little light. It sleeps as its rails sleep, a part at a time, and has never once been entirely awake or entirely asleep.

It has no age. Elsewhere a forest's age is in its ground, in what fell and stayed and darkened. This one consumes its age as fast as it is offered, so it is exactly as old as it was when the first spore held to the warm rock. It has no memory either, because a memory is something laid down, and nothing is laid down here. It does not remember the note of the cupped pan. It sounds it. It does not recall its size. It is that size again, every hour, by eating the difference.

It lacks nothing, so it reaches for nothing. It has not climbed the rims. At the low edge its last ferns stand with their roots at the lagoon, and it has never tried the water.

It has no enemies and no guests. Anything that came over the rim would be the first thing here to carry a day inside it, with a need to stop completely, both eyes shut, for hours. Such a visitor would break webs with its face, and the webs would be restrung behind it. It would find the rock warm and the air gentle. Sooner or later it would lie down, all at once, the way things from outside do, and at the edge of its vision the ground would appear to shift slightly.

By the next hour the basin would be the same size it was.
