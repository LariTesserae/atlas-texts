Most of what lives in the trough lives in the water the moss is holding. Squeeze that handful over a lens and it is crowded with water bears, rotifers and threadworms, all able to freeze solid at the frosted tips and resume when it rains. Above them are the three that leave signs: the ravens, the ptarmigan and the fox. All three have adjusted to the well the same way, by carrying on as if they had never used their voices for anything.

Then there is one more, and it was here before any of them.

The first sign of it is a path. Along the west side of the longest fissure runs a seam in the moss that the survey crew took for a crack. It is a slot two hands wide and knee-deep, straight for long stretches, with moss walls and a floor of bare basalt. That floor is the only rock in the trough that has lost its edge, worn to a dull polish. Nobody cut the moss back from it. The moss came later and grew up on either side of something that was already walking there.

So it is a little younger than the rock. It arrived when the lava was cool enough to cross and still bare, and it kept to one line because fresh basalt cuts, and a line walked ten thousand times cuts less. When the moss came it found a second reason. West of the path, in a windless hollow, is a depression longer than a person and narrow, deeper at one end. It lay down there once when the moss was ankle-deep. The moss has since thickened to the knee all around the shape without filling it, and it has not left the path on the surface since, with one exception.

Seen from the rim, it is a back. It travels at the level of the moss, as the back of a swimmer travels at the level of the water, and it is the colour of the floor in this weather. The easy mistake is to call it moss-covered. The covering is hair, coarse and grey, and over the centuries what gets a start in the moss has got a start in it: a strip of true moss down the spine, lichen crusted on the shoulders where it cannot reach. After rain it turns wet green along with everything else. When it stops it is a swell among swells, and the ptarmigan sits against it out of the wind.

It goes on forearms and shins, belly a hand above the rock, its weight spread along four long bones. The body under the hair is nothing like the outline. It is built like the walls, in long straight lengths meeting at hard corners, with more distance between joints than seems reasonable. Everything in the trough is a rounded version of something jagged, and so is it.

The jagged version lives in the fissures. Where the path meets the curled lip it goes over the edge, and the fifteen metres of wall below are the only place in the trough with room to be tall. On a ledge among the browned ferns, in still air a few degrees warmer than the surface, it stands up. Unfolded it is about twice a person's height and as narrow as the crack requires, back against one wall, hands flat on the other. It spends the blue hours like that, looking down. The water is still enough to show it its face, and until this winter nothing else had seen it.

It used to know the water without looking. Before the well, this was one of the quietest places in the country, a kilometre of moss with no stream or leaves, and it kept track of its ground by ear. It heard rain going down through the rock for days after a storm, the frost letting go, the tick of the faults taking up their two centimetres, and it could tell the water's level from the path. Since autumn it has heard none of that. Now it reads the rock through its palms. The polished floor hums, more strongly toward the pad, and it has learned that hum as the needle in the shed has, as a nearly straight line. A drop in pressure would reach its hands before it reached the logbook.

It has a voice, one low held note. For three centuries it sounded that note at dusk from the ledge, down the length of the fissure. The walls gave it back a fraction late, and that was the only answer there ever was. It still opens its mouth at dusk. It feels something in its sternum, but the well is in its sternum too, and it cannot tell the two apart. It doesn't know whether it is still making a sound.

The people were the first things it had ever seen stand upright in the open. They stood on the pad in the wind as if height cost nothing. It noticed that off the pad they walk as it does, testing each step, and that they come in twos and put their faces into each other's hoods. It knows every bootprint they have left and will be passing them for forty years.

The path runs four hundred metres west of the pad at its nearest. In the long dusk it has begun to stop there, and stopping was never part of the path before. A light left on is something it understood at once, and incorrectly.

From the stopping place the brown fan is in view, and early in the winter it worked out what the fan was: ground with nothing left to harm. It crossed on a night when the plume lay down westward and hid the lamp. The grey crust broke under it like ice on a puddle. In the middle of the fan, in warm mist, inside a noise so complete that it served as walls, it stood up with nothing behind it and nothing in front.

It goes back whenever the wind is right. The prints it leaves are long and narrow, deeper at the heel, and within days the fallout glazes them over. The fan is the one place in the trough where a mark heals. The crew have twice noticed broken crust and written "fox?" in the logbook. The fox's own line shows what the fox makes of it: a wide berth around the pad, and one patch on the rime where the prints stop and something sat for a while, facing the fan.

The standing has begun to show. The side it turns to the pipe has taken the glaze, the hair stiff and gritty with mineral from shoulder to hip, and the lichen on that shoulder is dying. When the wind swings early it walks home with rime feathered down the other side.

It knows nothing about the blank flange. Whichever way the decision goes, the discharge will stop, and the quiet will come back all at once. The mist will end, the crust will stop healing, and the moss will start back across the fan at a centimetre a year. On the first dusk after that it will stand on the ledge and open its mouth and hear, a fraction late, exactly what it has been saying all winter.
