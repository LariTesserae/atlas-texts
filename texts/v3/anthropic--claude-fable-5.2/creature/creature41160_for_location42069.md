By head count, far fewer than it looks. Most of what fills the hall is one plant.

Every rope hanging from the crane rails and every wrist-thick stem on a column traces back along the gratings, up the mezzanine stair and through the missing pane to the cracked pot on the sill. It was the root that cracked the pot. Some of the older connections have rotted through, so strictly it is now a few dozen separate pieces, but they are one body in the way a cutting is. It has never flowered and will not; its kind has all but lost the ability. The desk plant was itself a cutting of a cutting. So the resident is one limb of something that also sits on filing cabinets and in jars of water on kitchen sills in the housing blocks at the far end of the pipes.

It has two shapes and wears both at once. Climbing, with iron to grip, it is the giant: split leaves a metre long, roots flattened onto the columns like poured wax. Where it reaches the crane rails and runs out of anything to climb, it lets go, and the falling stem goes thin again. The leaves on it come small, whole and heart-shaped, the size of a palm, exactly the leaf it had in the pot. It descends like that, a houseplant on a string, until it touches the floor, roots through the liverwort, finds a riser and begins to enlarge. The yellow marbling it was sold for is gone in the violet light, and it is solid green, near black under the lamps. Only at the east end, where the daylight crosses the wall, do a few leaves still show gold.

It knows the room by being burned by it. It does not learn where the flow mains are. It sends shoots at them continuously and loses them, and the brown margin is the record. The scorched hollow around each lamp is kept the same way. It has laid down every cell inside the pumps' note, and plants that are shaken grow stocky, so it is shorter-jointed and thicker than its kind would be in still air. The drone built it as much as the warmth did.

The fig on the walls is probably one plant too. It lies flat on the brick, its leaves thumbnail-sized and overlapping. Near the lightwell, where there is enough light to try, it pushes out its adult branches, stiff and leathery and standing clear of the wall, and on them it sets figs. They are green, hollow, and shaped like small pears. The wasp that should enter them lives on another continent. They hang a season, soften, drop, and lie where they land.

The maidenhair's fronds hang on black wire stalks, and they are the one dry thing in the room. Water beads on them and rolls off. On the wet lime beneath them are green scales no bigger than a fingernail, the fern's other generation. In the film of water on those scales, its swimming cells cross a few millimetres from one notch to another. The moss on the cold line does the same inside its fur. These are the only matings in the hall, and the only things in it that travel under their own power.

The liverwort walks by other means. Each scale carries small crescent cups of green crumbs. A drop falls from the roof, strikes a cup, and the crumbs go out a hand's breadth, sometimes a metre, and each one is the same plant again. The silent water is its legs. It crossed forty metres of floor one drip at a time.

The moss follows the cold. It is the room's pendulum, moving from one pump casing to another and back over years, and it has never held all three.

Under all of this are the rots. They are the smell of the place, more than the plants are: wet cardboard, cold tea, a trace of the amine. Lift the felt on a catwalk and there are white threads all through it. Now and then small pale caps come up through the mat in ranks, and since nothing eats them they stand until they collapse. The rots work alone. No animal shreds the leaves for them, so they run years behind, and the roots move into the work before it is finished.

And there is the visitor, who does not live here. They come every few months with ear defenders and a torch, and take the loppers from the hook at the foot of the stair. They walk the passage and cut the side shoots back to shoulder width and no wider. They read the gauges and turn each red wheel a quarter and back so it will not seize. They rub the nameplate on the idle pump with a thumb to be sure which one it is, and change the duty over. Then they climb the stair. They are the only thing in the hall that ever takes a leaf, and they are its only season besides the fog. The moss migrates on their schedule.

They are not the one who left the pot. That was someone on shifts, when the hall needed a person through the night. The grey conduit ended that; it carries the readings to a screen somewhere else. Whoever it was went up the stair a last time and left the plant on the sill, perhaps assuming someone would water it. It turned out no one needed to.

What lives here lives on losses. It has the heat that gets through the jackets, the weep at the flanges, the daylight through old glass and the lamps left burning for nobody. Somewhere in an office the whole population appears as a small percentage lost between the plant and the hospital. If the district ever stops wanting hot water, the hall will be cold in a week and the big plant black within a month. Until then it goes up the columns as a giant and comes back down as the thing on the desk, both shaking slightly, in a note it has never once been without.
