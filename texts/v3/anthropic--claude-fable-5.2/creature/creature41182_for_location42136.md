The evidence is a coat, two full oil cups and a line of red paint. From these the town has assembled a keeper, and it speaks of that keeper in the passive: the engines are minded, the cups are filled, the line gets done. Most people assume several men working turns, since the boiler house runs on turns. Its fire-doors open on the street side, and the stokers who come and go by the whistle have never seen the yard their wall backs onto.

There is one keeper, and she has no turn.

She is not seen crossing because she crosses in the rain. Six or seven minutes is enough for ninety paces and both engine houses, if nothing wants more than oil. While it falls, anyone looking down from the steps sees a black floor, sharp lamps and water. When it stops she is already indoors, and the yard smokes and shines, crossed by no one.

No one remembers her arriving. Every street in the lower town tilts toward the same place, and the streets do not only carry water. She was a piece the flow carried off and left somewhere, and she rooted.

She is a narrow woman the colour of the light. She wears a singlet and trousers cut off at the knee, and no boots, because leather lasts a month here. Her hair is cropped with the packing shears to keep it out of the slack belt. Her hands and feet have not been dry in years, and the pads have set in permanent ridges, pale and seamed like the hollow joints of the floor. They grip wet iron well. The rust liquor from the walls has stained her to the wrists.

She walks on the drop. Each foot goes down with a blow, so the jolt never catches her mid-stride. When the beat drifts she walks the stumbling four-count without thinking, and when the two blows close she stands still and lets them land together, as you would wait for a wave. She no longer flinches at the valve. The banana leaves still do.

Her hearing is mostly gone above ground. She hears through her shins and the flat of her hand: a hot bearing, a gland starting to blow, a change in the length of the suck. What she listens for now is the gap between strokes and whatever is in it. Her own pulse is somewhere under the two engines, a third one slightly out of time with both, and the smallest.

Her food comes down in a lidded can left on the top step and goes back up empty. Nothing in the yard is eaten, and she keeps to that. She has stepped over ten thousand fallen figs. But she drinks there. She tilts a taro leaf and takes the rain off it, iron and cylinder oil, and within the hour some of it is back in the lid by way of her skin. The town holds that the Sump has only ever pumped the one water. The town does not think to count her among the pumps.

A cat from the upper streets comes down most evenings as far as the bottom step and sits with its feet drawn up out of the film. It will not come further, and she has stopped trying to coax it. They look at each other across the brick until the valve lets go. Then the cat is gone and she has not moved.

What she leaves undone is as exact as what she does. She oils the engines and has never cut the fig. She lays her palm on the chimney above the second band, feels what is happening under it, and takes her hand away. The crane was for lifting what one person cannot, a cylinder cover or a beam bearing. She watched the creeper lace it to its rails and did nothing, which was a decision. The next heavy thing that breaks will stay broken.

She is also the only thing in the yard that moves a plant on purpose. A hyacinth offset goes from a crowded tank to an open one. A broken length of taro gets pressed into a silted joint with her heel. She does the work the missing animals would do, without the eating.

When she stops an engine for repacking, the packing is usually sound. She shuts the steam, sits on the plank bench in the engine house with the door open, and watches the corner through the night. By morning the green has put on a foot and every leaf-tip is hanging its bead, and the drip is there in the gap between the other engine's strokes. She goes to the gauge board. The pointer is on the line. Then she opens the tin of red lead and paints the line again. She does not paint it because the pointer has moved, for it has never moved. Each coat marks one more morning the level held on one engine and a corner of leaves, and the tally stands off the enamel like a scar.

The repackings run longer than they used to.

All this ground was estuary, and for a long time she argued with it the way the engines do, by contradiction. Somewhere in the years she changed her method. Taro and hyacinth are mud's own plants, and they answer the water in terms it already accepts. She keeps the level, and the engines are only one of her means of keeping it. She is shifting the load from the iron to the green, a corner at a time. She means to be finished before the fig is.

At dawn she stands at the tank margin while the taro weeps. Her arms hang. Water runs down her wrists, beads at the fingertips, falls, and beads again. In that air it could be condensation or sweat, and she has given up the distinction.

The coat is wool and cut for wind. There is no wind in the yard. It hangs inside the east door for the upper streets, for the morning the board reads true with both engines cold. A fern has started in the breast pocket. She has seen it and left it, as she left the fig.

She has stopped each engine in turn and watched the yard take up the difference. There is a third stoppage she has not tried. For that one she would lift the coat off its hook, shake out the fern, climb four steps and stay away until morning. She would not be at the board at dawn to read the result. She wonders which corner would put on the foot. She thinks the pointer would hold, and she is nearly sure. The line wants another coat first.
