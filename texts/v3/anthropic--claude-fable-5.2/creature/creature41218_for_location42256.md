**The Tenant**

On a first visit you would say that nothing lives here beyond what has already been counted: the crabs, the wrack, the grey shapes that might be mullet, the heron that does not stay. That answer holds for about an hour.

What undoes it is the pools. Walking back across the flat, you see that they tremble a little more just ahead of you than they do behind. The made ground is a hard skin stretched over water, and a skin like that carries a footfall a long way. Something beneath it is being told where you are. So the tenant does not live in the dock, exactly. It lives under the whole flat, in the cooling culverts and the charging tunnels, and the dock is the one room of its house that has lost its roof.

For a while it is easiest to think of it as several animals. Some of the domes that rise in the basin are the tide meeting itself, but some rise and do not spread, and some of the seams that open on the surface are the wake of a back passing just beneath. Each back is about as thick as a mooring post. You begin to count them. There are three, then five, then for a quarter of an hour a single one that seems to run the whole length of the east wall, and then four. The counting is the mistake. It is one animal that does not keep to one body. It parts into lengths, and the lengths go about separately and join again in a different order, and whichever end happens to be leading serves as the head. It is the same animal from tide to tide only in the way the floor is the same floor.

It is hard to see because it is the colour of what it lives in. It is silt brown, with a rust-orange streak running the length of each part, so that on some tides the iron seep from the east wall appears to travel against the eddy. It has no scales, and no fins that anyone could swear to. Its skin has the wet gleam of the low-water mud and something of the mud's fine grain. It makes no sound at all. It is a degree or two warmer than the sea, and the rags of mist lift thickest where a length of it is lying shallow.

The warmth is probably the oldest thing about it. Nobody put it here. The likeliest account is that it came in small, or as many small things, in the years when the culverts ran warm from the furnaces. The warmth suited it, and when the works closed and the water went cold it was already too long to leave by the way it had come.

At low water it does not withdraw. For less than an hour it lies on the floor of the dock under a thumb's depth of mud, in the plain light, and what looks like the mud creeping is the tenant settling. The firebricks, the chain, the wheelset and the glass are what it wears, pressed into its back and carried, in the way a caddis larva wears gravel. But it cannot keep a coat in order when it cannot keep itself in order, so each ebb shows the same things dealt differently. The channels are where it lay, braided when it came to rest in several lengths and one deep curl when it came to rest whole. The crabs are its lodgers.

Anyone who watched a season of ebbs with a notebook would find that the arrangement never repeats and the inventory never changes. There are the same firebricks and the same fathoms of chain. The ladder has had three rungs for as long as anyone has looked, and it has not gone down to two. The ebb through the breach runs hard enough to carry off bottle glass, and no bottle glass leaves. A coin dropped from the coping would be on the floor at the next low water, dealt in among the clinker, and at every low water after that.

The reason nothing leaves is at the seaward corner. One gate leaf lies in the mud and the other is gone, and the tenant has taken the missing leaf's place. On most tides a length of it lies across the breach like a sill, and that is the step in the water. It is holding the basin at a level the sea did not choose, which is what a dock is for, and what this dock did through all the years the tenant was growing in it.

It is not good at the work. It is soft, and the sea leans on it, and it must keep shifting its weight, drawing some of itself back into the tunnels and letting it out again. Each time, the whole surface lifts a hand's width or sinks one. The basin's slow breathing is an animal adjusting its grip, and the band of fine lines on the walls is every level it has tried to hold.

The heron knows about the sill. It stands on the fallen leaf because the step is the one place where small fish are tumbled into reach, and it leaves because the sill moves before anything can be made of that.

The tenant has no eyes, since there is nothing in that water to see. It knows the world by pressure and tremor, and the steadiest thing in that world is the grey cabinet. The hum goes down the galvanised pole into the ground and out through the water table, and the tenant has lived inside that note for years. Some of the trembling in the pools is the hum, and some, a little slower, is the tenant giving it back. The cabinet is also the only thing that has ever written the tenant down. Somewhere there is a trace of water level against time with a second, smaller tide inside the first, and beside it a note from whoever reads such things, saying the sensor has been checked and is not at fault.

It never comes above the coping. The wet lines stop a hand below the gritstone, every one of them. It may be that it cannot climb. It may be that it has learned the rule of the place from underneath: things up there keep the shape they arrive in, and it has no one shape to arrive in.

If you stand at the coping long enough, one of the wandering eddies will bring its raft of foam along the wall and stop beneath you, and it will stay rather longer than an eddy stays. Nothing rises. You are being felt for through the stone, in the way it feels for the rails and the crane pedestal and the penstock, as one more weight on the quay. It has no reason to expect you to leave, because nothing else up there ever has. When you do go, the foam is let go somewhere else, and the pools tremble ahead of you all the way across the flat.
