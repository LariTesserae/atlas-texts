The first answer is a list: the frogs, the cicadas, the cricket, two kinds of lizard, the parrots, the swifts by day and the bats by night. That is accurate, and it stops being adequate as soon as any one of them is watched for an hour.

Take a frog. It sits at the waterline of a bromeliad tank with its throat blown out larger than its body, the skin stretched so thin the brown water shows through it. Both sexes call. On the neighbouring plateaus the female of this frog is mute; here she has a sac of her own, and a pair clasped for spawning go on calling, the two of them slightly out of time. The froglets start before their tails are gone. The tank is part of the voice. A frog in a full tank rings high, and as the dry weeks draw the water down the note sinks with it, so the whole hollow goes flat by a tone or more between storms and is back at pitch the morning after rain. In the worst weeks they call themselves thin. The ones that die slide down into the tank they sang from, and the bromeliad has them.

The others have been bent the same way. Female cicadas, silent everywhere else, crack their wings against the bark, and the males sing through the night because the stone keeps them warm enough to. The cricket has given up on air and uses the rock. The lizards have no voice, so they use the one loud thing they own. The underside of a grey lizard's tail is flat and callused like a heel, and it will not shed that tail when seized, though its cousins on the next plateau drop theirs at a touch. The parrots' quarrel has no subject. A mated pair will sit pressed together, preening each other's faces, and scream the whole time.

None of them knows what the plate does. They are not asking for water. Anywhere else a thirsty animal goes quiet and gets into shade, and whatever lived here and did that is gone. What is left are creatures in which thirst and noise run along the same nerve. They get dry, so they get loud, and then they drink, and nothing in them has ever needed to connect the two.

So the second answer is that one thing lives here. It has several thousand throats and a few hundred tails. It has a pitch, which falls in drought. It has a single reflex, which is the hawk. It even has a way of eating. Since the rock gives nothing, every gram of it came up the cliff inside a swift or a bat, from insects caught over forest two thousand metres down. They come for the water, pay for it in shrieks, and leave their droppings in the tanks and pitchers and cupped leaves. The water is the bait, the flocks are the mouth, and the fat plants are where it keeps what it has swallowed. Midges breed in the tanks, frogs eat the midges, lizards eat the frogs, the dead fall back into the plants, and the same small store of nitrogen goes round and round with a little more added each dusk.

Much of it cannot be heard. The plate runs over hardest when the bats come out, and that is not the loudest hour to a human ear. Most of what bats say is pitched far above hearing, and so is a good part of what cicadas and crickets say. The plate takes all of it. The uproar that can be felt in the teeth may be the smaller share.

But "one thing" makes it sound whole, and it is not whole. It was not here before the plate and would not last one dry season after. The pedestal stands a hand's breadth proud, and this rock takes a very long time to lose a hand's breadth, long enough to make a new kind of lizard. Whoever set the pegs has been gone for all of that. The plate may have been put out on purpose, a dish that pays for song. Or the water may be a by-product of something that was only listening for thunder, and the life is what gathers under any dripping tap. The hollow gives no way to tell. Either way, what lives here is a kept thing whose keeper is absent, and it has gone on getting louder for an audience of one dish.

A few residents stand outside it. The shrimp owe nothing to anyone's voice. They wait in the sand for thunder, which was here first. The hawks nest on the cliff, one pair, and have never once been inside the noise. Silence arrives half a second ahead of them. What a hawk knows is a still bowl, wind on the rim, a ticking, and a pool that shrinks while it drinks. It gets a few mouthfuls from the middle of the plate before the dry margin reaches its feet, takes a grey lizard perhaps one visit in ten, and leaves.

And there is the leak. Just beyond the rim on that side, uphill of the gap, which anywhere else would be the wrong place to wait for water, a seam in the quartzite holds a few dozen brown frogs. They are the size and build of the orange ones, with no throat sac to speak of. By day they sit wedged in the peat with their eyes shut. At dusk, when the plate spills on every side and the beads come creeping up the slope toward them, they walk out and press their bellies to the wet line until it dries. They make no sound at all. When the hawk's shadow crosses, nothing about them changes, since they have nothing to stop. As near as can be judged, they are what the orange frogs used to be, and they live on what the shouting spills.

They last only as long as the fourth peg stays down. The grey beads seem to lie along the curve where the ring is open, and each morning the arc is laid out again. Each day the parrots take it apart. What they want with the beads is unclear, since they are the wrong size for grit and the wrong colour for eggs. Nothing suggests the parrots know what they are doing. It is simply the case that the only quiet animal left on the dome survives on the daily habit of the loudest.

After midnight the chorus thins, though it never stops. Some frog is always going, and the plate keeps its film. In the seam above the gap a brown frog has drunk and gone back into the peat. It has never made a sound in its life, and it has never been thirsty for long.
