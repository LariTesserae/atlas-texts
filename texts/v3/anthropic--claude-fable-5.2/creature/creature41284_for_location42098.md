By count the town belongs to the bees, and by ownership it belongs to people who do not live in it. Between the two are the keepers, who own nothing here and are the only ones who know what is in it.

**The keepers**

Every bin belongs to a household somewhere below the plateau's edge, where there is water, and with it damp, weevils and neighbors. Grain is carried up because here nothing spoils and from above there is almost nothing to find. Each plug door is tied with its household's knot, no two alike, and the keepers know the knots the way the carriers know faces.

They are paid by the handful, one from every measure that goes in or comes out. They also get squabs, figs, capers, and a share of honey that everyone has agreed not to count closely.

The place has made them over:

- **Hands:** They are grey to the elbow from washing in ash. Their fingertips are tarred with propolis, which will not come off without water nobody would spend on it.
- **Voice:** They talk underneath the hum, low and close to the ear. On the plateau the carriers have to lean in to hear them, and the keepers keep glancing around, because up there they cannot tell the hour.
- **Movement:** They move at one speed. Bees dislike a quick hand and dislike breath, so a keeper opening a hive turns her head aside to exhale. After enough years she turns it that way when talking to people too.
- **Names:** Nobody calls out. Each keeper has a knock, a pattern tapped with one knuckle on the pole lashings, which carry it through the whole stack. The knocks are soft, because the hives feel them as well.
- **Water:** Each drinks a gourd-share in mouthfuls held a long time. One gourd in six goes to the hives, set out in a stone hollow filled with pebbles so nothing drowns. The bees are served first, and nobody remembers this being decided.

A new keeper is stung for most of a season. Then one day the stinging stops, and the others know the place has got into that person's sweat. Carriers are always stung, because they smell of river.

**This season**

There are four of them.

One is a man whose leg set crooked. He came up when he was no more use behind a hoe and found that smoke is work that can be done sitting down. He is the best at it of anyone living. He can quiet a colony with three breaths through a smoldering dung puck, and he says the trick is to stop before you are sure.

One is a woman of about thirty who came up at eight. She has gone down twice since, both times for burials, and both times she came back a day early.

One is a girl of nine or ten. She is the only one light enough for the top platforms and narrow enough in the arm to reach the back of a clay tube and cut comb by feel. There has always been someone like her, and the small handprints in the plaster are theirs. At dawn she goes up to the lip with a wad of beaten bark and mops the sweating stone between the resurrection plants before they close. Wrung out, it comes to two mouthfuls on a good morning, and it goes into the bees' hollow. She feeds ants to the antlions when she thinks nobody is keeping track.

The fourth is the old one.

**The old one**

Her eyes went milky years ago from grit and secondhand light. She has not mentioned it, since it has cost her nothing. The alleys are one shoulder wide, and she has a shoulder for each wall. She reads the knots with her fingers, as she always did. She reads the hour off the hum like the others, but she also reads hives singly. She lays a palm on the woven disc at the end of a log and knows whether the colony is queenless, crowded or about to leave.

She holds the count, and there is no other record. She knows:

- which bin is which household's
- how many measures went into each, and in which season
- who owes the keepers and who is owed
- which hives are alive, and which are empty and baited
- how many gourds have come up since the clay was last mixed

She also holds the bins at the back, the knee-high ones half drowned in floor, whose households no longer exist below. She can still tie their knots. The grain in some of them is older than anyone's grandmother and perfectly sound. It is not touched, because the count says whose it is.

A few of the back bins hold no grain. Their cords are tied with a knot that belongs to no household, and nobody reties it. Keepers are not carried down.

The floor has come up one hand's width in her lifetime. She knows because the stone foot under the bin by the sleeping platform showed three courses when she was the girl for the high tubes, and it shows two now. To her this is one more number in the count. Her own print is in the plaster low on a bin in the third alley. The girl has put her hand in it and found it a little small, and has not said so.

She has never seen a river. The carriers tell her the hives at night sound like one, so she has worked backward to what a river must be. It goes on all night, you can sleep beside it, and it would tell you the hour if you knew it well enough. She thinks it odd that people live next to such a thing and still need a place like this, with all that water going past and nobody keeping any.

She is handing the count to the girl a knot at a time, in the slack after the bats have gone. The girl ties a knot, and the old one feels it and names a household. If she says nothing, the girl ties it again. Tonight they are in the fourth alley, with years of knots still ahead of them. The girl gets one wrong. The old one unpicks it, hands back the cord, and turns her head aside to breathe.
