Start with what goes against the sound, because there is so little of it. The growth goes where the roar is strongest. The dust goes there, and the stone thickens there. Only the cells are in the bare lanes by choice, each put as far from the dark bands as it could be and still have a floor. Whatever lives here lives in the lanes.

From the rim it takes a long time to find. Nothing in the basin moves at a speed the eye can use. Then a grey fleck in a lane on the far side is no longer where it was. Later it is at the edge of a band. It crosses the band quickly and is slow again in the lane beyond.

There is only ever the one fleck, though the ground argues for more: a dozen cells, forty-two bars, cords of every age. It is natural to say *they*. But every knot on every cord is the same knot, turned the same way, and the spans between knots agree to a finger's breadth, on the cords sunk in rock and on the one swinging free. The felt in every wall was pushed home by the same thumb. If there were many, they were as alike as the views of the first peak. It is simpler to say there is one, and that it has been here longer than it can account for. Its memory is of the pale kind, which keeps a mark for years and then no longer. It comes on its own early work as on a stranger's, and does not touch it.

Close to, it is about the height of a person and narrower. It wears the dead growth: grey felt, bound on with cord, thickest at the three places where the sound comes in. There is a pad under each sole and a plate strapped over the breastbone. A wad sits between its teeth, held by a band that passes under the jaw and over the skull. The wad keeps the teeth from buzzing against each other. It also keeps it from shouting, a habit it once had and got nothing from. None of this is for warmth. It has never been warm and does not miss it.

Under the felt it is the colour of ash, matte, and dry all the way through. It came out of the roar as everything here did, but out of the thin part, in a lane, and it is made of the stuff that has not settled on what to be. Nothing in it has ever been wet. Its eyes are as dull as the pale stone. When an edge cuts it, which happens most days, the cut lets go a pinch of dust and closes, and the dust drifts off toward the nearest dark band. It takes nothing in. There is nothing here to eat or drink, and it has never looked for any.

It marks easily. There are grooves worn across both palms by cord, and a flat on one shoulder the width of a lintel.

The left hand is another matter. It is black and glassy from the fingertips to a line halfway up the forearm, and the line is as sharp as a shadow's edge. The fingers still close, slowly, and do not feel what they close on. The outer edge of the palm will cut cord. It uses that hand now for what it used to do with the flakes, which lie where it last put them down.

How much of it there is depends on where it stands. Crossing a band of live growth, in the full roar, it is as distinct as anything on the mountain: hard-edged, every strand of the cord at its jaw separate. It goes bent and fast and stops for nothing. If it had an errand when it stepped into the band, it has usually lost it by the far side, and stands a while in the lane until the errand comes back. Out in the lane it straightens and slows. Further out it starts to go. Near one of the places the sound does not reach, a hand held out loses its fingertips to a vagueness like the upper third of the second peak, and it draws the hand in again. It knows to within a pace where in any lane it can think and still be counted on to stay. The cells are all on that line. It keeps to the black glass and will not step on the pale stone, which lies only where one of those places has lately been. It goes round, however far round is, and so it leaves no tracks.

The cells are hours apart because its work is. At the lip it lies on its front with its head over the edge and counts, on each old cord, the knots still showing above the rock. Each count is smaller than it used to be, by very little. Then it hauls the newest cord up span by span, checks it against its own reach, lowers it, and stays a while looking down the length of it at the stars. On the eastern floor it arrives ahead of the hour, waits for the shadow to swing into its outline of stones, and then stands the stub of glass against its leg. The stub was ankle-high once.

Under the west overhang it takes the wad from its mouth and sets its teeth to the forty-first bar. This is how every bar was tuned, a chip and then the teeth, long before any of them shook enough to see. The forty-first still answers, a little sourly now. It has walked on its bearers and is not pushed back. Then it sets its teeth to the forty-second and gets only the mountain, the same as from any stone. It takes up the mallet. It puts the mallet down again where it was.

What it wants to know is which will reach the bottom first, the rock or the roar. Quiet with a floor under it is the one thing it has wanted, and the cords and the bars are how it asks whether any is coming. It has a third count to lay beside those two, and that one it carries.

When the rounds are done it goes in under the mat of whichever cell is nearest, lies down on its right side, and puts its left arm out through the slot as far as the elbow. The slot is a hand wide because that is what goes into it. The arm fills it. With the arm in place the cell is as tight as the well-made ones were, the ones that are doorways now. It built those too, and whether it was ever inside one is past the reach of its memory. No sound comes in by the air. What comes in comes up the arm, and it is enough to hold the floor.

It is just enough to hold the sleeper. Lying there it thins to an outline, a line a shade paler than the dark, with the packed felt of the far wall showing through its middle. Outside, on the bare rock, the hand lies whole and black, blurred at its edges with trembling.

Whatever the roar stands in long enough turns to stone, and it stands in the arm every night. The line on the forearm is the sum of all the sleep it has had. By its own reckoning, in spans and fingers, the black will reach the shoulder at about the time the tone comes down to the long bar. After the shoulder there is no more of that arm to spend, and the other is the one that ties the knots. It has not chipped the bar since it worked this out.

On waking it fills in again from the elbow. It feels for the floor first. Then it draws the arm inside and looks at the line.
