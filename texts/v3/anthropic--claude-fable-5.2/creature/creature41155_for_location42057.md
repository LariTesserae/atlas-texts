The first of them you meet is the hoistman, and he looks at your boots before your face. If there are nails in the soles he has you out of them. He gives you felt overshoes off a rack and a length of cord for anything heavy in your pockets. Later you realise his was the loudest voice in town.

**Hands, backs**

They are pale, as you'd expect, damp at the hairline, and dressed for the heat in sleeveless linen gone soft from washing in condensate. But the things to look for are elsewhere.

One is the palm. Nearly every adult carries a curved white scar there, the shape of the scoop that made it. It is got once, usually before the age of six, and no one is scolded for it. They say the child has *met the wall*.

One is the back. When two people meet in a lane, one turns and presses into the padding to let the other by. The padding is moss, and moss stains, so everyone under is green across the shoulder blades, and those who give way most are greenest. It is the nearest thing the town has to rank, and it runs the opposite way to rank.

One is the little finger. Watch anyone put down a cup. The finger goes underneath and lands first, and the cup settles off it without a sound. They do the same with spanners, pot lids and sleeping infants, and don't know they do it.

Everything worth dropping is tied to someone. Tools hang on wrist loops and cups at the belt, and toddlers are clipped by a harness to a running line along the gallery rail. A child's first lanyard is braided by somebody older and kept long after it is outgrown.

**Voices**

They talk under the engine, a phrase on the out-breath, then a wait through the intake. Conversation is slow, and nobody interrupts, because the gap belongs to the beam. Laughter is all air and shoulders. Nobody claps, since a clap is a blow, and approval is a short hum. Every tune the town knows has drifted until it sits on the dynamo's one note. Lullabies go eleven lines to the minute. Babies born here sleep through a venting valve and wake when the engine stops.

Names have worn down too, since hard consonants don't carry at that volume. A grandfather came down the shaft a Bartholomew, and his grandson is Ollo.

Nobody will speak to you with a tool in hand. They set it down first, finger under, and then look up. Visitors take this for sullenness, but it is only that they do one thing at a time.

**Work**

There are three shifts, so the town is never all asleep. Like the fig, each household keeps its own calendar. One lane is at breakfast and the next is shuttered and dreaming.

The young are out at the headings, where it is hottest. Each bore tender sits with a bit that has been turning for months, one palm flat on the casing. A good one feels the rock change before the bit does and eases the feed a quarter turn. The other hand shells beans. Tenders sleep with their backs along the steam lines, as the squash roots do, and gather at lane crossings with their faces tipped to the lamp. They'd be surprised to have it pointed out.

Behind them come the felters, who wrap whatever new stone the town has leaned into. They hem their work. Then there are the fitters, whose whole craft is never forcing anything. They would sooner re-route a main than cut a limb, and they treat each other the same way.

**Quarrels**

Nobody can shout. Doors are felt-hung and will not slam. Nobody can stalk off either, because in an hour you will meet again in a lane, and one of you will have to turn and press into the moss. So two people who have fallen out change levels. The town knows a quarrel by who has taken to walking the sixth. It ends when one leaves a cup from their cold ration, still beaded, at the other's door. Drinking it before it warms is the acceptance.

Larger matters go the same way. There are no meetings, and yet one day it is simply so who sleeps in the room beside the Brook, or who takes over the hoist brake. Ask how, and you get the look they give when asked about the geckos.

**The log**

The log is the only history the town writes. Births are remembered, but drops are written down, so years are named for them, such as the year Hale lost the pinch bar down the well. You walk to the book and give your own name. A child's first time doing that is the nearest thing here to a coming of age. One man has eleven entries and is the best-liked person under. One old woman has none, and people find that slightly cold of her.

**Rains**

Age is counted in rains, the monthly ones. A child goes out to the headings at about a hundred and fifty. Nine hundred is old.

Children have no ball games. They make string figures and step the fourth level end to end without touching a rail. They know all thirty geckos by lamp and hold opinions about them. They also play a game called windows. Two children face each other, one moves, and the other follows a little late and a little slower. The skill is in being the dimmer one.

When a real window opens the children go shy in front of it, as before an older cousin. The adults bring stools. You can look at someone in the glass for a long time without it counting as looking, and most courtships under have started that way.

**East**

Nobody decides to go east. The town moves west and south after the heat, and at some point a person lets it go on without them. The cool rooms are easier to sleep in. The old live among the ferns without tending them. One retired pump-keeper still oils her shuttered pump at every rain. *Gone east* means old, and also sulking, and nobody finds the overlap odd.

The dead go up. Apart from current, they are the only thing the town sends.

**The number**

It goes down by one each year. The oldest can remember it in at least four different hands, and nobody has seen it chalked. Like the lamps, it has one keeper and never two.

Every child sooner or later does the sum against their own rains. You can tell which ones have, because they are quiet for a few shifts and sit longer at the windows. Nobody says anything. A pale fig turns up on the child's bedding, set down so softly it hasn't bruised.

Then the child goes back to the game. At the west heading someone eases the feed a quarter turn. A felter hems a lintel wrap to last longer than the number. And somebody of forty rains is shown again how to put down a cup: finger under, then let it arrive.
