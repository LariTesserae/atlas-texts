People, mostly, and at first they are hard to tell from the place. Their cloth is drawn out of the same feed as the walls, a thread pulled through a ring that sings, so it is buff when dry and slate when wet, and it is almost always wet. After the afternoon rain the whole population goes dark. Over the next hour it pales from the head and shoulders down, as the buildings do. The only colour on anyone is at the sleeve.

They stand close without noticing. A stranger's shoulder against yours has about the standing of a wall. The back is different. Coats are cut thin between the shoulder blades so that a palm can reach, and that hand's breadth is the nearest thing a person has to a front door. Mourners wear a thick panel there. So do people who are sulking.

Their voices are small. A voice is an indoor tool, for use across a table, and no one has ever needed to make one carry. Outdoors they laugh with their shoulders. The hum is what gets trained. It is known the way a face is known, and it ages the way a face does.

They do not hear the roar, only changes in it. They sleep through a wall going up thirty metres away and wake when a tone two streets off goes slightly flat, as people elsewhere wake when a clock stops.

A child spends its first year strapped to a chest, where there are three pulses: the adult's, the city's and its own. Sorting these out is the first work anyone here does, and not everyone finishes. Later the children hold notes over cups of canal feed, trying to raise a skin on it. One in a few hundred can. The rest give up at about nine and take to flags.

Age is counted downward. The pavement you were born on sinks its hand's width a year and is paved over. A small child is ankle-deep, then knee-deep, and in the year your first street lies deeper than you are tall, you are grown. The old are six and seven metres. Nobody digs, but everyone knows the bearing of their own street, and knows it is still down there in the warm water, going slowly to chert.

Nobody has a house in the sense of a thing to go back to. A household holds a bearing and the city passes through it. Rooms are lifted, narrowed and turned to face a different canal. People own what they can carry and feel little for walls. What they feel is for the round. An old resident will stand in a new arcade, roughly where the arcade stood eleven years ago and twenty-two, and be entirely satisfied.

They do not hurry. Running jams a walkway, and the walls have taught them there is nearly always time. They leave a failing frontage the way you would leave a room where someone has fallen asleep.

Most work touches the tone somewhere. There are crews who draw the walls up, and feeders, and tuners. There are also walkers, who go along the spans all day with one palm flat to the surface. It is the same gesture as the private one, hand to the back, and it does the same job: the span says how it is, through bone. Thirty years of that and the palm goes dull, and the walker is found other work.

In every block there is someone a little slower to leave than the rest. The hand-sign for them is the sign for *late*, made slowly. No one interferes.

One of them works a mirror at a fish stall, on a canal junction that was a storey lower this morning. Her name outdoors is a turn of the wrist and two short blinks of amber. She is a little over six metres. She walked spans from the year she was grown until her right hand stopped telling her anything. Now she flashes prices across the water and can put the light in a buyer's eye at forty paces. She takes fish from the row with a wire loop and likes to watch the row close up by one place.

It happens near her four or five times a year. The gulls lift and she watches the white go up the street. Her dog goes. The crowd thins around her without fuss. She folds the mirror and walks the other way.

It isn't nerve. She has been wet to the knee twice and did not care for it. It isn't the quiet either. She went inland once, as far as the squat houses, and her ears rang for a week, and she thought the silence there a poor thing, spread thin over everything with nothing standing round it. A dead spot has edges. The whole block stands at its rim.

Inside she can hear water against the bank, and her own sleeve. She has one pulse in there. It is the slower of the two she has always carried, and it is not quite even. Only the crabs have come the same way. A failing wall is coming unbound, and they pour down it in a white crowd to graze. In the quiet she can hear them eating, a small dry ticking. Now and then one loses its grip on the gloss, drops into the canal and does not come up.

She puts her right hand on the wall. This is what she comes for. A sound span says a great deal, quietly, and her palm can no longer read any of it. A failing one says a single thing. First there is nothing, slick and warm. Then come the neighbours leaning in, two tones not yet agreed, a throb she can feel to the elbow. It slows as they find each other, and slows, and is gone. It is the one sentence left that is plain enough for her hand.

If the throb has not begun to slow by forty beats of her own pulse, she gets up and leaves, without hurry, and does not turn round for the splash. Usually it slows. The gloss goes off the wall under her fingers. The second pulse comes back in beneath her own. One gull drops to the parapet, and then the rest.

The dog is waiting where the good ground starts, lifting one forefoot and then the other. She puts her palm between its shoulder blades and hums. It has never heard anything, and it leans into her hand. She tells it where they live, which is a bearing and today's date. Tomorrow she will have to tell it again.
