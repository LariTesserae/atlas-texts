Two people live here, for about twenty days a year, and for those days they are not quite two people.

They come up over the lip in late summer, when the goats are fat and the hay-cutters have begun stacking. One has been before and one has not. That is the rule of the pair, and it is why the shelter was never built wider. The bowl cannot be explained down below, among rivers and dogs and children, so each person is brought once by someone who was brought. At the cairn the elder unties the old strip, ties on a red one, and lays a finger across closed lips. That is the mark pecked into the top stone, a mouth with a finger across it. It is the last thing said for three weeks, and it isn't said.

They are small and slow-moving, dark as willow bark from the light. Each wears a band of horn across the eyes with a slit cut in it. They carry their chert ready-struck, because knapping clicks. They set things down and never drop them. They laugh with their shoulders and sneeze into a fistful of goat hair. Their palms are rubbed white with rock flour, and when one of them sees the stoat, a hand goes up, open. The finches answer it, the hay-cutters go under, and the ripple crosses the bowl as it would from any other animal. Nobody decided to warn the same creatures they trap. It is the only language spoken here, and after a few days they can't help speaking it.

They take one goat, sometimes two. At first light the new one walks out from the boulder and stands between the herd and the eastern arête, upright and unhurried. The goats drift toward the lip, meet the line of stones, and turn along it. At the notch the elder is crouched under a grey hide dusted with flour. The work is done close, with a short spear and then with both arms, so that the animal is held as it goes down and laid on the rock like a sleeping child.

They do not crack the long bones. Marrow is the best food there is, and they give it up. The bones are carried out onto the slabs and left, and later the vulture takes them up and does the one loud thing anyone does here. The pair hold still with everything else, listening for whether the wall will answer. Their hearth has ash in it, and small scorched skulls, and not one split bone.

By the tenth day the hand-signs fall away, since they are no longer needed. One wakes when the other wakes. One reaches and the cup is there. They sleep back to back on willow twigs and share out their heat exactly. What occupies the bowl by then is a single quiet animal with four hands, patient, mostly kneeling, which exists nowhere else. It comes apart on the descent, at the first stream loud enough to cover a voice. There they stop and both talk at once, hoarse, for a long time.

But twenty days is a visit. The goats visit too, and the finches leave with the snow. Count who is here in the dead of winter and the answer is the hay-cutters under the drifts, awake beside their stacks, and the people along the lip.

Before any hunting, the new one's first labour is to choose from the boulder field a stone of their own crouching size. They walk it across the bowl, end over end, each turn eased down onto a pad of moss, and set it upright in the line. It takes days, and it is how the quiet gets into the arms. So the line is everyone who was ever brought, hunkered a few strides apart with their backs to the drop, growing rime on the windward side like any other stone. The goats can't tell them from the living. That is the whole method. At the notch there is a gap the width of one crouched body, and the elder under the grey hide is not hiding there so much as taking a seat that is kept.

This year the strip on the cairn is pink. The traps have sprung and no one has reset them. The stoat is whitening and the bundle is still in its pit under the lid stone. Perhaps there was trouble below. Perhaps an elder died with no one yet brought, which would be the one way the chain could break.

The bowl has not been told. The finches flash their wings. The hay-cutters stamp once. At dawn the goats come over the arête in file, lick the pale streak, and leave along the line of crouchers before the sun reaches the wall. At the notch the lead nanny stops. She looks for a long moment at the empty place, as she would at a stone that might be breathing. Then she goes through, quickly, and the others follow her, keeping to the far side.
