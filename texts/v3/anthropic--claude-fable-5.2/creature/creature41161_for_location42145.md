Start by counting, and the count goes wrong.

There are the moths, the snails and the bird. The bird belongs to the cloud and only visits the edge. The snails belong to the moss the way the dew does. The moths are harder to dismiss, sitting flat on the insulator sheds and staying put when a hand comes near. Set them aside for now. They come back.

Then there are the plants, and here the count fails. A botanist could list a few dozen kinds. But try to lift one cushion out and it does not come out. The brown felt underneath is continuous. It runs from the scarp to the cone without a seam, one sheet of the dead, holding all the water there is. Everything green stands in it, drinks from it, and will end in it. The ridges where the cushions press together are less like borders than like the sutures of a skull. What lives on the plateau is one thing, six kilometres long, and younger than the pylons it lies under.

It has one breath a day. In the evening the cloud comes over the lip and the whole mass loosens and takes its ration. In the morning it glitters. By noon it has closed its surface and kept the water below, where the sun cannot get it. It makes no sound and could not hear one. It has no shade to keep anything in, because the light goes all the way through it. For a while that seems to be the whole description: a wet, bright, silent thrift, gaining a hand's width a year.

Then walk under the line.

The dark ridge has explanations, and they are good ones. The conductors comb the cloud like everything else here, and what they comb drips straight down. They carry current and run a little warmer than the air. And when the cloud lies on them in the evening, wet wire at that voltage leaks. No hiss reaches the ear in this place, but after dark there are three faint violet threads in the fog, running from tower to tower. The discharge burns a trace of the air into nitrate, and the drip carries it down. On rock this young, nitrogen is scarcer than water. So under each phase there is a strip that is watered first, warmed slightly, and fed. The darker colour is what being fed looks like.

The glow explains the moths as well. They come to the ultraviolet, as moths do. Their larvae are the kind that graze lichen and the green film that settles on anything damp, and they graze it off the sheds before they drop to the bands to pupate. No one washes the insulators. They are eaten clean.

It may even explain the lean. By day the light here comes from above and below at once and gives a plant no direction to prefer. The only light on the plateau that has an address is the one in the wires at night. The ferns bend toward it.

None of this explains the lattice. A climber spreading by chance would take all four legs and every face. This one has gone up the hollow core of each tower. The conductors hang out at the arm ends, and the core is the one route that rises while staying farthest from all of them. Nor does it explain the gravel. Spores fall on that metre of stone every day. The cloud wets it every evening. The hedge on the fence stands a stride away. Nothing grows there.

So the thing in the moss knows something. It is hard to say how. There may have been a night, early on, when one frond reached far enough. There would have been a flash that nothing heard, and then the tremor would have stopped. That tremor is in the footings and the fence wire, and in the wet rock under the bands. It was there before the first spore landed, and the whole green body has never been without it. If it stopped for an hour, that hour would be the only event in the creature's life. It would be enough to teach a distance.

It is tempting to call this the wild taking the works back, but that has the order wrong. There was nothing here before the works. The line is older than the moss, as old as the ground, the way a river is older than its forest. What lives here is a native of the line. It was raised on the line's leakage and has shaped itself to the line's tolerances. It does not care for steel as such. The moss crosses the trolley rails without changing height, sags through the torn mesh, and treats the cistern as one more rock. It cares for what is live. It grows as close to that as it can and no closer. In its slow way it keeps the place: it sends its moths up to the porcelain, holds its own height under the spans, and leaves the gravel alone.

If it has a front, a place where it is most itself, it is the yard fence in the evening. The hedge there has grown to the height of the wire and stopped. A hand pushed into it finds the mesh inside, and in the mesh, faintly, the same tremor as in the transformer casing. Across the gravel the transformers stand bare. The cloud comes up. The three threads come on in the fog. The moths settle with their wings flat. The hedge faces the machines across a metre of stone, with the hum in its stems, and does not cross.

It does not know what the current is for, or where it goes when it drops over the scarp into the white. It knows the terms. One day the wells in the cone will fail and the meters will stop. The wires will still comb the cloud and the bands will still get their drip. But the threads will not light, and the rock will go still. I think it would notice. I think the ferns would go on leaning for years.
