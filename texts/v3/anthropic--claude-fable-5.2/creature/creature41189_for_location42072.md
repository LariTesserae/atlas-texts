The first answer is the plain one. What lives on the saddle is the skin.

It is an orange-brown film, about as thick as the wet left by a thumb. It has no parts and no edges of its own. It takes what the water loosens from the clinkstone and lays itself down wherever a drip has a habit. After cloud it swells and goes slick, and in sun it draws back to a stain. Each day some of it is carried south over the lip, and each day the cloud comes up and it thickens again. It is probably the same skin. Whether it is one thing or a multitude cannot be settled, here of all places.

Because it can be poisoned, it keeps a record. Round every peg is the clean halo. On the pillars, at shoulder height, there are other clean places, one to a pillar, each a hand wide and roughly the shape of a hand. They sit on the side a person would touch in passing, and they run in a line through the field. The oldest have filmed over to a faint ghost. The newest are bare grey stone. Whoever beat the pegs had the green of them worked into both palms, and touched each pillar once as it was counted.

So there is a second resident, and the skin is how it shows.

## What the built things say about a body

- **Height:** not tall. Shoulder height for the tally rows is low, and where a pillar filled, the last rows were cut at the knee, kneeling in the running water.
- **Seat:** narrow. Each hollow was worn by someone who sat a very long time and always faced the same way.
- **Reach:** the ring walls are one arm-span across, so that a person seated in cloud can touch the whole wall and know they are inside it. They are roofless because a roof would hide the shadows.
- **Hands:** both used. The cobbles were turned end for end and worked until each end was white. There are more cobbles than one climb could carry, so at first the counter came and went.
- **Number:** one. The ring walls come out as three or four depending on the direction walked, and the counter never settled that either.

## What they say about a mind

There is no shrine here. No face is pecked into any pillar, nothing was left as a gift, and no stone was stood up to be spoken to. Everything built is an instrument, and the instruments can be read in order as a single line of thought. The count would not close, so the counter paced the ground. The paces would not agree, so the counter cut a trough to find level. Level was not where the slope said it should be, so a plumb was hung. The plumb leaned, so a second was hung at the far end to see if it leaned the same way. Then came the ring walls, for sitting still and watching one stretch of shadows through a day.

This was a person who, each time a method gave way, made a tool to measure how far it had given. In hundreds of rows there is not one number written down that was not got. The abandoned rows were abandoned honestly.

The cairns are the other thing. Cairn lines are what a person builds when the way home has stopped being obvious. They run east to the mountain and west to the mountain, and the counter walked both. The mountain led back here.

## How it is here

**It carries what the field will not.** Nothing on the saddle is wrong where it stands. Each shadow agrees with the next. The disagreement can only be found by something that goes the whole way round and comes back to compare. The pillars do not go round. The water does, and the skin with it, but neither has anything to hold a difference in. The counter had a stride, a spine and a sense of the time of day. It went out by the east cairns and came in by the west, hundreds of times, and each circuit handed it a little more to carry: thirteen paces, a few degrees, part of an hour. The field has no seam in it, because the seam is whoever walks it.

**It arrives in pieces.** The saddle delivers the crack before the fall, and it delivers the counter the same way. The touch comes first, so the line of clean hand-places runs on past the newest tally into pillars not yet counted. The sound comes next, tock, tock-tock. Then come the strokes, pale, with rock flour in the water beneath them. The body is the last of it, and at noon it has not come.

**Its hour is the amber one.** Hundreds of circuits each mislaid part of an hour, and the counter's day has slid off the noon that stands over the field. When the cloud comes up with low light inside it, there is sometimes a figure in the cloud. It is small and wet through, like everything else. It has a cobble in one hand and the other hand flat on a pillar. It stands a few degrees off true, leaning toward the mountain, as still as the pierced stone on its strip. It strikes, and there is no sound, because that sound has already gone by. Its shadow is long and lies the same way as every other shadow in the cloud. For as long as the amber holds, a count would hold too. But the cloud is too thick to see the next pillar, so it counts by hand.

**It does not breathe here.** The pauses in the pecking are the length of a breath. The breath itself is being taken somewhere in the amber and has not come up yet.

## What it is doing now

It would be easy to call this a trapped thing, but the rows say otherwise. A count is true once, for the one who made it, and the counter learned that before it learned anything else about the place, and went on working. Each row is a different length, and each was right. The hard stroke at the end of each has been read as temper. It reads better as the only way a tally has of saying *once*.

Somewhere back among those rows the counter stopped trying to find out how many pillars there are and began keeping an account of how many there were each time. That is a different kind of work, and it does not need to end.

The cloud clears to noon. There is no figure. The skin is thick and slick in the drip lines, and the bells start. On the nearest pillar the row stands at four strokes. On the next pillar along, uncounted, a clean place a hand wide shows in the orange.

The sound of the fifth stroke has already come through. It was the one tock louder than the others, some while ago, and the pause after it was longer than a breath needs.
