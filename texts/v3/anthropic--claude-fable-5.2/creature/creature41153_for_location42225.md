From the old cliff you would say no one lives here at all. Then a figure appears on one of the roads, small as a grain, and takes most of an hour to pass between two halos.

They walk slowly because the rock punishes a fall, and because the road has to be read while it is walked. Every so often the walker glances down to the left and the right, the way a person elsewhere checks the time. They wear thick soles of layered fern fibre and keep their hands loosely closed. Almost everyone raised on the plain has a white line across one palm, from the day in childhood they put a hand down on a blister rim.

Up close they are pale for the latitude, and the children run long in the shin. The joke is that anything kept near a wall goes for height. They look slightly to one side of whatever they are attending to, a habit learned from the buildings, whose edges will not hold a gaze, and they do it with faces too. A direct stare is how they know a visitor from up the cliff.

There are fewer than three hundred of them. Each structure holds one household, but not every structure has a door.

The hour to see them is before dawn, when the water comes down. Someone from each house stands at the foot of the wall with an aroid leaf creased into a spout and fills jars from the sheet itself. Nobody draws from the pools, which belong to the frogs.

Then the ring is worked from the inside outward. By the wall they cut the pale shoots, fern crosiers that have never had enough light to turn green, and eat them raw, standing. In the thick band they dig corms and cut back whatever is closing the path. They seldom go to the outer edge, where there is little to pick and every leaf has its back to them.

The corms have to be cooked, and wet fern makes a poor fire, so they bake in the rock. The corms are wrapped in leaf and left in a warm crack under a slab for a day and a night. Near the sea every house has a crack that will do it. Near the cliff the flows have gone cold within living memory. An old woman there, who as a girl baked on her own doorstep, now sends her basket out three rows. Most of what moves on the roads is food going seaward to the heat or coming home from it. When a slab is lifted, the geckos are found lying round it like spokes, and they are moved aside with the back of a hand.

At midday, when the note comes, they are indoors and lying down. They call it the house drinking. Children sleep through it and visitors cannot. Whatever takes the light takes it at the skin, so inside a flame stands straight, and a hand held near it throws one shadow, away from the flame. Older children show this to younger ones as a trick. One wick is all they burn, because eyes made on the plain want no more. That is the rectangle you see when a door opens.

Their manners come from shadows and frogs. To be "even" is to be on the road, in public, owing nothing, and to speak evenly is to speak as to a stranger. To be "dark on one side" is to be home, or partial, or in love. If you must enter someone's ring, you stop at the first wet fronds and wait until a frog has called. Then you have been announced.

They talk the way the frogs call. One speaks, then there is a gap long enough to be sure they have finished, then another answers. A child who talks over someone is not scolded, only waited out.

At night there are no shadows to steer by, and the rule changes. The wet road reflects the sky, and the stars are whole only above the road, because near the roofs they smear. So you walk on round stars. If you lose them, you go by smell. The halo flowers are white and heavily scented for the moth, and each household has planted its own, ginger at one and something like jasmine at the next. You know whose ring you are in before you touch a leaf. People are named the same way, as "her of the ginger house."

A handful of them are older than the ground. They were children on the clifftop when surf broke at its foot, and one fished from a rock that now lies under her own garden. They came down when the cracks stopped showing red at night. They are the only ones who remember a shadow that followed the sun, and they mention it without regret.

The young who climb the cliff to trade shoots for blades and lamp oil stand in full noon with one hard shadow that points at no house. They say it feels like not being spoken to, and they come down quickly.

They did not build the structures and know no one who did. Asked whether a house is alive, they give the answer they would give about the sea. What they do know is the order in which things arrive, because it can be watched at the seaward rows:

1. Rock comes first, then road, then the lean in the light.
2. The edge of a wall comes before the wall, and for a season it is an outline with weather still passing through it.
3. The wall comes, and its first sweat.
4. Pale threads come, then moss, then the first moth, then fern.
5. A frog comes. They say the heron brings frogs as eggs stuck to its legs, which is why a heron in a pool is never disturbed.
6. One morning the sheet of water parts around a rectangle that was not there before.
7. After that come people.

They say they are what comes after frogs, and they mean it plainly. Nobody has claimed to know what comes after people.

One thing they add of their own. When a door is found, whoever goes to live behind it carries out a basket of soil from the ring they were born in, some corms, and a cutting of the family flower.

Nobody lives in the last row. The structures there have no doors, the rock blisters a foot through its sole, and the note does not stop at midday. They have no word for whatever lies beneath, pushing the shelf out under the surf. They name what it does, the laying, and leave it there.

But the young go out to that edge at night, because the red line is the only frank light they have. Beside it a face is lit on one side and dark on the other, as faces are everywhere else in the world. It is where you go to look properly at someone you are thinking of marrying, and to have "seen someone at the edge" settles the matter.

Beyond that, on the bare new shelf, are the empty places the roads curve around. People walk out to them alone, usually once. If you stand at the centre of one at noon, your shadows come in under you from every side. It is the only spot on the plain where a person stands on their own shadow. No one stays long, because of the heat.

Ask how many live in the city and the number you are given is too large. They are counting the places, including the doors not yet found and the walls not yet come.

They have also done the other sum. At a pace a year, the rings will all have met in four or five hundred years, and every road will be a tunnel. In the one place where two rings have already met, it is too dark under the fern for shadows, and the two households have stopped saying whose fern is whose.

Last wet season, two frogs there called at the same time, one over the other. Word went out along the roads, basket by basket. Over several nights some forty people walked in, more than any of them had seen in one place, and stood in the dripping dark to hear it. Nobody spoke, and nobody was waiting for a turn. By their own reckoning they were about four hundred years early, and they stayed until the calling stopped.
