The first answer is the ferns, and it holds until you kneel at a seam in the floor plates and work a finger down beside a stem. The stem doesn't end. It runs into a cord under the iron, as thick as a wrist, woody and hot, and the cord follows the still line in both directions as far as you can reach. Every band is one root. Where the bands branch, the root branches. Where they close into loops, it has met itself and grown together. A sporeling that takes hold on a still line is absorbed within a season, its thread of root welded into the cord. So there are not ten thousand plants in the hall. There is one, or near enough, and it has the shape of the figure on the floor.

It is less like a plant the longer you look. It has no use for the light. When the grey column from the shaft crosses a band, no frond turns, and the fronds under the amber bulbs are no bigger than those in the dark. The cord lies against iron that sits over hot rock, the fronds stand in air that is hot but less so, and it lives on the difference, the way a mill lives on a fall of water. That is why the fronds are warm. Heat is passing up through them all the time, and one cut from its stem goes cold in minutes and limp within the hour.

The overlapping is for the rain. A slated band sheds nothing into the lanes. Every string of water runs inward, frond to frond, down to the crowns and through the seam to the cord. It drinks a few times a day, all of it at once, in the same ten seconds.

What it has in place of senses is its edge. Along both sides of every band, root tips lie at the margin where the iron begins to move. A tip that pushes onto shaking floor is rubbed raw and dies back, and a tip on quiet floor thickens. That is all it knows: the outline of its own stillness, felt along several hundred metres of border, continuously, for centuries.

Everything it does comes from that. It travels, a hand's width in the life of a frond, growing on one side and dying on the other. It has crossed its own old positions many times and has no idea the felt ridges are there. It finds heavy things the same way. The tool cart stands in what should be a shaking lane, but its weight deadens the plate under it, and a spur of root found the quiet and came up through the tray. The lockers and the boilers were taken the same way. If you stood in one place for some years, it would come up around your boots. It would not have noticed you, only that the floor had stopped.

Nothing has ever bitten it, and that shows. It has no spine, no hair, no bitterness in the sap. A torn frond smells of warm iron and nothing else. It has never had a rival to shade or an enemy to poison. Its one relationship is with the pipes, and its whole character is agreement. It goes exactly where it is permitted, to the width of a stem.

The pods are harder to account for. The spores are dust and suit an ordinary life: they ride the air, land everywhere, and hold where the floor allows. They suit a figure that creeps. The pods grow only on the oldest fronds, at the throat end, where the iron plates give out and the cord runs onto bare rock. It must have lived there before there was a floor, as some small ring around a vent.

The shell of a pod is like horn and takes a good blade. Inside there is no dust. There are thirty or forty grains, black, heavy, flat-sided, like coarse shot. Tip them onto a bare lane and they dance. They skitter over the buzzing iron, fetch up along the dead margin of the nearest band, and stop in a neat row, on ground already taken. Nothing comes of them there.

The shell is two valves, one thicker than the other. Flick the thin one with a nail and it gives a small clear note; the thick one gives a lower. When the fourth note sounds, the thin valves answer it. You can feel it with a fingertip, a row of them fizzing together and pulling at their stalks, and that is what stiffens the fronds and tilts them. The thick valve is tuned to one of the three low notes, which is still a shade too high. The seam needs both.

A plate does not always change its figure by degrees. Bow it a little differently and the lines slide. Bow it past a certain point and the figure lets go all at once, and the sand jumps to another, with lines where the open spaces were. The pipes are sinking toward such a point. On that day every band in the hall will be standing on shaking iron, the cord will be worked loose along its whole length, and there will be nowhere to creep to. The fourth note is that other figure, trying the floor for a breath or two and losing it again.

The thing in the hall cannot know this, but it is built as if it did. At that interval the pods will spring their seams, all within the same few breaths, and throw their grain onto a floor that is moving wherever the grain lands. The floor will carry it. It will run across the iron as sand does and stop where sand stops. Within a minute the new figure will be drawn across the cavern end of the hall in black grain, exact to a finger's width. The old figure will still be standing over it, red and warm and already coming loose. The rest it will have to do the slow way, by spore, outward from the throat, as it presumably did the first time.

So what lives here is one patient body that has spent the whole age of the lamps holding still, which is what it does best, and that carries along its oldest edge the means to move once, quickly, like something else altogether. Until then it keeps to its lines. The fourth note comes, the fronds by the throat lean together, and the pods pull at their stalks. Then the note goes, and they settle back.
