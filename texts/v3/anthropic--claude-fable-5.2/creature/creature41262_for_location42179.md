The first thing anyone knows of it is a hole in the sound.

The frog sheet is solid from ridge to ridge, and then, off to one side and a little above head height, a piece of it is missing. The gap is about as big as a sleeping house. It travels at the pace of a person walking carefully, and the frogs come back in behind it one struck pebble at a time, the way the growth closes behind anyone who pushes through. The ridge people call it by the word they use for the space where a tooth was. On a first crossing that is all it is: a place where the noise isn't, going somewhere.

After that it turns up under the hands.

- **Cups:** A line of leaf rosettes, each one dry. A finger finds no water, no tadpoles, nothing twitching. The line wanders off between the trunks. By the next night the drip has filled them again.
- **Cord:** A hand resting on a route line feels it sag and spring, sag and spring, nine times or fourteen or twenty. Each weight is about that of a net bag with two fledglings in it. Children are taught to count and not to pull.
- **Trunks:** Every tree fern near a skylight carries the hole's note up out of the rock. Sometimes a palm on the moss finds a second trembling laid over the first, quicker and slightly sharp. That means they are asleep in the skirts overhead.
- **Shell:** Crab shell lies heaped under a fern that has no notch cut in it.

So the gap is a file of small animals, and the silence is the frogs along its length holding their breath.

Now and then a tree tips with a band asleep in it, and one is found afterwards and carried up to the crest, where there is light to look by. It is always a disappointment. It is a long narrow animal the colour of wet bark, with a body the length of a forearm and a tail the same again, bare underneath. The eyes are like seeds pressed into clay, and the ears are crumpled shut. It has whiskers longer than its head, and hands, naked-palmed, that look as if they had been used for fine work. The people turn it over and say that this is only what it leaves behind.

The old forest on the ridges has the same animal. Up there it lives in pairs and whistles at dusk. The ones on the flow came down when the ferns did, no longer ago than the people's own grandmothers, and nothing about their bodies has changed. They have only stopped whistling, since nothing here would hear it. They have also stopped living in pairs, since two is too few to keep hold of each other in this.

A band moves as a line. Each animal keeps its whiskers on the tail of the one ahead, and the one at the front keeps hers on the world. Every so often she stops and lays the underside of her jaw along a fig root. The roots run down every crack, and they bring up what the rock is saying: which hole is nearest, how far it is to the next, whether the crust between is thick or only a rind over a tube. The file bunches behind her while she listens with her chin. Then she goes on, and it pays out again.

They do not touch the ground. The dead skirts of the tree ferns interlock at head height into a thatch that runs unbroken around each skylight, and they live inside it, threading it, never setting a palm on the rock. Their nests are fern wool, the soft rust-coloured hair that sleeves the young fronds, packed into a hollow in the thatch and never used twice. Asleep, a band is a single knot of bodies, all purring a shade off the hole's note, so that the sound is hidden in the drone and only a hand on the trunk can find it.

They eat by what the valley is doing. When the tubes inhale, they sit in the skirts at the lip and pick moths out of the downdraft with both hands. When the tubes breathe out, they sleep in the warm column, and their fur takes the ammonia so deeply that a nose cannot tell a band from a hole. In crab season they hang by the tail from the lowest fronds and lift crabs out of the carpet as it passes under them. At dusk they wait beneath the boil of birds for whatever collides and falls. And every night they go along the cups. The valley will not give a person a drink, but it gives them forty a night, a mouthful each, with the frog and the tadpoles and the twitching larva going down with the water. That is why the frogs stop.

They hold no ground, because no ground here holds. A band keeps to two or three notes and moves among them as the groves tip and the steam wanders. By the time a fern is cooked grey, the nest in it is a week empty. When a roof falls and a new note joins the drone, the nearest band has it through the roots before the thud has finished travelling. They are at the fresh lip within a night or two, for the birds knocked down and for ledges nothing else has learned yet.

Between the rings the forest thins to moss, the thatch gives out, and there is only clinker, which would open their palms. For most of their time on the flow that kept each band to its own ring. Then the people began stringing cord. A route line is a bridge at hip height over the rubble, and the bands took to the lines within a season. They do not read them. A knot, to them, is a place where the cord is thicker and the grip better. But they use the lines so steadily that the people are no longer sure whose routes these are. A cord no person has visited in two years, leading nowhere a person can go now, is still polished along its top by palms.

There is one band the people know apart from the others by its count: two dips almost on top of each other, a pause, then the rest evenly. The first is an old female whose tail ends a hand short, so the animal behind her has to travel with its nose nearly in her fur. She is heavier than the others. She stops on the cord more than they do, mid-span, for no reason a person can find, and the whole line hangs there with her over the glass until she is satisfied. Some of the lines she crosses were strung by people now dead. Those who have had her go over the back of a hand where it rested on the cord say she neither hurries nor flinches, and that her palm is dry and very warm.

Toward morning, when the lid goes to slate, she is at a notched trunk in the middle of the flow. The note ahead is two nights old and lower than its neighbours, and she has had her jaw on the roots around it since the roof went. The cord from the trunk is taut, and she takes it. It carries her out over the clinker, with the band paying out behind her, to a lip of broken crust and over it. The breath coming up is warm. The birds are coming down past her in streams. She goes on, hand under hand, to the last knot and hangs there by her feet and what is left of her tail, in the middle of the falling birds, with both hands free.

Above her the rest wait along the taut part in their order, whiskers to tail, back to the notched trunk. The cord swings.
