Like the green in the roof, what lives here goes out when you look straight at it. It has to be approached the way the room is entered, sideways and downhill.

The first answer is the plain one: the moths live here, and the harvestmen, the bats, the salamander in every flute. But the bats go out through the slot at dusk to feed over the ridge, and some do not come back. Wrens are taken in the open, a moth lasts a season, and a weasel is old at five years. Still there is a salamander in every flute, a dozen wrens with their tails outward, and one weasel within reach of them. The cord has been retied in the fibre of plants that have since changed their range on the mountain, and it carries the same knots.

So the animals are no more what lives here than the water is the pool. Water passes through the pool all day and leaves by a thread, and the pool is the level that does not move. Bodies pass through the room the same way, and something else keeps its level.

It is tempting to imagine a keeper, something large and patient at the back. But there is no back. The room is twenty paces by twelve and every surface is taken, and a keeper would have to forbid things, and nothing is forbidden here. The weasel is not restrained. It has nothing to wake for.

It is closer to say that one animal lives here, and it is wearing all the others. Breathe on the harvestmen and the mat shivers as a single body. The room does the same more slowly. A wren resettles, and the movement passes into the moss, along the weasel's flank, up the root curtain to the crickets' trailing antennae, and dies out among the moths. The hush like cloth being folded is one sound.

Consider what kind of animal it is. The walls are an unbroken film, the ferns are one cell thick, the snails can be seen through, and the salamanders have no lungs and breathe across their shining skin. Nothing in the room has an inside it keeps to itself. The animal is all surface. It is the wet lining of the pocket, folded so many times that it has moth-wing and bat-fur and liverwort for texture. It breathes as the salamanders do, across its whole self, taking the mountain's cold through a metre of peat. If it has a pulse, it is the drip.

It has no mouth, and this is the most exact thing that can be said of it. Nothing eats here and nothing calls. Whatever comes through the slot leaves its mouth outside, as the people leave the blade, the scoop, and the needle on the ledge. The frog on the pool's lip is a frog in every part except the part a cricket would fear. The people's rule about empty hands is a translation. Long ago they saw what the weasel had to set down to come in, and they worked out what the equivalent was for themselves.

It should not be mistaken for gentle. It has made no peace between the weasel and the wrens, and it has no view of either. It is what is left of an animal when the wanting is taken out: weight, warmth, breath, and grip. Mostly it is grip. Every root-tip holds its drop. The crickets hold the roof and the harvestmen hold one another. The bats hold on in their sleep without effort, because a bat's foot is locked shut by the bat's own weight. Holding on costs it nothing. It does not grow, since the flutes are as deep as they will ever go. It finished before the trees had their shapes, and it has been the same size ever since, which is the size of the room and of the number on the cord.

Two things in the room are not part of it. The first is the white shoots. They are the only things here that want something, and they have spent themselves reaching for the slot, growing long and thin. They will not arrive. It does nothing to them. It lies around them as the pool lies around a fallen leaf.

The second is the pair of hollows in the floor, and they are the part of it that matters most. An animal without a mouth or eyes, in a dark that offers nothing to adjust to, cannot go over itself and learn whether it is all there. So over a very long time it has worn into its one floor a place for a part it does not own. That part has hands, and it stands an arm's length in, where the daylight gives out. When a visitor takes the cord and goes along it knot by knot, the animal is feeling down its own body with the only fingers it has ever had. For the length of the count the visitor lives here exactly as the wrens do, as one more body in a fitted place, mouthless and holding a root.

The count comes out the same. I think that is what it has in place of satisfaction. It is no pleasure, only the fact of being all present, known for as long as the last knot takes. Then the hands withdraw, the feet back out, and the hollows are empty. It returns to not knowing, which for it is the same as sleep and the same as waking.

The visitor's hunger comes back somewhere below the ledge, about when the fingers close on the chert again. The people from lower down have no word for what they visited, because no word is spoken inside and outside there has never seemed to be a need. Asked where they have been, they say the number. It is the nearest thing to a name it has, and they say it quietly.
