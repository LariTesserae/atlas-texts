No one lives here, if living somewhere means moving through it. What lives in the chamber is a patch of growth the exact shape of the light, made of five kinds of small, still things.

All of it came down the fissure, the same way the light and the pipe did. Spores and single cells are light enough to ride air down a crack. For as long as the pump hall has been lit, dust from upstairs has sifted down the joint in the rock, and what landed in both light and water stayed. Cave keepers have a word for this, lampenflora: growth that follows lamps underground. The same dust fell everywhere else in the chamber, onto dark rock, and came to nothing.

On the lit strip of wall, the algae lie like one coat of paint. The film lines each scallop, so the palm-sized hollows that fast water cut are now green cups. It stopped thickening when the top layer of cells began to shade the bottom layer below what a cell can live on, so its depth measures the lamps. Toward the margins, where the light thins, the green gives way to the brown of the diatoms. Each one is a cell in a glass case, set down on the rock and left there. Nothing has ever taken a mouthful of any of it, and the film has no gaps or scrapes.

On the floor, the dozen stoneworts stand in their oval of silt like pins in a map. Each is a thin grey-green stem, a hand or two tall, with whorls of short branchlets at intervals, anchored by colorless threads. In moving water they would lean and sway. Here they stand straight, because the pull of the strainer above them is too slight to bend them. They are far apart because the silt has so little in it that each needs the ground around it to itself.

They are brittle because they are partly stone. The water holds as much dissolved limestone as it can, and when a stonewort takes carbon out of the water to build itself, the water next to its skin can no longer hold it all. A little comes out as a crust on the stem. The walls stopped growing and dissolving long ago, and the stalactites are finished. This grey crust on twelve stems is the only stone still being made here, a grain at a time.

At the lip of the fissure the moss cushion sits right on the waterline, with its lower edge on the pale band. Because the level never changes, the moss has never been drowned or dried, and it is dense and even, the darkest green in the place. The two ferns come out of the crack just above it. They are pale because the light is weak, and their fronds are thin enough that the light passes through them and continues down into the water. When one of them makes a new frond, it unrolls over weeks. That is the fastest thing that happens here.

Taken together they are closer to one inhabitant than five, and that inhabitant is a border. Inside the border everything is at its limit: the film as thick as it can get, the stoneworts as many as the silt can feed, the ferns as large as the lamps allow. Outside it, within a meter, there is nothing. With no grazer, flood, or season to push the border back, it sits where the light sets it and has no reason to move.

It keeps no time. The lamps give it no night to close for, and the water gives it no winter. The ferns have never had a reason to die back, and the stoneworts have never had a cold month to wait out. It makes oxygen so slowly that the gas dissolves without ever forming a bubble, and eventually it leaves through the strainer with the water.

It is not a hungry or waiting thing, and nothing in the room suggests it lacks anything. It runs the way the pumps run, taking what arrives and holding its line. The light is spill from lamps left on for the machines, and it is unlikely anyone in the pump hall knows that their basement lighting keeps a garden forty meters down.
