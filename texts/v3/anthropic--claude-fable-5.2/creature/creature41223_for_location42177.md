The first one found is usually empty. It lies along a branch a little way under the mat, as long as a forearm and pale, with four toes on each front foot and five on each hind foot, all of them gripping. It can be watched for a day and will not move. Touched, it turns out to be hard, hollow, and thin as eggshell. It is a skin. The water has glazed it as it glazes the bark, and whatever wore it has left through a split along the back.

The living one is close by, generally a hand's width higher on the same branch. At a glance it is the same object, and it holds as still. The difference is at the head. The animal has three frilled gills on each side, the same pink as the roots that hang in the steam, and about once a minute it flicks them. In water that never moves this is necessary; without it the animal would sit in a sleeve of its own used breath. The cast keeps only the six short stalks the gills stood on. Anything pale on a branch here is one or the other, and the flick is the way to tell.

Between flicks the animal does nothing, so the silica settles on it. Over a year or two its skin takes a clear, stiff finish like the glaze on a pot. The gills stay soft because they move. The rest of it goes on wearing the coat until something cracks it.

That much is the salamander, and it would be possible to stop there: few, slow, one to every hundred trees. But it has to eat, and there is only one other animal in the lake.

This is the small glassy thing that grazes the felt. Near the floor it is the size of a fingernail and so clear that the bark shows through it, apart from a green thread of gut and two black eyes. It presses its mouth to the trunk and moves forward about a hand's length a day. The trails wind, but followed far enough they all have the same habit. They begin at the bottom and they go up.

In good light the grazer can be seen to have three clear frills on each side, folded flat behind its head. Near the floor it has no legs. Halfway up the trunks it has front ones, with four toes. Near the top it has hind ones, with five, and the glass of it has started to cloud.

So there is one animal, not two. It hatches on the valley floor and spends about ten years grazing its way up to the light, which is about what a seedling's root spends coming down. Most do not finish. A trail followed upward usually stops in the middle of a trunk, cleanly, with nothing at the end of it. Somewhere near that place there will be a pale one, off its branch and laid along the bark, and it will have been lying there for a week before the trail arrived. The waiting can be watched for as long as anyone likes. The taking happens between one look and the next: one gulp, like a vent's but smaller, and the water is still again.

The grown ones eat the young ones because there is nothing else in the valley made of meat. The small eat the forest and the large eat the small, and nothing comes in over the rim to add to this or take from it. The swifts have no reason to descend. A grazer that is not eaten arrives under the mat, stops grazing, takes a branch, and whitens. What decides which ones arrive is not clear. It may be no more than which trunk they started up.

The young begin at the bottom because the grown ones go down to leave them there. Every few years, when a vent clears and the dome of warm water comes up through the trunks, each pale animal it passes lets go of its branch. It does not swim. It falls, as everything falls here, straight and slowly, turning a little, for some minutes, and lands at the foot of its own tree. From there it walks over the stone leaves to the vent field. This is the only time one is found near another. The eggs are left under the lifted edges of stone leaves at the margin of the bare ground, where the water shimmers. They are as clear as the water and hard to find.

Then each walks back and climbs. The climb takes a season, and the glaze does not survive it. By the time the animal is back at its branch the coat is crazed all over, and the branch is no longer quite the right place, because the surface has gone up a hand's width since it last chose one. It settles that much higher, the coat splits down the back, and it walks out and leaves the old skin holding on.

This is why the first one found is usually empty, and why there is never only one. Below every living animal is a line of casts, each a hand's width above the last, overlapping like shingles along the branch and on down the trunk. The top few are clear. An arm's length down they have whitened. Further down they are opal, and the tree's own yearly skins of glass have closed over their feet and begun to take them in. Each is slightly smaller than the one above. The line ends at a cast no longer than a finger, at the height where the surface stood in the year that animal came up under it.

So it can be read like the bark: a hand's width to a skin, a few years to a hand's width. Most lines run two or three metres, which is about a century. Some run twenty.

At least one runs the whole way. Its tree stands in the deep part of the basin toward the dam, over a row of rounded stones where the stream was; they are the only rounded stones in the range. The line begins an arm's length under the mat and goes down more than forty metres, some four hundred skins, without a gap. Going down, the casts whiten and turn to opal, and then they are so far inside the banded trunk that they are seen as if through a window. For most of the distance they hardly differ. In the last few metres they change. They shrink quickly. The stalks behind the jaw get shorter, become bumps, and are gone. The tail, a blade higher up, narrows to something round. The last cast is not on the tree. It is on the floor at the tree's foot, half under a stone leaf: finger-long, blunt-headed, smooth behind the jaw, with the short tail and spread toes of a thing that walked on wet litter and breathed air.

From the thirty-first cast upward, the left hind foot has four toes. So has the animal at the top.

The forest that was in the valley did not die, and this was in the forest. It was under the ferns when the exit closed. When the water came up through the litter it went up with the trees. It has gone up with its own tree ever since, a hand's width at a time, and has given up nothing at the bottom.

It lies now on a branch just under the mat, with everything it has been standing below it in the trunk: glass, then chalk-white, then opal, then banded stone. By mid-morning the pearling reaches it. Bubbles form along its back and its jaw and on its toes, as they do on every leaf around it, and on the casts beneath it all the way down. The casts keep theirs until they let go. The animal flicks its gills, and a small bright scatter goes up to join the rest under the mat.

It is a hand's width above its last skin. Between the surface and the lowest notch in the rim there is room for about three hundred more. After that the lake will have somewhere to go, and for the first time a thing let go in this water may not fall straight. What the animal will do then cannot be read from the casts. For now it does what it did yesterday. About once a minute, the gills move.
