Someone keeps the turbine. This can be known before anyone is seen. The water that builds the towers builds just as patiently inside steel, on nozzle rings and valve seats and in the throats of the wells, and a machine fed on this steam and then left alone would have closed up solid years ago, like a dead vent. It is still turning. So in one place in the basin the sinter is being taken off at the rate it is laid on, and there the work is done by hand.

It is one pair of hands. The path from the shed to the wellheads is a single foot wide, worn a finger deep into the crust, and it goes the way the towers allow. On the shelf above the governor there is one cup. There is one pallet on the floor, and one pair of gloves patched across the palms with pieces of an older pair.

She is small and deep-chested, as people born at this height are, and she moves at the pace the air permits, slowly and without stopping. Ash has worked into her coat, her hat, and the creases of her knuckles, so that from any distance she is the color of the birds. The raptors notice everything that moves on the floor, but they stopped noticing her long ago.

She is older than the paint. She remembers the wellheads red, and before that she remembers the ground where they stand. She was born in the stone hut when it had a roof, to people who cut sulfur and sent it out on the cart track. When the sulfur stopped paying, most of the families went over the rim. Hers stayed long enough for the drillers to come. She was the girl they hired to carry for them, and later the young woman they paid to stand in front of the gauges twice a day and write down what they said. When the drillers left too, she was the only person who knew where everything was. Nobody asked her to stay. She doesn't know what stood on the concrete pad. It was already four bolts when she was a child.

The handwriting on the clipboard is hers. She kept the log for years after there was anyone to read it: inlet pressure, bearing temperature, date. Then one day she turned back a sheet and saw she had written the same two numbers on every line. She can't read the last entry now any more than anyone else can, but two of the three things it says are still true. These days she says the readings aloud, once, standing at the gauges, in a level voice that doesn't reach the door. That is nearly all the talking she does.

Her round begins when the steam is standing straight. She walks the pipe out to the nine wells, and under the bare stretches the two rows of lizards part ahead of her and close behind. Six wells are flowing. Three are shut in, because the far end of the wire has never asked for more than six, and on those three she turns each valve a quarter open and back so the scale can't fix it where it stands. The wellhead gauges have been wrong for decades, in ways she has memorized. What she trusts is the back of her hand. She holds it near a flange, or against a bearing housing in the shed, and keeps still for a few breaths. Then she either opens the oiler a little or leaves it alone. The drillers taught her the gauges. Nobody taught her this, any more than anyone teaches the chicks, which dig themselves up out of the ash alone and walk off already knowing what warm is.

She used to fight the rodents for the lagging. She rewrapped the pipe with sacking and wire each time they stripped it, for more years than made sense. Then one year she worked out what the bare steel cost her at the turbine and found that the wire had never missed it. Now the rodents have the lagging and the birds have the warm strip underneath, and their pits run beside her path for kilometers, which is convenient. She takes one egg from a ring and doesn't return to that ring until the birds have shifted it. The fox takes more than she does and digs worse. They pass in the lanes most days without either one changing pace.

She could close the leaking flange. The gasket for it, cut years ago, hangs on a nail beside the clipboard. Instead she stops there each morning and holds her wrist next to the little tower. Every other tower in the basin was old when she was born. This is the only one she has seen begin, and she wants to know how fast it goes.

By the time the cloud has put its lid on the rim she is inside. She saves the noisy work for four o'clock. The spare strainer basket, furred white, goes on the bench, and she knocks it clean with a hammer and a bar while the rain is loud on the corrugated roof. The scale comes off in curved flakes the color of the towers. She sweeps them out the door, where over the years they have made a low pale apron that the rain has begun to cement.

When the rain stops and the field shines, she goes to the pools with two cans. She gets there after the fox. The birds and rodents and lizards are already shoulder to shoulder at the margins, and they don't shift for her. She fills her cans at arm's length, farther in than any of them drink, where the water is right for tea. On the way back she stops at a tower behind the shed and draws up a wire basket she lowered into its throat that morning, with the egg in it and a few potatoes. Every animal here knows the point where the heat becomes too much. She is the only one with a use for the other side of it.

The potatoes come in sacks that appear at a cairn in the notch four or five times a year, along with flour, tea, salt, a tin of grease, and lamp bulbs wrapped in cloth. She never arranged this. She has seen who brings them only from above, as two or three dark figures and a mule, small on the snow. She leaves nothing at the cairn in return. What she sends goes down the wire.

The lamp over the door has no switch. She wired it straight off the generator herself, so that it would show the one thing worth knowing from far away. In daylight, from the basin floor, it looks like an oversight. At night, from the valleys beyond the rim, it is a star in the notch that doesn't set. When a bulb fails she changes it before dark.

She knows the valleys in return through her feet. At dusk, as the cloud thins and the rim comes back, the thrum in the shed floor deepens slightly and the governor opens on its own. That is the lamps coming on below, a few hundred of them, more than when she was young. Late at night it eases. On some mornings it comes on early and heavy, and she knows it is cold down there. She has never been to where the wire ends. Everything else in the basin faces inward and keeps what warmth it can get. She lives at the gap in the wall and sends hers out.

She sleeps on the shed floor without blankets, at the distance from the turbine pedestal where the concrete is right, and a few times a year she moves the pallet a hand's width in or out. The thrum comes up through her back. She sleeps soundly on it and would wake at once if it changed. Now and then she wakes anyway, for no reason she could give. She gets up in the dark, holds the back of her hand to the bearing housing, and stands still. Then she gives the oiler a quarter turn, or doesn't.
