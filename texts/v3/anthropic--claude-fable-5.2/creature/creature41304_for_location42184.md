Start with one, since the eye asks for one. Take a middling animal, two hands wide, half a day in from the rim.

Its shell is a dozen or so plates, each six-sided and ringed from the center out like a cut branch, with the newest ring at the margin where plate meets plate. The rings are too fine to count. The shell is the color of the rock because it is the rock. Lichen takes the stone up a grain at a time, the animal takes the lichen, and what the animal cannot use it lays down at the seams. The body underneath is soft, grey, and seldom seen. The legs end in pads and leave no mark. The rasp is a hard plate set crosswise that cuts on the push stroke only, like a carpenter's scraper. The animal feeds by rocking a finger's width forward and back for as long as the film is damp. When the rock dries it draws down until the shell rim meets stone, and the snails are shut in the dark with it.

At the front are two pits that can tell cloud from sun, and it has no better eyes than that. It knows which way to face by which end gets wet first in the evening, and it keeps that end behind. It knows its neighbors by their pressure, six of them as a rule. When the pressure comes off one side, it has learned everything it is going to learn that month.

That is a grazer, and it is the hardest thing on the plateau to keep hold of, because by the time it reaches any size it has become a place. The fog arrives from behind, so moss starts in the rear seams, and the turf, when turf comes, is deepest over the tail. An old one is green at the back and bare at the front, like a hill with a weather side. A lizard lives four or five years, and the shell under it has been walking for three hundred. The grazer is the least lively thing on its own back.

What goes anywhere up here is the gap. A gap starts at a wall. An animal as wide as a washbasin steps up out of the field onto the last shell of the crescent, and the place where it stood is left open, dark and wet, scraped to bare stone in exactly its shape. The one behind steps in. But the one behind does not hand on all of what it was given. It spreads a little into the room. Grazers grow at no other time, since there is no other room. The shell lays down one ring after a move and then waits, so the rings on a plate are also a count of steps taken. The vacancy that passes backward is a ring narrower than the one that arrived. It goes on that way for decades, out through smaller and smaller animals, each keeping its sliver. What reaches the cracks at the rim is a gap the size of a thumbnail, and the thing that walks into it fits.

So every dark patch drifting over the pale field is one animal's absence being shared out along a line to the edge, and a grazer's width is the sum of what it kept.

A gap also waits. Nothing steps onto bare stone while it has lichen left where it stands. Over thin ground the vacancy sits open until the film has grown back thick enough to be worth the step. That takes longest on the poorest rock, which is the rock that most needed leaving alone. No animal decides this. Each declines to move for nothing, and the sum of the declining is a fallow.

Stand back from the one gap and the one animal, and what remains is a layer. The herd is a single grazer deep and covers everything. Under it is the lichen, as thin as paint, and under the lichen is shell again. There are the plates in the scraped rock, the walls slumped to swellings, and the swellings worn to whalebacks. Below those there is presumably more of the same, for as far down as the cliffs are high. Whatever was here first, peaks and all, is a long way under. Nothing has gone down the face to see where the six-sided pattern stops. The gliders hang against it all day and take no interest.

Most of the buried shells are still hollow. That is why the surface holds the night's water where it falls, and why it is warm at every hour. It is a stone sponge with a cupful in each cell, and water is slow to give up heat. In old ground a passing gap sometimes uncovers a ring of dark river pebbles set flush among the pale plates. It is some bird's curb, from a pan that silted up before the nearest wall was begun.

So the thing that lives here is the plateau. It is alive the way a reef or a tree trunk is alive, in a skin on the outside, with everything it has ever been holding that skin up. The skin feeds over its whole surface, a shadow's worth per plate per day. It is born at the margin and dies toward the middle, and it thickens where it dies. The thickenings are cupped toward the weather, so each death improves the catch of fog for whatever comes after. Its only supplies from outside are what the cloud carries up the cliffs: water, a little dust, a little dissolved stone, and a few pebbles a year in the beaks of birds. On that it has raised itself out of the forest country, one shell's thickness at a time.

It has habits. It drinks once a day and is dry by noon. It does not flinch, because nothing has ever turned one of its animals over. There is no room to turn one over in. It makes no sound on purpose. A voice is for reaching something that is not touching you, and nothing in the herd has ever been out of touch. The tick of shell on shell says that there was room and now there is not, and nothing else here needs saying. The other residents have fallen in with this. Insects blown up from the forest buzz on the rock for a day and stop. The birds stand. Each frog sits in its cup under the drip, and whatever passes from frog to frog passes by water.

Whether the whole table has an outline, six-sided or otherwise, could only be judged from the top of the gliders' stack, and the gliders are looking down for insects. From the ground there is only the evening to see. The lowland air comes over the edge, knee-deep and then overhead. Every animal for several days' walk has its green end to the cliffs and its face to the middle. Each one is a step nearer the middle than it was, and a ring wider.
