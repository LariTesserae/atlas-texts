You see them first as figures at a distance, because that is the distance they keep. Each wears a wrap of undyed wool in one of the four colors the animals come in, sandals of plaited tussock, and hair tied back with cord. They walk down the middle of the causeways at a pace that would take them anywhere on the shelf without breathing harder. When a wool-bearer lies folded across the paving they go around it, and there is room to, and that is what the width is for.

Up close they are ordinary highland people, broad in the chest and dark in the cheek from the light. They look at you directly and then go on with what they were doing. If you speak, they answer in a voice pitched to reach you and stop there. Nobody on the shelf calls out. You begin to understand that the houses were set by ear. Each hearth stands where a neighbor's ordinary talk arrives whole and a neighbor's murmur does not arrive at all. They live inside each other's hearing and outside each other's reach, and they seem to find this the correct amount of each other.

They are exact, and it does not look like effort. A woman lifts the grass lid from a cistern, dips one gourd, and stands holding the lid until the surface has come back to its finger below the rim. Then she covers it. A man digging tubers takes what fills his basket and presses the smallest back into the carried soil with his thumb. Inside each house the fire is banked, never out and never high. The common greeting, as far as you can make it out, is a question about water, *Is it full?* The reply is *To a finger*, said with the mild pleasure people elsewhere bring to good weather.

It takes longer to see what is strange about them, because it is an absence. They are not worn. This is not youth. There are grey heads here, and one man so old his hands have gone to root, though even he looks as if he arrived at that age this morning and found it fit. Look at the one twisting fiber in her doorway. Her hands are quick and sure, and there is no callus on them. The doorstone under her has no hollow. It goes further in than that. They use each other's whole names every time, none shortened by use. Two who share a hearth look at each other across a row of tubers with the attention of people recently met. You can detect no grudges and no old jokes. Whatever rubs people smooth against each other over years has not happened to them, or has happened and not taken. They are like the rock they live on. Every one of them is still a clean fracture, something that happened recently. You suspect the spacing is partly courtesy, since at arm's length such people might cut.

A few of them stand nearer an edge than the rest. There is a boy of seven or eight with a loose front tooth, which he pushes forward with his tongue when he thinks and then lets fall back. There is a woman on the west side very near her time, who sits in the sun with her hands laced under the weight. Three hearths along is the old man, who is very near his, and who sits in the same sun. They can hear each other without raising their voices. If both things happened on one day the people cord would be knotted exactly as before, and it may be that this is understood, and that neither is willing to go first. Neither seems troubled. She is not weary of carrying. He is not afraid. The tooth does not hurt.

On the east side there is the mason. The wall with eleven courses is his, and so are the block lying ready and the hammerstone on top of it. He comes to it most days. He lifts the hammerstone, warm on its upper face and cool beneath, and taps the one set block of the twelfth course along its length, listening to how it sits. It sits well. He looks at the next block, at the bed waiting for it, at the fit he can already see. Then he puts the hammerstone back and sits a while with his forearms on his knees. He is not idle and he is not prevented. He is in the part of the work he likes best, when the stone is ready and has not yet been set, and he has been allowed to stay in it. If you asked him when the wall will be finished he would say *soon*, and it would not be a lie.

Most of the city lives in the hour he lives in. The daily things go round and close, the gourd dipped and the channel answering. The large things lean at their edge like the geese at the edge of the tarns, about to, and comfortable there.

The animals live here on the same terms. The rock-hares hold the wall tops like a second citizenry, one to a stretch of wall, spaced as the houses are spaced. The two dark birds, which anywhere else would be waiting on a death, turn on their updraft waiting on nothing. Nobody has knotted a cord for the hares or the finches or the frogs. They are not counted, and they do not change either, and of everyone on the shelf they seem the least surprised.

Only one person here is ever surprised, and she is the keeper of the cords.

She is a woman of middle age with a long back and a habit of touching things as she passes them. No season comes to this shelf to tell her when a count is due. A count is made when she begins to wonder. Something catches at her, a finch going up from a seed head or a silence a little too long between hammer taps. She stands still on the plaza cobbles, and then she goes to her house for fiber. She walks every causeway. She lays a hand on the stone foot of every granary. People come to their doorways when they hear her sandals and stand to be looked at. This is the nearest the city comes to gathering, each one in turn seen by the same pair of eyes on the same day. She counts the herds lying down. She counts the houses, and the eleven courses have their own small knot. She counts herself last, touching her breastbone, as though she too might have gone missing.

Then she sits under the roofed frame and ties it, and lays the new cord along the last one, knot against knot. They match. They have always matched. She looks at them for some time anyway. Her face then is hard to name. It is closest to the face of someone who has gone in at night to listen for a child's breathing and has heard it. She hangs the cord with the others. They hang as thick as thatch, and nobody has ever counted them.

Her fingertips are callused. The skin of her right thigh, where she rolls the fiber, is rubbed smooth. It is the only wear on the shelf. She is the one inhabitant with a past, and all that is in it is going to look and finding everything still there.

Each time, before she draws the last knot tight, there is a moment when the loop is still open and could be slid a finger's width along the cord. Her hands know it. They rest in it for about a breath. Then she pulls the knot snug where it belongs. Across the shelf the mason sets his hammerstone back on the block, and the boy's tongue lets the tooth fall back into its place.
