The first answer is the animals, and it is not wrong. White geckos, drumming crickets, gray moths, wax-colored isopods, a dozen blind fish, and moss in collars the size of a palm are all alive here.

But look at what they live on. The geckos live on warmth that leaks from the conduits. The moss lives on light from markers set out for someone's feet. The fish live on water shaken off the ceiling, and the crickets drum in a minute of quiet that was not made for them. Everything visibly alive in the chamber lives on spill, and none of it goes indoors. The calcite stops at the doorsill as cleanly as the damp does. No gecko lies on a dry floor. The animals have moved in around a household without being part of it.

The second answer is that no one lives here and the town is simply kept, warmed and dried by the thing in the well. That answer lasts until someone sits in one of the rooms through a few ventings.

The murmur inside a house is easy to take for the tone outside, heard through a wall. But the tone outside is a stack, and the murmur is one plain pitch, about as loud as someone breathing in the next room. In the house next door it is a different pitch. Along a terrace, in and out of each doorway, every house has one and no two are the same. Each street has its own pitch because it is the sum of the houses along it. An intersection changes the chord because it changes who is in earshot.

The note has a place in the room. It is always in the upper room, a pace back from the window, at about the height of a chest. Nothing is there, and a hand passed through the spot feels only warm air. But the note is loudest there and falls off in every direction, the way a voice is loudest at the mouth. The windows all face down the slope toward the well.

So the third answer is sixty-six, by ear, one to a house. The well has to be thought about again. From the plaza it seems to be the source of the sound. From inside the houses it seems more like the place where the sound is added up. The conduits are warm and solid, and nothing about warm stone says which way anything in it is traveling. The column in the shaft, which neither sways nor rises, may be less an engine than a meeting.

Once they are counted, they stop being alike.

- **The low house:** It stands on the plaza, nearest the rim. Its note is the one that comes up through the soles on the ramp. Inside it cannot be heard at all, but a palm laid on the floor finds a fine, steady tremble. When the tone climbs before a venting, this note does not move. The others rise away from it.
- **The pair:** A house on the second terrace has a slow swell in it, one every four seconds or so. It holds two notes a hair apart, which have neither closed the distance nor widened it.
- **The high house:** It stands at the top of the slope, nearest the ramp. Its note is thin, like a wet finger on the rim of a glass, and it is always the first to climb. It starts a full second before any other. The fish in the sump, at the far end of the town, are flat on the stone before the second note has moved.
- **The late one:** It lives in a two-story house midway down. After each lull the notes come back to strength within a few seconds of one another, except this one. It is last every time, by about a breath.

The venting is something they do together. It goes down the hill. The high house climbs, then its street, then the terraces below in order, until every note but the lowest is standing a third above where it lives. They hold. Then the column lets all of it go at once, and that is the crack. It goes into the conduits and through them into the rock at a dozen points. The roar is only what is left over in the air, and the rain is an accident of the roar. The fish, the moss, the rimstone pools, and the pavement of broken straws are all fed by the side effect of something the town is saying.

The lull is not rest. The notes drop to half and hold level, and they stay at the windows. A room full of people does the same after one of them has called down a long hallway. They are listening.

What they are listening for can be heard too, from the right place. The still spots, such as the corner of a doorway or the fourth step of that one stair, are where the sixty-six cancel one another. During the full tone they hold almost nothing. In the lull they hold what is left when the town is taken away, which is a single note. It is thin and comes from no direction.

When the interval is short, the note is there before the gutters have finished running. When the interval has stretched toward fourteen minutes, it comes at the very end of the lull, just before the geckos stop, and it is fainter. Either the rock between changes with a tide or with the day's heat, or the one answering is not always the same distance away.

At first the note seems to fit no street here. But the buildings number sixty-seven, and the notes sixty-six. On the third street from the top, the pitch steps down house by house toward the plaza, and between the fifth house and the seventh the step is double. The sixth house has no murmur in it. The far note is that step.

So the last answer is the plainest. A household of sixty-seven lives here. Sixty-six are at home, each at an upper window facing the well. One is away, somewhere on the other side of a great deal of solid rock, and has been for long enough that the stairs grew basins and the eaves learned how long a straw can get. Every eleven minutes or so the ones at home rise a third, call, go quiet, and are answered.

The sixth house is kept like the others. Its room is warm, clean, and dry, and its conduit is as warm as any on the street. From its window the rain can be seen falling past in the amber, with the red rim beyond. A pace back from the window, at the height of a chest, there is only air, and the town keeps that too.

Then the lull ends. The late one comes in last. At the top of the slope the high house holds level and waits to begin again.
