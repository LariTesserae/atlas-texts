Most of what lives here is the mat, and the mat does nothing that can be watched. The geckos and the snails keep to their vents and their stems. There is one other.

From the top of a rib it is first taken for a crozier. It is the right colour, the rust of the scales, and it has something of the shape, with the back rounded and the head tucked. Then it passes between two stalks and is taken for an animal. Only when it reaches the edge of a rib and sets something down do the hands show.

Close to, it stands hip-high. It is covered in down, fine and dense, lying flat along the back and shoulders and thinning to nothing on the face, the palms and the soles. It is the kind of down a body grows before it is born and is meant to shed. When it pushes between the croziers, fur against fur, there is no sound at all.

Under the down the skin is thin. The light goes through the ears and the webs of the fingers and comes out amber. The head is large for the body. At the crown is a place the size of a coin where the bone has not closed, and the pulse can be seen in it, slow and even. The eyes are a dark slate that has not settled on a colour. There is one tooth, or the edge of one, a white line in the lower gum.

It would be simple to call it a child, and the hands and the proportions allow it. Little else does. It does not fuss, or tire, or look around for anyone. A child is small for the time being. This is the size it is, and it is as old as the basalt, which is still cooling.

On the mat it walks upright, with the forward tilt of something that learned to walk this morning, each step a small fall that it catches. It has never been seen to fall. On the basalt it goes down on its hands and moves low and flat, like the geckos, and the heat does not seem to trouble its palms.

It drinks from the croziers, tipping the smaller coils toward its mouth for the cupful in the centre and letting them spring back. At the vents it waits beside the gecko for a drop to run down the stone, and they take turns. The gecko does not move off for it. It has not been seen to eat. Sometimes it puts a moss capsule or the tip of a horsetail in its mouth, holds it against the one tooth, and takes it out whole.

It carries a stone. The stone is flat and water-worn, of a kind with the fourteen in the path, and it rides in the crook of the left arm against the down. No loose stone lies anywhere else in the chamber, and where this one came from is not known. It is set down for drinking and for climbing and picked up again afterwards.

It rests in the ring on the largest rib. The wall comes up to its ribs, so it has to climb in. It sits curled with its chin on its knees, wound the way everything else here is wound. It has not been seen to sleep. The lids come halfway down and stay there a while, and then they lift.

From the ring it makes a round, though with no dusk to count by, nobody can say how often. It goes down onto the mat and across the path, stepping on every stone. On the fourteenth it stands for some time with the fifteenth in its arm, facing the rib ahead. Then it steps off onto the moss and goes on toward the middle, where the floor dips, to the arch. It could pass the arch on either side. It goes through every time, and reaches up as it passes to touch the knot.

Beyond the arch it climbs. The fur on the great stalks is thick enough to grip, and it goes up twenty metres with the stone left at the foot. At the top it sits in the centre of the coil, in the cupful of water, and stretches a hand toward the pale stems hanging from the roof. They stop an arm's length above the coil. That is a grown arm's length, and its own covers about half. It keeps the hand up and watches the snails, which are near enough to count. It stays up there longer than anywhere.

Last it goes to the tongue of lava by the eastern wall. It leaves the stone where the mat ends, dry and slightly crisp, and goes down on its hands and sets them in the prints, one after another. They fit. They are the only outline of itself it has ever seen, since it casts no shadow and the mat closes behind every step. At the fifth it stops. It looks at the sixth, the shallow one, then at the moss beyond it, then at the wall, where the salt meets the black stone. Then it draws a breath, the kind taken before speaking.

That breath is the only sound it makes. It takes one when a drop lands close by, and when it comes on the gecko at a vent, and when a visitor at last stands in front of it. It looks up with its whole attention. The face holds the look that comes just before a first smile. It breathes in, and what follows is the breath going out again, quietly.

Then it goes back to the edge of the mat, picks up the stone, fits it into the crook of its arm, and starts back.
