Taken all together, what lives here is a ring. It is a palm wide and runs around the intake cracks. If it could be lit and watched for a month, it would look like one animal: a pale, napped, faintly seething gasket that eases outward a finger's width when the weather turns and settles back when it clears. By number it is nearly all mites. They are the colour of the salt they stand on, and they are what the ring is made of, the way a felt is made of hair.

A ring has no mouth, though. Along the lowest crack on the windward side, where the draught pulls hardest, it comes apart into a row.

The row is crickets. Each is about the length of a fingernail, dark as the glass rind, humpbacked and wingless. The hind legs are folded high, and the antennae are longer than the rest of the animal put together. They stand facing the cold, each one antenna-sweep from the next. That spacing is the only territory in the pocket. A cricket owns the arc its antennae can reach and whatever the draught throws into it.

The fourth from the bottom of the row is a female, and she has the best place on the crack. The fissure doglegs there around a cube of salt, and the incoming air has to turn. What it carries cannot turn as fast, so it strikes the face of the cube and drops. She stands at the foot of that cube. She took the station two moults ago, when the cricket that held it split its skin and was soft and slow for an hour. She was smaller then. She stepped into the gap and has not stepped out of it since. She is adult now and will not moult again, so the place is hers until something takes her.

Her face is in air near freezing. The tips of her hind feet rest on sulfur needles warm enough to be slightly tacky. Between her head and her heels lies the difference between a cold morning and a hot afternoon, and she has never in her life been one temperature. She steers by that gradient. If her face warms, she has drifted in. If her heels cool, she has drifted out. She corrects by the width of a foot.

She does not hear the roar. She has no ears worth the name, and the roar has never stopped, so it is simply how the crust feels, a hum in the shins of all six legs. What she reads is whatever is laid on top of it. A tick from the hot side is a blade of hematite letting go, and means nothing. A patter is grit falling back. Feet are different. Another cricket's tread arrives in threes. A spider's tread arrives in eights and then stops, and the stopping is what she attends to.

Her left antenna is a third shorter than her right. One of the pale spiders had it by the tip in her last instar, and she left the tip behind. It would have grown back at a moult, but there are no more moults. She stands a few degrees askew to sweep the draught with the long one, and the cricket on her left has gained a little ground because of it.

Her day has two tides. In the afternoon the wind arrives with the forest in it. First the air changes on her palps, something green and faintly rotten in it under the acid. Then things begin to hit the salt. An aphid is still a little sweet. A midge is mostly husk. Moth scales are no use to her and drift past to the mites. For one week of the year nearly everything that lands is a winged ant, fat and oily, and the whole row eats until it can hardly close its mouthparts. For a longer stretch it is pollen, which whitens the salt until the mites darken it again. She keeps a calendar of a forest four kilometres beneath her, entirely in its dead.

The other tide is before dawn, when the lip of the crack sweats. The film is thin and tastes of the white feathers it has dissolved, sharp and bitter, and it is the only water there is. She lowers her head and drinks from the crust, and so does the whole row. Two body lengths behind her, something with eight feet has stopped moving, and it is drinking too.

Under the dogleg, the same eddy that drops her food drops everything else as well. Moults, droppings and wing fragments settle there, the pinch of dark crumbs. She hatched in it. She came out of it pale and no bigger than a salt grain, and climbed through the back of the row eating cast skins until she was large enough to hold a place at the front.

Her abdomen is heavy now. Her ovipositor is a dark needle as long as her hind shin. From this station, and from no other on the crack, she can back half a body length down the lip with her forefeet still on her own ground, far enough for the needle to reach. She does this in the slack hours after the drinking. She feels behind her for the crumbs, pushes the needle in among the skins of things that stood where she stands, a few of them her own, and leaves an egg, then another.

Then she hauls herself back up, sets her feet, turns the long antenna into the draught, and waits for the afternoon.
