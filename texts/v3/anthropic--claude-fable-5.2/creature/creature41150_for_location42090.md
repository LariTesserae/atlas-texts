Take one. Any one would do, which is the first thing to understand about them, and it stops being true the longer you look.

She is shoulder-high at the hump and lighter than she appears, because half of what you see is hair. The guard hair hangs to her fetlocks in grey, bone, and rust-black, in about the proportions of the crust. On an animal her age this is no longer only resemblance. Along her spine, where she cannot rub, the lichen itself has taken hold, a thin scurf of the real thing rooted in the coarse hair. She moves slowly enough for it.

Her muzzle is broad and flat-fronted, the lips hard as a heel, the lower teeth set forward like a scraper and worn to a low yellow ridge. Her eyes sit far back on the skull, each pupil a horizontal bar. With her head down she can see nearly the full ring of the horizon, and what she watches in it is other heads. Six are near enough to matter. If one lifts, she lifts. That is the entire language, one word, and she has passed it on some thousands of times without ever learning what it was about. Twice she was the one who said it first.

Her throat makes breath and nothing else. She did not cry when she was born, on the southern rim, under the orange part of the cloud. For her first thaw she stood on the stone border of her mother's polygon while her mother took the dome. By the second thaw she was one polygon off. By the third she was four, and that was the end of it. For them, growing up is a distance. She has not touched another of her kind since, unless the stubs count. She leans on each one she comes to, and the stone smells of whoever leaned there yesterday.

Her day is one dome. She starts at the border and works inward in a slow spiral, scraping to the crust, and by the time the light has gone down its one shade she is at the center, where she folds her legs and lies on the patch she has cleared. In the morning she stands and steps over the stones. She has never thought of this as travel. The ground arrives, she eats it, and more arrives.

But she is in her third time around now, and the ground has started to arrive familiar. She knows a split boulder and a stub with a lean to it. The lichen under her mouth this week grew back over a scraping she made herself, or one made by whoever stood where she stands, which for her comes to the same thing. She is eating her own leavings at nineteen years' remove.

A hunter has been three polygons behind her and one to the side for most of a year. It is pale and long, it steps when she steps, and it lies down when she lies down. She stopped lifting her head for it some time ago, and so have the six around her. It is waiting for the morning she does not make the step. Farther back a pair of birds stand on a border with their wings folded, like two clerks outside an office that has not opened.

In her left ear is the oval, with rime on it most mornings. It reads 30418862. It went in during her second year at the notch, with a smell of hot metal and a punch through the cartilage, the only loud thing ever done to her. Nineteen years later a hooked pole turned the ear toward the rim, and a lowlander read it out in pairs: thirty, forty-one, eighty-eight, sixty-two. Those are the only voices she has ever heard. In her whole life, spoken sound has consisted of her number, said twice. The glow on the cloud ahead is a little brighter each month. She does not connect it with anything, yet when the night air starts to pour past her knees toward the edge, she turns that ear into it.

She has molted thirty-eight times. Each thaw the underwool lifts off her in grey mats and she drags her flanks along the stone borders to be rid of it. It rolls into the troughs and at night it leaves. The voles take their share first, and the run beneath the dome she is lying on is lined with her. The rest goes over the rim. By weight, far more of her is now in the lowlands, as blankets and coat linings and felt, than is standing here.

One morning she will not make the step. The hunter will stop being ignored, and the birds will walk up. The animal behind her will step into her polygon on schedule and find nothing there worth lifting its head for. Within a week the spacing will have closed over the place the way the crust closes over a hoofprint. By the next thaw the ear will be gone and the oval will be underground, in a nest of wool, beside a dozen others, bright and unread.

The dome she did not finish will keep what she left on it. Nineteen years on it will carry a slightly thicker crust than its neighbors, and some animal not yet born will step onto it, have a slightly better day than usual, and step off.
