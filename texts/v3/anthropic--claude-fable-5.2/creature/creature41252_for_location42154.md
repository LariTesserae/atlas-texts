**What Comes After the Pigs**

It is known first as an interval. The deer drink and go. The pigs come down the chute in a rattle of small feet, drink loudly, and go. Then the bank stays empty a little longer than it should, and something arrives that the mud takes differently.

It cannot be seen. What there is instead is a slow crush of leaves, one footfall and then a wait long enough for the ticking of the termites to close over it. There is breath, drawn in short samples and let out long through something soft. And there is a smell of warm hide, dried pool mud, and fermenting fig, which reaches the water before the animal does and hangs in the chute after it has gone.

In the paste at the edge it leaves prints wider than a spread hand, with three blunt toes and, on the forefoot, the nick of a fourth. They are pressed deeper than anything else on the bank. The hind foot lands in the mark of the fore, almost on a line, so the trail it wears is no wider than a pig's. The body is wider. On the paths through the thorn it fills the tunnel wall to wall, and the thorns drag along its flanks with a sound like a thumbnail on canvas and close behind it. The hide is thick enough not to mind. Beside the paths, a little higher than a pig could reach, the trunks are rubbed dark and smooth and faintly greasy to the palm.

So it is a heavy animal, three or four pigs' worth, built as a wedge: low in front, high and round at the rump, made for pushing. It has a short neck with a ridge of stiff hair, and its skin is sparsely bristled and as warm as the bark. Ticks cluster at its ears and in the folds of its legs. Its eyes are small and do little work here. The work is done by the nose, which is the upper lip and nostrils drawn out together into a short trunk that is never still. The tip is wet, which in this season is worth remarking.

At the head of each chute there is a place where the litter is scuffed back and the clay is stained, and everything that passes stops to read it. The stain says female, old, not in season, here last night and the night before. This is how the arrangement at the water is kept. She reads the deer as an hour gone and the pigs as minutes gone, and she waits in the thorn until the pig smell has gone flat before she comes down.

She does not drink from the edge. She walks through the trodden paste and into the pool, so slowly that it does not slop, and the fish part around her legs. The crocodiles do not move. She is too large to be food, and they are too far gone in waiting to try. She drinks with the water at her belly, and then she lets herself down until only the trunk is out, and stays. The water is as warm as she is and does not cool her. It is the one hour in the day when her weight is taken off her.

The rest of the night she spends eating. She finds the fruiting fig by the shuffling of the big bats and stands beneath them, taking what they drop, turning toward each small thud in the litter. She works the trunk in among the thorns and strips the leaves the evergreens have kept. In the hour before dawn she feeds fastest, because every leaf on the floor then carries dew, and on the nights she does not go to the pool this is how she drinks.

By day she lies in a silted oxbow where the shade never thins, in a bowl in the clay that fits her exactly, because she made it by lying there. Ants cross her as they cross everything. A gecko sometimes hunts along her back. She twitches an ear, and one of the ears is split to the base from something long ago.

She is old enough to hold the basin whole. She knows which pool goes first and which goes last, the one under the cut bank at the bend, where clay lies beneath the sand. She knows the year that pool went to mud. That year she stood in the dry bed and dug with her forefeet until the hole darkened and filled, and for a month the deer and the pigs drank from it after her.

Six times she has brought a calf through a dry season here. Each was born striped and spotted pale on dark, the pattern of sun through leaves, for the days it would spend lying still in the litter. The last left two rains ago. Sometimes its smell is on a chute, a night old, and she stands over it for a while.

Once each night a pressure comes up through her feet before it reaches her chest. She stops chewing. The trunk lifts and tests the air and finds nothing, as it has found nothing every night of her life. The thing passes and has never arrived. The termites do not pause for it. She does, every time, and then goes on.

Toward morning, when the ridges finally cool enough for their air to slide downhill, it sometimes brings from the east a thin, sharp, burnt smell that belongs to nothing in the basin. She curls her lip to it, holds it, and lets it go. She has never noticed the orange stain. Her paths turn back at the last levee.

She has a voice, a thin rising whistle that seems to come from something much smaller. She has not used it this season, because there is no one to answer.

She will smell the first storm days before it comes. When the sand is river again she will walk into it, go under, and walk along the bottom. For a few nights, while the frogs are loud, she will whistle and be answered from somewhere upstream. Until then she comes down the chute after the pigs, and each night the water is a step farther from the prints she left the night before.
