At first it looks as though no one does. Nothing is built here. Nothing is stacked, fenced, or dug, and nothing stands higher than the oldest mounds.

The signs are small. On a trail of spent powder, just behind a mound, there is a row of dark pebbles, each no bigger than a knuckle, set a finger apart. In the grey thatch on a mound's windward side, where the rosettes died centuries ago and have gone dry and springy, there is a hollow the length of a body. And some mornings, very far out, a point of light flashes, another answers from a different quarter of the plain, and then there is nothing for days.

People live here. There are very few of them, and they are spaced the way the cushions are spaced, for the same reason. One household keeps to one mound, and only the house-sized domes have a household at all. From any of them to the next is a full day's walk, more often several.

They live in the wake. Like the lichens, the silver grass, and the red mats, they are something that found footing in ground a mound had finished with. They take nothing from the green. The dead thatch on the windward side is theirs to sleep in, and a little of it is theirs to burn, since the mound has already let it go. Everything else comes off the trail: grass seed, grass fibre, dried lichen, wax from the beetles' backs. When the flowering front arrives they stretch nets of grass twine across the wind, and the pollen cakes on them in yellow slabs that keep for years in this air.

The front is also the only time they gather. Households walk with it for a few days as it crosses their ground, beside neighbours they have known all year only as flashes. Whatever needs arranging between them is arranged then, quickly, with yellow dust on everyone.

From a distance one of them could be taken for the grazer. They are narrow, unhurried, and as pale as the ground, because they work spent powder into beetle wax and wear it on every bit of skin the sun can reach. Over the eyes they tie a band of woven grass with a slit in it. They are deep in the chest. They walk at one pace all day and do not run.

Their voices are low. The air does not carry sound, and they have stopped asking it to. Two of them talking stand with their heads nearly touching. For distance, each household has a blade of clear gypsum as long as a forearm, older than anyone can account for, and with it they throw the sun. There are not many things to say this way. I am here. A grazer is on my trail. The front has reached me. Come. Someone has died.

Water they get before dawn. One of them goes out in the dark with a bone scraper and a horn flask and takes the rime off the shaded faces of stones, working ahead of the sunrise. The flask is stoppered and carried under the clothes, against the belly, until the rime melts, and then it is drunk at once. Apart from what is inside living things, there is no other liquid water on the plateau.

In a bad season they cook stone. They heat crushed crystal in a lidded pot over a thatch fire, and the lid sweats. They dislike doing it. It leaves the same dull chalk the roots leave, so they take the crystal only from the bare ground between trails, off to the side, never from ahead of the mound. Ahead is not theirs.

When a child is born, someone walks to the nearest stub of hard rock, which may be two days off, and brings back a pebble. It is set at the windward edge of the mound so that it touches the last living rosette. Then the mound moves on. By the time the child is grown, the green has drawn back from the pebble by the width of a finger. Three fingers is a long life. Nobody here counts years. They go and look.

The pebbles of the dead stay where they are. A household's line runs back along the trail, a finger to a generation, and eighty names, all the names anyone knows, lie in a row a tall person could stretch out beside. Past the oldest pebble the trail keeps going, grey and straight, toward the rim, for kilometres. They know what that means. They do not think of themselves as having been here long.

The dead are carried a little way up the trail and laid on the powder. Nothing rots here. The bird that hangs overhead some days comes down in its own time. They call it patient, and mean it as praise.

What a household does with the rest of its days takes longer to see, because from one year to the next it hardly shows.

Every mound has ground ahead of it, and every household has walked that ground. Somewhere along the line, a hundred paces on or a thousand, the grey stroke of another mound's old trail lies across it. The household knows how wide it is. They know what a mound looks like after it has crossed one.

So they carry stone. Around midday, when the wind sets inward, one of them goes out to the between-ground with a frame on their back, levers up a load of fresh crust and blade, and walks it down to the crossing with the wind behind them. There they bed it into the spent strip, edge to edge and an arm deep, tight enough that the wind cannot get a finger under it. The wind gets under it anyway, and dust devils go through, and some of each year's work is done again the next. At dusk the wind turns and they walk home on it.

At one mound the paving was begun forty pebbles back. In that time the mound has come one pace nearer. It has nine still to go before its leading edge touches the first laid stone, and it will be longer in the crossing than it has been in the coming. No one has ever seen this work. No one has seen it fail either.

Asked why, they would probably not understand the question. They live on what the mound leaves behind it. It seems plain to them that something should be put down in front.

The one who carries there now is old. The green stands three fingers and a little more from her pebble, and she checks it less often than she used to. Her knees have made her slower on the walk out, so she takes smaller loads and lays them more carefully. It comes to the same patch by evening, about the size of a sleeping mat.

Tonight she comes back on the turned wind with the frame empty. Behind her the stone has begun to tick. She eats a piece of pollen cake the width of her palm and sees that the flask is beside the scraper for morning. Then she lets herself down into the hollow in the thatch, on the side that is now out of the wind. The moths are coming up out of the dead rosettes all around her for their hour. At her back the mound has closed and is holding what it took from the day.

It is a few degrees warmer than the night. So is she.
