The ones that are easy to count come first.

The toads are in every mat. Pull a sheet apart and they drop out like seeds, a dozen to the square metre, with their limbs folded and their eyes shut. They lay in the moss, and the tadpoles grow up inside the sponge without once seeing open water. A toad on bare rock is between addresses.

The birds follow the rolls. One will hang above a moving mat for an hour with its wings barely open, pinned in the airstream. When the roll strikes a tor and bursts, the bird drops on whatever shakes out. Then it tilts once, white, and others arrive from nowhere.

The plants in the cracks are the oldest things alive here. A stem as thick as a thigh carries a rosette no bigger than a hand. The wind takes the rest.

So far, everything either holds on or lets go. There is a third way to live here, and the first evidence of it is in the fog-nets.

Each stake is a single limb bone. It is shorter than a forearm and thick as a wrist, flared at both ends, as heavy in the hand as the stone it is wedged into. Nothing in the forest below carries a bone like that. It is the arm of something that pushes.

After that, the rolls look different. Most are wound the way wind winds, slack, with the outer edge flapping. A few are tight, with the edge tucked under. The slack ones bowl. The tight ones trundle and stop short of things. When the chord slides, a tight roll squats a full breath before the gust arrives, so that the wind which scatters every other mat finds this one already leaning.

The flank, when it shows, is bare skin, brown as the tea-water and always wet, with a fine pebbled grain. Where it has pressed against the mat it keeps the print of the moss for a while, like a face that has slept on a sleeve.

Seen whole, which happens only at the drifts, the animal is as long as a child is tall and much lower: a broad wedge of body on four short limbs, with a tail as thick as its neck. The head is blunt. The eyes are small and sit under a clear lid that never opens. There is no ear. Under the jaw hangs a fold of loose skin that in some ancestor was surely for calling. Here it is laid flat on the stone whenever the animal stops, and through it the animal takes the chord.

Inside the roll it lies along the hollow core and walks the inner wall, the way a person might walk inside a barrel. Treading one way, it brakes. Treading harder, it gains ground, a body-length or two, straight into the wind. Shifting its weight toward one end brings the roll round in a long arc. It is the only thing on forty kilometres of stone that goes where the wind is not going.

Mostly it uses this to stay out of the holes. The shafts give every note back late and low, and that returned note carries farther through rock than through air. A tenanted roll begins its turn three hundred metres out. In thick cloud the sinkholes can be mapped blind by watching where the tight rolls curve away and the slack ones keep straight on.

It eats its house from the inside, cropping the inner turns down to the felt and taking the toads with them. A roll lived in for a week is hollow as a sleeve and so light that the animal's own weight is most of what holds it to the ground. Then it has to reach a tor.

When a southerly sits for a day and the green piles up ten metres deep against the rock, there may be five or six of them out on the heap at once. They move flat to the moss, with the care of things that have no practice at being seen. They do not look at one another. They lay their throats on one sheet and then the next. Sometimes two settle on the same sheet and both go under its edge. When the drift peels, a roll leaves with two bulges in it.

Then there are the stopped flutes that have no clay in them. They are packed with moss alone, rammed in to an arm's depth, tighter than fingers could manage, and they carry no print of any kind. They are on the lee side of tors, and they are the right pipes.

Which came first is not recorded. The people who climb the cairn line may have learned the trick by watching where the tight rolls came to rest. The ring-walls suggest it. Each has a gap on its downwind side that is wider than a person needs. Beside one of the pecked bowls someone has also pecked a spiral, with a short straight stroke leaving it against the turn. If the people have a word for the animal, that is probably it, since a word made of breath would not carry here.

The people themselves are known only by what their hands did. The thumbprints in the clay come in several sizes, some small enough that children were trusted with the work. They sat rather than stood. They burned dried mat, and the hearth-black is greasy with it. They took the bones for their stakes, and nothing on the bones shows a cut. The dead can be had for the gathering at the foot of any drift.

At one shelter the tethered garden is no longer flat. The weights have been shouldered off in a ring. The mat is wound tight around its own braid, which goes in at the open end and has not been chewed. It is the only roll on the plateau that is in the same place two days running. The clay in the nearest plug is soft enough to take a fingernail.

When the chord slides and the quiet goes off across the stone, every loose green thing lifts an edge. This one comes up against its cord, strains once, and settles. At the open end there is a wet brown flank. Below it, pressed into the moss, is a second flank the size of a hand, and then a third.
