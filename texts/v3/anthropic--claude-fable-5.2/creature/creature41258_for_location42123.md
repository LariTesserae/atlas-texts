People, to begin with. There are about eighty of them in eleven households, with eleven jars hung by eleven doors. You could stay a month before you trusted your count, because they are never all in view and they never call out.

That is the first thing you notice once you are down the stair: they add nothing to the noise by mouth. Someone who wants you crosses the floor, takes hold of your forearm, and speaks beside your ear, close enough that you get breath and onion along with the words. All speech happens at that distance. Anything said aloud is therefore private, and they keep it for private things: courtship, grief, correcting a child, what a thing is worth. The rest goes by hand. Hand-talk can be read from the far gallery, which makes it public, and they are blunt in it as they never are by mouth. Insults are made with the hands. So are jokes, and so is the news that the snake is on your wall. The sign for the kestrel is a fist dropped from head height, and the children play a game with it in which everyone must hold still for one breath.

Their soles are like hide by the age of five. Before that the children bleed a little on the sherds and nobody fusses. The women who build pots have one great toe standing away from the others, callused along its edge; that is the turning foot. The men who wedge have heels gone broad and flat. Hands are grey to the wrist, because slip dries in the creases and stays there, water being for the cistern. Their fingertips are smooth, the ridges worn away by clay. Anyone who keeps goats goes into the lowest rooms bent over twice a day, and the old duck at doorways that have plenty of height. They own very little to keep warm in and have never needed it.

For most of the day they have no shadows. A child taken up the stair for the first time finds one attached to its feet on the flat ground and will not walk until it has been shown that everyone has one. Up there sounds arrive one at a time with nothing behind them, and a dog barking a long way off seems to be at the elbow. They stand too close to the people they trade with, who step back, and they step forward again. They sleep badly in the quiet. They go up for clay and thorn and to carry the pots away, and they come back down with a hand in the groove. Their shoulders drop at about the level where the sparrows start.

They do not hear the hum. They hear its absence. On windless days the walls are dumb and the yard goes wrong: pots are dropped, children cry, and somebody strikes a call-jar for no reason and is stared at.

The jar by the door is the mother's. She made it, and the household comes to it. A man answers to his mother's jar until he marries and then to his wife's, and it is said to take a year before his head turns to the right one. When the mother dies the jar is taken down and never struck again. It goes into that year's rim course with the others, on its side, mouth outward, bedded in mud, with no mark on it. Her eldest daughter hangs her own jar, made years before and kept wrapped in a mat, which nobody else has heard. The yard learns the new pitch that evening. The children of the household take a few days.

So the wall has the mothers in it, two or three to a course among the hundreds of plain jars, all the way down to the big-bellied ones with their lips rubbed off. The wind makes no distinction. The chord that tells them where the weather is coming from is partly made of jars that once brought somebody in to eat. Nobody makes much of this. But a woman at the settling pans will lift her head when the wind backs, hold the paddle still, and then go on stirring. And nobody minds what moves in. An empty jar would be the strange thing.

In this they are like everything else in the yard. They live behind the mouths as the bats and bees live in them. They keep hours, too. Mornings they are loud, at noon they are indoors, and at dusk they gather round the lamps with the geckos. They leave the snake alone. On a toad night no one can be called, so everyone stays wherever they were when the rain began, and children sleep in whatever household they happened to be visiting. A fair number of the people in the yard were begun on a toad night and have been told so, by hand, in public.

Watch long enough and one of them comes apart from the rest. The chord has to stay readable. A course of jars set anyhow would shift it, and the sour note from the northwest might come out sweet with the pots still on the floor. So someone has to say where each jar goes. At present that is one woman, and her hand-name is a fingernail flicked off the thumb.

She is about fifty and short. She hears better on the left, the side her mother spoke into. In the weeks before the course she goes round the new jars as they dry, flicks each one, listens, and has them stood along the foot of the wall in an order nobody else can follow. Some she refuses, and those go up the stair to be sold. Then she spends four days on the parapet with the men who lay the course, pointing. She is short-tempered the whole time. From the rim the yard is only a roar, and she cannot pick out her own quern in it. It is the one time in the year she hears the place as a stranger does, and she does not like what a stranger hears.

The mothers' jars she beds herself. This year there is one, belonging to a woman she wedged clay beside for thirty years. She has put it on the south side above the pigeons, because that is where a note like it was wanted. She also thinks the woman would have liked the afternoon sun, but that is not a reason she would give.

Her own mother is nineteen courses down on the east wall, near enough the kiln to have gone dark. A gecko has the jar mostly. The snake goes through it on its rounds, and the sparrows either side go up in alarm, and she does not look round. When the wind is in the south she can hear it, or believes she can. She would not swear to it with her hands.

Her own jar hangs on the second gallery of the west wall. It is a little flat of the kiln household's, so that small children sometimes run to the wrong door. She knows which side of the rim it will go on when the time comes, and has told no one, since whoever sets it will be whoever can hear, and they will either hear it or not. She also knows where her daughter keeps the wrapped jar. She found it under the sleeping mats and flicked it once through the matting. It is sharper than hers. She thinks it is too sharp. She has not said so by mouth or by hand.

When the mortar is dry enough to touch she comes down. By the fourth course the roar has come apart into mallets, querns and goats, and by the bottom she can hear which of her grandchildren is running. Then she waits, as everyone is waiting without showing it, for the first real wind across the new rim. When it comes, the chord is the one they know, with a slight new edge to it. No one looks up and the mallets do not break rhythm. That is what she was listening for. She sits down at her turntable, sets her toe against it, and pushes.
