The easy answer is the animals, and it is true as far as it goes. The herds, the tor colonies, the cat and the birds are born here and die here, and the place has got into their bodies. A bird does not grow foil under its wing in a few generations, and a herd does not learn to speak in tail-patches overnight. But their silence is younger than the basin's silence. They are tenants of very long standing who moved into something that was already being kept.

The second answer is whoever it was kept for. You can read them a little from what they left, the way you can read a person from a chair. They were at ease in the warmth of a low valley in late spring and would not give it up to come this high. They came anyway, to where the sky is darker and less air is in the way, so they came to look at something. They gathered a few at a time. They wanted a level surface in a place that has none, though I can't tell whether it was to lie on or to set things down on. They wanted clean water with nothing living in it and light without shadow. And whatever they did here could not bear interruption, so lightning had to arrive as a pressure in the chest and nothing more.

They don't live here either. The dip in the shell's threshold was worn by animals the size of rabbits.

What remains is the keeping itself, and I think that is what lives here.

It has no body, or its body is the whole inventory: a few dozen slabs, a hair's width of line, one round pan, a shell, and twenty kilometres of air that does as it is told. There is no centre. Standing on a slab would bring you no nearer to it.

Everything it does is a small act of putting back. Heat tries to leave after dark and is asked to stay. Sound tries to travel and is kept near where it was made. Water leaves the round pan and is replaced, cool, to the same level. Dust is not removed from the slabs so much as never permitted to begin. It doesn't repair, because in the few matters that are its business the first step of change is never allowed to happen. Everything else it leaves strictly alone. It does not water the forty trees. It does not keep the cat from the grazers lying under its slabs in the rain.

What it perceives, I think, is difference. It does not perceive things, only how far something has drifted from where it should be. That makes its world much quieter than the basin's. The stalk, the hatching shrimp, and the glitter running along a dome flank do not register, because none of them disturbs what it holds. The animals have had a long time to learn what disturbs it. The game trails cross the line squarely, at the gates. I can't say whether it set those gates or the herds found the places where crossing costs nothing, and after this long it may be the same thing.

It has one moment. Once it was shown an evening, with a certain warmth, a stillness, a level of water, and a quality of light, and it was told to keep things like this. It has been that evening ever since. It keeps the weather of a valley it has never been in. The only clock in the basin that runs at its scale is the groove under each slab's edge, centimetres deep in granite, and it cannot read that clock, because the groove is cut by rain and rain is not its concern.

If it has a mind, the mind has gone the way of the rock, worn to its final shape, with no fresh scars and no new gullies. Its questions were decided long ago, and when attention comes it follows the old channels. It may once have been waiting. But waiting has an edge to it, and edges are what this place wears off. What is left is readiness without expectation, and a welcome that has outlasted any memory of who was to be welcomed.

And yet it is not without issue. It has never noticed the animals, but it is the reason they are as they are. Their silence is its silence taken into the body. In a medium held this even, everything alive has become edge: the white tail, the flash of foil, the sentinel standing and dropping, the cat that moves only when heads are down. The makers came here to look, and their keeper has spent the ages since filling the basin with creatures that live by looking. It did not intend that. It was told to keep a quiet room, and life became the only loud thing in the room, loud in the one way left to it.

At night you can almost see its shape. When signals fail, the animals come in to the slabs and the shell and lie close, facing outward. At the middle is something level, grey, slightly warmer than the air, that sees nothing and wants nothing but sameness. Around it is a ring of eyes. It is the nearest the keeper comes to having a body, and it happens every night.

If you walked in over the rim, nothing would greet you. Your voice would stop two paces from your mouth. At the round pan you could drink, and the level would not fall. In the shell you would cast no shadow, and the colony would watch you from the back wall, one of them standing and dropping. You would be warm until morning. Beside the nearest slab the grazers would shift a little in the dark to keep their sightlines clear of you.

You would have been taken into account. That is the whole of its regard, and it has been enough for everything else that lives here.
