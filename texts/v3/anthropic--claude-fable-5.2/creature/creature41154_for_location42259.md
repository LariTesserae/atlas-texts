What lives here is met first with the face. The insects round the posts are small and soft-bodied, and a dozen caught in a closed fist feel like a pinch of chaff. They do not bite. One will settle at the corner of a mouth or an eye, drink, and lift off again into the orbit. None ever lands on a post. Nothing does. The vines come up to within a finger's width and lie along the sand there, and a tendril that reaches further finds nothing to hold and falls back. The insects hatch from the standing water and the wet sand and live a few days, which is too short for any of them to learn that the posts take turns.

Their young are some of what bumps against the shin. The rest are tadpoles, fat and slow, and a soft-shelled thing as long as a finger, with legs and feelers, that walks over a still foot rather than round it. Nothing in the water avoids a leg. Bumping is how these animals find out what is in front of them, and a shin is treated as one more stem. That holds for most of the chamber. A hand closing round a frog is not expected, and the frog does not jump.

The bottle-knock frog is the smallest, no bigger than the top joint of a thumb. It sits up off the floor, on fern fronds and the broad leaves, and it grips a finger with pads at the ends of its toes. It goes on calling inside a closed hand, and the knock can be felt in the palm. The rasp is the big one. It fills a hand, loose-skinned and lumpy, and it sits in the moss in a bowl pressed to its own shape. Lifted out, it lets its legs hang and keeps rasping. Both have eyes, large under the thumb, which draw down into the head when touched, as a frog's eyes do anywhere. What they are for in here cannot be said. But a frog picked up near a post is nearly always found facing it, and that may be on account of the insects or of something else.

The whistler has never been held. The whistle comes from the next fern over, or from the wall, or from above head height, and it has moved again by the time a hand arrives. It is the one animal in the chamber that gets out of the way. Nobody knows its size, or what taught it.

The crickets live on the walls, behind the vines, in the film of water. Their feelers are longer than their bodies and find a hand before the hand finds them, with a touch like a single hair drawn across the wrist. They step onto fingers and off again. Their call is a thin dry trill, and it does not move. Whichever post is busy, the walls give the same line of sound, and the frog chorus travels round inside it.

The clickers have never been touched in flight. They pass close enough to push air against a face and they do not strike it, a head being one more thing the click finds. What is known of them is what comes down. Their droppings land heavier and warmer than roof water. There are feathers, stiff, as long as a hand. And there are fruit stones, smooth and still slick, the largest the size of a thumbnail. Under the thickest part of the colony the moss is full of them, and the air there smells sweet and sour over the green. Now and then a nestling comes down. It is heavier than it should be, a bag of fat under down, with a wide soft beak and stiff bristles round it. It does not last on the floor. By the next visit there is a little down in the moss and nothing else.

They are the only tenants who leave. Once a day the roof empties in a long volley by their own route, and some hours later it fills again, and those two volleys are all the chamber has to say that night has come to the plateau and gone. Up on the surface at dusk they are suddenly overhead, clicking, out of no opening anyone has found, and then they go over the rim and down toward the forest. What they bring back is fruit. This country has spent a very long time being carried away, and they are the one thing carrying anything up.

The stones fall everywhere. The ones that fall within a ring grow, and this is very likely how the vines and the broad leaves came to be here at all. The ones that fall in the open stretch between posts sprout as well. The foot finds them before the hand does: a stem as thin as string, knee-high, with no leaf worth the name, leaning toward the nearest post. It folds at a touch. Afterwards something soft and capped grows on it, as it does on the old stones and the fallen fronds, and breaks wetly under the fingers. It is the only growth in the chamber that leans toward nothing.

Everything else rooted has to deal with the turn. A fern at a slackening post lets its fronds down over some days until they lie on the moss and rot there. What stays is the crown, hard as a knuckle, with coils on it the size of a fingertip, packed tight. When the post's turn comes round again, the coils loosen a little each day. The vine does it differently. A hand can follow one stem out of a ring, across the open sand, and into the next ring, where it is in full leaf while its other end is bare. Nobody has followed one to an end that was not a growing tip, and it may be that the vine is a single plant lying in all three rings at once.

The frogs walk. In the middle days of a turn the rasps cross the open sand a few lengths at a time, and for that while it is possible to tread on one where the hand usually finds nothing. The small knockers seem to go by way of the vine. Not all of them go. Every slack ring keeps a few that stay and call anyway, with almost nothing to eat.

One post stands nearer the standing water than the other two, a few paces up the sand from its edge. When the turn reaches it, the chorus arrives at the water, and the frogs lay. Under the broad leaves that overhang the sheet there are lumps of stiff froth the size of a fist, crusted on the outside. In the water there are strings of soft beads wound round the stems. Some weeks later the tadpoles are at their thickest against the shin. Then they thin, and the chorus has moved on. That is the nearest thing the chamber has to a year.

No one lives here. There is no hearth and no smell of old char, no worked stone, nothing dropped, no bone bigger than a frog's. The posts are the only sign of anybody, and whoever that was left nothing else. They are also the only things in the chamber that answer a visitor. Two people, one just in from the wind and one an hour in the warm, can lay hands on the same post at the same moment, and each will find it at their own temperature. That is as near to being noticed as anyone comes in here, and it may not be notice at all. A frog set on top of one slides off dry.

It does not add up to much. All the frogs in the chamber would go into a couple of buckets. They keep to their skin on the rock, with the warm empty air in the middle, and go round with the posts. The one that carries up through the sandstone on a still night is the smallest of them, the size of a thumb joint, sitting on a leaf it has never seen and, most likely, facing a post.
