Most of what lives here has been seen already: the herons, the snipe, the willows on their braids, and the eels that climb the broken staircase at the gap as elvers and spend half a century in a river with nothing under it. But listen long enough under the rain that is not falling and there is another sound with a beginning. It is a knock, knuckle on alder, dry and short. After a while it is answered from somewhere else in the carr.

They are hard to see. The mist gives no shadows and no distances, and at first they are taken for other things. A long dark shape goes over in a hanging stream, carried by the current, too large for an eel. Something stands in the sedge under a channel with a heron's stillness, and then puts up an arm.

People is the nearest word. The tallest would come to a stranger's shoulder. Their skin is the brown that peat-water turns whatever lies in it long enough, and their hair has gone the red that hair goes in a bog. Their feet are broad and they set them down flat, without hurry, on sphagnum that would not hold anyone who walked as if the ground were ground. They wear nothing, since nothing here is made, and they are beaded all over like the bark. They are not cold. The air has never been warmer or colder than it is, and a body can come to terms with a temperature that never moves.

How many there are cannot be said. Counted from a hummock there are seven, then five, then nine, and none has come or gone. They do not count themselves. They know who is there and who is not, which is a different knowledge, and it holds.

They may be what is left of whoever made the grains. If so they do not know it, and they can no more pick the made grains from a handful of silt than anyone can. Whatever they were once here to do, what they do now is begin things, and not many.

They do not speak as speech is understood past the rim. Their ancestors did, and all of it is still there. A word said in this valley loses its beginning and keeps the rest, the long open part, standing where it was said. The ground has gone down since. So the old talk hangs overhead in levels like the rivers. The newest of it is a little above a raised hand, a murmur of worn vowels from a language whose consonants are gone. Above that the talk is older and fainter, up toward the snail-track of the first river, where the first of them stood on the first floor and said whatever they said. North of the fen, high up, two voices are still raised at each other. Below the lowest level there are no words in the air at all. At some point they stopped.

What they use instead is all beginning: a click of the tongue, a pop of the lips, a knuckle on wood, the flat of a hand on water. Each is over as it starts, with nothing after it for the valley to hold. It is a small, plain language of here, come, eel, no, mine, look up. It is the only thing done in the valley that leaves nothing behind.

Their hearing has gone the same way. The children hear everything. By the time they are grown they have let go of whatever does not begin, as anyone lets go of the sound of their own blood. They no longer hear the standing roar on the north side, the rain, the ground tone of old thunder or the frogs. To a grown one of them the valley is nearly silent, and a heron's croak or a knock carries across it clean. Real rain, one day in three, is the loudest thing they know, every drop a start. A stranger raising his voice over the falls is, to them, simply shouting.

They eat as the herons do. A hanging stream is a shelf. They put a hand up through the underside of it, to the wrist or the elbow, and bring down cress or brooklime or an eel gripped behind the head. Nobody knows which learned it from the other. There is no season for anything. Catkin and bud are on the branch together, and cranberries ripen on the sphagnum beside their own flowers. So there is no harvest and no lean month, and they keep no store. Keeping is done for them.

They go about by water where they can. They climb a willow's roots to its river, lie down in it and are carried toward the gap with their faces to the cloud. They climb down by another willow before the silt ends. The children do it for pleasure and are watched, because at the gap the rivers lose their beds. Coming back, they walk. They know the old ground by the leg. Where flat peat turns suddenly uphill they lean into it and rest at the top, and the children run down the far side of a hill that is not there, faster than flat ground allows. They live in the middle, where there is more ground than the valley has room for, and a family can be a long walk from another that is in plain sight.

Where others would have a fire they have a blue pool. They sit round the hard sky of some other day and it lights them from underneath. These are the only shadows in the valley, thrown upward onto the mist, and the children make shapes there with their hands. The pools hold more than sky. The face that looks up may be the one leaning over, or one that leaned there on another day. Their dead are in the peat, unmarked, kept as peat keeps anything, but it is in the pools that they are met: a grandmother, younger than anyone alive ever saw her, drinking. Now and then a pool shows a child nobody has borne yet. They click to it: here.

There is a willow in the carr, rooted in a stream at head height, with a hollow in its braid where the infants are laid. Someone sang there once, before the stopping, and the song is there still, one tune, held, without its start. It was sung at the hollow. The tree's level has been thinning ever since and the tree has been let down a hand's width a century, but the song has not come with it. It stands now an arm's reach above the child. Around and beneath it stands the crying of every infant laid there, the oldest worn thin as a gnat. A stranger would think the tree was full, but it usually holds one, or none. The infants hear the song. Every one of the grown heard it once, and none of them can hear it now.

They sleep in the fen, in the winding notch of clear air where the rain does not land. It is the one dry place they have. The reeds lean away on both sides and they lie along it head to foot, in the bed of a creek that has not been cut. Above them on the alders is the bleached line. The tallest of them, standing, cannot reach it. Under the rain is the surf. The children hear it and are not told what it is, because there is no click for it, and by the time they are grown they hear it no longer. It has no beginning yet. When it has one they will hear it, all of them at once. It will be the first sound in their lives that starts and does not stop.

In the sedge, a little way from the heron, one of them stands under the same stream with an arm half raised. Somewhere in the carr a knuckle strikes alder twice. The sound begins and is over, and nothing of it stays.
