Strictly counted, almost nothing lives here. There is a thread of green along the south wall at the waterline, one fern with three fronds under the spout, and two grass seeds that have split in the silt at the drain. They are lodgers, and they arrived by accident on spray and wind.

But the place is plainly occupied. Something breathes out over the flags at dawn, coughs in the pipe, and gulps at the drain. Something holds the level to a finger's width all night with no one watching.

At first it seems very young, about the age of the crust on the lip. It is loud the way a new thing is loud, with no idea there is anyone to be quiet for. It has no shadow and nowhere to keep a secret. It even tastes of its house, the way a child smells of the room it sleeps in.

That is wrong, though. The water was warm before there was a pipe to carry it. What lives here is old and has only recently been brought indoors. For longer than the quarry has been open it lived on the hillside as a seep in the grass, a wet place that steamed a little on cold mornings. It never had walls, so it never had a voice. Water running out into turf makes almost no sound. The roar is not shouting. It is an old tenant in the first room it has ever had, and the room answers back.

It stands with its head in the heat and its feet in the cold. The thin cool sheet on the floor is the one part of it that still touches the ground it came from. The net of bright lines that crawls over it by day looks like nerves, but that is borrowed from the sun. At night it is only a voice and a temperature.

It does not wait, though the tank was clearly built for someone. It is never the same water twice. What stays is a set of habits: the level, the slow turn along the left wall and back along the right, the stutter and recovery when the pipe swallows air. It keeps nothing. The yellow dust goes to the notch each afternoon and is taken down in rags.

The leaf is the exception. It goes round, pauses at the notch, and is carried round again. The tenant has no mind to make up, but the leaf is the nearest thing it has to a thought. It is one small dry idea, turned over and over and not yet let go.

It has already started on the house, without hurry or malice. The eggshell crust on the lip is its own handwriting, the same work it did on the hillside, laying down stone a skin at a time. Given years, it will round the spout into a tongue and soften the chisel rows. It will coat the sunk cord until the cord is a ridge in the ninth step. Meanwhile it loosens the lime from the new plaster and carries it off, undoing the mason's work a taste at a time.

The palm print by the sixth step sits close enough to the surface that the slap from the spout keeps it wet. All day the water touches the hand of the person who built its room. It does not know this. It doesn't know about the fern either, or that birds will eventually find the hole.

It knows how to arrive at the same rate, warm, in the dark, with the shadow gone up the steps. For now it is the only one home.
