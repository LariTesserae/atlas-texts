You come up through cloud. The air thins. The heat rises to meet you — not the heat of sun on stone but something wetter, deeper, the warmth of a body. You are above any tree line that ever existed. There are no trees. There is no line.

The plateau opens below you like a shallow bowl, thirty kilometers across, perhaps more, rimmed by ridges that were grown rather than eroded. The rim is serrated — not with peaks but with spires: narrow, tapering, calcified, the color of bone and rust and dried blood. They are still growing. You can hear them from a distance, a slow continuous creaking, like a ship under sail, but the ship is the mountain and the wind is the mountain's own metabolism.

The ground is not ground. It is a mat — a continuous, interlocked substrate of plant tissue, living and dead and mineralized, extending from rim to rim without break. There is no soil beneath it, no bedrock, no natural substrate of any kind. The mat *is* the world here. It is saturated. Water moves through it constantly, upward and sideways, drawn by capillary action and geothermal heat, and it weeps from every surface. The mat is not flat; it is a terrain of low swells and shallow depressions, of ridges and runnels, all made of the same fibrous, sponge-like material. In places it is soft underfoot, yielding like a bog. In places it has calcified into something harder than concrete, sharp-edged, fresh-fractured, the fracture surfaces still damp and glistening.

And rising from the mat: structures. Not thickly scattered — the density is low, the spaces between them wide and breathing — but present. Everywhere. Towers. Arches. Walls. Vaults. Chambers. Spires. They are grown, not built: extruded from the mat by the same capillary forces that feed the ground, mineralized as they rise, then colonized by living tissue that continues to shape them long after the initial extrusion has stopped. The newest growth is pale, almost translucent, soft enough to dent with a fingernail. The oldest is dark, stained, heavy with mineral deposits, grooved and fluted by centuries of water flow into patterns that look almost deliberate — fluting, ribbing, the suggestion of columns and capitals, though no hand has ever touched them.

The towers are hollow. They are always hollow. They rise twenty, thirty, fifty meters, then open at the top into cups or funnels that collect water and channel it downward through internal conduits. You can hear the water inside them — a constant rushing, gurgling, the sound of a plumbing system that is also a body. Some towers have cracked and split, and their interiors are exposed: layered walls, like the rings of a tree but vertical, each layer a different mineral composition, a different color, a different epoch of growth. Water sprays from the cracks in fine mist that catches the light and makes rainbows that hang in the air for minutes before the wind tears them apart. The mist settles on your skin. It is warm. It tastes of iron and something sweeter, something almost like sap.

The arches span between towers, or between nothing — they begin at one point in the mat and end at another, with no apparent structural logic, and yet they hold. They are grown under tension, like a bridge that pulls itself into shape from both ends simultaneously. Some are thin as a finger. Some are broad enough to walk across, their surfaces ridged and grooved by the same water that feeds them, their undersides hung with mineral stalactites that are still growing, still damp, still warm to the touch. They creak in the thermal wind. They are always on the verge of failure, and sometimes they fail — a crack like a gunshot, a slow buckling, a collapse that sends a cloud of mineral dust and living fibers into the air. The dust settles. The mat absorbs it. New growth begins before the wreckage has finished falling.

The chambers are the strangest. They are enclosed spaces, or nearly enclosed — vaults, domes, tunnels — grown from the mat and roofed over. Some are small, a meter across, their interiors smooth and white like the inside of a shell. Some are vast, their ceilings lost in mist and shadow, their floors covered in standing water that reflects the pale light from the entrance and makes the whole space feel like the inside of an eye. The geometry is wrong. The chambers are larger inside than the openings that lead to them. The tunnels curve in ways that don't make sense, looping back on themselves, or ending in walls that are clearly unfinished — rough, fibrous, still growing, the living tissue pulsing slowly with the movement of water through it. The air inside is hotter, wetter, thick with spores or pollen or something like it — a golden haze that coats the throat and makes you cough. The walls secrete. They weep. They breathe — slowly, visibly, expanding and contracting by millimeters, the whole structure alive and working, and you realize that the chamber is not a room but an organ, and you are standing inside it.

The heat comes from below. Geothermal, or something like it. The mat is warm to the touch, hot in places, and steam rises from vents and fissures across the plateau — not constantly, but in bursts, in gusts, as if the ground is exhaling. The steam condenses on the cooler surfaces of the towers and arches, runs down, is absorbed by the mat, drawn downward, heated again, rises again. The water cycle is closed, local, continuous. It rains here — not from clouds, but from the structures themselves, from condensation on cold mineral surfaces, from the exhalations of the chambers. The rain is warm. It falls in sheets, in curtains, in sudden localized downpours that appear and disappear as the wind shifts, so that you can stand in dry sunlight and watch a column of rain fall fifty meters away, hammering the mat, filling a depression that overflows and sends a new runnel racing across the surface. The sound is constant: dripping, rushing, spraying, the hiss of steam, the creak of growth, the crack of failure.

There are no animals. Nothing flies. Nothing crawls. Nothing swims in the standing water, which is cloudy with mineral sediment and plant exudate and utterly still except where steam bubbles up through it in slow, fat, lazy bursts. No insects drift through the golden haze of the chambers. No birds perch on the spires. The ecosystem is plants — or one plant, or something that occupies the ecological space of plants — and nothing else. The mat is a single organism, or a colony, or a consortium: layers of photosynthetic tissue, layers of mineralizing tissue, layers of water-conducting tissue, all interlocked, all communicating chemically through the water that moves through them. The structures are organs. The towers are lungs. The arches are limbs. The chambers are — what? Stomachs? Wombs? Minds? The place is a body building itself a body, and it does not know why.

The place is young. The erosion is minimal. The features are sharp, fresh, recently emerged. This caldera was not here a thousand years ago — perhaps not five hundred. The mat grew across it, the structures rose, the water cycle established itself. And it is still growing. The dynamic is turbulent: structures rise and fall, the mat advances and retreats, steam vents open and seal, chambers form and collapse. A tower that was intact an hour ago is now a split, oozing ruin, and a new spire has begun to rise from the mat where nothing was before — a pale, soft, glistening extrusion that hardens even as you watch, that begins to creak and sway within minutes of emerging. The place is in a state of continuous, chaotic creation and destruction — not cyclical, not rhythmic, but storm-like, hyperactive, building and unbuilding itself faster than it can be mapped. You could draw a map in the morning and by afternoon it would be fiction.

The scale is regional. You can see across the plateau to the far rim, a hazy line of spires. Between you and the rim: the scattered towers, the arches, the chambers, the open mat, the standing water, the steam. The emptiness between structures is as important as the structures themselves. You can walk for an hour between towers and see nothing but the mat and the low swells and the water and the slow creep of new growth across the surface. Then a chamber opens in the ground ahead of you, or a cluster of spires rises from what was empty an hour ago, or an arch spans a gap you didn't know was there, its surface already grooved with water channels, already dripping, already alive. The place is sparse and total at once: total in its coverage, sparse in its presences. It is a landscape that is mostly space, and the space is alive.

The light is normal — high-altitude sun, thin air, sharp shadows — but filtered through mist and steam into something diffuse, something that seems to come from everywhere and nowhere. The colors are mineral: bone-white, rust-red, ochre, sulfur-yellow, the pale green of new growth, the dark brown of old. The mat is the color of a bruise — purple, yellow, green — where it is actively growing, and gray-white where it has calcified. The standing water is the color of weak tea, of urine, of something that has been used and is being used again. The chambers glow from within, lit by the reflection of their own wet walls, a pale golden light that has no source.

The sound is loud. Not a single loudness but a texture: the rush of water in the towers, the drip and splash of condensation, the hiss of steam, the creak and crack of growth and failure, the wind moving through the arches and chambers like breath through a throat. Sometimes a tower collapses and the sound is enormous — a crack, a roar, a long grinding fall — and then a silence that is almost shocking, and then the sound of water already running over the wreckage, and then the creak of new growth, and then the next collapse, somewhere else, and the next, and the next, until the place is a symphony of falling and rising and the water never stops.

This is a place that is making itself. It does not know what it is making. There is no intention, no design, no animal to inhabit it, no tool to shape it, no device to measure it. Only growth, and water, and heat, and the slow, turbulent, patient violence of life building itself a body it will never use — a city with no citizens, a mind with no thoughts, a architecture with no architect, growing and falling and growing again on a high plateau where the air is thin and the rain is warm and the ground is alive and there is nothing else, nothing else, nothing else at all.
