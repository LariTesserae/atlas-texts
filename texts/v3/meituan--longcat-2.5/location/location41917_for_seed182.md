# Slake

The mountain split open within living memory — a thermal event, a venting, a collapse that opened a single chamber beneath the ridge, cathedral-sized, its ceiling vaulted and its floor broken open. The heat came up first. The water followed. And people came after, as they do, because heat and water and raw stone are a kind of wealth. They named the settlement Slake, for the warm river that slakes thirst, and for the old word for quenching stone.

The town fills the cavern the way water fills a cup. It is packed: every ledge built on, every wall climbed by stairs and ladders, bridges strung between formations, platforms hanging from the ceiling on chains and iron rods socketed into fresh rock. There is no open ground. The lowest level is the river. The highest is the shaft — a wound in the ceiling where daylight arrives thin and blue, and where the town's scaffold-towers lean toward it like plants toward a lamp.

The cavern is young. Nothing here is rounded, nothing weathered. The walls are fresh fracture surfaces, glassy and conchoidal, still flecked with the stress marks of their making. Stalactites hang like candle wax but are hard and edged; a fall means a cut. The floor is broken basalt and obsidian, sharp as teeth. The river descends through a series of sinter terraces — pools stepped like a temple's bathing ghats, young and sharp-rimmed, growing visibly over a lifetime. The water is warm, opalescent with dissolved silica, and it deposits its load wherever it touches: crusting pipes, rimming pools, glazing the walls in bands of cream, rust, and old blood.

The air is hot — thirty-eight, forty-two degrees near the works — and it breathes. The vents run on a long cycle, days long. They exhale, and the whole cavern steams; condensation blooms on every surface; the walls weep in slow, branching maps; the mosses unfurl. Then the vents draw back and the air clears and dries, relatively, and metal ticks as it cools and the lamps seem suddenly brighter. The damp is intermittent but total when it comes. Nothing stays dry. Cloth, paper, wood all carry a film of moisture. Metal rusts unless kept warm. On the old walls, saltpeter and silica bloom in patterns that the town reads a little, like weather. The smells are wet stone, sulfur, hot iron, vegetal sweetness from the gardens, and — faintly, near the glow-moss panels — ozone.

Where the warm water goes, life piles up. The terraces are gardens of ferns with fronds like green hands, some a meter across; mosses in deep green, grey, black, and the cultivated strains, pale phosphorescent blue-green; fungi in shelves, cups, and strange branching forms — some eaten, some rendered into dyes and resins, some used in the works to settle metal from solution. Vines thread through railings and their roots find purchase in the sinter and split it slowly, patient as water. On the drier upper ledges there is nothing: bare, sharp rock. The boundary is exact — the line of the seep, drawn like a surveyor's mark around the town.

The town itself is built from what the cavern gave and what could be lowered through the shaft. Basalt blocks, sinter slabs sawn from the terraces, scrap metal in bundles, wood, chain, canvas. Everything is bolted, lashed, or socketed. In the oldest districts the construction has begun to fuse with the stone: sinter has crept up the walls, around the pipes, through the floor grates, and the boundary between built and grown is gone — a wall is a formation is a wall. The works cluster at the hot end, where the vents are: boilers, pumps, condensers, a glassworks that works the obsidian, dye vats, a mill. The town lives in the gradient behind them — warmer near the works, cooler toward the shaft — and pipes run everywhere, along walls, across bridges, down into the river, carrying steam, warm water, condensate. Everything is crusted on a schedule. The town's labor is as much against the stone as with it; gangs go through with chisels and brushes, and the stone grows it all back.

It is mostly quiet. The river's low voice. The tick and ping of cooling metal. The drip of condensation, irregular, everywhere. The pumps — deep, slow, a heartbeat heard through a wall. Voices low; people speak softly here, partly because shouting is pointless in the warm dense air, partly custom, partly something older. No birds. No insects singing. The stone absorbs sound, and the moss absorbs sound. The founders believed that loud noise wakes the stone, and whether or not that is true, the habit persists.

It is dim. At midday the shaft sends down a pale blade of daylight that moves slowly across the floor and serves as the town's clock. Furnace mouths give amber and rust-red. Gas-jets line the main ways. Jars and panels of cultivated glow-moss give green, and are planted in patterns that some people say are only decorative and some people read, quietly, like the mineral blooms. Shadows are thick. The far end of the cavern — the deep works, the hot wall — is a red-dark glow that never fully goes out.

Fauna is rare. Blind, pale fish in the river, small as fingers. A white crab in the hottest pools, slow and untroubled. Moths the size of thumbs, pale, that live in the glow-moss and are not hunted, by custom. Something like a salamander in the seeps, rarely seen. Nothing larger. Protein comes from farmed fungi, river fish, and what comes down the shaft.

The rules of the place are simple and were learned the hard way. The cavern is still forming. The sinter grows — around anything warm and wet. A tool left in a seep is stone-encased in a month; a basket in a season; a boot, left by a sleeping drunk against a warm wall, is a lesson the town keeps on a shelf. Do not sleep in the warm damp. The stone wants what is warm. The vents breathe, and the town plans by the breath: the drying days for work in the hot end, the steaming days for the gardens and the slow tasks. The heat moves slowly through the rock, and the town follows it, opening new works, abandoning old ones to the warm dark. The rock is always sharp — tools wear, boots wear, but the stone never rounds; every generation breaks itself a little on the same edges its grandparents did. Time here is patient. The founders are still alive. The terraces grow centimeters in a year. The great works took decades. Nothing is urgent.

People say the cavern dreamed the town into its warm wet places, and the town is dreaming the cavern into shape in return — piping the heat, planting the moss, feeding the terraces with their effluent, carving and being carved, chipping the stone back and watching it grow. It is strange. It is home. The children of Slake do not fear the dark. They fear the sharp edges, and the warm seeps, and they learn early to move softly, and to listen for the vents, and to keep nothing warm and living too close to the wet stone.
