The plain is vast and dark and new. It stretches to the horizon in every direction — a low, endorheic basin of fresh basalt, recently poured, still cooling, still settling. The scale is continental. You could walk for months and see nothing but the same black stone, the same heat shimmer, the same pale sky, the same white threads of steam rising from the cracks. The basin is ringed in the distance by the rims of older terrain — escarpments, volcanic cones, the hazy silhouettes of highlands — but they are far, and the plain itself is the world. The elevation is low, barely above the planet's datum, and the lowland traps the heat. The air is hot and heavy and still, a warm, mineral-scented stillness that presses down like a hand.

The world is young. Geologically, it is an infant — the surface is still in its construction phase, everything made, everything raw, nothing yet worn smooth. There is no soil. There is no sediment. There is no erosion history. The rock is igneous, freshly extruded, and it has not yet been altered into anything else. The plain is all construction, no destruction — a world being built, not a world being worn down. The rock is black and blue-black and brown-black, streaked with rust and sulfur and the white of salt deposits. The surfaces are glassy or dull, sharp or jagged, but they are all new. The edges are knife-sharp. The world looks like a foundation that has just been poured, like a slab that has just set, like a construction site before the building rises. Everything is made, and it shows.

The heat is the first thing you feel. It rises from the rock — a steady, radiant warmth, hot enough to be uncomfortable through boots, hot enough that you would not want to kneel, hot enough that the stone radiates a visible shimmer in the middle of the day. The air is hot and dry, with a faint sulfurous edge, the smell of struck matches and hot metal and volcanic gas. The sun is bright and white, the sky pale blue and milky with aerosols and water vapor, the horizon a haze of heat and dust and distant steam. The light is harsh on the dark rock — the contrast is extreme, the shadows in the cracks are black and depthless, the glassy surfaces flash and glare like broken mirrors. The world is all dark stone and pale sky, a two-tone world, a charcoal drawing with a white horizon.

The plain is not flat. It is a chaos of fresh lava forms, a taxonomy of flow and fracture. Pressure ridges rise and fall like frozen waves, their crests sharp, their flanks glassy. Lava toes bulge and split, ropy and billowy, the surface iridescent with a faint rainbow sheen, like the skin of a soap bubble stretched over stone. Flow channels wind between levees of cooled rock, their floors smooth and undulating, their walls steep and sharp. Collapsed lava tubes leave long, sinuous trenches, their roofs fallen in, their interiors dark and warm, their edges jagged. Tumuli bulge upward where the pressure of the still-fluid interior pushed against the crust, domes of stone with radiating cracks. Rafted blocks of solidified surface ride on later flows, displaced, tilted, stranded — islands of older rock on a sea of newer. The flows are ropey and billowy where they moved slowly — pahoehoe, slick and glassy, undulating like a frozen sea. They are jagged and blocky where they broke and crumbled — aa, a sea of sharp, angular clinker that crunches and shifts underfoot, that catches at boots and resists passage, that glitters with a million glassy points. The rock is all fresh. No weathering rind. No dust. No rounding. The world is all sharp edges and fresh surfaces and the glare of new stone.

The cracks are everywhere. As the rock cools and contracts, it splits into polygonal patterns — columns, prisms, irregular polygons, a geometry of shrinkage that covers the plain like a net. The cracks are narrow and deep, dark and cool, and in many of them steam rises — thin white threads that drift and dissipate in the hot air, the breath of the still-warm interior. In some places the cracks have widened into fissures, and from them comes a low, steady hiss, and the air shimmers with heat, and the smell of sulfur is stronger. In other places the cracks are filled with a thin, white mineral deposit — salts and sulfates left by evaporating water, a faint tracery of white on the dark stone, like frost, like mold, like the veins of a leaf. In a few places the cracks are wide enough to descend into, and from their depths comes a warm, damp breath, and the sound of dripping — rare, slow, deliberate, a water clock measuring the planet's time.

The water comes as rain. Not often — the sky is mostly clear, the plain mostly dry — but when it comes it comes in sheets. The clouds build in the afternoon, white cumulus towers that darken at the base, that flatten at the top into anvils, that spread and merge into a roof of gray. Then the rain falls — a sudden, heavy downpour, warm rain that has fallen through hot air, that sputters and steams as it hits the ground, that raises great hissing clouds of steam from the hot rock. The rain drums on the stone, hisses on the glassy surfaces, roars in the fissures, sputters in the pools. The water does not soak in; the rock is too fresh, too impermeable, too hot. It runs off in sheets, gathering in the low places: the collapsed lava tubes, the flow channels, the depressions, the cracks. The pools form quickly — dark and still and warm, reflecting the sky and the steam and the white clouds and the dark stone. They are shallow or deep, clear or murky, and around their edges the rock is stained with minerals — white, yellow, orange, green, a chemical rainbow. The rain lasts an hour, maybe two, and then it stops, and the sky clears, and the heat returns, and the steam rises, and the pools begin to shrink.

The pools are the centers of life. In them, microbial mats grow — thin, dark films of cyanobacteria and algae, green and black and rust-colored, spreading slowly across the wet rock. The mats are the first colonists, the pioneers, the only visible life. They are thin and fragile, the color of the rock barely changed, a faint darkening, a faint sheen, a faint velvet. In the damp cracks, around the pool edges, there are patches of a simple plant — a moss, a liverwort — low, dark green, cushiony, barely rising above the stone. The life is sparse and patchy: a pool with a mat, then miles of bare rock, then a crack with a patch of moss, then nothing. The life is scattered, low-saturation, balanced with emptiness. There are no animals. No insects, no birds, no movement but the wind and the steam and the rain. The world is quiet, and the life is quiet, and the silence is profound.

The water does not last. The heat and the dry air evaporate it quickly, leaving the pools shrunken and ringed with mineral crusts — white salt, yellow sulfur, orange iron, green copper, powdery and crystalline and efflorescent, like frost, like mold, like a chemical garden. The pools are temporary, ephemeral, appearing after rain and disappearing in the heat. The water that remains sinks into the cracks, following them down into the warm dark, and is gone. The cycle is active: rain, pool, evaporation, condensation, rain again. The world has a water cycle, but it is sparse, intermittent, a dampness rather than an abundance. The water is a visitor, a guest, a brief presence on the stone.

At night — if there is a night, if the planet turns and the sun falls — the rock cools, and the water vapor in the air condenses on the stone. The plain sweats. The rock surfaces become damp, glistening, and the mosses and mats swell with moisture, darkening, brightening, drinking. In the morning the sun rises and the rock heats, and the moisture rises as steam — thin, drifting, ghostly — and the cycle begins again. The condensation is the world's gentle water, the rain its dramatic water. The water is always moving, always cycling, always present in its absence.

The silence is profound. There is no wind in trees, no birdsong, no insect hum, no running water. The plain is silent except for its own small sounds: the sharp crack of rock contracting, like a distant gunshot, as the stone cools and splits; the hiss of steam from a fissure, a low white noise; the spatter and drum and roar of rain; the low, thin moan of wind over the stone; the crunch of clinker underfoot; the rare, slow drip of water in a crack. The sounds are rare and small against the vastness. The silence is a presence, a weight, the quiet of a world that has not yet filled itself with noise. You can hear your own heartbeat. You can hear the blood in your ears. The world is that quiet.

The processes are active. The rock is still cooling, still contracting, still cracking — the plain grows new cracks every day, the sound of its settling a faint, sporadic percussion. The water is still dissolving the fresh basalt, still precipitating new minerals, still making the first clay, the first soil, in the cracks and around the pools. The chemical weathering is slow but real: the water reacts with the minerals, alters them, turns the fresh rock into something else, something older, something that will one day be soil. The microbes are still spreading, still photosynthesizing, still building their thin mats, still making the first organic matter, still adding their faint chemistry to the stone. The atmosphere is still convecting, still building clouds, still raining, still cycling. The basin is a heat engine, a convection cell the size of a continent: the hot rock heats the air, the air rises, the air from the highlands flows in to replace it, the rising air cools and condenses and rains, and the cycle continues. The plain has its own weather, its own climate, its own wind. The world is in motion, but slowly, geologically, the pace of cooling rock and spreading cells and falling rain. The dynamic is a slow, hot, damp dynamic — the dynamic of a world in its first chapter, a world still deciding what it will become.

The erosion has begun. The first cracks are the first erosion. The first pools are the first erosion. The first dust, lifted by wind, is the first erosion. The world is starting to wear itself down, starting to break itself into soil, starting to make the substrate that will one day hold more life. But it is only beginning. The world is mostly still raw, still fresh, still made. The erosion is emerging, embryonic, a process just coming into being. The world is pre-formation, under construction, just-coming-into-being. It is not yet a world of rivers and valleys and rounded hills. It is a world of stone and steam and rain and the first thin films of life. It is a world of sharp edges and fresh surfaces and heat and silence.

You stand on the plain and you feel the heat rising through your boots. You look out and you see the dark stone to the horizon, the haze, the pale sky, the distant cones, the white steam threads, the dark pools, the faint green of the mats. You hear the silence. You smell the sulfur and the hot rock and the faint, clean smell of rain on stone — petrichor, the smell of dust and water and released oils. You are on a world that is still being built, still cooling, still deciding what it will become. It is vast and hot and quiet and damp and sparse and raw and new. It is a world of stone and steam and rain and the first thin films of life. It is a world under construction. And it is beautiful in its rawness, in its newness, in its silence, in its heat, in its patience. It is a world that has not yet been worn smooth. It is a world that is still making itself.
