# The High Seam

The plateau is young. You feel it in the edges — every ridge is still a knife, every gully a fresh cut, and the stone under your boot has never learned to be round. Some few thousand years ago the land buckled along a great fault and rose, fast, two and a half kilometers into the air, carrying its groundwater up with it. The water has been leaking out of the cuts ever since. The place is wet because it is wounded, and it is warm because the wound is still warm. Stand anywhere on the Seam at noon and the rock through your soles is gently hot, like an animal's skin.

Basalt columns stand in organ-pipe ranks along the escarpments, fresh-fractured, their joints still sharp enough to split a Hide. Obsidian seams run glassy between them, and the people cut their blades from these seams — black glass edges that hold finer than any metal. In the saddles between ridges, pumice fields lie pale as bonemeal, and your footsteps crunch there loudly, the one place sound is allowed to live. The only way up from the lowlands is the Throat: a raw stair of faulted rock on the southern rim, a wound within the wound. Almost no one comes up it. Almost no one goes down.

## The Weeping

It does not rain on the Seam. It has not rained in living memory; the oldest ledgers, the oldest voices, all say the same. The water comes instead from the rock itself and from the air.

Every night the plateau's stored heat meets the thin cold sky, and moisture condenses out of the darkness onto every surface — stone, moss, the reed roofs of the town. The gutters drink it. All over the Seam the rock weeps: long faces of basalt run with a fine perpetual sweat that beads and threads and drips into carved runnels, and these runnels feed stone channels, and the channels feed the cisterns. The people call this the night harvest, and it is measured as carefully as any crop.

By day the mist burns off and the sky goes hard and bright, thin-air sunlight drawing crisp shadows on black stone. By evening the warm pools give their breath back, and the mist rises again. The crater lake they call the Still has no outlet and no inlet anyone can find; it simply sits in its bowl of young rock, mirror-calm, warm at the edges, fed by springs that rise through its floor. A marked stone post stands in the shallows — the Tongue — and its notches have not moved in generations. Below the lake, water seeps out through a chain of cream-and-rust sinter terraces, each step a shallow warm pool that holds a piece of the sky, and gathers at last into the marshes of the Low Weep: a broad flat of rushes and sedges and standing water, loud with — nothing. Just water, and wind, and the soft collapse of banks.

## Green and Empty

The green is patchy, and the patches are lavish. Wherever water moves, the moss grows in black-green and rust and ochre, thick as a mattress, springing back after your foot has left it. Gully mouths are stuffed with ferns. The marsh is a wall of rush and bulrush and sedge. On the drier flats, cushion knotwort humps itself into dense pads crowned with tiny ochre flowers — the only flowers, and the only real food crop.

Between these corridors: bare rock. Sharp talus, pumice, glass seams, nothing. The green sits in the wet lines of the world like moss in the veins of a leaf, and everywhere else is stone. The impression from a ridge at noon is of a vast black plate stamped with scattered green script.

And there are no animals. No birds nest on the Seam; a few pass high overhead in their season, dark specks crossing the sun, but they never land, because there is nothing here to eat. No insects of any number — a few pale springtails in the hot spray of the fumaroles, nothing more; no bees, no flies, no humming at the knotwort. The Still holds no fish; its water is too warm, too mineral, and its shallows only carry scums of algae in orange and cream and jade, which the people scrape and press into cakes. The loudest living sound on the whole plateau is water. The children grow up knowing "the furry ones" and "the feathered ones" only from old stories, the way children elsewhere know dragons. The world here is young, the Keepers say, and we were the first to find it.

## Semm

The town sits above the Low Weep where the channels converge, terraced into the slope in low, dense bands of angular basalt, dry-fitted without mortar, the joints so tight you cannot slide an obsidian blade between them. Streets are channels: every lane carries a stone-lined runlet of clear, shallow, fast water, and the town's sound is their sound, a continuous soft trickling below the threshold of speech.

There is almost no timber. What exists is ancient, carried up the Throat at the founding, dark with handling, and it lives only in the Level House — beams, a stair, the great gauge frame. Everything else is stone, fired clay, reed, bone. Sluices are stone slabs riding in sinter-polished grooves, opened by levers of bone and horn. The people cook without fire: the steam pits, stone-lined shafts dug toward the warm seams, bubble day and night, and food — knotwort tubers, fern shoots, moss-flour loaves, rush-seed mash — is wrapped in leaves and lowered in clay pots to steam. No chimney has ever smoked in Semm. The skyline is only roofs, reed-and-clay, the color of the rock.

Three pieces of copper exist in the town, cold-hammered, kept in the Level House. One is a bowl. One is a gauge plate for the master sluice. The third no one will say much about.

## The Keeping

Everything in Semm serves the levels. The water budget of the Seam is a closed account: what weeps from the rock and condenses out of the mist equals what drains to the marsh and what rises off the Still. The Keepers maintain it the way other towns maintain walls — as the first and final purpose of the place. Each district's runlet is tuned by its sluice to a particular note; the whole town is a chord held below hearing, and the Keepers walk the rounds at dusk listening, a hand in the water, adjusting a gate by a finger's width. The population too is held — births are few, planned, spoken of as "giving water." The number of Semm has been, for as long as the ledgers run, four hundred and some.

Nothing changes. When a wall falls it is rebuilt to the same line; when a channel silts it is cleared to the same depth; the ledgers of this generation and the oldest ledgers record the same figures, the same spring rates, the same hours of mist. Change is the one heresy, the one thing the water has no place for. The old saying goes: *We do not build new walls. We hold the levels, and the levels hold us.* Loud noise is the second heresy, half practical, half belief — sound, the Keepers say, is the one thing the levels cannot hold. So the people speak low and walk softly, and when a sluice gate clacks it carries across the whole town like a door closing, and conversations stop until it fades.

Once in a generation, perhaps, someone climbs the Throat. The town receives them in silence, gives them water, watches. Some stay a season and go back down, breathless and chilled. A few stay, and learn the levels, and are absorbed as quietly as rain — though it does not rain.

At dusk the mist comes in off the Still and the warm pools, pearl-thick, swallowing the rooftops. The channels run on, untended for the moment, their voices rising slightly as the night harvest begins — the rock's fine sweat beading, threading, dripping into the runnels. No birds call. No insects answer. The Tongue in the Still stands at its old notch. The levels hold.
