**The Weeping Bowl**

From the last pass, on the clear cold mornings, you see it before you understand it: a ball of light resting on the plateau, as if the sky had dropped a piece of itself and not yet noticed. It is fog held in a bowl of rock, lit through by a sun that seems closer than it should. The air at this height is thin and cold and tastes of stone. You climb down toward the light. The light does not move, and the silence grows.

The bowl is perhaps three hundred paces across, ringed by cliffs of banded sinter thirty or forty feet high. No stream runs in and no stream, properly speaking, runs out — the water is all below, pressing up. Hot springs break out everywhere: at the base of the cliffs, in the walls of the town itself, under the floor of the central pool. It never rains here. The sky is a thin, empty, hard blue. Every drop of water in the bowl comes from the ground or from the fog, and the fog is harvested like a crop.

The cliffs weep. Thin sheets of water descend the banded faces, feeding thousands of threads and rills that braid down to the terraces. On the rim, reed nets are strung between stone posts — the town's true fields — and the fog is combed out of the air and drips, drips, drips into cisterns. What the nets miss condenses on every surface. Walls sweat. Rope sweats. Your knife sweats in its sheath. Overnight the whole bowl rimes with frost, and in the morning the frost sublimates off the warm stone in long slow ribbons, so that the town appears to steam even where it is dry.

The floor of the bowl is a staircase of pools. The sinter built them long ago — the oldest channels are worn smooth as elbow bones — and now the growth and the wear are in balance, a balance the town maintains with bone spades and bare hands. Hundreds of terraces step down from the rim to the central vent-lake, milky blue, giving off tall columns of steam. Each pool overflows into the next in a hundred small falls. The sound is not loud. It is the sound of a library under a roof of sky: drip, thread, thread, drip, and beneath it the long low hiss of the vents, which is the nearest thing the bowl has to a voice.

The town fills the bowl like a hand fills a glove. They call it the Hush, when they call it anything. A few hundred low houses of rough clay and sinter-block, windowless against the cold, thick-walled, roofed with reed and moss; the moss grows so thick that from the rim the roofs look like a meadow that happens to have doors. Causeways run between them, and bridges over the channels, and sluices, and weirs, and little stone vanes that lean the steam columns this way and that so the warm breath of the vents is delivered to the terraces that need it. The columns stand at angles against the wind, slow and white, like the pillars of a temple that is still being dreamed.

No smoke rises in the Hush. There is almost no wood — a few wind-cut trees at the rim's lip, no taller than a person; tussock grass bowed by frost; cushion plants hard as felt, the size of sleeping animals, faintly sweet when crushed; rust and saffron lichens on the cliffs. Fuel is the dry dung of the herds, and it is saved for the forges. Everything else is cooked in the earth: food wrapped in reed and lowered into the hot pools, or laid on warm stone. The town's breath is steam, not smoke.

The people are small and wool-clad and barefoot on the warm stone. Their skin carries a faint bloom of mineral dust, as if they were already half-turned. They work constantly, and they work quietly: skimming brine shrimp from the hot pools with reed sieves, cutting and carrying sinter blocks, clearing the channels of crust, herding the camelids, mending the fog nets, pressing algae cakes, salting fish from the outflow. At midday the market terrace fills — salt from the lowlands, obsidian, wool, bone, copper in thin rings, dried shrimp, fog-water sealed in gourds — and the crowd is dense and silent as a herd. Traders come up from the warm countries breathless and sunburned, and learn quickly to lower their voices. Voices do not carry in the fog; a shout dies in ten paces. And there is the older reason: the vents answer. No one shouts, because sometimes the hiss rises, and it is not good luck.

The dead are here too, and not separate from the living. A body laid in a shallow warm pool is crusted over in a season — bone, wool, face, the folds of the hands — feature by feature, like a statue being printed in slow motion. The family sets the finished figure into the house wall, or stands it along the causeway, or lays it in a niche above the door, where the steam keeps it warm. The oldest houses are half ancestral stone. Nothing left on the warm terraces keeps its nature: a dropped obsidian blade, a reed basket, a dead vizcacha — all come back crusted, handed up out of the water like fossils. The people do not mourn this. They say the bowl keeps everything, and the keeping is not death, only a change of weight.

Nothing here freezes that the vents can reach; nothing here dries that the fog can touch. These are the two rules, and there are others. The light, for instance. The sun is so bright it has no shadows: the steam takes the light and spreads it, and at noon the bowl is a bowl of milk, depthless and white, every surface equally lit — the pale flamingos, the white sinter, the steam — so that distance lies and a far wall looks near. The people tell the hour by the color of the pools, which change with the weather — jade, rust, bone, blood-orange — and by the lean of the steam columns. And the bowl breathes. Over days the central lake rises and falls by the height of a person, and the channels run backward, and the pools fill and empty, and the town moves with it: gates opened, gates closed, a slow lung of water.

The animals are many, and all of them are quiet. The woolly camelids come down from the rim pastures in herds the color of the rock, loads of salt and brush on their backs. Vizcachas sit in rows on the warm cliffs like a jury. In the warm shallows, frogs the size of a thumb, dark as the water, by the thousands, inflate their throats — and no sound comes. It is a silent croaking, a congregation of mimes, the strangest thing in a place of strange things, until you have watched a while and it becomes the most natural. Pink clouds of brine shrimp drift in the hottest pools; small silvery fish, no longer than a finger, hold against the current of the outflow; water striders stitch every still surface. Flamingos stand on one leg in the milky water, pale as the steam, filtering shrimp through beaks like bent sticks. They never call. The ducks in the reed channels never call. The condor over the rim never lands. At dusk the bats come out and sew the steam together, and they are quiet too, or quiet enough.

The loudest sounds in the bowl belong to the stone and the ice. The sinter crust cracks at night with a noise like a knuckle, like a bone; and once in a season a slab of rim ice lets go and comes down in a white boom that rolls around the cliffs and dies in the fog, and for a moment everyone stops work and listens, and then the hiss resumes, and the work resumes, and the fog closes over the sound as water closes over a stone.

Stay a week and you will learn the rhythm, which is not a rhythm but a weather: geysers that were quiet for a month wake and batter their pools; a channel chokes and is cleared; the market fills and empties; the camelids are moved; the pools turn from jade to rust; the frost writes its name on every wall and the steam erases it. Stay a season and your own knife will begin to bloom with crust where you left it on the warm stone, and you will understand that you are being kept too — gently, in no hurry — the bowl patient as geology, and that in time you will be set into a wall, warm and white and exactly as you were, in a town that does not speak, under a sky that never rains, in a bright fog that never lifts.
