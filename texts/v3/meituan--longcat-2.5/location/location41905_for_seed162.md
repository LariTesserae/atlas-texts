# The Vast Riven

The Vast Riven is a warm, high, unfinished country. It has the scale of a continent and the manners of something that has only just stood up. Its mountains are young enough to be sharp; its rivers have not yet learned patience; its soil is still arguing with the rock. There is no frost to speak of, no winter, only a long warm dimness in which life grows layered, damp, and industrious.

And there are no buildings. Not a road, not a wall, not a rope, not a ruin. The industrial character of the place is not carried by artifacts. It has gone feral into anatomy, weather, and stone. Nothing here is manufactured. Everything is manufactured from.

Stand on one of the black basalt ribs and the land falls away in fresh angles. The ridges are knife-edged, the gullies still V-shaped, the talus unrounded and bright where it has split. Arêtes have no softened shoulders; canyon mouths have not yet learned the round lips of old age. In places the rock is glassy, twisted into ropes and blades as if it had been moved while still half-liquid. Elsewhere it is rust-veined, sulfur-yellow, manganese-blue, or plated with a copper film that weathers green overnight. Rain touches a new face and runs off it without staining it; rain touches an old face and finds a hundred kinds of hunger waiting.

Water here is dampness with ambition. It does not rule the land; it visits. Rain falls in cells rather than across the whole sky: a dark column walks over one valley, fills the seeps, drowns a patch of moss in a single hour, and moves on. Half a kilometer away the dust remains dry enough to powder underfoot. Fog collects in the lower basins and is immediately harvested—by leaves that drink from air, by beetle shells grooved to funnel beads into mouths, by spiderlike filaments strung between ferns until whole thickets glitter as if newly varnished. Rivers are young and therefore rude. They cut, they drop, they vanish into fissures, they come back as warm springs smelling of metal and eggs. There are no true lakes, only a few mirrors no deeper than a body, and some of them are gone by evening, withdrawn into cracks or lifted back into the haze.

The plateau is high enough to keep a white cloud-sea below its rim. From the interior, one lives above that sea and never touches it. The lower world becomes a bright rumor, a level of pure brightness under the cliffs. The Vast Riven makes its own weather because it is large enough to disagree with itself. A storm can be born over the eastern pale, grow fat on warm updrafts, age into a gray-haired front, and die against the western escarpment without ever finishing the crossing. On a clear afternoon—clear only in the local sense, the light still amber and thick—you can see three weathers at once: a rain cell dressing one slope in black silk, a dust devil unpicking a dry ridge, and a high electrical stillness gathering over the anode-reed beds like a held breath.

The sun is a dull coin behind spore-smoke, volatile oils, ash, and the exhaled perfumes of too much green life. Shadows have no hard edges. Distance collapses into pearl. At noon the light is dim enough that the land’s own lamps do well: catalytic hearts glowing through body walls, blue corposant on reed tips, slow pulses in fungal husks, the cold fire of seedpods turning. Night is not black here but brown and busy, full of soft movement and chemistry.

The vegetation is lush, but not gentle. It stacks itself toward the light in near-touching layers. A cable-tree’s crown may be a platform of epiphytes; under it grows a second canopy of turbine-ferns; under that, anode-reeds and spleenwort; under that, a mattress of moss and liverwort deep enough to swallow a boot. Every fallen trunk becomes a terrace, every terrace a garden, every garden a battlefield. Roots grip with the determination of hands. Leaves overlap like scales. In the wettest pockets the green is so saturated that a broken stem bleeds not sap but a bright emulsion, milky or orange or metallic, which the nearest films and insects begin to farm before the sap has finished deciding which way to run.

Much of this green life is chemical. Resins harden in air into amber beads. Nectaries pour out acids strong enough to etch a line in fresh stone. When a slope is injured—by a falling rock, a combustion front, a browser’s broad foot—the whole hillside may change scent within seconds, from wet green to crushed pepper, then to something sweet and warning-like, then to the smell of hot pennies. These are not perfumes in the human sense. They are messages, bribes, alarms, treaties. The plateau is dense with presences: in the air, spores and mites and gliders and seed-flecks; in the soil, nematodes, larvae, fungal cords, mineral-eating bacteria; in the water films, rotifers and temporary shrimp and the transparent young of things that will not keep their present shape. A handful of moss can be a crowded city. A single sun-warmed stone can be a bazaar.

This is where the industrial has gone to seed.

There are factories here, but they have no walls. There are engines, but they are anatomical. Herds of piston-grazers move through the fern prairies with multi-chambered stomachs pumping like slow machinery; they vent steam from their nostrils, and their dung is not waste so much as a refined intermediate, already half-owned by insects. Some warm-blooded browsers carry catalytic hearts—hot organs that burn marsh gas from their own fermentation. Stand near one on a cool morning and you can feel the heat through its hide. Its body glows faintly through the thinner fur of the flank, a dull orange lantern that moves. Predators find these herds by warmth. The herds, in turn, find the best fungus by the metallic taste of the soil. The whole slope is a circuit.

On the ridges, turbine seedpods spin in the wind. Each is a small machine made of seed-coat and intention, pumping sap, charging the air, stimulating its own germination with static. Lines of them on a bare crest turn together when the wind rises, not because they are uniform but because they have entered a temporary consensus. At dusk the anode-reeds begin to arc. Their tips are metal-capped, grown rather than fitted; blue fire walks from tip to tip, splitting water into sharpness and breath, fixing nitrogen, writing brief bright signatures across the wet dark. After a dry spell the reeds may burn in a blue sheet and seem to die. By the next rain they are back, taller, with more caps.

Fire itself has weather-like behavior. A combustion front does not simply spread; it decides. It runs along an amber seep, hesitates at a wet mat, releases a pocket of gas, ignites it, and jumps. It can travel against the wind by first making a wind of its own. The black gloss it leaves behind is read by bacteria, tasted by fungi, and within days softened into a seedbed. Nothing here is only destroyed. Destruction is one of the plateau’s pollination methods.

The fauna are abundant, but the abundance is often quiet. You know them first by sign: combs of browse, shed skins folded like letters, feathers dropped in patterns, soil turned by snouts and broad feet, little cairns of empty snail shells around a stone where something patient has fed for years. At dusk the mayfly-things rise in columns, each adult living only long enough to mate and leave a translucent husk on a reed. The air fills with them, yet the sound is mostly the soft leathery flick of wings and the click of bodies finding one another. Gliders with stained-paper membranes cross the gaps between cable-trees without a call. Birds strut, peck, and display; a few make clear notes, but most communicate in low clicks, foot-drums, and the snap of beaks. Frogs with throats like bellows wait for the pressure drop before rain. When they finally chorus, the sound is less heard than felt, a low note in the jaw and the arches of the feet.

The large animals are walking thickets. A browser’s hide may carry algae in the creases, lice in the warm folds, orchids rooted in the dust of its own shed skin. When one lies down at noon, it becomes a meadow with a backbone. Lithophage tortoises graze the mineral films, their beaks edged with a dark metal they have concentrated from the soil; old individuals leave trails of bright scratch-marks across basalt, as if the stone were being annotated. Pack hunters are rare. The Vast Riven prefers swarms, schools, columns, choruses—many presences moving as one body without ever building a wall around the unity.

The place is turbulent. It grows, kills, dissolves, recrystallizes, and germinates in the same hour. A ridge can clear its throat in the night: not a landslide exactly, but a birth, stone shouldering upward through its own scree while roots tear and re-anchor in midair. By morning the new crest may already be crowded with pioneer moss and with impressions of shells that belong to animals not yet evolved. The land is not lying; it has not finished telling the truth. In raw fissures, gravity becomes a habit rather than a law. Needles of stone fall slowly, as if considering. Rain hangs in beads and travels sideways along leaf veins. A valley crossed twice can give back a different valley, not because the path has changed, but because the plateau has revised its opinion.

Distance is honest only in the morning. By afternoon, maps soften. Compasses do not point north; they point toward the nearest large metabolic process, which may be a herd, a burning reed bed, a catalyst-hearted sleeper under the moss. Sound, too, behaves strangely. Most of the land’s violence is spent on chemistry and motion, not noise. The moss takes the footfall. The haze swallows wings. A storm five valleys away arrives first as pressure behind the eyes, then as a darkening of the amber light, then—if it has missed its appointment with distance—as thunder so low it seems to come from inside the body. The common sounds are small: a drip, a click, a seedpod shutting, the brief electric whisper of reeds. When the plateau is truly loud, the silence before it is worse than the noise.

Nothing made is kept. That is the rule that holds the other rules. A shed skin, a shell, a stone flaked by no hand and used by no hand, a foam gallery exhaled by larvae, a husk plated in manganese: all are eaten, buried, germinated, or persuaded back into the general economy. The world is in too much hurry to allow artifacts to accumulate. Memory here is chemical and behavioral. The Vast Riven remembers by changing its mind. Death is not an exit but a transfer. A fallen browser does not simply rot; it enrolls. Insects partition it, fungi negotiate, reed-roots tap its minerals, and the meadow that rises from its ribs may flower with forms first drafted in its marrow. Within a season, the difference between body and soil becomes a matter of tempo.

And still the light stays dim, warm, and amber. Still the rain arrives in cells, moves on, leaves the neighbor valley dust. Still the seedpod turbines turn on the crest, the combustion fronts decide, the catalytic hearts burn under their gardens of fur and algae. From the highest black rib, the Vast Riven looks less like a landscape than like an engine caught in the act of becoming a forest, or a forest caught in the act of becoming a furnace. Everything is near. Everything is working. Almost nothing makes a sound.
