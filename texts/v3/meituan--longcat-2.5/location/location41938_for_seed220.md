## The Hum

The descent is a long ramp of worn stone, spiraling down through warm, still air. Halfway down, you feel it in your chest before you hear it — a low vibration, a pressure, a note below hearing. By the time it resolves into sound, you are already inside it.

The cavern opens without warning. The ramp ends at a ledge of smooth stone, and beyond it is a space your eyes cannot measure. The ceiling is lost in darkness above. The floor falls away in terraces, and on those terraces, shapes: structures, walls, the suggestion of streets — all of them dark masses against a darker dark. The air is warm — bath-warm — and humid, and it carries the smell of wet stone and something faintly metallic, like a struck bell still ringing in the nose.

The sound is the first thing and the last thing. It is a drone, layered: a bass note you feel in the sternum, a mid-range hum that seems to come from the walls themselves, higher tones that move and weave like something alive. Under it, a rhythm — a slow pulse, a breath, a heartbeat. The air moves with it. You feel pressure changes on your skin, small pulses of warmer and cooler. The sound is physical. It is in your teeth, your bones, the fluid of your inner ear. You do not hear the city so much as stand inside it, the way you stand inside weather.

And over everything, the drips. Water falls from the unseen ceiling — single drops, or thin streams — and each impact rings out. In this space, a drip is not a small sound. It is a clear tone, a bell-note, amplified by the shape of the cavern, by the construction, by whatever the builders did to this place. Drips overlap, answer each other, echo into the drone. Sometimes there is a pause — a silence of seconds — and then a drop falls and the whole cavern seems to ring.

The city is built across the terraced floor, following the natural contours of the stone. It is not large — hamlet-scale, perhaps a few hundred structures — but the space around it is vast, and so it feels smaller than it is, a cluster of forms in a huge dark volume. The buildings are not rectilinear. They curve, they spiral, they vault. They are made of a pale, dense material — stone, but not quite; it has a faint translucency, a sheen like ceramic or bone, and no tool marks anywhere. Some of it is warm to the touch. Some of it hums faintly, resonating with the drone.

Many structures are intact. Many are collapsed — heaps of pale fragments, worn smooth, being reclaimed by the damp. The streets are channels as much as paths. Thin streams run in them, carrying water from the ceiling, from condensation, from the warm rock itself. The water is warm, mineral-rich, tasting of stone. It collects in pools in the low places — still, dark surfaces that reflect the faint light.

At the center of the city, a column. It rises from the floor — a natural formation, perhaps, or something grown, or something built — and disappears into the darkness above. It is warm. You feel the heat radiating from it as you approach. Attached to it, or grown into it, are the engines: shapes that might be machinery, might be mineral formations, might be something else. They are the source of the drone — or its anchor, its amplifier. They pulse. Every few minutes, the sound swells — a wave of pressure, a rise in the bass note — and dust lifts from the ground, hangs in the air, falls slowly. Sometimes the stone itself groans — a deep, grinding note — as if the whole cavern is a body and the engines are its heart.

The light is very dim. The main natural light comes from fungi — pale growths on the walls, on the fallen stone, on the bases of buildings. Some glow: a faint blue-green, a cold light that does not reach far. They cluster where the damp is strongest, where the warmth rises. Mosses grow in the wettest places, dark green, almost black in this light. Some of the tech still emits light — panels, conduits, seams in the buildings — a dim amber, a dim green, enough to see the shape of a wall, the glint of water. Your eyes adjust to a deep twilight. You see masses, edges, the reflection of glow-fungi in still water. You do not see the ceiling. You do not see the far side of the cavern.

The ecology is sparse. The fungi are the base — slow-growing, feeding on the damp stone, on the warmth, on whatever the engines exhale. In the pools: small pale creatures — crabs, shrimp, something like both — blind, slow, their shells thin and translucent. Insects drift in the warm air — beetles, moth-like things — feeding on the fungi, on each other. On the walls, sometimes, you see movement: a long-legged shape, pale, listening — a cave cricket, or something like it, its antennae catching the vibrations of the drone. And sometimes, in the deeper dark, something larger — a shape that moves along the walls, that stops, that listens — a listener, perhaps, or a thing that has evolved to hear the city's song. It is rare. You do not see it often.

The rules of this place are not the rules of the surface. Sound is force here. The resonance can move matter — dust, water droplets, small loose objects. Stand in the open when the engines pulse, and you feel the pressure wave push against you, see the dust lift and hang. In some chambers, the drone is so focused that dust forms standing waves — visible patterns, like a Chladni figure made of dust, hovering in the air. In some pools, the water's surface holds patterns of ripples that do not move, that simply hold their shape, as if the water has forgotten how to fall back.

The buildings are tuned. Their shapes are not arbitrary — they correspond to harmonic relationships, to intervals and overtones. The city's layout is a map of sound. The engines run on vibration — they convert the cavern's natural resonances into heat, into motion, into the drone. The tech is not fully understood. It seems to grow, to self-repair, to exist in states that current physics cannot describe. Some structures flicker — not in light, but in presence — shifting slightly, as if vibrating between positions. Some passages are only open at certain phases of the pulse. Some rooms are only audible at certain tones.

The city is active. The water cycles: condensation on warm surfaces, drips from the ceiling, streams in the channels, pools, evaporation. The thermal cycle: warm air rising from the engines and the warm rock, cooling against the distant ceiling, sinking. The engines cycle: pulses of sound and heat, slow rhythms, faster rhythms, patterns that might be language or might be machinery. The fungi grow, slowly. The creatures move, feed, listen. The stone settles, groans, sings. The city breathes. It has been breathing for a long time — the erosion is old, the forms are settled, the stalactites are worn smooth, the flowstone is in balance. It is a place that has become itself, that is in its own state, that is not dying and not being born but simply continuing, humming, dripping, warm and dark and loud and mostly empty.
