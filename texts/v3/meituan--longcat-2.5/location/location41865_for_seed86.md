# The Underneath

A rift opened in the deep past — not long ago, by the measure of stone — and the land folded downward into itself like a wound that never finished tearing. What remains is a basin of caverns so vast that distance behaves strangely within it. The main chamber, called the Bowl by the surface-dwellers who sometimes descend, stretches four or five miles across, its ceiling lost in blackness hundreds of feet overhead. Passages branch from it like the roots of an inverted tree, some narrowing to crawl-spaces, others opening into their own smaller halls, each with its own sound, its own smell, its own colony of life. The whole system sits below the water table's reach in most places, and the rock remembers its making: the walls are sharp, freshly-fractured, *young*. There are no rounded chambers here, no slow-smoothed hollows. Every surface is angular, broken, geometric — basalt columns split clean as axe-work, shelves of shale stacked like pages, boulders that fell from the ceiling and shattered into spear-shards that still litter the floor where they landed. The rock has not yet learned to be gentle.

The air is cool and holds water the way a held breath holds warmth — not wet enough to soak, but never dry. Condensation beads on the stone in the high passages and runs in thin, intermittent threads down the walls. In the lower galleries, water moves with purpose: an underground river enters through a throat of broken rock on the eastern edge of the Bowl and exits somewhere beneath the western ridge, and its voice fills every connected space. It is not a loud river — its volume is modest — but the acoustics of the caverns amplify and multiply it. The sound of water becomes the sound of *many* waters, layered and refracted, until you cannot tell whether the stream beside you is the one you hear or whether the sound arrives from some distant tributary, bent around a corner you cannot see. Drips fall from the ceiling in every chamber, and each drip announces itself with the clarity of a struck bell. In the deepest silence you can hear individual drops hitting stone fifty yards away, and the time between the flash of a drip's fall and its sound tells you exactly how high the ceiling is above you.

Life owns this place. That is the first fact and the last fact and the fact that presses against everything in between. The darkness is not empty; it is *occupied*. The river is thick with pale, eyeless fish the length of a forearm, their skin translucent enough to show the shadow of their organs, and they move in schools that turn as one body, flashing like a single thought. Crustaceans the size of a hand pick along the waterline, and smaller things — smaller than a fingernail, smaller than a grain of rice — coat the wet stone in layered carpets of movement. Insect colonies cover the lower walls in shifting, living murals: pale beetles with elongated legs, cave crickets whose antennae are longer than their bodies, soft-winged things that have no name on the surface and no need of one. The ceiling, when you raise a light to it, *moves*. It is not stone up there but a crust of roosting life — bats, tens of thousands of them, layered four deep in places, their combined body-heat raising the temperature near the roof by several degrees, their combined breath adding moisture to the already damp air. They do not roost in silence. The colony is a continuous low roar of shifting bodies, of small chirps and clicks and rustles that merge into something like the sound of a fire heard from very far away. When a predator passes below — and something does, something large enough to send the bats up in a swarm — the sound becomes physical. The air itself moves. The wing-beats arrive as gusts, and the noise is a wall of sound, a rolling thunder that bounces from every wall and returns to you changed, enormous, everywhere.

And there are larger things. The floor of the Bowl is not bare rock — it is *scored*. Tracks run through the silt in every direction, some of them wide enough to serve as paths, some of them made by feet with too many toes or no toes at all. Something with a heavy, deliberate gait circles the river's edge at night. Something faster uses the high passages. You find scat, and shed skin, and the picked-clean bones of bats that fell or were pulled down. The ecology is complete and self-sustaining: the bats bring nutrients from the surface in their bodies and their droppings; the droppings feed the insects; the insects feed the fish and the larger hunters; the hunters feed the stone when they die, and the stone, slowly, feeds the fungi.

The fungi are the only vegetation, and they are sparse — not a forest, not a garden, but a scattered architecture of pale growth. Shelf fungi cling to the dead and dying in flat, overlapping layers, bone-white or faintly blue. Cordyceps rise from insect husks in single stalks, delicate as glass. In a few places where water seeps steadily, thick mats of a pale-green moss spread across the floor in patches the size of rooms, soft enough to muffle a footstep, bright enough to seem lit from within in the total dark. And there is light — not much, but not nothing. Certain of the shelf fungi phosphoresce, a cold blue-green that is just strong enough to read the shape of a nearby wall by, just strong enough to make the bats' silhouettes visible when they pass through its glow. In one chamber near the river, a cluster of these fungi covers an outcrop the size of a house, and the light they cast is steady and strange and faintly beautiful, the color of deep water. There are no other colors in the Underneath. The palette is the palette of wet stone: grey, black, the brown of silt, the white of bone and fungus, and the faint luminescent blue.

The people who come here — and they do come, though they do not stay — are from the surface, and they come for the bats. Guano, in quantity, feeds crops. They come with woven reed baskets and bone-tipped digging sticks and rope made from surface grasses, and they work quickly and leave before the season turns. They do not build here. There are no structures, no markers, no made things that stay. They descend through a sinkhole on the northern edge of the rift and they follow the sound of the river and they leave before the bats return from their evening feeding, because the bats' return is a thing the body does not forget — a river of wings pouring back through the sinkhole's throat, the air pressure dropping, the sound rising until it is inside your chest. The people take what they came for and they go, and the Underneath does not notice their absence. It does not notice their presence.

Because the Underneath does not change. This is the strangest thing about it, and the thing that the people who descend feel in their bodies before they can name it. The water level is the same. The bat colony is the same. The fungi glow at the same intensity. The fish run the same paths. There are no seasons here, no floods, no droughts, no die-offs, no population explosions. The system is closed and balanced and it has found its equilibrium and it will hold it. The dead are replaced at exactly the rate they fall. The stone does not erode faster than it can afford to lose. The river carries exactly as much sediment in as it carries out. Even the temperature is fixed — cool, always cool, the cool of deep earth, the same in the highest passage and the lowest. Time passes, but the Underneath does not age. It is not waiting. It is not holding its breath. It is simply *complete*, a world that finished becoming itself and then stopped, and the only evidence that time moves at all is the slow, slow growth of the fungi, which add a layer so thin that a human lifetime might see a finger's width of new growth on the oldest shelves.

And the sound. The sound never stops. The bats and the water and the insects and the drips and the creak of cooling stone — it is always there, always the same, always loud enough to make silence impossible. Visitors say that after a day in the Underneath they cannot sleep in the quiet of the surface. They say the absence of sound is its own kind of noise, a ringing, a pressure, a wrongness. They say the Underneath gets into the body and stays there, a hum behind the hum of the blood, the memory of a place that is always awake, always watching with its eyeless fish and its clicking insects and its thousand-thousand wings, a place that has no tomorrow because it has no need of one.
