**The Grownd**

It is a high plateau, broad and open, warm and wet. The air is thick with water and light. You stand on a floor of living fiber — a mat of roots and stems woven so densely that it feels like turf, like a lawn cultivated for centuries, but it is not soil. It is plant. The ground is plant. The ground has been grown, not deposited. It is firm enough to walk on, but it yields slightly, like a sponge. Water is everywhere at the surface: shallow pools, narrow channels, seeps that rise from the mat itself. The plateau is a vast sponge, saturated, and the water moves slowly, lazily, through a network of root-lined channels. The water is warm — body temperature, or slightly above — and it is clean, clear, slightly sweet from the nectar that drips from the flowers overhead.

The structures rise from this wet floor — massive, architectural forms grown from plant matter. They are scattered, widely spaced, so that each stands in its own clearing of open marsh. Much of the plateau is empty: open wet ground, pools, channels, the low mat of vegetation, and then, rising from it, a structure. A column. An arch. A chamber. A tower. They are not close together; they are presences in an emptiness, like standing stones, like the ruins of a city — but they are not ruins. They are alive, and they are new.

Some are columns — thick as tree trunks, but straight and smooth, rising twenty or thirty meters to support broad canopies of leaves that spread like roofs, like umbrellas, like the ceilings of pavilions. The columns are a deep, almost black green, and their surfaces are ribbed, spiraled, like the trunks of palms but more regular, more deliberate. The canopies are bright, almost yellow-green, and they are dense enough to cast shade — but the shade is dappled, because the leaves are arranged in patterns, overlapping, like shingles, like tiles, like the scales of a roof.

Others are arches — formed from stems that have been bent and fused together, creating vaulted spaces beneath. The arches are smooth, parabolic, like the ribs of a cathedral, but they are alive, and they are covered in a fine fuzz of moss, pale green, silver in the light. Beneath the arches, the ground is drier, and the space is like a room, like a nave, like a shelter. The air beneath the arches is cooler, slightly, and the sound is different — the wind moves through the vault and hums, a deep, resonant note, constant.

Some are platforms — terraced like gardens, like rice paddies, but at a huge scale, with shallow pools held in place by woven root-walls. The pools are perfectly level, perfectly flat, and they reflect the sky and the surrounding structures. The terraces are planted with low, dense vegetation — mosses, small ferns, creeping things — and they are bright green, vivid, almost luminous. The root-walls are pale, almost white, and they are smooth, like plaster, like concrete, but they are alive, and they are warm to the touch.

Some are hollow chambers — rooms, essentially — formed by stems that curve inward and join overhead, creating enclosed spaces with doorways and windows of woven vine. The chambers are dark inside, cool, and the air is thick with the scent of green, of chlorophyll, of nectar. The doorways are arched, the windows are rectangular, and the floors are flat, level, made of the same woven fiber as the plateau floor. The chambers are habitable. They are empty.

There are bridges between some structures — narrow spans of interlaced fiber, arched, with railings of woven vine. The bridges cross the channels, the pools, the open wet ground. They connect structure to structure, and they are empty. No one crosses them.

There are towers — vertical accumulations of vine and stem that spiral upward, tapering, like minarets, like termite mounds, like the spires of a cathedral, but made of living tissue. The towers are the tallest structures, and they are the ones that make the sound. They are hollow, or partially hollow, and the wind moves through them, and they resonate. They are flutes. They are organ pipes. They play one note, or one chord, constant, unchanging.

The atmosphere is warm, steamy, luminous. The sun is bright and the light is normal, clear, but the air is so saturated with water that it glows. Mists drift between the structures, not thick enough to obscure, but enough to soften the edges of things and make the distances dreamlike. Condensation is constant: every surface is wet, every leaf drips, every stem beads with water. The sound of dripping is everywhere, a continuous patter, like rain that never stops but also never falls from the sky. The water falls from the plants themselves.

The sound is loud. The plateau is never quiet. The dripping is the base layer, a constant percussion. Over that, the wind moves through the structures — through hollow stems, under arches, across the mouths of chambers, through the towers — and the plant architecture resonates. It hums. It moans. It whistles. The vaulted spaces act as organs, and the wind plays them. The towers are flutes. The chambers are drums. The whole plateau is an instrument, and the wind is always playing it. The sound is deep, resonant, constant — a low hum that you feel in your chest, punctuated by higher notes, drips, splashes, the creak of fiber under tension. It is not unpleasant, but it is overwhelming. You cannot hear yourself think. You cannot hear your own footsteps. The place is loud with water and wind and the voice of the plants.

The light is warm, bright, constant. It is always midday, or always late afternoon — the sun never moves, or moves so slowly that it doesn't matter. The light filters through the canopies, creating patterns on the wet floor — dappled, shifting slightly as the leaves move in the wind, but the patterns are always the same, because the wind is always the same, and the leaves are always the same. The light is golden, warm, and it catches the mist, so that the air itself seems to glow. The shadows are soft, diffuse, and they are always the same length.

The temperature is warm — 25, 28, 30 degrees — constant. The wetness makes it feel warmer, steamy. The air is thick, heavy, warm. Breathing feels like drinking warm water. The warmth is constant, and it comes from the sun, from the plants, from the water, from the air itself. The plateau is a greenhouse, a warm room, a bath.

There are almost no animals. A few birds — large, white, long-legged — wade in the shallow pools, motionless, watching. They are silent. They do not nest here; they simply stand in the water, waiting. There are insects, perhaps, small and iridescent, hovering near the flowers, but they are few. There are no mammals visible, no reptiles, no frogs. The world belongs to the plants. The birds are visitors, not inhabitants. Even they seem part of the stillness.

The equilibrium is perfect. The plants grow, but they do not grow. The water falls, but it does not accumulate. The mist rises, but it does not thicken. Everything is in balance. The structures are raw, unweathered — their surfaces are smooth, their edges sharp, their colors vivid. There is no rot, no decay, no moss growing on the moss. The erosion has not begun. The world is perpetually new. It is as if it were all grown yesterday and will remain exactly as it is forever. The light is always the same warm, bright, diffused light. The temperature is always the same warm, wet warmth. The sound is always the same loud hum. Nothing changes. The place is a still point, a frozen moment of growth, a garden that has reached its ideal form and stopped.

And yet it is strange. The structures are too regular, too deliberate. They resemble a city, a plan, a design — but there is no designer. The columns are spaced too evenly. The arches are too symmetrical. The chambers are too habitable, with their doorways and windows and flat floors. The bridges connect structures that are too conveniently placed. The whole plateau looks like a model of a city, or a blueprint, but it is made of living plants and there is no one who built it. The intention is visible, but the intention is absent. The world is a made environment with no maker, a constructed world with no constructor. It is as if the plants themselves decided to build, or as if the idea of building grew here without a builder.

The ground, too, is made. It is not soil; it is a mat of woven roots and stems, a carpet of living fiber that has been grown into a floor. It is level, even, deliberate. The pools are placed, not random. The channels are straight, or curve in smooth, planned arcs. The whole plateau is a garden, but a garden without a gardener, a construction without a constructor, a city without citizens. The only inhabitants are the plants themselves, and they are the city.

Perhaps the whole plateau is one organism — a single plant, or a single colonial entity, that has grown into the form of a city. The "buildings" are its organs. The water channels are its circulatory system. The roots are its nervous system. The towers are its voice. The whole plateau is a body, and the body is shaped like a city. It is a city-body, a body-city, a living construction in eternal equilibrium. It is warm and bright and dripping, and it is forever.
