# The Interior

The ground rises in every direction. Not sharply — gently, the way the floor of a vast bowl lifts toward its rim. But there is no rim. The ground simply continues, lifting, until what lies ahead becomes what lies above, and the far interior of the world arches overhead like a sky made of land. You are standing on the inside of something. The curve is unmistakable once you let your eyes relax and stop trying to flatten it into a horizon. The land bends up. The land bends over. The world is hollow, and you are within it.

The ground is alive. Not soil with plants growing in it — the ground *is* plant. A dense, interwoven mat of root and rhizome and compressed stem, layered over ages into a substrate with the give of turf and the resilience of living wood. It is warm underfoot. Damp. It breathes — a slow tidal respiration you feel only when you stand still long enough to stop expecting it. The rise and fall is minute, perhaps a finger's width over the span of a long exhale, but it is unmistakable. The world has a pulse.

At the center of the hollow hangs the light. It has no disc, no edge, no shape you can fix. It is a warm dimness, a glow that seems to emanate from a point too small to resolve — as though you are looking at something very bright through very thick haze. The light it gives is the light of deep twilight, of a room with a single candle behind frosted glass. Enough to see by. Not enough to cast sharp shadows. The warmth is everywhere, uniform, the warmth of a body rather than a fire. The air is heavy with moisture. Water condenses on every surface — on the curved walls of the grown structures, on the broad leaves of the ground-cover, on your own skin. It gathers in beads, runs in thin streams, drips from edges. The sound of dripping is the closest thing to a constant voice here, but even that is soft, irregular, swallowed.

And it is quiet. Profoundly quiet. The kind of quiet that is not an absence but a pressure against the eardrums, a presence. The fauna is abundant — you see them once your eyes adjust to the dimness. Soft shapes moving through the low growth. Things with too many legs and things with none. Pale, unhurried creatures that watch you with eyes reflecting the central glow as faint pinpoints. They make no sound. Their feet make no sound. When they brush against the living ground, the mat absorbs the contact without a whisper. You wonder if they have vocal cords. You wonder if sound behaves differently here — if the geometry of the hollow bends vibration back on itself and cancels it out, if the living substrate drinks frequency the way it drinks water, if silence is not an adaptation but a property of the space itself.

The structures are the reason you came. They are grown, not built — though the distinction blurs the longer you look. Someone took living wood and shaped it. Trained it. Grafted it. Pruned it over centuries, over millennia, with hand tools. You can see the evidence: the healed scars where a branch was cut and the wound grew over, the grain flowing around old cuts like water around a stone; the faint spiral patterns where fiber ties once held a limb in place, the fiber long rotted away, the wood remembering the constraint in its curvature. Simple tools. Hand tools. The marks are everywhere — small, patient, repeated. No saws large enough to fell a tree. No metal that would have left a different scar. Just blades of stone, of shell, of hardened wood, and time, and the living responsiveness of the material.

The structures are enormous. Not tall — the dimness and the curve of the world make height difficult to judge — but vast in extent, sprawling across the living ground like topiaries grown to architectural scale. Arches of living wood span distances that would require engineering. Chambers are formed by the intersection of grown walls, their surfaces smooth, their proportions considered. Passages wind through the mass, and the wood is still alive, still growing, still being shaped — or perhaps shaping itself now, the original growers long gone, the structures continuing the work on their own. Some chambers are open to the dim sky. Others are enclosed, their interiors dark, their floors carpeted with the same living mat that covers the exterior ground, as though the inside and outside of the structure are not different categories but continuous tissue.

The spaces between structures are enormous. You can stand at the edge of one grown complex and look out across the living ground and see the next structure as a faint irregularity on the curving horizon, so far away that the dim light barely reaches it. The emptiness between them is not barren — the ground is alive, the fauna moves through it — but it is empty of presence, of the marks of intention. The structures are presences in a vast, quiet, living void. And the void is not empty. It is full of moisture, full of the slow processes of the living ground, full of the silent movement of creatures. It is the emptiness of space between stars, the emptiness between notes in a long silence. A hollow that is not a lack but a condition.

The rain — if it is rain — falls from the interior surface far above. Water that condensed on the distant ceiling of the world, gathered, fell. It falls slowly in this warm, thick air, and it falls as fine mist as much as droplets, so that the air itself seems to be descending, condensing out of the haze. The ground drinks it. The living mat wicks it away. The structures weep it from their surfaces in thin, continuous streams. There are places where the rain collects in grown basins — shallow bowls of living wood, their surfaces sealed with resin or some secretion of the plant itself — and the water stands there, still, reflecting the dim center-light as a faint smear of warmth. You kneel and touch the water. It is body-warm. It tastes of green, of mineral, of something faintly sweet.

The highlands are where the structures cluster. The interior surface is not uniform — in some regions the living mat has grown thick over ages, raising the ground into broad, gentle swells, and it is on these swells that the grown architecture concentrates. The highlands are not sharp or dramatic; they are the slow accumulation of living material, the patient rising of a substrate that never stops growing. Walking from a lowland to a highland, you feel the ground thicken beneath your feet, the mat becoming deeper, softer, more resonant. The structures on the highlands are larger, older, more complex — as though the elevated ground offered something the builders wanted, or as though the structures themselves contributed to the rising, their root systems adding mass, their slow growth lifting the land around them.

The ecology is not separate from the architecture. The living ground is a single system — or a consortium so integrated that the distinction is meaningless. The fauna feeds on the ground-cover, on the soft new growth at the edges of the mat, on the nutrient-rich sap that weeps from the structures where the bark has been worn or cut. Some creatures live within the structures, in the chambers and passages, and their droppings feed the wood, and the wood grows around them, and the relationship is not parasitism or domestication but something older and more mutual. The creatures are silent because the system does not require sound. Chemical signals, vibration through the substrate, the slow language of growth and response — these are the currencies of communication here. Sound is redundant. Sound is lost energy. The silence is not an evolutionary adaptation but an ecological principle.

Time is difficult. Not because it flows differently — though perhaps it does — but because there is so little to mark its passage. The light at the center does not move, or moves so slowly that you cannot perceive it. The structures grow, but too slowly to see. The fauna moves, but silently, without urgency. You find yourself losing track of how long you have been standing in one place, how long since you last spoke, whether the dimness has deepened or if your eyes have simply adjusted further. You sit on the warm living ground and watch the moisture condense on a nearby leaf, gather, fall, and the fall takes a long time, and you are not certain it has fallen yet, and then it has, and the sound it makes is almost nothing, a soft percussion like a fingertip on skin.

There are things about this place that do not resolve. You walk through a passage in one of the structures and the geometry is wrong — not dramatically, not impossibly, but subtly, the way a dream is wrong. The passage is longer on the inside than the outside. The chamber at its end is larger than the structure that contains it. You tell yourself it is a trick of the dimness, the absence of clear sight-lines, the way sound is swallowed so that you cannot gauge distance by echo. But you are not certain. You walk back through the passage and it is shorter. You walk through again and it is the original length. You stop walking and stand in the passage and the walls are warm and damp and alive and the silence presses against you and you understand that measurement is a habit, not a law.

The central light pulses. Not regularly — or perhaps regularly over a span too long for you to perceive the cycle. But you notice, over the course of hours or days or however time is passing, that the dimness deepens and shallows by degrees so slight that you cannot say when the change occurred, only that it has. The warmth shifts with it, or seems to. The condensation rate changes. The breathing of the ground changes. Everything is coupled to the center, and the center is not steady, and the steadiness you perceived was simply the steadiness of a process too slow to see in motion.

You find a place where the ground is thin. The living mat, usually so dense, so continuous, gives way to something else — a surface beneath it, hard and smooth and not plant. You kneel and brush away the growth and find a surface that is not wood, not stone, not anything you have a name for. It is warm. It is faintly resonant, as though it is hollow beneath — but everything is hollow here, the whole world is hollow, so what would hollowness mean? You press your ear to the surface and hear nothing, or hear something so faint and so slow that it might be your own blood, or the pulse of the ground above the surface, or the sound of the world breathing from the inside.

The creatures gather at the edges of the grown complexes. They do not enter — or they enter only at the periphery, the open chambers, the passages that lead nowhere enclosed. They watch the structures with their pinprick eyes. They watch you. Their silence is not hostile or fearful. It is simply the condition of their existence. You try to speak and your voice sounds wrong — too loud, too sharp, a violation of the hush. The creatures do not startle. They have no startle response to sound. They watch the vibrations travel through the ground from your footsteps, your voice, and they continue watching, and the watching is not curiosity but something more like recognition, as though they are waiting for you to understand something that they have always known.

What they know is this: the world is alive. Not metaphorically. Not in the sense that ecosystems are alive. The world itself — the interior surface, the structures, the ground, the moisture cycle, the dim light at the center — is a single living system, and it was made, or grown, or cultivated, by hands that are no longer here, and it has been growing ever since, and the silence is its voice, and the dimness is its sight, and the moisture is its breath, and you are within it, and it is aware of you in the way that a body is aware of a microbe — not with attention but with response, not with intention but with the slow, patient, living reaction of something that has been growing for longer than you can imagine and will continue growing after you are gone.

You lie on the warm ground and the moisture condenses on your skin and the breathing of the world lifts and lowers you by a finger's width and the dim light at the center of everything is steady and warm and enough and the silence is not empty but full and the structures weep their thin streams and the creatures move through the low growth and the world turns — slowly, slowly, the way a seed turns in the soil, the way a root turns toward water, the way something alive turns toward the light it cannot see but knows is there, at the center, always, burning dim and warm and enough.
