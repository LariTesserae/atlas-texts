# The Terraces of Keth-Avol

The ground is made. This is the first thing to understand, and it takes time to fully sink in — your feet are on a constructed surface, a deck of ceramic-composite hexagons, each a meter across, laid over structural members, over insulation, over the next deck, over the next structural layer, down and down through a vertical kilometer of built depth to where the mountain's original rock sits entombed somewhere in the foundations, invisible, irrelevant. You are walking on a made world. There has been no natural ground here for longer than anyone can remember.

The hexagons are warm. The whole deck is warm — not hot, but the steady warmth of something heated from below, the exhaust of industrial processes that continue in the lower levels, the slow exhalation of engines and furnaces and chemical vats that have been running for generations. The warmth rises through the structure and radiates from every surface, and in the damp air this creates a microclimate that is, by any reasonable standard, tropical.

The terrain extends in every direction in broad, shallow undulations — not hills, not exactly, but the product of differential settlement across a foundation that spans an entire mountain summit. The original peak is gone, consumed, replaced by a constructed topography of terraces and platforms and long, gradual ramps. The highest points are perhaps three hundred meters above the lowest, and the whole is so large that its edges vanish into haze long before you can distinguish them from the sky. It is a region, not a building. A made landscape at the scale of a valley, all of it built, all of it old, all of it weathered into equilibrium with the forces that act upon it.

The seams between the hexagons have widened over decades of thermal cycling, and in every seam grows a thin line of green — a moss so adapted to the mineral substrate that it has no common name, only a maintenance-code designation. It is not the only plant. The Terraces are lush, aggressively, almost impossibly lush. In the low points where condensation collects, ferns have established themselves in dense colonies, their roots gripping the accumulated organic slurry of dead moss and windblown dust and insect husks that collects in every depression. Small trees — actual trees, two and three meters tall, with woody stems and broad leaves — grow from the large planters that line every major walkway, their canopies meeting overhead in the lower districts to form a continuous green ceiling. Vines descend from the upper terraces in curtains, rooting where they touch the deck below, connecting level to level in a web of living cables. The vegetation is not decoration. It is infrastructure — or rather, it has become infrastructure, integrated into the water management systems, the air filtration, the thermal regulation of the built environment. The engineers who designed the Terraces did not plan for this, but the engineers are long dead and the plants have had centuries to negotiate their own arrangements.

Water arrives from the air. The Terraces sit at the altitude where clouds form, and the entire constructed surface is a condensation engine. Every evening, as the temperature drops, moisture precipitates from the passing air onto the warm deck and runs in thin sheets toward the drainage channels. The channels are not merely functional — they have become ecological niches, lined with mosses and small flowering plants, inhabited by insects and the small lizards that hunt them. Rain comes in heavy, warm downpours that last an hour or two and then stop, leaving the surfaces steaming in the bright high-altitude sun. Pools form in the low points — not puddles, but permanent water bodies, some of them tens of meters across, their bottoms lined with ceramic composite, their edges colonized by reeds and floating plants. The water is clean. The entire system — condensation, rain, collection, drainage, evaporation — is closed, recirculating, as much a part of the industrial infrastructure as the engines below.

The engines below. The Terraces are not a ruin. They are a working industrial complex, and the sound of that work is constant — a low, pervasive hum that you feel in your chest before you hear it with your ears. It comes from everywhere and nowhere, transmitted through the structure itself, a composite of a thousand mechanical processes: the deep thrum of the primary power plant somewhere in the lower levels, the higher whine of ventilation systems, the rhythmic compression of pumps, the distant percussion of something being forged or pressed or assembled in the manufacturing districts. Overlaid on this is the sound of the surface — wind across the open decks, rain on metal and ceramic, the calls of birds, the rustle of ten thousand leaves, the chatter and movement of a population that is dense and present and alive.

The fauna is abundant. Birds nest in every crevice — small, bright-plumaged species that have adapted entirely to the built environment, feeding on insects and seeds, raising their young in the shelter of ventilation housings and structural joints. Larger birds patrol the open terraces, landing on the railings of the great ramps, watching for the lizards and small mammals that forage in the vegetation. The mammals are mostly small — rat-sized creatures with prehensile tails that move through the vine-curtains with the confidence of canopy dwellers, emerging onto the decks to scavenge. In the larger pools, fish have been introduced — or have arrived, no one is certain which — and they are thick in the water, surface-feeding in the bright afternoons, rising to insects that skim the surface. The insect life is overwhelming in its variety and numbers: beetles in the drainage channels, butterflies in the planter beds, clouds of small flying things that rise from the vegetation when you disturb it. The entire built environment has become an ecosystem, and the ecosystem is thriving.

The light is intense. At this elevation, the sun is a physical presence — bright, white, slightly blue-shifted, pouring down on the pale ceramic surfaces and reflecting back upward so that the light comes from below as well as above. The vegetation glows. The water surfaces flash. Shadows are sharp-edged and dark. There is haze — the perpetual haze of warm damp air at altitude — but it diffuses the light rather than dimming it, so that the whole landscape is bathed in a bright, even illumination that seems to come from everywhere at once. At midday, the Terraces are almost too bright to look at directly. At dawn and dusk, the light goes through a series of color transformations — pale gold, rose, deep amber, violet — as the sun's angle changes and the haze catches the wavelengths, and the whole constructed landscape, every surface and every leaf, shifts with it.

The materials of the Terraces are old and worn and beautiful in their wear. The ceramic hexagons are polished smooth by foot traffic in the high-use corridors, rough and pitted in the places where no one walks. The metal railings have gone through every stage of oxidation and been painted and oxidized again, so that their surfaces are stratified, geological, layers of color and texture that record decades of maintenance and neglect. The concrete — there is concrete, vast expanses of it in the older districts — has been repaired so many times that it is a patchwork of different ages and compositions, some patches pale and new, some darkened by age and moisture, some colonized by lichens that have found a foothold in the alkaline surface. Everything is slightly rounded, slightly softened, the sharp edges of original construction worn away by the patient action of weather and time and use. The Terraces have achieved a kind of settledness, a sense of being exactly what they are, of having been what they are for a very long time.

And time moves slowly here. The changes that occur are gradual — the slow settlement of the deck, measured in millimeters per year; the incremental growth of the trees in the planters, adding a few centimeters of height each season; the steady accumulation of organic matter in the drainage channels, cleared by maintenance crews on a schedule that has not varied in living memory; the slow, patient creep of the moss across every available surface, advancing and retreating with the seasons but always, over decades, advancing. The industrial processes below operate on their own timescales — continuous, steady, the same output today as yesterday as last year. The population changes slowly, births and deaths and arrivals and departures, but the overall density and distribution remains constant, a steady state maintained across generations. There is no urgency. The Terraces do not change quickly. They are in balance — with the weather, with the loads they carry, with the life that has colonized them, with the machines that animate them. They are what they are, and they will be what they are for a long time to come.

You stand on a made world, warm underfoot, surrounded by green life growing from constructed surfaces, the sound of engines in your bones, bright high-altitude light on every surface, water condensing from the air itself, and the whole of it — every hexagon, every fern, every birdcall, every pump and furnace and vine — held in a vast, slow, settled equilibrium that has taken centuries to achieve and that shows no sign of ending.
