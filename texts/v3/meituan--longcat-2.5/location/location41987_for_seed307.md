# The Womb-Vein

A pocket. Nothing more. A fist-sized hollow in fresh volcanic glass, though "fist-sized" depends on whose fist — the space inside unfolds further than the opening suggests, the way a sound unfolds from a single struck note. The entrance is a crack in a cliff face so high that the air outside is thin and cold, but inside the crack, inside the pocket, it is hot. Wet-hot. The kind of heat that lives in the mouth and behind the eyes.

The walls are young. You can tell because they are sharp — not worn, not rounded, not touched by time in any recognizable way. They are still becoming. Obsidian sheets no thicker than a fingernail stand in overlapping planes, their edges fresh and glittering, and between them, mineral water weeps continuously from pores too fine to see. The water is hot when it emerges and cools as it travels, so the upper reaches of the pocket are beaded with condensation that gathers, swells, and falls — a rain that only exists here, that has never known sky, that falls a few inches and is caught on the next shelf of glass.

The floor is not a floor. It is a dense mat of bodies.

They are small — most no larger than a grain of rice, some the size of a fingertip, a few larger ones the length of a hand, though they are pressed so tightly together that their true shapes are hard to determine. They are pale, translucent, segmented in a way that suggests something between insect and worm and crystal. They do not move much. The density of them absorbs sound, movement, intention. A ripple passes through the mass occasionally — a slow contraction, a shift, like a single breath drawn through a body made of many bodies. They feed on the mineral films that precipitate from the weeping walls, scraping them with tools.

The tools are simple. Flakes of freshly broken obsidian, held in appendages that might be legs or might be mouthparts. Tiny probes made from the hollow shafts of their own shed exoskeletons. A few of the larger ones carry scrapers shaped from the walls themselves — deliberate fragments, struck at angles, carried to the best feeding spots. There is no metal. There is no fire that they have made. But there is use. There is the quiet, patient work of edges against surfaces, of one thing shaped to act upon another.

The heat is not uniform. It rises from below, from a seam in the rock that pulses — not with rhythm, but with intention, or something like it. The seam is the reason for the pocket. It is a vein of something that is not quite magma, not quite water, not quite gas. It is a place where the rock is still deciding what it will be. The sharp features, the young walls, the weeping — all of it comes from this seam, from the material that rises and precipitates and hardens and then, sometimes, *un*hardsens, returning to a state that has no name in the language of geology.

This is where the reality frays.

Near the seam, the obsidian planes are not always where they were. Edges that were sharp become rounded, then sharp again. Sometimes a surface is wet when it was dry, or dry when it was wet, and the change happens between one moment and the next without transition. Occasionally something is present that was not present before — a shape that might be a creature but is made of the same mineral as the walls, or a tool that is too complex, too finished, too *intentional* to have been made by the pale many-bodied ones. These intrusions do not last. They soften at their edges, lose coherence, and are reabsorbed into the weeping stone, leaving behind only a faint residue — a smell, a taste, a memory of a color that has no name.

The creatures accept this. They do not startle. They scrape their mineral films and hold their flake-tools and press against one another in their dense, quiet mass, and when something appears that should not be, they simply shift around it, incorporating the space it occupies into their own, until it is gone and the space is theirs again. They have no leaders that can be identified. They have no visible communication. But they move as one when they move at all, and their tools are always oriented toward the seam, as if they know — in whatever way they know anything — that the seam is the source, the center, the reason.

The light is strange. It comes from the walls themselves, faintly, in patches where the mineral precipitation has formed thin films that catch and hold something — not sunlight, not bioluminescence, but a kind of slow, warm glow, the color of skin held up to a candle. It is enough to see by. It is enough to make the water on the upper walls gleam and the translucent bodies of the creatures show their internal structures — dark lines of mineral-rich fluid moving through them, slow pulses that are not quite hearts, not quite circulation, but something that serves the same purpose.

There is no wind. There is no weather. There is only the heat, the water, the growth, the pressing-together, the slow work of edges, and the seam that pulses and sometimes opens further and sometimes closes almost to nothing, and the things that come through when it opens, and the quiet, patient way the many receive them and wait for them to dissolve.

It is a small place. You could hold it in your hands if you could bear the heat. But it contains more than it should — more bodies, more process, more becoming, more *presence* — packed into its sharp young walls, and the silence of it is not empty silence but full silence, the silence of a room where everyone is listening, where everyone is waiting, where the walls themselves are still wet and still deciding what they will become.
