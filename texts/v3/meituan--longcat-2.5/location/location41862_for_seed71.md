# The Worn

It is a plateau, but that word is too small. It is a world—a continent of stone and pale green, high enough that the air is thin and the light is sharp and the cold is a constant companion. It stretches to a horizon that doesn't always behave. Sometimes the horizon is a line. Sometimes it is a fold. Sometimes it shows you the plateau again, as if you were looking at a model of it rather than the thing itself, as if the world had been worn so thin in that direction that you could see through to the back of it.

It is old. Older than it should be. The mountains that once stood here—and there were mountains, ranges that would have made the current plateau look like a footnote—have been ground down over billions of years to this: a vast, nearly flat expanse, still kilometers high, still breathing its thin air. The erosion is complete, or as complete as anything ever is. The world has settled into its final shape. What remains is the residue of that settling—ancient river channels cut so deep and wide they are now shallow valleys, so shallow they are almost imperceptible. Lake beds dry for epochs, their floors cracked into polygons, their shorelines marked by faint lines of darker stone. The bones of the world, smoothed and rounded and left behind.

The cold is constant but not extreme—the cold of altitude and thin air rather than of polar darkness. The sun is bright. The sky is pale blue, or white, or, in the broken places, a color that has no name. The temperature hovers around freezing, dipping lower at night, rising slightly during the day. Snow falls occasionally, but not heavily—the air is too thin, too dry, for real storms. What precipitation there is comes as fine crystals, or as rain that falls in strange ways: in sheets that hang suspended, in droplets that float upward before descending, in perfect spheres that drift like bubbles and burst silently when they touch the ground.

---

Water is present but intermittent, and the people who live here have learned to read it like a language. The plateau is crossed by rivers—meltwater from the remaining ice caps at the edges, from glaciers that have survived the long warming and the long erosion—but the rivers are old and tired. They do not reach the sea. They flow for a while, sometimes for hundreds of kilometers, and then they sink into the stone, or evaporate, or simply stop, their water spread out into shallow lakes that freeze and thaw and freeze again. In the low places—and there are low places, though they are subtle, though they are more like suggestions of low places—water collects: shallow pools, seeps, areas of permanent damp where the stone is dark and the vegetation is thickest.

Condensation is the true water cycle. The plateau breathes—it cools at night, warms during the day, and the temperature differential pulls water from the air. Every surface is damp in the mornings. The stone sweats. The vegetation drips. The settlements are built to catch this water—stone channels, cisterns, troughs, specially prepared surfaces that weep moisture—because in a place where rain is unreliable and rivers disappear, the daily condensation is the true source of life. The people have a ritual for it: in the early morning, before the sun has burned the damp away, they walk the channels and collect the water, singing quietly (or not singing—the plateau discourages loud sounds), and they store it against the dry days.

---

The vegetation is lush but not green, or not green in the way that green should be. It is a pale, cold green—the green of lichens, of mosses, of alpine cushion plants, of things that grow slowly and densely and low to the ground. It covers the plateau in patches: vast mats of it in the damp places, thinning to scattered cushions on the drier ground, disappearing entirely in the highest or most exposed areas. It is not forest. There are no trees, or almost none, nothing that rises more than a meter or two. Instead it is a carpet, a ground cover, a living crust that spreads over the stone and the soil and the ancient ruins alike.

The people call it *thel*—a word that means, roughly, "the growth," but also "the patience," also "the quiet," also "the thing that waits." It is everywhere—not just in the damp places, but in cracks, on ledges, in the shelter of stones, a living skin on the dead rock. In the wet places it grows thick and deep, forming spongy mats that hold water like a reservoir, that you can walk on (carefully) and that will slowly, slowly close behind you. It flowers—or something like flowering—producing pale, translucent structures that might be flowers or might be something else, that catch the light and seem to glow faintly in the dusk, that are the only bright things in the landscape. It reproduces by fragmentation, by spores, by mechanisms that are not quite clear, and it is very slow—a patch the size of a hand might be a hundred years old, a mat the size of a house might be a thousand.

The people use it. They harvest it carefully, taking only what will regrow in their lifetime, leaving the rest. They dry it and weave it into cloth, into rope, into insulation for their houses. They burn it—it burns slowly, with a pale flame and a smell like cold stone. They eat it—it is bland, but nourishing, and it is the only food that grows on the plateau; there is nothing else to eat, no animals, no insects, no birds, no fish. They use it medicinally—it has properties that are not quite understood, that seem to shift with the broken places, that work differently in different locations. They have a relationship with it that is half practical, half sacred. It is the only living thing (besides themselves) that they share the plateau with, and they know it, and they are careful with it, and they are grateful to it.

---

There are no animals. None. No insects, no birds, no mammals, no worms, no anything that moves on its own and eats and breathes and dies. The silence is the first thing you notice—not the silence of a quiet place, but the silence of a place where something is missing, where the background hum of life is absent and you can feel its absence like a pressure, like a weight, like a held breath. The wind moves through the vegetation and it does not rustle—or rather, it does, but the sound is wrong, too soft, too even, as if the plants do not respond to the wind the way plants should. The water flows and it sounds like water, but there is no splashing, no buzzing, no birdsong, no alarm calls, no footsteps of anything but your own. The *thel* grows, and you can hear it if you listen—a faint, soft, almost imperceptible sound, like the sound of frost forming, like the sound of dust settling, like the sound of time passing—but it is the only living sound, and it is so quiet that you have to stop breathing to hear it.

The ecology is broken, or it never worked the way ecologies are supposed to work. The *thel* does not need pollinators—it reproduces without them. Nothing eats it—or rather, it is not eaten; it just grows and dies and grows again, accumulating in layers, in mats, in peat-like deposits that build up over centuries, over millennia. The dead *thel* does not decay quickly—the cold slows everything—so it piles up, compresses, becomes something like soil, something like fuel, something like a record of the plateau's long, quiet life. The people burn the oldest layers, the deep layers, the layers that have been compressed into something almost like stone, and they use the ash to mark their roads, their houses, their dead.

---

The settlements are scattered across this vastness—towns, not cities, clusters of stone buildings, walls, terraces, channels, cisterns. They are built in the stable places, or in places that have been made stable by generations of work, or in places where the instability is predictable and can be accommodated. They are low, thick-walled, small-windowed, built against the cold and the wind and the strangeness. They are old—some of them are the oldest continuously inhabited places in the world, or would be if the world were normal. They are built on top of older settlements, which are built on top of older ones, layers going down into the stone, into the ancient lake beds, into the bones of the world. The deepest layers are the oldest, and the oldest layers are the strangest—the stone is worked in ways that do not match the current tools, the architecture follows rules that do not match the current understanding of geometry, the rooms are larger inside than out, or smaller, or the same size but in the wrong shape.

The towns are organized around water—around the condensation channels, the cisterns, the seeps, the rivers that still flow. They are organized around the stable zones—the places where reality behaves, where distance is distance and time is time and things stay where you put them. They are organized around the old roads—the paths that connect the settlements, that follow the ancient river channels, that avoid the broken places or pass through them at the right times or in the right ways.

Within the towns, there is order. A central cistern, usually, fed by channels from the surrounding roofs and walls. A gathering space, open to the sky but walled against the wind. Dwellings clustered around these, their doors facing inward, their walls sharing heat. A district for the dead—not a cemetery, but a still, a place where time runs slowly, where the bodies are kept, where the living come to consult them. A district for the *thel*—drying racks, weaving rooms, storage for the fuel. A district for the making of things—stone-working, bone-working, the simple ceramics, the rope-walk, the dye-pits (though there is little to dye, and the colors are pale). A wall around everything, not for defense against enemies (there are no enemies, or there are enemies but they are not human) but for defense against the wind, the cold, the brokenness.

The construction is stone—not cut stone, mostly, but shaped, fitted, stacked. The people who live here have no metal, or almost none—a little meteoric iron, a little native copper, worked cold or with simple fires. They have bone, they have wood (from the few stunted trees, from the driftwood that accumulates in the ancient river channels), they have sinew, they have fiber from the *thel*. They have hand tools—scrapers, blades, needles, awls, grinding stones, pounders, polishing stones. They have fire, they have simple ceramics, they have rope and twine and cloth. They have knowledge—deep, accumulated, oral, practical—of the plateau and its rules.

---

The roads are worn into the stone. Not just the surface—deep into the stone, cut by generations of feet, by pack animals (in the oldest layers), by wheels (in the middle layers), by feet again (in the newest layers). The oldest roads are smooth, polished, almost melted-looking, as if they have been worn down by more than traffic—as if the stone itself has flowed, or remembered, or given up. The roads are marked by cairns, by standing stones, by the ash of the *thel*, by symbols that mean *this way is safe* and *this way is not* and *this way is safe only at certain times* and *this way was safe once, and may be again, but not now*.

---

The people are sparse—not many of them, scattered across the vastness, connected by the roads and by the trade and by the shared knowledge of how to live here. They are cold-adapted, thin-air-adapted, strange in small ways—paler than they should be, slower in their movements, more patient, more observant. They speak quietly, or they do not speak at all—the plateau encourages silence, and the reality breakdown makes sound unpredictable. A word spoken in the wrong place can echo for hours, or arrive before it is spoken, or come back changed. They have a language that is full of words for silence, for patience, for waiting, for the specific quality of the light at different times of day, for the specific behavior of the broken places. They have a language that is also full of words they do not use anymore, words for things that used to be here and are not—words for animals, for birds, for insects, for trees, for flowers, for things that the plateau has forgotten and that they have forgotten, but that live on in the language like fossils.

---

The weirdness is the defining feature. The plateau is old enough, worn enough, that the fabric of reality has worn thin in places. The erosion did not just affect the stone—it affected the laws. Distance is unreliable. Time is unreliable. Matter is unreliable. Causality is unreliable.

In the broken places—and they are scattered across the plateau, some small as a room, some large as a valley—things do not behave. The people have names for the different kinds of brokenness, and they know them, and they avoid them or use them or live with them, depending on the kind.

There is the **fold**—a place where distance is compressed, where you can walk a day and end up where you started, or where you can see a town that is a week away as if it were just over the next ridge, or where the horizon is closer than it should be, or where the sky is closer, or where the ground is closer. In a fold, the world is smaller than it looks, or larger, or just different.

There is the **echo**—a place where sounds repeat, or arrive early, or come from the wrong direction, or come from a different time. You can hear your own footsteps a moment before you take them. You can hear a conversation that happened yesterday, or last year, or a hundred years ago. You can hear your own voice saying things you have not said yet. The people do not speak in the echoes, or they speak very carefully, in words that are safe, in words that will not echo badly.

There is the **still**—a place where time runs slowly, where a day outside is an hour inside, or where an hour outside is a day inside, or where time runs backward, or where time runs in circles, or where time stops. The stills are dangerous, but they are also useful—food does not spoil in a still, or it spoils instantly, depending on the kind. The dead are sometimes kept in the stills, preserved, waiting for a time when they might be needed, or when the brokenness might be healed, or when someone might know what to do with them.

There is the **walking**—a place where the dead are visible, where they walk, where they go about their business, where they do not know they are dead, or where they do know and do not care, or where they are not the dead but something else, something that looks like the dead, something that wears the faces of the dead. The people have rules for the walkings—do not approach them, do not speak to them, do not follow them, do not let them see you, or do let them see you, depending on the kind. Some walkings are dangerous, some are harmless, some are sacred, some are just strange. The people visit the walkings sometimes, carefully, to consult the dead, to ask questions, to leave offerings. The dead sometimes answer, or they sometimes just repeat what they were doing when they were alive, or they sometimes say things that do not make sense, or they sometimes say things that make too much sense.

There is the **wrong sky**—a place where the stars are visible during the day, or where the sun is a different color, or where there are two moons, or where the sky is a different shape, or where the sky is closer, or where the sky is farther, or where the sky is the ground and the ground is the sky. The wrong skies are rare, and they are dangerous, and they are also beautiful, and the people have stories about them, and songs, and warnings.

There is the **floating**—a place where gravity is weak, or inconsistent, or reversed, or sideways. Stones float. Water floats. People float, if they are not careful, if they do not tie themselves down, if they do not know the rules. The floatings are dangerous, but they are also used—the people have learned to move heavy stones in the floatings, to build in the floatings, to use the brokenness as a tool.

There is the **memory**—a place where water flows uphill, or remembers its past courses, or flows in directions that are not downhill, or where the stone remembers its past shape, or where the *thel* remembers being something else, or where the air remembers being solid, or where the ground remembers being sky. The memories are the most common kind of brokenness, and the most subtle, and the most dangerous, because you do not know you are in one until it is too late, until you have walked in a circle, until you have seen yourself, until you have forgotten which way is which.

---

The broken places are growing. Or they are shifting. Or they are becoming more active. No one is sure. The oldest people remember when a certain valley was stable, and now it is not. The youngest people have never known a certain hill to be anything but a place where the stones float and the sky is the wrong color. The knowledge of where is stable and where is broken is constantly updated, passed between towns, encoded in the roads and the building sites and the rituals. The people have a map of the brokenness, but the map is always out of date, always being redrawn, always being argued about. Some people say the brokenness is the plateau's way of dying, that it is wearing thin because it is old, because it is reaching the end of its life. Some people say the brokenness is the plateau's way of staying alive, that it is the only thing keeping it from flattening completely, from disappearing, from becoming nothing. Some people say the brokenness is the plateau's way of dreaming, that it is dreaming of what it used to be—of the mountains, of the rivers, of the animals, of the world it lost. Some people say the brokenness is the plateau's way of remembering, that it is holding onto the past, that it is refusing to forget.

---

The dynamics of the plateau are active, ongoing, visible. The weather—the condensation cycle, the occasional rain, the snow, the wind—is constant. The rivers shift, the lakes fill and empty, the *thel* grows and spreads and retreats. The broken places shift, grow, shrink, merge, split. The settlements grow, shrink, are abandoned, are reoccupied, are rebuilt. The people move, trade, marry, die, are born, adapt. The erosion continues—slowly, imperceptibly, but it continues. The plateau is still settling into its final form, still wearing down, still approaching the end of its long life.

And through all of this, the quiet. The silence. The absence of animal sound, of insect buzz, of bird call, of the thousand small noises that make a normal landscape alive. The wind, the water, the creak of stone, the footsteps, the voices (quiet, always quiet), the sound of tools on stone, the sound of the *thel* growing (if you listen closely, if you are very quiet, you can hear it—a faint, soft, almost imperceptible sound, like the sound of frost forming, like the sound of dust settling, like the sound of time passing).

The silence is not empty. It is full—full of absence, full of patience, full of the weight of all the time the plateau has lived and all the time it has left. It is the silence of a world that has finished most of its living and is now just waiting, just settling, just being, in the thin, cold, sharp light, for as long as it has left.

This is the Worn. This is the high, cold, ancient, quiet, strange, living, dying, settled, active, vast, sparse, damp, lush, broken place. This is where the world has worn itself down to its final shape, and the final shape is this: a plateau, a carpet of pale green, a scattering of stone towns, a network of worn roads, a sky that is sometimes the right color, and a silence that is deeper than any other silence, because it is the silence of a world that is old beyond measuring, that is tired beyond words, that is strange beyond understanding, and that is still, still, still here.
