# The Salar

*A basin, a city, and a single continuous act of building.*

Stand at the rim — there is a rim, though it too has been quarried and terraced and built upon — and look down. The basin is broad, a shallow bowl perhaps a day's walk across, and it is full. Not full of water, though water is everywhere. Full of construction. There is no ground here. The original lakebed, the mud, the salt crust, the very geology — all of it has been covered, built over, built on, built through, generation after generation, until the whole floor of the basin is a pavement of salt and clay and bone and gypsum, and the slopes are terraced with walls, and the whole visible world is made.

The people who live here call it the Works, or the White, or simply Here. Outsiders, on the rare occasions when there are outsiders, call it the Salar, the Saltpan, the City That Weeps.

Everything is built. The streets are paved with salt bricks, some as large as a torso, fitted tight, their seams filled with bitumen and crushed gypsum. The bricks are pink, or gray, or amber, depending on the clay and the minerals and the age; old pavement is dark and glossy with absorbed moisture and centuries of foot traffic, new pavement is pale and rough and squeaks underfoot. The floors of important buildings are inlaid with bone — white splinters of animal bone set in patterns, or whole jawbones and vertebrae set in clay and polished until they glow. The walls are of salt block and clay brick in alternating courses, or of rammed clay faced with salt, or of stone dragged up from the deep quarries at the basin's edge. The roofs are flat, and on the roofs there are more rooms, more courtyards, more salt gardens, more cisterns. And over the streets, in the dense quarters, there are canopies — sheets of asbestos cloth, or woven sinew, or great slabs of gypsum raised on poles — so that much of the city is roofed, and the light that reaches the streets is dim and diffuse, a perpetual twilight even at midday.

Why roof the city? Because of the rain.

Rain is the enemy. Rain dissolves the city. The salt that is the city's bones — its walls, its floors, its treasure, its reason for being — is water-soluble. A single hard storm can soften a wall, can weep a carved relief back into blandness, can undermine a foundation, can wash a whole district's pavement into a pink slurry that runs downhill and collects in the lower basins. So the city is roofed and canopied and drained. Every surface is sloped to a channel. Every channel runs to a cistern or a pool or a deep drain that carries the water out of the city and down to the lower sinks where it is stored or evaporated. The whole city is a machine for catching and moving water, and the people are always, always repairing.

But rain is also life. The basin is low and the sky is often gray, and moisture is everywhere. The salt is hygroscopic — it pulls water from the air. On damp days the walls sweat. Beads of moisture form on the salt blocks, run down in little tracks, collect on the floors, are wiped up or channeled away. The air smells of wet salt and wet clay and smoke and bitumen and the faint sharp mineral tang of the asbestos cloth. It is cool here — the basin traps the damp and the roofs keep off the sun, and the evaporation from all the channels and pools draws heat away, so the city is cool even in the warmer months, and the deep cisterns are cold enough to numb the hand.

The light is dim. The sky, when you can see it between the canopies and the roofs and the towers, is a pale gray-white, hazy, often fogged. The sun is a bright smear. The city makes its own light. Salt lamps — blocks of translucent salt with a pocket for oil or fat, glowing pink and amber — stand in niches and hang from chains. Gypsum windows, thin sheets of selenite, let in a diffused, milky light. Kilns and forges glow. Fires burn in courtyards. At night the city is a constellation of warm points in the dark, and the roofs cut out most of the sky, so it is always a little like being underground.

And the sound. The city is loud. It is never quiet. Rain on the canopies is a roar — when it rains you have to shout, and the people retreat indoors and wait, listening to the water hammering the cloth and the stone and the salt. The channels are always running, a continuous rush and gurgle. The wind funnels between the towers and through the covered streets and whistles through the gaps in the canopies, a constant low howl with higher notes. The salt itself is loud: it creaks, it pings, it settles, it cracks as the temperature changes and as moisture moves through it. A city of salt is a city that never stops talking in a low crystalline voice. And over all of this, the people: hammers on stone, chisels on salt, the scrape of blocks being dragged, the grind of the cutting-sand, the shouts of workers, the calls of the markets, the footsteps of thousands on salt pavement — a crunch and grind that is the city's baseline. And the animals: the bats in the rafters, chittering and squeaking; the brine flies at the pools, a constant low buzz; the cries of the rare birds that nest on the towers; and sometimes, from the deep cisterns, a strange low call that might be the well-wyrm.

Yes, there are animals. Not many. The city is built on a salt lake, or on the memory of one, and the water in the channels and pools and cisterns is salt water, and in it live things that can tolerate the salt. Brine shrimp, tiny and pink, swarm in the sunlit pools — the people harvest them, dry them, eat them, feed them to the bats. Brine flies cloud at the edges of the pools, a living haze, and the bats eat them, and the birds eat them. In the deep cisterns, where the water is darkest and coldest, live blind white fish, and blind white salamanders, and things like pale eels that have never seen light. The people fish for them with bone hooks and sinew lines. And in the deepest, oldest cisterns, the ones that have been in use for centuries, there are stories of a larger thing — a well-wyrm, a cistern-serpent, pale and blind and old, that is sometimes blamed for a collapsed wall or a missing child, and sometimes revered as the spirit of the water. Whether it exists is debated. The bones of something large have been found in the deepest sumps.

There are no plants. None. No grass, no trees, no moss, no lichen — the salt forbids it, and the roofs forbid the sun, and there is no soil anywhere because there is no natural ground. The closest thing to flora in the Salar is the salt gardens. In courtyards and on rooftops and in special chambers, the people cultivate crystals. They set out shallow trays of brine, or blocks of rough salt, or fragments of gypsum, and they tend the growth — adding brine, scraping away unwanted growths, shaping the environment — and the crystals grow into spires and flowers and fans and delicate branching structures, pink and amber and clear. A salt garden is a slow, patient thing, a sculpture that grows over months and years, and the best gardeners are respected like painters or poets. Some gardens are ancient, inherited, generations of careful tending, and they glow in the lamplight like frozen bouquets. There are also fungal things in the darkest, wettest corners — pale, thin, strange growths on the oldest wood and bone and sinew — but they are not plants either, and the people scrape them away or ignore them.

The people are the Salt-Builders, and their whole civilization is the business of building and rebuilding. Their tools are simple: stone hammers, copper or meteoric-iron chisels and saws, bone awls, abrasive sand for cutting salt, levers of stone and bone, rope of sinew and asbestos fiber. No wood — there are no trees. Scaffolding of bone and asbestos cloth and sinew. They cut salt with saws and with abrasive sand and with hardened copper chisels; they shape it by dissolving it, by casting it in molds, by growing it in gardens; they glue it with bitumen and with clay slip and with a kind of cement made from burned gypsum. They are always building. A district might be finished for a generation, and then a new rain, a new flood, a new need, and it is scaffolding and raw walls and piles of cut blocks again. The city is never finished. It is always becoming. There are quarters that are ancient and polished and dense with carving, and quarters that are raw and new and smell of fresh-cut salt, and quarters that are half-ruined, half-rebuilt, where the old walls have dissolved into strange soft shapes and the new walls stand among them like teeth among old bone.

The people live by the rain calendar. There is the Dry Season, when the sky is pale and the air is damp but no rain falls, and the city is at its busiest, building and trading and holding festivals under the canopies. There is the First Rains, when the clouds come down into the basin and the fog is so thick you cannot see across a courtyard, and the walls begin to sweat, and the people watch the sky and wait. There is the Great Rain, which comes every few years, a storm that can last days, that floods the channels and overwhelms the drains and dissolves whole districts, and after which the city is a mess of pink slurry and fallen walls and the people begin again, hauling blocks, replastering, repaving, replanting the salt gardens. There is the Long Damp, when the water is high and the air is thick and the city steams and the well-wyrm is said to stir.

The people eat salt — of course — and brine shrimp and blind fish and bats and the rare birds, and they trade for anything that cannot be made from salt and clay and bone. They make everything. Their clothing is asbestos cloth and leather and woven sinew, dyed with mineral pigments — ochre, malachite, cinnabar, azurite. Their jewelry is salt crystal and gypsum and bone and copper. Their art is carving — the salt is soft enough to carve with simple tools, and the walls of the old city are covered in reliefs, in patterns, in scenes of the basin and the lake and the well-wyrm and the rain. Their music is percussion — drums of stretched hide, lithophones of salt and stone that ring when struck, the natural ringing of the city itself.

The rules of the world here are simple and absolute:

Water wins. Always. Everything the people build, the water will eventually take. The city is a holding action against the water, and the water is patient.

Salt is alive. It grows, it sweats, it dissolves, it creaks, it changes. The people do not think of it as a dead material. They tend it. They speak to it. They say a salt wall has a temper, that a salt floor has a mood.

Everything is made. There is no nature here. There is no wild. There is only the city, and the water, and the things that live in the water, and the sky, and the people. The people are the only thing that is not made — and even they are shaped by the city, by the salt, by the damp, by the dark.

The city is a single object. It is not a collection of buildings; it is one continuous built thing, a single sprawling organism of salt and clay and bone, growing and dissolving and growing again, and the people are its cells, and the water is its blood, and the rain is its weather, and the well-wyrm — if it exists — is its dream.
