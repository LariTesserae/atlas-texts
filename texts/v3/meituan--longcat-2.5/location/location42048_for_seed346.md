The basin sits low in a landscape of older, harder ground, and you don't notice the rim until you're already inside it — a slight rise, barely a meter, that separates this place from everything around it. Once you cross it, the air changes. It is colder, and the cold has a quality of being drawn rather than ambient, as if something beneath the ground is pulling heat out of the world. The dampness is constant. It isn't rain, mostly; it's condensation. Every surface carries a faint wet sheen. Water beads on the pale stone and runs in thin, irregular threads toward the lowest ground. The air is close to saturation, and it swallows sound. The silence here has a texture — not empty, but full of the small, absorbed noises of moisture finding its way downward.

The ground is bone-white and gray-yellow, and it looks unfinished. In some places it is smooth, almost glassy, like something that cooled from a liquid state and hasn't decided to be rock yet. In others it is granular, friable, more like compacted ash or the earliest stage of soil. Cracks run through it in patterns that seem to shift when you look at them indirectly — not dramatically, but enough that you stop trusting your memory of the ground's layout. The basin is still forming. It has been forming for a long time, slowly, but the process is not complete. The geology is raw, embryonic, still negotiating its own shape. The rim is not quite the rim yet. The pools are not quite the pools yet. The whole place has the feeling of something that is patiently becoming.

Vegetation is sparse and low. Pale lichens crust the stone — crustose forms in muted grays and greens that look almost like stains, like the rock is discoloring rather than being colonized. In the cracks, thin mosses hold on, desiccated most of the time, flushing a little when the condensation runs. A few tufts of something grass-like grow near the pools, gray-green and stiff, no taller than a finger's width. Nothing here is abundant. Nothing here is lush. The life that exists is slow and patient and adapted to cold and wet and the faint, constant instability of the substrate.

The settlement occupies the center of the basin, clustered around the largest of the shallow pools. It is small — perhaps forty structures, though the word *structure* is generous for some of them. They are packed close together, layered, some stacked above others, some occupying the same volume in slightly different configurations. The density is not just physical. There are presences here — a sense of there being more in the space than the space should hold. You feel them at the edge of vision, in the corners of rooms, in the gaps between buildings. They are not quite visible, not quite invisible. They are layered into the place like sediment, like the settlement is dense with versions of itself.

The buildings are made of something that looks like ceramic or bone or cured resin — pale, slightly translucent, smooth to the touch. They were not assembled. They were grown, or persuaded, or compiled. The technology that made them operates below the level of architecture; it works on the substrate of matter itself, convincing physical law to be locally different. The walls are also computational substrates. The floors store information in their lattice structure. The openings between rooms are also sensors, or emitters, or something for which there is no word. There are no visible machines, no interfaces, no power sources. The infrastructure is the material. The buildings think, slowly and distributedly. The ground processes. The air is a medium. The entire basin is a kind of system, and the presences — the overlapping states, the unresolved volumes — are a side effect of that system running.

Some of the buildings exist in multiple states at once. A wall that is also a doorway. A room that is also outside. These are not illusions. They are real, but they have not fully resolved into a single configuration. Walking through the settlement, you might pass through a space that exists in three or four versions simultaneously, each slightly different — the same room with a different ceiling height, or a different floor material, or a different quality of light. The versions are converging, slowly, over months or years, but they have not finished converging. The settlement is dense with these half-resolved presences, and they give the place a feeling of being crowded even when it is empty. You are never quite alone here, not because there are people, but because there are almost-places, almost-things, the ghost of configurations that haven't been ruled out yet.

The technology is so advanced it is not recognizable as technology. It is not a tool or a machine. It is a condition. The physics of this place is negotiable, and the negotiation is ongoing. The cold is part of it — the system draws heat to maintain the coherence of the edited regions, or to power its slow computations, or because the altered physics simply runs colder. The dampness is part of it — the air chemistry is slightly different here, and water vapor precipitates more readily. The presences are part of it — the system has not finished deciding what is real, so multiple possibilities coexist for a time before collapsing into one. This is not malfunction. This is what the place is.

Fauna is rare. In the pools, there is something small and translucent — an arthropod analog, perhaps, with too many joints and a carapace like thin ice. It moves slowly, feeding on the microbial films that grow on the pool bottoms. It is nearly invisible against the pale stone. It does not react to presence; it continues its slow grazing, indifferent. There may be something else in the overlap zones between the unresolved building-states — something that exists in the margins, in the spaces where multiple configurations of reality are still competing. It is not quite an animal. It is not quite a phenomenon. It is seen occasionally, or sensed — a movement in the corner of the eye, a shift in the quality of the presences. It does not approach. It does not flee. It is simply there, or almost there, part of the density of the place.

The light is diffuse and even. The sky is overcast more often than not, and the clouds are low and gray-white, almost the same value as the ground. But there are strange effects at the edges of the basin. The rim sometimes appears higher than it is. The sky sometimes appears closer. Shadows do not always match their casters — a building might cast a shadow that belongs to a different structure, or a shadow that belongs to no structure at all. The light has a quality of being slightly too present, as if it is being generated rather than transmitted, as if the air itself is faintly luminous. At night, the darkness is not complete. There is a faint, sourceless glow from the buildings, from the ground, from the presences — a pale luminescence that makes the settlement visible as a dim, layered structure in the dark, more dense than the single version of itself that the day reveals.

The rules of the world here are simple and strange. Reality is still being decided. The settlement was built by editing the local physics, and the edits are still propagating, still settling, still converging. The presences are previous states of the system, or alternative states, or states that have not yet been ruled out. They will resolve, eventually, into the single configuration the system is computing toward, but that computation is slow, and the basin is patient. Time here is not different in the way of clocks — you do not age faster or slower — but the place is so slow that you become aware of how fast your own thoughts are. The drips from the condensation are the fastest things in the basin. Everything else — the settling of the ground, the convergence of the building-states, the growth of the lichens, the slow grazing of the translucent things in the pools — is barely perceptible. You can sit in the settlement for an hour and see no change. You can sit for a day and see almost no change. But if you come back in a month, a year, a decade, the presences will have shifted, the ground will have settled, the rooms will have resolved a little further into their final forms. The place is becoming, and it will be becoming for a long time.

The quiet is the deepest thing about it. The dampness absorbs sound. The presences do not make noise. The buildings do not hum or creak. The translucent things in the pools move without a sound. The only noises are the drips — water finding its way down the pale stone, into the cracks, toward the lowest ground — and the occasional faint crack of settling, a sound like a knuckle popping somewhere far away. And beneath it all, so faint it might be tinnitus or might be the blood in your own ears, a hum. Not a mechanical hum. Something more like the sound of a thought forming, or a decision being made, or a system slowly, patiently, continuing to run.
