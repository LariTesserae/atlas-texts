The rock is warm.

This is the first thing you notice, when you arrive — the warmth of the stone beneath your boots, body-temperature, steady, like touching skin. It is not hot. It is simply *warm*, as if the plateau has been sitting in mild sun for a very long time and has not yet cooled. But it has been billions of years. The warmth should have dissipated. It has not.

The Varn is old. It is among the oldest exposed surfaces on the planet — a plateau of dark, fine-grained stone, worn down over geological time until it is nearly flat. The mountains that once stood here are gone. The higher plateaus that once rose above this one are gone. What remains is this: a vast, gently undulating surface, high above the surrounding lands, edged with cliffs that drop into cloud. The rock has been polished by time to a soft, almost organic smoothness. There are no sharp edges, no fresh fractures. Everything is rounded, worn, settled into its final form. The stone carries faint striations and grooves — not fossils, not crystals, just patterns, like the grain in old wood, like something written in a language no one remembers. The people who live here have always been here. They do not ask about the patterns. They are part of the Varn, as the moss is part of the Varn, as the mist is part of the Varn.

---

The plateau is high — so high that the air is thin and breathing is a conscious act. The city sits near the center of it, where the rock is flattest and the mist collects most thickly. The city is not large. Perhaps twenty thousand people, perhaps fewer. It is dense, layered, built close together — not sprawling, but compressed, as if the buildings are huddling for warmth against the vast openness of the plateau. The architecture is blunt and functional: low, broad structures with thick walls and small windows, made from the same dark stone as the ground beneath them. The buildings are quarried from the plateau and shaped by machines that are old and quiet, and they seem to grow out of the rock rather than sit upon it. There is no clear boundary between the natural surface and the built one. A street becomes a floor becomes a wall becomes a cliff-face becomes a street again.

Moss covers everything. It is the dominant form of life here — thick, spongy, deep green and grey and silver, growing in layered carpets over every surface. The people clear it from the paths and the machine-shafts, but elsewhere it grows unchecked, softening edges, absorbing sound, holding moisture. Lichens hang from the few trees — stunted, wind-shaped things with trunks no thicker than an arm, their branches spreading horizontally like frozen green smoke. Ferns unfurl in the cracks and gutters. The city is being slowly reclaimed, and the people let it happen. They touch the walls as they walk, the way you might touch a familiar face. The moss is damp and cool under the fingers. The stone beneath it is warm.

---

The mist is nearly perpetual. It rolls in from the cliffs, thick and slow, and settles over the plateau like a second surface. The sun is a vague brightening behind the clouds — a diffuse glow with no source you can point to. At midday, the light has the quality of deep twilight: shadowless, soft-edged, blue-grey. At night, it is dark, but not completely. The mist catches and holds what little light there is, and the city's windows glow amber against it, warm light spilling from the rock-powered generators into the fog, diffusing, softening. There are no sharp shadows anywhere. Everything is slightly blurred, as if seen through water.

Water comes from the mist. There are no rivers on the plateau — the water table lies far below, and the stone is impermeable. The people collect the fog in fine-meshed nets strung between poles and hung from the eaves of buildings, in wide shallow pans of dark stone set on rooftops and in squares, in channels carved into the rock that lead to cisterns. The mist condenses on these surfaces and drips, and the drip is the loudest sound in the city — a steady, unhurried percussion, water falling into water. The collected water is cold, clean, faintly mineral, and it carries the warmth of the stone it rested on. It tastes of time. It tastes of the rock's long memory.

---

The air is thin, cool, damp. The people who live here have adapted over generations — they are tall and thin, with large lungs and slow heartbeats and body temperatures a degree below what the lowland physicians consider normal. They move deliberately, without hurry. They speak quietly, in a language full of long vowels and soft consonants that sounds like wind moving through wet grass. The thin air carries sound poorly, and the moss absorbs what remains, so the city is mostly quiet. You hear the drip of water, the hum of the machines, the occasional call of a bird — a small, dark, quick thing that lives in the mist and feeds on the slow, pale insects that crawl through the moss. There are no large animals. The ecosystem is dominated by the mosses, the lichens, the fungi. The people eat the fungi — cultivated varieties that grow in the warm caves below the city, on beds of composted moss and lichen, white and pale grey and faintly blue, nutritious and mild and earthy. The caves are humid and dark and warm. The fungi grow on the walls and ceilings, and the people harvest them with knives and dry them and store them in stone chambers. The staple of every meal. The taste of home.

---

The machines are old. They have been running for generations, maintained and repaired, their parts replaced one by one until it is unclear what remains of the originals. They are powered by the warmth of the rock — heat engines that draw on the gradient between the stone's steady warmth and the cold thin air above it, turning that difference into motion. The factories are underground, carved into the plateau, and they produce precision components: optical lenses, gyroscopic instruments, inertial guidance systems, things that require the cold, the thin air, the absolute stability of ancient stone. The products are small and valuable and shipped away by cable car, descending through cloud to the lowlands, where they are used for navigation, for astronomy, for war. The machines are quiet. They hum, very faintly, at a frequency just below hearing. You feel it in your teeth, in your bones, in the soles of your feet. The rock hums at the same frequency. Or perhaps the machines are humming the rock's frequency. Or perhaps, after so long, there is no difference.

---

The plateau extends. It is regional in scale — you can see across it to the cliffs at the edges, to the other features of the Varn. There are quarries where the rock is cut and shaped, the stone warm beneath the cutting tools. There are mist-collection fields, where the water is gathered in vast shallow pans that sit on the surface like mirrors of the sky. There are agricultural terraces, where the fungi are cultivated in long, low sheds built from the same dark stone. There are watchtowers at the edges, and viewing platforms where you can stand and look out over the cloud sea. The cloud sea is below, and sometimes it rises, and sometimes it falls, and sometimes you can see the lands beneath it — distant, hazy, green, another world. The cliffs are the edge of the world. The wind is stronger there, and the moss is thinner, and the rock is exposed, warm, smooth as skin. You can sit on the edge of the plateau and dangle your feet over the cloud sea and feel the warmth of the stone and the cool of the mist and the thinness of the air, and you can see the curve of the horizon, and the distant mountains, and the sky above, pale and vast and indifferent.

The city is dense, but the plateau is open. The density of the built environment gives way to the openness of the stone, and the openness is framed by the cliffs and the cloud sea. You can walk for hours across the plateau and not reach the edge. The city is one feature among many, and the many features are all part of the Varn.

---

The weirdness is in the warmth. It is not volcanic — there is no magma nearby, no hot springs, no geothermal vents. The rock is simply warm, as if it has been sitting in mild sun for a very long time and has not yet cooled. The people do not question it. They build with it, they sit on it, they sleep on it, they are warmed by it. They have grown up with the warmth and the hum and the slow drip of water, and these things are normal to them. But they are strange. The warmth has no explanation. The hum has no source. The patterns in the rock have no meaning. The mist thickens and thins, rises and falls, in cycles that do not quite match the day-night rhythm or the weather — something else drives it, some slow pulse in the stone, some respiration of the plateau. The people feel it. They live with it. They do not name it.

And the erosion — the deep, ancient, finished erosion — is the strangeness that underlies everything. The plateau is old, so old that it has reached its final form. The rock is still eroding, but at a rate of a millimeter per century, perhaps less. The plateau is settling into its end state. In a few million years, it will be gone, worn down to nothing, and the city will be gone with it, and the warmth will have cooled, and the mist will have found somewhere else to condense. The people know this. They live with it. The city is not permanent. Nothing is permanent. The rock is warm, and it will cool. The plateau is old, and it will disappear. The city is a temporary arrangement on an ancient, dying surface, and the people have made their peace with that. They live in the present. They touch the warm walls. They breathe the thin air. They eat the fungi. They watch the mist. They hear the drip of water and the hum of the machines and the faint, subsonic pulse of the rock. They do not hurry. There is no need to hurry. The Varn will be here for a while longer, and then it will not, and that is simply the way of things.

---

Everything is in motion, but slowly. The mist rolls in and out. The water drips and collects. The moss grows. The fungi spread. The machines hum. The rock is warm. The people move through the city, slowly, deliberately, touching the walls, speaking softly. The products are made and shipped away. The birds call. The insects crawl through the moss. The light shifts, dimly, behind the clouds. The pace is geological. The city is old, and the rock is older, and the mist is as old as the air. The Varn has been here for a long time, and it will be here for a while longer, and then it will be gone. For now, it is here. The rock is warm. The city is quiet. The moss is growing. The water is dripping. The machines are humming. The mist is rising. The Varn endures.
