The basin is a continent of wet, warm, living matter. It is low — barely above the level of the water that saturates it — and it is vast. The horizon is a rumor. The atmosphere is thick with moisture and spores, and the light is so faint that the edge of the world is never visible. You stand on the mat, and the mat is the ground, and the ground is alive.

The mat is a continuous crust of vegetation, tens of meters thick in places, formed from the accumulated bodies of countless generations of plants. It is not a collection of individuals; it is a single, coherent tissue, a living substrate that covers every surface, fills every hollow, encases every rock. The rock beneath is young — fresh volcanic stone, sharp and unweathered — but it is hidden, held in the grip of the mat. The mat is the world's skin, and the rock is its bone.

The mat is soft underfoot, but not yielding. It has the give of a dense foam, or of a living tissue. It is warm — warmer than the air, warmer than your skin. The warmth comes from below, from the rock, and it is conducted upward through the roots and the water. The mat is wet — not soaked, but saturated, like a sponge that has been squeezed and released. Water is held in the pores of the tissue, and it moves slowly, drawn by capillary action and by the pumps of the plants. If you press your hand into the mat, it comes away wet, and the water is warm.

The plants are not like the plants of a sunlit world. They are dark — deep purple, black, rust-red — because the light is faint and they have no need for bright pigments. They are warm, because the world is warmed from below, and their tissues are saturated with water. They are structured like machines: hollow stalks that pump fluids, broad leaves that condense and channel moisture, bladders that hold and mix chemicals, roots that extract and transport minerals. The whole ecosystem is a refinery, a factory, a chemical plant — but it is not built. It is grown.

The most prominent features are the distillation columns: tall, segmented stalks that rise from the mat like the pipes of an organ. Each stalk is a stack of chambers, and in each chamber, fluids are heated, separated, and recondensed. Warm sap rises from the roots, enters the base of the stalk, and is pumped upward by a slow, steady pressure — the heat of the rock below drives the circulation. At each chamber, some of the sap evaporates, condenses on the cool inner wall, and drips down to the next chamber. The stalks are warm to the touch, and if you press your ear against them, you can hear — or feel — the slow, silent pulse of the fluid moving inside. The pulse is measured in minutes, not seconds.

Around the base of the stalks, broad leaves spread out like trays, catching the moisture that condenses from the air. The leaves are grooved, channeling the water into the stalks. The leaves are also coated with a thin film of metal — copper, iron, zinc — deposited by electrochemical processes in the leaf tissue. The metal flakes off in patterns that look like writing, or circuitry, or the veins of a leaf. The flakes fall into collection pools, where they accumulate as a fine, bright powder. The pools are perfectly circular, perfectly still, fed by the drip from the leaves and drained by roots below. The water in them is pure — distilled by the plants, filtered through layers of tissue. The pools are mirror-like, reflecting the faint glow of the chemiluminescent plants that grow around their edges.

The chemiluminescent plants are scattered throughout the mat, but they are most dense around the pools and the vents. Their glow is a pale blue-green, the color of foxfire, or of decay. It is the only light in the world, apart from the faint, deep-red glow that filters down from the upper canopy. The glow is enough to see the immediate surroundings — the stalks, the leaves, the pools — but not enough to see far. The world beyond a few meters is darkness.

The air is warm and humid, thick with moisture and spores. The spores are released by towers — tall, thin structures that rise above the mat and open like flowers, releasing clouds of fine particles into the air. The spores are not for reproduction; they are for chemistry. They carry enzymes, minerals, and signals between the plants. They are part of the atmosphere, part of the weather. The air smells of metal and wet earth and something sweet — the scent of the plants' metabolism.

The weather is generated by the mat itself. The heat from below and the moisture from the plants create convection, and the convection creates storms. The storms are slow and vast — they move across the mat like thoughts, like breathing. Rain falls constantly in some areas, rarely in others. Fog is common, and the fog is thick enough to obscure everything beyond a few meters. The weather is part of the equilibrium: the same patterns repeat endlessly, and the plants have grown to fit them.

There is no sound, or almost none. The mat absorbs sound; the air is thick and dampens it. The only sounds are the occasional drip of water from a leaf, the soft creak of a stalk under its own weight, the hiss of a spore tower opening. Even these are rare. The world is silent. The silence is not empty; it is full of the presence of the living world. The silence is the sound of the factory running — a factory that runs so smoothly it makes no noise.

Nothing changes. The plants grow, but the pattern is stable. The stalks that are here today were here a thousand years ago, and they will be here a thousand years from now. The pools are at the same level. The storms follow the same paths. The system is in equilibrium — a steady state, a frozen moment that lasts forever. The only movement is the slow, imperceptible growth of the mat, and the slower, more imperceptible death and replacement of individual cells. But even this is balanced: growth equals decay, and the mat remains the same.

The rock beneath the mat is young. It emerged from the planet's interior a short time ago — a few million years, perhaps, or a few hundred thousand. It is sharp, angular, unweathered. The mat has grown over it, encasing it, holding it in place. In some places, the rock pushes through the mat — ridges, domes, fissures. The rock is warm, heated from below, and water weeps from its surface. The water is mineral-rich, and the plants around the rock are different — denser, smaller, more colorful. They are the chemosynthetic plants, drawing energy from the chemicals in the water.

The vents are the hearts of the local ecosystems. They are fissures in the rock where hot water seeps up from below. The water is rich in sulfur, iron, and other minerals. The plants around the vents are specialized — they extract the minerals, concentrate them, and excrete them in pure form. The vents are surrounded by deposits of bright minerals — yellow sulfur, red iron, blue copper — that accumulate in the mat. The deposits are harvested by the plants, or they accumulate, or they are washed away by the rain. The vents are active, but they are stable — they have been active for as long as the mat has existed, and they will continue to be active for as long as the mat exists.

If you cut through the mat — or if the mat cracks, as it sometimes does — you can see the layers. The top layer is the canopy: broad, flat leaves, overlapping like shingles. Below that is the understory: a dense network of stalks and stems, packed tight, forming a kind of scaffolding. Below that is the mat proper: a thick, spongy layer of dead and living tissue, saturated with water. Below that is the root layer: a network of thick roots, radiating from the stalks, carrying fluids and minerals. Below that is the mud: a layer of fine, black sediment, rich in organic matter. Below that is the water: the saturated zone, where the water table is at the surface. Below that is the rock: the young, sharp, warm rock.

The layers are distinct, like the floors of a building. Each layer has its own ecology, its own chemistry, its own purpose. The canopy captures light and condenses moisture. The understory transports fluids and provides structure. The mat stores water and nutrients. The roots extract minerals and distribute them. The mud filters and stores. The water carries dissolved chemicals. The rock provides heat and minerals. Together, they form a complete system — a factory, a refinery, a living machine.

The light is faint. If there is a sun, it is a dim, red star, barely visible through the thick atmosphere and the upper canopy. The light that reaches the surface is a deep, warm red — the color of blood, or of embers. It is not enough to see by, not really, but it is enough to make out shapes. The plants are dark against the slightly less dark sky. The chemiluminescent plants provide the only other light — a pale, cold blue-green, the color of foxfire, or of decay. The glow is enough to see the immediate surroundings — the stalks, the leaves, the pools — but not enough to see far. The world beyond a few meters is darkness.

Time is hard to measure. There is no day or night, no seasons, no change. The only time is the time of growth — the slow, imperceptible growth of the mat. The mat grows upward, but it also grows downward, and outward, and inward. It grows by adding layers, by extending roots, by thickening stalks. But it also dies, and decays, and is recycled. The growth and the decay are balanced. The mat is a steady state — a frozen moment that lasts forever.

The storms are the only clocks. They follow the same paths, at the same intervals, and they can be used to mark time. But the intervals are long — years, perhaps, or decades — and the storms are not precise. The only other clocks are the plants themselves: the stalks grow in segments, and each segment represents a period of growth. But the segments are not regular, and the growth is not constant. The only reliable measure of time is the mat itself: the thickness of the layers, the size of the stalks, the depth of the pools. But these change so slowly that they are useless for measuring anything shorter than a lifetime.

The rules of this world are the rules of chemistry and growth. The plants are not individuals; they are organs of a larger body. The mat is a single organism, or a collective, or a system — it is hard to say. It processes matter and energy with industrial efficiency, but it has no purpose, no goal, no builder. It is simply running. The industrial quality is not a sign of intelligence; it is a sign of efficiency. The plants have evolved — or grown — into the shapes that work, and the shapes happen to look like machines.

The strangeness of the place is the strangeness of a natural system that looks artificial. The stalks are too regular, the leaves too symmetrical, the pools too circular. The metal deposits on the leaves form patterns that look like writing, or circuitry, or the plans of a factory. But there is no plan. There is no designer. There is only the slow, blind, efficient growth of life in the dark.

There are no animals. No insects, no birds, no worms, no movement but the slow growth of the plants and the slow flow of the fluids. The world is pure vegetation, pure process, pure chemistry. It is a world without eyes, without ears, without mouths — a world that does not see, or hear, or eat, but only grows, and processes, and runs.

The stillness is the most unsettling thing about the place. It is not the stillness of death — the mat is alive, warm, and active. It is the stillness of a machine that is running perfectly, without vibration, without noise, without waste. The mat is in equilibrium, and the equilibrium is stable. Nothing changes because nothing needs to change. The system is complete, self-contained, self-sustaining. It has no outside, no environment, no context. It is the world, and the world is it.

The place is a world. It is a continent of wet, warm, living matter, a factory without builders, a machine without purpose, a garden without gardeners. It is vast and dark and silent and still. It is alive, but it is not busy. It is running, but it is not going anywhere. It is a system in equilibrium — a steady state that has lasted for as long as anything can remember, and that will last for as long as anything can imagine. The mat is the world, and the world is the mat.
