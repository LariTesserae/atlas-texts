# The Lumenflat

There is a floor older than the mountains above it.

The cavern is valley-sized, one of the great rooms of the world, and it has been wearing itself smooth since before anything alive had a name. The stone plain undulates in long, satin swells, polished by ages into something like the inside of a bowl. There are no sharp edges anywhere. Stalactites overhead have been sanded down to soft needles; fallen blocks have been rounded to loaves; even the debris of the city, generation after generation of it, has been ground into the floor until the streets themselves are dished, worn slightly concave by ten thousand years of bare and soft feet. The rock here is *finished*. Geology has completed its work. Whatever change still happens in the Lumenflat happens in life, not in stone.

There is no sun. The light is fungal.

The plain is a meadow of luminescence — glow-caps, candle-stalks, crusts and shelves and puffballs the size of a person's head, all of them glowing in cool shades of green, blue, silver, and slow violet. Colonies hang from the ceiling in their millions, so that the vault overhead is a false sky, hazed and bright, pulsing almost too slowly to see. At the height of the bloom-tide the whole cavern is lit like a cloudy noon: shadowless, sourceless, cold bright. Colors look rinsed. Breath shows as mist in the air — it is always cool here, brisk enough that the herd-folk go wrapped in mycelium-fleece even at high tide. When the bloom ebbs, the world sinks to a deep dusk, and the city's lamps carry the day.

The stone sweats. That is the water of the place — not rivers, not rain you stand in, but a pervasive damp: condensation sheeting the walls, gathering in the dimples of the floor, falling from the needle-stones in slow silver threads. Now and then, where the ceiling has cracked long ago into what the folk call **sky-seams**, true rain comes down in ropes like slow needles, and everyone stops to watch it. The water stands in worn basins across the plain — **drink-pans**, mirrors of the glowing sky where transparent crayfish pick their way and eyeless fish move like slivers of light. Sarn, the city, has cut channels and strung vast nets of fungal fiber between its towers to catch the condensation and lead it, drop by drop, into the cisterns. Water is never wasted. Water is counted.

No plant grows here. The world is entirely fungal, and it behaves like weather. Patches of turf erupt overnight, spread in silent green fire, and die back just as fast; slime-mold rivers migrate across the plain while no one watches; in the bloom-tide the air fills with a faint glittering dust, spore-snow, ash from a green and gentle burning. The map of the meadow changes week to week. The plain is never the same plain twice.

And it is crowded with life. Blind, pale, near-silent life. Beetles with carapaces like polished stone. Crickets that leap, freeze, leap. **Tumblers** — soft-footed, six-legged grazers the size of a pony — crop the fungal turf in herds of thousands, moving across the Lumenflat like a slow brown river, and when something large passes in the Dimmer beyond the glow's edge, the herds bunch and press together without a sound. The city keeps no dogs. It keeps tumblers. It keeps the fish, the crickets, the beetles; it keeps, above all, the quiet.

Because the stone swallows sound. That is the first rule of the Lumenflat, learned by every child: the rock is porous, thirsty, and a shout dies inside a dozen paces. The plain beyond the city is a theater of pure pantomime — you can watch a herd move, a bloom spread, a district cut back its invading turf, and hear nothing at all, only the slow performance of busyness. Even inside Sarn the hush is nearly total: the crunch of feet on sinter, the clink of a near tool, the creak of rope, the low voices of people who have no habit of raising them. The market churns all hours of the glow-cycle — thousands of bodies, gesturing, trading, haggling — and it is *silent*, a bazaar of mime.

So the folk speak in light. Their language is **gleam-speech**: patterns of glow, lantern-code, signals flashed across districts on polished sinter mirrors. Shopfronts hang with jars of captive luminescence, spelling their wares in colored script. A mother calls her child home with three flicks of a hand-lamp. The city at dusk is a constellation of small green fires, all of them saying things. Grief, warning, welcome, bargain — it is all done in light. The folk are pale, tall, large-eyed; they go barefoot or soft-shod; they work with stone, bone, chitin, fired clay, and a rare blue copper that is worn only as status, never as tool. There is no smoke in Sarn. There is no fire. The lamps are living things, fed and tended, and the food is fermented, raw, fungal. It is a city without flame, and the absence of it defines everything.

The city is layered the way the plain is old. Older quarters have been swallowed whole by flowstone and rebuilt on top of them; the great towers are ancient stalagmites encased in masonry; bridges leap the channels on arches that were old when the sky-seams first opened. The folk are always building, always cutting back, always hauling — the bloom is both crop and invasion, and a district can be overrun in a single pale season, turfed and roofed in glowing green, and must be cut out again. This is the turbulence of the place: not the stone, which is done, but the life on it — the bloom-tides that roll across the plain like silent storm fronts, bright walls of light racing at a walk; the spore-storms; the herds swirling; the market churning; the constant building and reaving; and sometimes, in the pale seasons, the glow nearly dies, and the city lives on its stored light and grows thin and careful, and everyone waits for the tide to turn.

A few strange things belong to the Lumenflat, the way other places have weather quirks. The dead are laid in the meadow, and the fungi thread the bones, and for a season the graves glow brighter than the turf — the **Bright Acre**, where the ancestors are literally luminous, and people go there to sit among them. On certain walls the condensation gathers in patterns like script, and the **Weep-readers** read it — omens, they say, though the stone is old and has no more messages to send. Once in a generation, perhaps, a sky-seam widens or the clouds above thin, and a true shaft of sunlight comes down into the cavern: one warm gold spear on the green-glowing plain, and every person in Sarn stands inside it, silent, face up, while the whole world holds its breath around them.

And beyond the turf's edge, the Dimmer: the dark that does not glow. Herders tell of shapes there that the light will not touch. The city does not speak of it. The city speaks in light, and the light does not go there.

The plain performs its busyness in silence. The glow-tide turns. The stone sweats. The dead shine gently in their acre, and the living move among them like slow, soft-footed keepers of a finished world — an ancient floor, a bright dark, a cool and quiet storm of green fire that never makes a sound.
