# The Nursery

You find it by a smell — wet stone and something sweeter, drifting out of a crack in the hillside that you'd have to turn sideways to enter. The last squeeze of the fissure is cold on your shoulders. Then the rock lets go of you, and the first thing is the light.

There shouldn't be light. You're well under the mountain, in a single chamber no bigger than a parlour — twenty paces across, a ceiling you could touch in the middle, two shallow alcoves like half-closed fists. But the room reads like a cloudy noon. You can see your hands, the grain of the rock, the veins in the stone. The light is everywhere and agrees with nothing: shadows fall in three directions at once. A moth crosses your vision and throws one shadow toward the floor and another toward the wall, and neither of them moves quite with it.

The light is alive. Curtains of small moths drift through the chamber in slow rivers, each one carrying on its wings and fur a film of glow-fungus — the same fungus that crusts the walls in patches, the same that silver-sheets the pool. Where the moths thicken, the air itself seems to shine; where they thin, the walls take over, a degree dimmer. The whole room pulses, slowly, like a lung. You feel it in your teeth before you see it — a pressure, a heartbeat from somewhere far below the floor.

Then the quiet. The chamber is so quiet that your own pulse in your ears is the loudest thing in it. A drip every forty seconds from the youngest stalactite. The tick of the ceiling settling — the fracture face up there is fresh, raw, recently broken, its edges not yet softened by any water, glinting with mica like scattered coins. The soft static of springtails. The low wing-hum of the moth-curtains, like a finger on the rim of a glass. And sometimes — not always — a sound returns that you haven't made yet: your boot scuffs the floor a half-breath before the scuff arrives. The room is young, and its memory hasn't learned the right order of things. Most people learn to move silently here. Most creatures already have.

Water is everywhere and nowhere. The walls sweat. The chamber is a condensation engine: the fissure breathes, the walls bead, the moisture runs in threads to the floor, and the floor channels it — along faint trails polished by feet too small to see — toward a single shallow pool at the room's centre. The pool is the warmest thing here. Silver. Slightly too still. Its surface shows you the chamber, but not the chamber as it is: the alcoves are in different places in the reflection, or there are more of them, or there is something standing where you are not. Don't look long. The pool doesn't mind, but it doesn't stop, either.

Around the pool runs a ring of pebbles, each one a different stone — greywacke, white quartz, a speckled red thing that matches nothing in this mountain. The ring is rebuilt after every hard round of drips. Nothing is ever seen doing the rebuilding. The moths drink at the pool's edge, and their reflections drink a half-second later.

The dripstone is a newborn. The stalactites are no thicker than pencils, each tipped with a bead of water that swells, glows faintly with its film of fungus, and falls — a falling spark — and the pool answers with a ring of light spreading outward. Sometimes a bead lets go upward. The ceiling drinks itself back. No one has seen the first drip that made the first stalactite. The chamber is still deciding how tall it wants to be.

The west wall holds a hamlet of mud pots, each the size of a thumb, built by something caddisfly-like but larger. Some are occupied. Some are empty. Some have been unzipped from the inside. Under the ceiling lip hangs a row of paper cocoons, each lit from within by a dim green lamp — pupae that glow until they don't; a couple have already gone dark, a couple have holes. On the flat slab beside the pool, scratches: many, parallel, deliberate. Tally, or song. Fresh ones cut through older ones, and all of them weather quickly, because time moves fast near the pool. A mound of clay and chewed grit the size of a loaf sits in the north corner, warm to the touch; inside it, a nest of shed skins and glowing fungus. Something broods there, or something has finished brooding. On a ledge, a small skull — bird, maybe, or bat — with the cranium slightly too large.

Nothing here is technology. Everything is built by mouths, by enzymes, by patience. And yet the pebble ring and the scratch-songs leave a question open, the way the room leaves all questions open.

The springtails rise like smoke when anything large moves. Crickets with long legs stand in the open, listening. Beetles graze the wall-films in slow arcs. Spiders have strung webs between the stalactite tips, and the webs glow with trapped motes — the spiders harvest light. In the pool, silver shrimps swim tail-first, as if rewinding. The salamander is pale and large as a forearm, translucent at the edges, slow; it has known you were coming since before you entered — the early echoes told it. And there is the centipede: the colour of candle wax, a forearm long, hunting the moth-curtains in straight lines — stop, go, stop. The moths thin where it passes. The light develops gaps. The room's pulse stutters. It is the reason for the quiet.

Nothing here has decided what it is yet. Several moths carry one wing-pattern on the left wing and a different one on the right, as if painted by two hands. The salamander's young are born with differing numbers of toes. A beetle near the mound seems to be two beetles, offset by a finger's width. Time is loose here, so evolution is fast. The chamber is a nursery not only of the mountain.

And the rules, such as they are: time runs in currents. Near the pool it races — you can watch fungal filaments thread across a damp patch over the course of a conversation. In the north corner it pools, thick — a moth hanging there beats its wings once a minute. The walls sweat in scripts and maps and almost-faces, hold for a while, then slide or forget. Speak, and you will hear yourself a half-breath early; better to whisper. The corners of the room are a degree off from where geometry says they should be. The far wall measures differently the second time you pace it. The entrance is always still there when you look back — but its height has changed, so it's better not to check.

This is what the place is: a room still becoming. The stone is raw. The dripstone is in its infancy. The species are provisional. The light is only just learning to be light. Through the ceiling crack, pale rootlets reach down, blind, tasting the glow — the world above, intruding. At the fissure mouth, where a little outside daylight leaks in, there is moss: the only green in the chamber, and the only thing here that is not strange.

Sit still long enough and the room will begin to forget whether you arrived or whether you hatched. The salamander will rise and reposition itself, patiently, closer. A moth will land on your hand and dim itself to match your warmth. The pool will hold the chamber's face, faintly wrong, faithfully. And above the ceiling, out of sight, a bead of water gathers, gathers — and decides, down or up — while the Nursery, listening, leans a fraction closer to being whatever it will be.
