The water comes first. It is everywhere, and it is warm — not hot, not cold, but body-warm, blood-warm, the temperature of something living and metabolizing. It fills the basin to a depth of anywhere between a hand's breadth and a man's height, and it is never still. It moves, slowly, from the vents in the deep places toward the outflow at the eastern rim, and in moving it lays down what it carries. The basin is a caldera, or a graben, or a wound in the world that has not yet decided what it is — the geology is young and the stone is still becoming. The floor is not floor but process: a slow accretion of minerals, layer on layer, the water depositing its load as it cools and spreads and evaporates and seeps back into itself. The result is terraces. Shallow, curving, irregular terraces, stepping down from the rim to the deepest water, like the growth lines of a bone or the rings of a tree laid flat. They are new. They are soft enough to mark with a fingernail, firm enough to stand on, pale as old teeth or fresh cream or the inside of a shell. And they are growing. A rim that was knee-high last year is waist-high now. A pool that was ankle-deep is chest-deep. The change is too slow to watch, but return in a season and the difference is plain. The stone is coming into being.

The light is dim. The basin sits high, but the sky above it is thick — with cloud, with vapor, with the warm breath of the water rising and condensing and rising again in a slow cycle that has been going on for longer than the settlement and will go on for longer still. The sun is a bright place behind the white, a direction without a shape. Shadows are soft, edges are soft, the world is softened. The water catches what light there is and holds it, so the basin glows from below as much as from above — a diffuse, warm, directionless luminosity, as if the stone itself were faintly lit. At night the glow does not entirely die. The water holds the day's warmth and the minerals in it hold a faint phosphorescence, or the bacteria do, or something does — the basin is never fully dark. The stars, when the cloud breaks, are very clear and very close, because the air is thin at this height and the vapor is below, and they seem nearer than they have any right to be.

The settlement is built on the upper terraces, where the stone is oldest and the water thinnest. It is a town, or a city, or a long village — it depends on how you count, and the people who live there have their own words for it. The buildings are of the same stone as the terraces, cut from them and shaped with blades of copper and bronze and hardened wood. The walls are thick, the roofs are flat, the windows are small and face inward toward courtyards or channels. The settlement is dense — the structures packed close, separated by narrow walkways and by the channels that bring water in and carry it away. The channels are the streets. The walkways are raised, or floating, or built of woven plant matter laid over the stone. In places the settlement has grown up in layers — a second story built over a first, a bridge over a channel, a platform over a pool — the construction following the terraces as they rise. The people build slowly, because the stone is slow, and they build *for* the stone. They cut where the terrace is ready to be cut, they shape where the accretion has made shaping possible, they fit their walls to the curves of the growing rims. Nothing is straight. Nothing is level. A wall that was plumb a generation ago has begun to lean, because the stone beneath it has grown unevenly. A floor that was flat has developed a gentle curve, a dish, a shallow bowl that catches the warm water and must be channeled away. The people account for this. They have always accounted for this. The settlement is not imposed on the place. It is part of the place's becoming.

The vegetation is lush where it can take hold. The warm, mineral-rich water feeds things that other water cannot. Mats of bacteria and algae color the shallows — cream, orange, rust, a deep arterial red, a pale green like the skin of a leaf held to the light — spreading over the stone in slow tides of color. Higher plants grow at the water's edge and in the shallower pools: reeds, rushes, something like papyrus, something like lotus but not lotus, their roots dangling into the warm water, their leaves spread wide to catch the dim light. In places the vegetation has been cultivated — raised beds of woven reeds filled with sediment hauled up from the channels, floating gardens anchored to the terraces with cords of plant fiber, slow canals planted with dense stands of edible or useful plants. The people cut and weave and plant, and the plants grow quickly in the warmth, unfurling new leaves in days, flowering in weeks, setting seed in a season. The edges of the basin, where the water is thinner and the stone is older, are thick with green — a lush fringe around the pale stone and the warm water, a tangle of root and stem and leaf that rustles in the faint wind and drips with condensation.

The animals are few. The water is warm and heavy with minerals, and most things cannot live in it. There are fish — small, pale, slow, with fins like fans and mouths like filters — that graze the bacterial mats in the deeper pools, moving in loose, unhurried schools. There are insects at the edges, and birds that come to feed on the insects, and something like a crab or a crayfish that scuttles in the shallows, its shell crusted with mineral deposits so that it looks like a moving stone. The people keep a few animals — a small, tough breed of goat that can tolerate the mineral water, a kind of waterfowl that nests in the reeds, a fish that has been encouraged in the channels and is harvested with baskets. But the place is not rich in animal life. The dominant presences are the stone, the water, and the slow green of the plants.

The sound is quiet. The water does not rush or crash — it seeps, it spreads, it drips from terrace to terrace in thin sheets and thin streams, a sound like breathing or like the whisper of a large room. The vents in the deep places make a low, continuous murmur, too low to hear clearly, felt in the chest more than the ears. The wind is muffled by the basin walls and the thick air. The people move slowly and speak quietly, and their tools — the copper blade on stone, the wooden pestle in the mortar, the woven reed being cut — make small, contained sounds. The loudest noise is the occasional crack of new stone forming, a sound like a knuckle popping or a branch snapping, or the splash of a channel being opened, or the call of a bird at the rim. The silence is not empty. It is full of the small sounds of slow processes, the tick of mineral accretion, the drip of condensation, the shift of stone settling into its new shape.

The strangeness is in the combination, and in the details. The water is not like other water. It leaves a film on the skin, a residue of minerals that catches the light and smells faintly of sulfur, or of metal, or of something else — a warm, organic, slightly rotten smell that is not unpleasant, that becomes almost sweet with long acquaintance. The stone is not like other stone. It is warm to the touch, even in the air, because the water that made it was warm and the warmth stays in it, so the people sleep on stone platforms that hold the day's heat through the cold high-altitude night. It is slightly porous, slightly soft, and it grows — a wall that was level last year is not level now, a step that was flat has a slight curve, a channel that was straight has begun to bow, and the water in it has begun to pool where it should flow. The light is wrong — dim, diffuse, sourceless, coming from everywhere and nowhere, so that faces are hard to read and distances hard to judge. The silence is wrong — too deep, too full, too patient, a silence that seems to be listening. The warmth is wrong — it comes from below, from the water and the stone, not from the sun, so the air can be cool while the ground is warm, and the people move through a world of contradictions, their heads in the chill and their feet in the heat.

And there is something else, something the people know and do not speak of directly, or speak of only in certain ways, in stories, in the names they give to the terraces and the channels and the deep pools. The place is making itself. The stone grows, the water rises, the terraces extend, and the people are part of this — their channels guide the water, their cuts shape the stone, their waste feeds the bacteria, their breath adds to the vapor that rises and falls — but they are not in control. The place is becoming something, and they are becoming with it. Some say the basin is a body and the terraces are its bones and the water is its blood, and that the vents are mouths and the water is a voice, too slow to hear, speaking a language of mineral and warmth. Some say the stone is a record and the growth lines are a script, and that the place is writing something that will take ten thousand years to finish. Some say the people were made for this place, or the place was made for the people, or that there is no difference. The people do not agree on what it means, but they agree that it means. The settlement is not just a settlement. It is a participation in something larger and slower than themselves, a collaboration with a process that does not know they exist but could not proceed as it does without them.

The time of the place is long. The terraces grow over years, the water level changes over decades, the settlement expands over generations. The people build for their grandchildren, and their grandchildren build for theirs. A channel dug in one lifetime may not reach its destination in that lifetime. A terrace that is being prepared for building may not be ready for fifty years. The people are patient. They have to be. The place teaches patience, or it kills the impatient. The work is slow, the food is slow, the light is slow, the warmth is slow. The basin does not hurry, and neither do its people. They rise with the dim light, work in the warm water, eat what the plants and the fish and the bacteria provide, and sleep on the warm stone under the close stars, and in the morning the terraces have grown a little, and the water has risen a little, and the settlement is a little different from what it was, and they begin again.
