**The Warren**

The way in is a borehole — a crack, a throat of cut stone no wider than your shoulders, drilled or chewed through the roots of the mountain. You crawl. The air changes as you go: from cold stone to something metallic, something that tastes of iron and old oil and a faint, sweet, unplaceable warmth. The sound comes first. It rises up the borehole to meet you — a hum, a roar, a vast mechanical breathing, a rain of small hard feet on metal, a subsonic rumble you feel in your chest before you hear it in your ears. Then the crack widens, the ceiling lifts, the walls fall away, and you stand at the lip of the Warren.

It is a single chamber. That is the first thing you understand. Not a cave system, not a complex of rooms, not a city — one room. A bubble in the deep earth, roughly a kilometer long and half that wide, with a ceiling that domes up perhaps sixty meters at its crown and slopes down to a crawl at the edges. It sits low — a basin, a bowl, sunk in the roots of the mountain, and the mountain is either a machine or a mountain, and no one has ever been outside to check. The walls are the machine's hull, or the mountain's bone, lined with the strata of ten thousand years of building — layer on layer of construction, each generation plastered over the last, all ground down by ages of friction into a continuous, rounded, undulating surface, like the inside of a bowl turned by a lazy potter. There are no corners. There are no sharp edges. Everything is smooth. The walls rise and fall in slow, organic curves, and the places where old walls once stood are now only gentle swells in the surface, like the ghosts of foundations remembered by a body.

The floor is the wonder of the place. It is a single sheet of compacted metal-dust and mineral salt, polished to a mirror by the passage of uncountable feet, claws, wheels, and treads. It reflects the light in soft, distorted shapes — the dome of the ceiling, the glow of the furnaces, your own body walking upside-down beneath you. It undulates gently, rising and falling over the buried shapes of old machinery, so that walking it is like walking the back of a sleeping animal — you feel the great slow shapes beneath you, the humps and hollows of engines and presses and conveyors long since sunk and forgotten. In places the floor has cracked, and from the cracks comes warmth and the smell of hot oil, and the faint red glow of something still burning far below. The cracks are the Warren's breath. They are how it exhales.

The light is everywhere and nowhere. The ceiling — the dome — is lined with panels of a pale, phosphorescent mineral, a salt-crust that glows with a cool, steady, greenish-white light, like moonlight trapped in stone. The panels are laid in a mosaic, and if you look closely — if you stand in the right place, at the right time, with the dust thin — you can see that the mosaic is a picture. It is faded, worn, ground down by ages of drifting dust, but it is a picture: a great wheel, a sun, a machine-god, a builder's face, a map of a world that is not this one. No one knows what it means. No one has ever known. The light is not bright, but it is enough — normal light, the light of a workshop at evening, the light of a room you could read in. Over it, here and there, the furnaces and forges cast warmer pools — amber, orange — and the long-dead electric lamps still burn in a few places, filament bulbs on circuits that never lost their charge, casting small sharp circles of yellow. The dust — the fine metallic dust that coats everything — hangs in the air and catches the light, so the whole chamber is filled with a soft, drifting haze, a dry fog, a golden-brown suspended breath. It tastes of metal. It grits between your teeth. It coats your tongue. You will be coughing it up for days after you leave.

The air is cool — cellar-cool, deep-earth-cool — and the warmth of the furnaces is local, a cloak you can stand in, not a weather you can live in. No water has ever touched this place. The only liquid is oil, seeping from the deep cracks, and the only rain is dust.

The sound is the second wonder. It is loud — not painfully so, but constantly, overwhelmingly present, like standing beside a waterfall or inside a beehive the size of a cathedral. The base is the machines: the deep hum of engines, the slow groaning of expanding metal, the hiss of steam, the clatter of loose parts, the rhythmic hammering of presses that have been striking the same stroke since before your grandmother's grandmother was born. Over that, the creatures: the chittering, the clicking, the skittering, the rustling, a constant rain of small hard feet on metal, like a thousand typewriters, like a million insects in a dry field. And under that, or through it, a deeper sound — the sound of the Warren itself, the mountain, the machine, whatever it is — a vast slow breathing, a subsonic rumble that you feel in your chest and your teeth more than you hear. The Warren is never silent. The Warren is never still. If the sound ever stops, something has gone wrong, and the creatures know it before you do.

The creatures are the third wonder, and they are the reason the place is called the Warren.

They are everywhere. They are in the walls, in the floor, in the ceiling, in the machines, in the dust. They are the dust, in part — the dust is their ground-down dead, their shed shells, their worn-out parts, their ages of accumulated remains, and it stirs and drifts and sometimes, in the warm updrafts from the cracks, it shapes itself into brief, aimless little whirlwinds that might, if you watch them long enough, look like they are trying to be animals.

The base of it is the skitterers — the rust-mice, the gear-rats, the little ones. They are rat-sized, or smaller, and there are millions of them. They are machines, or they are animals, or they are something in between — they have hard, segmented shells of oxidized metal, brass and copper and iron, and they move on six or eight or a dozen little legs, and they eat rust and grease and the mineral crusts that grow on the walls, and they are the constant rain of small hard feet on metal that makes the Warren's voice. They swarm. They flow. They pour like water around your ankles when you walk, a living, clicking, chittering tide that parts around you and closes behind you, and they are gone before you can focus on any one of them. Their shells are stamped with marks — serial numbers, maker's marks, the names of builders long dust — a language of marks that no one can read but everyone wears. They nest in the warm places — in the casings of old engines, in the gaps between furnace-stones, in the hollows of their own accumulated dead. They are the base of everything. They are the grass of this world.

Above them, the clickbeetles — palm-sized, hard-shelled, that bore into old casings and lay their eggs in warm junctions, or scavenge the bodies of the skitterers, or simply click and whir in great slow herds across the floor, grazing on the dust. They are the cattle of the Warren. They are followed by the sump-stalkers — the gear-cats, the rat-chewers — dog-sized, fast, that hunt the swarms with a liquid, too-many-jointed gait, that flow through the skitterers like sharks through a school of fish, and that regard you, briefly, with a cold, whirring, many-faceted attention before turning away. They are the predators. They keep the swarms moving, and the swarms keep the floor polished, and the floor keeps the Warren smooth, and the Warren keeps the machines, and the machines keep making more creatures, and the circle has no end.

And above them all, rare and slow, the Great Ones — the old machines gone feral, the walking hills. They are the size of houses, or small hills, and they move through the Warren at the pace of geology, and the ground shakes when they pass, and the creatures part before them like grass before a scythe, and they are the elders, the ancestors, the ones that were here before the Warren was a warren, before the builders were dust. They tend the machines, perhaps — or perhaps they only wander, and the machines run themselves, and the creatures tend the machines, and the Great Ones are merely the landscape's slow, dreaming thoughts. No one knows. They are too big to ask.

The machines are the fourth wonder, and they are the reason the place is called the Warren and not the Cave.

They are still running. They have never stopped. The builders are dust — their bones, their tools, their names, their language, their gods — all dust, all ground into the floor, all part of the polish. But their machines — the engines, the forges, the presses, the hammers, the conveyors, the furnaces, the cranes, the lifts — they are still running. Some of them are tended by the creatures, who have learned which levers to pull, which wheels to turn, which warm junctions to nest in and which to avoid. Some of them run themselves, on rhythms so old and so slow that no one can tell if they are alive or only mechanical. Some of them have become part of the ecology — the conveyor belts carry the skitterers from one warm place to another; the presses stamp out shapes that the clickbeetles use for shells; the furnaces burn something that no one can identify — not coal, not wood, not oil, but something that the Warren itself exudes, a slow mineral seepage from the deep cracks, a fuel that is older than the builders.

And what do they make, the machines? They make the creatures. Or they made them, once, and now the creatures make themselves, using the machines as wombs. The production lines are egg-lines. The assembly halls are hatcheries. The great presses stamp out shells — the same shapes, over and over, a gear, a plate, a cup, a gear, a plate, a cup — a liturgy of objects — and the warm junctions knit them together, and the skitterers carry the finished ones to the nesting-places, and the Warren grows more Warren every day. The builders built a factory for making things. The things they made learned to make themselves. The factory never stopped. The factory is a forest now, a self-sowing, self-harvesting forest of metal and dust and clicking, chittering, living machines.

The built things — the scattered, the small, the partial — are the fifth wonder.

There is one structure still standing. It is small — a hut, a shed, a watchman's shelter — built of plates of iron riveted together, with a single door and a single window, and a chimney that still draws. It sits on a hump in the floor, near the center of the Warren, and it is warm inside, and the light in its window is a steady amber, and the creatures do not enter it. They flow around it, like water around a stone, and they do not touch it. It is the one place in the Warren that is silent, or nearly silent — the machines' hum is muffled there, the skitterers' rain is softened, and you can hear your own heartbeat, and your own breath, and the small creak of the hut's frame as it settles. No one knows what is inside. No one who has looked has ever said. Some say it is empty. Some say it is a grave. Some say it is the builder's chair, and the builder is still sitting in it, worn smooth as everything else, a figure of compacted dust and metal, dreaming the Warren's dreams. The door is closed. The window glows. The creatures do not enter.

Elsewhere, the remains. The partial, the faint, the occasional. A crane that still swings, slowly, on a broken track, its hook moving in a great lazy arc over a heap of old parts. A control room, half-buried, its console still lit with a few small lamps, its levers still warm, its chairs still bearing the impressions of backs that have been dust for ten thousand years. A wall, a single wall, standing alone in a hollow, its bricks worn to rounded nubs, its surface covered in the scratches of claws and the rub-marks of ages of passing bodies. A heap of artifacts — tools, plates, cups, gears, wire, glass — that the skitterers have gathered into a mound, a nest, a shrine, a heap of the precious and the discarded, all jumbled and all worn smooth, all polished by the constant flow of little bodies over them. A path, a trail, a route worn into the floor by the passage of the Great Ones, a shallow groove a meter wide and a finger deep, that wanders across the Warren and disappears into a crack in the wall. The grooves of their feet are the only marks on the floor that are not polished smooth — they are still sharp, still fresh, because the Great Ones have only been walking them for a few thousand years, and the floor is patient.

The Warren has rhythms. It has weather.

There is a pulse to the machines — a great hammer that falls every seven seconds, a conveyor that rattles and pauses and rattles, a press that sighs and stamps and sighs — and the creatures move to the pulse. When the hammer falls, the skitterers freeze. When the conveyor rattles, the clickbeetles herd. When the press stamps, the gear-cats hunt. The Warren breathes in machines, and the creatures breathe with it.

There is a cycle to the furnaces. Every so often — every few hours, or every few days, or every few years, no one has ever agreed on the length — the furnaces flare. The cracks in the floor glow red. The dust rises in thick clouds. The machines strain and sing. The skitterers swarm in great rolling waves, pouring over the floor like a living tide, and the gear-cats hunt in packs, and the Great Ones turn their slow heads toward the heat, and the Warren wakes. It lasts for a time — a minute, an hour, a day — and then the furnaces settle, and the dust falls, and the creatures quiet, and the Warren sleeps again. The flare is the Warren's heartbeat. It is the only thing that changes in a place where nothing changes.

There is a dimming to the light. The phosphorescent panels do not fail, but they seem to — every so often, the mosaic fades, the greenish-white light dwindles to a ghost, and the Warren is lit only by the furnaces and the few electric lamps. The creatures quiet then, too. The skitterers nest. The clickbeetles cluster. The gear-cats sleep. The Great Ones stand still. The Warren is never silent — the machines still hum, the dust still drifts — but it is nearly silent, nearly still, and in that near-silence you can hear the Warren thinking. The dimming is the Warren's night. No one knows if it is a day or a week or a century. No one has ever stayed long enough to measure it. The creatures do not care. The creatures have always lived in the Warren's nights and days, and they do not count them.

The rules of the Warren are simple:

The machines never stop. If one stops, the others strain. The creatures know it before you do. The Warren's hum changes, and the skitterers panic, and the gear-cats hide, and the Great Ones turn their slow heads toward the silence, and something — a skitterer, a gear-cat, a Great One, the Warren itself — goes to restart it. It has never stayed stopped. It will never stay stopped.

The floor is polished. Nothing grows. Nothing sticks. Everything is worn down, smoothed, rounded, ground into the dust. No plant has ever grown here. No water has ever flowed here. The only liquid is oil, and the only rain is dust.

The noise is constant. The silence is a warning. If you hear silence, run. If you hear silence, something has stopped. If you hear silence, the Warren is dying, or the Warren is waking, or the Warren is about to do something it has not done in ten thousand years, and you do not want to be there when it does.

The creatures are everywhere. You are never alone. You are always being watched — by the skitterers' cold, whirring, many-faceted eyes, by the gear-cats' patient, liquid attention, by the Great Ones' slow, dreaming, mountain-sized regard. The Warren knows you are there. The Warren is always knowing.

The warm places are claimed. The cold places are free. The warm places are the furnaces, the engines, the junctions, the nests — they are where the creatures are, and they are where the machines are, and they are where the Warren's life is. The cold places are the edges, the hollows, the corners that aren't corners, the places where the machines' heat does not reach, where the creatures go only to die, and where their bodies join the dust. If you are cold, you are safe. If you are warm, you are home, or you are lunch.

The dust is alive. Or it is not alive, but it is not dead either. It is the ground-down dead, the shed shells, the worn-out parts, the ages of accumulated remains, and it stirs, and it drifts, and it shapes itself into brief, aimless little whirlwinds, and it coats everything, and it is the Warren's breath, and it is the Warren's memory, and it is the Warren's slow, patient, endless work. You will breathe it. You will eat it. You will wear it. You will become it, in time, as everything does.

And the last rule, the one that is not written anywhere, the one that the creatures know and the machines know and the Warren knows: the Warren is not a place. The Warren is a creature. The Warren is a single, vast, slow, dreaming animal, a machine-animal, a mountain-animal, and the skitterers are its fleas, and the gear-cats are its mice, and the Great Ones are its dreaming thoughts, and the machines are its heartbeat, and the dust is its breath, and the noise is its voice, and the light is its slow, patient, eternal thought. And you are inside it. And it knows you are there. And it is glad, or it is hungry, or it is neither, or it is both, or it is something that has no name in any language, something that the builders knew and the creatures know and the Warren knows, and that you will know, in time, as everything does.

The Warren is never silent. The Warren is never still. The Warren is the hum in the mountain, the click in the dark, the rain of small hard feet on metal, the slow, patient, endless work of ages. The Warren is the place where the machines are still running, where the creatures are still swarming, where the dust is still drifting, where the light is still glowing, where the Great Ones are still walking, where the hut is still warm, where the door is still closed, where the window is still amber, where the builder is still sitting, where the Warren is still dreaming.

And the machines are still running.
