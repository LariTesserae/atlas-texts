## The Nursery Basin

It sits high — you feel it in your lungs, in the thinness of the air, in the way sound carries differently here, flattened and close. A basin perhaps eight or nine kilometers across, ringed by peaks that are themselves unfinished: not mountains but accumulated *suggestions* of mountains, strata that haven't decided whether they're rock or something else, ridgelines that fade into a kind of visual fog where the world hasn't finished rendering itself.

The light is dim and copper-colored. The sun — if it is a sun — hangs low and fixed, or moves so slowly you can't perceive it moving. It might be a lamp. It might be a hole in something. It casts no hard shadows. Everything looks seen through deep water, or through glass that's been breathed on.

The ground is young. You know this the way you know a wound is fresh. The rock is pale — bone-white, ash-gray, the color of uncooked ceramic — and sharp-edged. It hasn't been rounded by weather yet. It hasn't been stained by lichen except in small patches, as if the lichen were testing the idea. The soil, where it exists, is a thin, dark, crumbly film that hasn't separated itself from the rock beneath. It's more scab than soil. The whole basin smells of warm stone and wet minerals and something else — something sweet and organic, like fruit beginning to turn.

Water is everywhere, but it hasn't organized itself yet. It condenses on every surface — the undersides of rocks, the backs of leaves, the curves of the structures. It weeps from the basin walls in thin vertical lines that might become streams in another thousand years. It rises through the porous young rock and pools in every depression, forming shallow, warm, mineral-rich ponds with no outlet, no inlet, no purpose yet. The air is saturated. Your skin is always damp. Your clothes never fully dry. When you breathe, you taste water and stone and that faint sweetness.

---

Scattered across the basin — sparse, never more than a few per hectare — are the structures. They are small. None taller than a person; most knee-high or lower. They seem made of the same pale rock as the ground, but denser, more intentional. Some look like seed-pods half-buried in the soil. Others like the fossils of machines that never had moving parts. A few are clearly active: they hum at a frequency you feel more than hear; they extrude thin, glassy filaments that harden in the air; they release clouds of what might be spores or might be something much smaller, that hang in the damp air like smoke and catch the copper light.

Near the center is the largest structure — a well, or a vent, or a wound. Three meters across, ringed by a low wall of that same dense pale material. From it rises a warm, sweet-smelling vapor that condenses instantly on any surface it touches, leaving a film of mineral salts. The sparse vegetation clusters thickest here. The animals come here too: insects by the thousand, small lizard-things, birds (or things like birds) with iridescent feathers and calls like broken glass.

The vegetation is sparse overall. Nothing taller than your knee. Lichens in pale greens and grays and a strange, faint violet. Mosses that seem to follow patterns — radial, spiral, grid-like — as if reading instructions written into the rock. A few ferns that unfurl too quickly, growing in time-lapse. Small succulent-like things that store water in translucent, veined leaves. Nothing woody. Nothing that could be called a tree. The plants feel like a first draft — a test run — as if the world is seeing what it will accept.

But the fauna is abundant. Loudly, insistently abundant. Insects fill the air — clouds, swarms, drifts — most tiny, many bioluminescent, so that the basin at "night" (when the copper light dims further, though it never goes out) is filled with a slow, drifting constellation of small lights. Beetle-like things with shells that click and whir. Moth-like things with wings that leave trails of faint glowing dust. Things that might be flies or might be seeds or might be neither.

Small lizards — or lizard-analogues — skitter across the wet rock. Warm-blooded, or seem to be; they move fast, and their scales are the same pale color as the ground, nearly invisible until they move. They hunt the insects, and are hunted in turn by the bird-things — a dozen species, none larger than a crow, with calls that are complex, loud, and strangely structured, almost like language. Some nest in the structures. Some seem to tend to them, or feed from them, or mate on them.

And there are larger things. You don't see them often. A shadow moving across the basin floor — large, slow, wrong-shaped. A shape in the mist above the central vent — a suggestion of wings, or sails, or something else entirely. Tracks in the soft soil near the pools — three-toed, webbed, much larger than any bird. Something is here you haven't seen yet. Something that might be the largest native species, or a maintenance process, or the basin's way of dreaming.

---

The sound is constant and loud. Water dripping, running, seeping, evaporating. The hum of the structures — a chord, not a single note, as if they're tuned to each other. The calls of the birds, the clicks and whirs of the insects, the rustle of lizards on rock, the wind moving through the basin in warm, damp gusts that smell of minerals and that faint sweetness. And underneath it all, a deeper sound — a subsonic pulse, felt in the chest more than heard, coming from the central vent or from the ground itself. The sound of the world still being built.

The rules here are not the rules outside. You understand this without being told. Gravity is slightly wrong — not much, but enough that you notice when you throw a stone (it falls too slowly, or too quickly, or in a slight curve). The boundary of the basin is not a wall but a change: the air becomes thicker, or thinner, or simply *different*, and beyond it the world is darker, colder, more eroded, more finished. You can see out. You can't quite see clearly. And you can't leave — or those who have tried found themselves walking back in again, from the opposite side, with no memory of turning around.

Time might be wrong too. The center of the basin seems older than the edges — the structures there are more complete, more complex, more *finished*. The edges are raw: fresh rock, thin soil, almost no life. It's as if time runs faster at the center, or as if the world is being built from the middle outward, or as if the center is where the process started and the edges are where it's still catching up.

The light never fully goes out, but it has a rhythm — a slow dimming and brightening over what might be hours or might be days, with no clear period. When it dims, the bioluminescence becomes more visible, and the basin transforms into something underwater, something subterranean, something that exists in its own private twilight. When it brightens, the colors become slightly more distinct — the violet of the lichens, the iridescence of the bird-feathers, the glassy transparency of the filaments extruded by the structures — and the whole place feels like a diorama, a model, a carefully constructed room.

---

This is a nursery. A seed-bed. A place where a world is being taught how to be a world, by machines so advanced they look like geology, in a basin so small it could be a garden, on a mountain so high it could be a heaven. It's warm, wet, loud, alive, and unfinished. The rock is still becoming soil. The pools are still becoming rivers. The insects are still becoming a food web. The machines are still becoming ruins — or still becoming finished. The boundary is still becoming a wall, or a door, or a memory.

And you are here, damp and breathing and listening to the sound of becoming, standing on ground that hasn't decided what it is yet, under a light that hasn't decided what time it is, in a world that hasn't decided whether it's a terrarium or a planet or a dream someone is having about a garden.
