# The Bowl

High on the massif, above the last twisted pines and the highest meadow, the mountain opens into a stone basin perhaps three hundred paces across — a glacial cirque, a raw amphitheater. The ice left here within living memory; it did not so much melt away as let go, calving and cracking into its own water, and the landscape it left behind is still essentially newborn. The headwall rises in three steep tiers of pale granite, its face scoured and unweathered, threaded with meltwater rills and hung with a few small snowfields and one remnant glacieret the size of a village square. The aretes on either side are knife-edges. The floor is polished bedrock — you can read the striations, the long parallel scratches the ice dragged like a butcher's hook — and scattered with erratics: house-sized boulders of foreign stone, dropped where the ice released them. The shepherds call them the ice's dropped teeth. Fresh moraine ridges, steep and loose, ring the floor; kettle hollows mark where buried blocks of ice melted and the ground slumped. Everything is sharp. Nothing has been softened yet.

For all its features, the Bowl is mostly empty. Vast stretches of polished rock carry nothing but light and shadow. The boulders stand far apart. Plants cluster only in the wet places. The only building is a stone shelter. The space between presences feels like the point.

The light is the first thing that happens to you. The air is too thin to filter the sun; it falls white and hard, and the pale rock throws it back, so the whole basin seems to glow from below. Shadows have knife edges. At the zenith the sky is a deep, almost black blue, and the horizon is ringed with other peaks, white and blue. Ice crystals hanging in the air make halos and sun dogs nearly every day — rings around the sun, bright false suns at the corners — so that the sky often looks crowded with watchers. On still mornings the basin is silent enough that you can hear the melt: drips from the headwall, the small gravelly talk of rills, the crunch of frost-shattered scree underfoot. The talus at the base of the headwall is shattered into glassy, curved shards, and it sings when you walk on it — a low, ringing, almost musical grind, as if the slope were an instrument being tuned.

Water is a visitor here, not a resident. The floor is mostly bare, dry stone; the damp lives in pockets. There is a small tarn, perhaps eighty paces across, fed by melt from the glacieret, its water milky turquoise with rock flour so that it seems lit from within. Braided rills appear on the floor in the afternoon, when the sun finally reaches the snowfields, and vanish by evening; the ground sweats, the seeps at the headwall's base swell, and everything — cairn stones, boot leather, the bothy's tin roof — wears a skin of dew and rime until the sun burns it off. The tarn has no visible outlet; its water sinks into the moraine, and you can stand at the edge and hear it gurgling down into the dark. Each morning, even in high summer, the tarn wears a thin skin of ice, and shortly after dawn it breaks with a sound like a struck bell. Near the east wall a small warm spring keeps a patch of ground open all winter; its water steams in the cold air, and its basin is crusted with bright orange and green bacterial mats — the only vivid color in the basin besides the lichens. It smells of sulfur and old coins.

The cold is absolute at night. The ground freezes a few fingers down; only the top skin thaws each day, and the freeze-thaw shatters the headwall from within, so the Bowl is never truly quiet for long — there is always a crack, a tumble, a small avalanche of stone from somewhere up in the walls. In the wet places, moss grows in dense lawns, deep green and almost black when soaked, springy as a pelt underfoot. Cushion plants button themselves flat against the rock; saxifrages and sedges fill the seeps; dwarf willow mats, no taller than a hand, creep along the ground. Lichens map the boulders in orange, black, and pale grey-green. In the short summer the basin briefly erupts in alpine flowers — a riot so intense it seems borrowed from a lower world. In late summer the snowfields bloom with algae, rust-red and orange streaks the old books call blood snow, which darkens as it warms and drinks the sun. On clear mornings the snow grows frost flowers: delicate curls of ice rising like petals, fragile as paper, collapsing at a breath.

Life is abundant, for all the emptiness. Pikas — small, round, unbearably busy — haystack the grass in the seeps and scold you as you pass. Marmots whistle from the moraine. A pale, thick-wooled wild goat haunts the headwall, more shadow than animal. A fox patrols the tarn's shore. Choughs and snow finches work the wind; a great raptor hangs in the thermals above the aretes for hours without moving. In summer the air over the tarn clouds with midges, and bumblebees bumble through the flowers with more enthusiasm than sense. And in the tarn itself, if you lie on the shore and look down through the milky water on a windless afternoon, you may see them: translucent, pale as candle wax, drifting. Larger than any crustacean has a right to be at this altitude. No one agrees on what they are. They do not seem to do much. They drift.

People have come here for the summers since before memory, and they have always left. A drystone bothy squats near the tarn's outlet, built from erratic slabs without mortar, its roof half-collapsed and patched with corrugated tin rusted to the color of the rock. Inside: a hearth of blackened stones, and a niche in the wall holding a bone needle, a flint blade, a coil of braided grass rope, a cracked wooden cup, a rusted kettle, and a few strips of something that might be old leather or dried meat. The niche is an offering-place. Travelers still leave small things — a coin, a button, a bright pebble — and no one takes anything out. Cairns mark the path from the lip to the water, some ancient and moss-grown, some fresh; the custom is to add a stone. Near the threshold stands a single standing stone, chest-high, scratched with a cross, a date, a name in no alphabet anyone reads now. Iron rings are drilled into the headwall at one point, with a rusted cable running down to a winch drum — the remains of some old attempt to win ice from the glacieret, or to haul something up. No one remembers what. A low, roofless stone pen, older than the bothy and perhaps older than the ice's retreat, crouches on the north side of the floor, built for animals that no one herds here anymore.

The rules of the place are simple and pitiless. The sun burns in minutes; the snow glare blinds by reflection. The cold kills the careless. Weather arrives in minutes — a fog bank will pour over the rim at midday and fill the bowl to the waist, then drain away as if it had never been. There is no wood; fuel must be carried up or dung dried, and fires are small, precious, and smoky. And the land itself is still moving: with the weight of the ice gone, the crust is rebounding, slowly, a few fingers' widths a year. The floor creaks. Fresh cracks have opened in the polished rock. The tarn's outlet shifts decade by decade. The basin is still settling into its freedom. The local rules are older and less explainable: do not camp beside the tarn at dusk — the ice is thin, or the whispering is bad, or the pale shrimp rise, depending on who you ask. Add a stone to the cairns. Leave the niche alone. Never take stone from the standing stone.

The Bowl feels like an unfinished cathedral. The headwall is a vast apse; the erratics are altars or broken columns; the tarn is a font; the cairns are votive markers, and the light — that white, crowded, haloed light — does the rest. The cold and the dry preserve everything: the rope, the needle, the frost flowers, the drifting translucent things. People come for the summer and leave, and the ice is still, in the shepherds' phrase, thinking about coming back.
