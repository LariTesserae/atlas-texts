**The Hollow**

You stand in a chamber that is older than any name for it. The ceiling is high enough that you cannot see it — only the suggestion of a vast, smooth arch above, lost in dimness. The walls curve away on all sides, worn to a continuous, flowing surface, like stone that was once liquid and forgot to stop moving. The chamber is roughly oval, perhaps thirty paces across at its widest, perhaps twenty at its narrowest. It is a room with a stone canopy, a grove with a roof of rock.

The stone is a warm, pale ochre, veined with darker mineral streaks that run down the walls like the traces of old rivers. The surface is smooth, almost soapy to the touch, worn by water that flowed here in ages past and still flows, intermittently, in thin threads and shallow pools. The warmth is constant — the stone is close to body temperature, as if the earth here has a slow, steady pulse. The air is warm, close, heavy with moisture. Breathing is easy, but the air feels thick, substantial, like breathing in a place that has been held for a very long time. The smell is of wet stone and minerals, faint and clean, with a deeper note of earth that has been sealed away from the sun.

The light comes from an opening somewhere in the ceiling — a crack, a throat, a collapsed vein in the rock. You cannot see it directly. The light it gives is diffuse, sourceless, the color of old bone or weak tea or the sky seen through deep water. It fills the upper air and fades toward the floor, so that the walls are brightest at the top and dimmest at the base, and the floor itself is in a kind of permanent twilight. The light does not move. It does not change with the hour or the season. It is the same at midday and midnight, in summer and winter, now and always.

The floor slopes gently toward the center, where the largest pool sits — perhaps three paces across, perhaps less, its surface perfectly still, perfectly dark, reflecting the dim light from above as a faint, diffuse glow. Smaller pools dot the floor around it, each one separate, each one full, each one still. The channels between them are shallow grooves in the stone, worn smooth, carrying water only when the seepage is heavy enough — after rain somewhere far above, perhaps, or when the condensation on the walls runs down in sufficient quantity. Most of the time the channels are dry, or nearly dry, holding only a thin film of moisture that darkens the stone.

Condensation beads on the walls in the upper reaches, where the light touches, and runs down in slow, irregular threads. The drips are the only sound — a slow, irregular percussion, each drop falling into a pool or onto stone with a small, clear note that rings in the silence and fades. The interval between drips is long enough that you begin to count them, to anticipate them, to wait for them. They are the only measure of time here, and they are not regular. The pools do not overflow. They do not evaporate. The water that drips in replaces what is lost to the air, and the balance is exact.

The moss grows only where the light reaches — on the upper walls, on the floor near the base of the walls where the glow is strongest, on the rims of the pools. It is a low, dense, dark green carpet, soft as velvet, and it does not spread. It has been this size for as long as anyone can say. The ferns are pale, almost translucent, their fronds small and delicate, and they too are static — not growing, not dying, simply existing in a kind of suspended animation. A single small shrub grows near the base of the far wall, its leaves thick and waxy, its branches twisted into a shape that suggests it has been growing toward the light for a very long time without ever reaching it. It is not dead. It is not alive in any active sense. It simply is.

There are no animals. No bats in the ceiling dark. No insects on the pools. No birds at the opening. No spiders in the corners. No fish in the water. The silence is not the silence of a place where animals are hiding — it is the silence of a place where they have never been, or where they left so long ago that even their absence has become a kind of presence. The air does not stir with wings. The water does not ripple with feeding. The stone does not echo with footsteps except yours.

And yet there are marks. On the far wall, near the floor, there are lines cut into the stone — shallow, worn, almost smooth. They are arranged in a way that suggests intention: a row of vertical strokes, a series of curved lines, a set of marks that might be letters or tallies or the traces of a language that has been forgotten. The cuts are old enough that the moss has begun to grow in them, and the edges have softened, and the stone has begun to reclaim them. They are almost invisible unless you are close, unless the light catches them at the right angle, unless you know to look.

A few blocks of cut stone sit near the wall, their corners rounded, their surfaces worn. They are roughly rectangular, roughly the same size, and they are arranged in a way that suggests they were once part of something — a wall, a platform, a seat, a marker. The arrangement is too fragmentary to reconstruct. Perhaps they were brought here. Perhaps they were cut here. The stone is the same as the chamber walls — the same ochre, the same mineral veins — so it is impossible to tell whether they are natural or worked. They might have always been there. They might have been placed. The distinction has ceased to matter.

There is a shallow depression in the floor near the blocks — a hollow, perhaps a pace across, its edges worn smooth. It might be a fire pit. It might be a natural hollow. It might be the place where someone sat, long ago, and wore the stone with their weight. There is no ash. There is no charcoal. There is only the smooth, worn stone, and the faint suggestion of a presence that has been absorbed into the rock.

The place is in equilibrium. The water that drips has always dripped. The pools have always been full. The moss has always been this size. The light has always been this dim. The stone has always been this smooth. Nothing enters. Nothing leaves. Nothing grows. Nothing decays. The only change is the slow, almost imperceptible wear of the stone — the drips that fall and fall and fall, each one carrying away an almost infinitesimal quantity of mineral, each one contributing to a process that will take longer than memory to complete.

Time here is not measured in hours or days or years. It is measured in drips. It is measured in the slow, geological patience of water on stone. The place has been this way for thousands of years, and it will be this way for thousands more. It is not waiting. It is not asleep. It is simply complete — a system that has reached its final form, a state that has achieved its own permanence.

You feel it when you stand still. The stillness is not empty. It is full — full of the weight of time, full of the presence of the stone, full of the slow, continuous equilibrium of the place. You feel that you are the only thing here that changes. Your breath moves the air. Your footsteps disturb the silence. Your body heat warms the stone where you touch it. And when you leave, the place will close around your absence as water closes around a stone — completely, without trace, as if you were never there.
