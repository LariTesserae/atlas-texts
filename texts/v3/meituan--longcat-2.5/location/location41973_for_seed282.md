**The Sweat-Room**

You reach it by a ladder down a dry well, through a hatch in the floor of the old cold store. The last three rungs are wet. At the bottom there is a doorway with no door, and the room.

It is seven metres by four, and every dimension has been colonised. The ceiling — cast in-situ concrete, poured in lifts whose seams show like strata — is buried under three generations of pipework: a 200-millimetre brine main entering high on the north wall, lagged in canvas and wire; a thicket of 25-millimetre copper; a sagging cable tray; a downpipe from the defrost drain above. Most of the ceiling is not visible. The walls are shelved with conduit, valve wheels, a disused fan housing, a steel control panel. The floor is mostly the sump. The room is dense the way a reef is dense — layered, claimed, every surface spoken for.

Everything here is made. There is no natural substrate; the rock is poured, the stone is mixed. And yet the room is growing a geology.

Concrete bleeds. Water finds the pores, the cracks, the formwork tie-holes, and carries the concrete's chemistry out with it — calcium, iron, a faint copper tang. Where it emerges, it deposits. From each tie-hole hangs a thread of stone. From the ceiling cracks, stalactites: most are young, soft, still extruding, their tips wet beads; a few are old enough to have thickened into cones banded cream and rust. Clusters of soda-straws — hollow, thin as quills — hang beneath the pipe runs and chime faintly when the vent breathes. Down the walls, where the seep lines run, flowstone has laid itself in curtains, and on the floor around each drip point the water has built tiny weirs: rimstone pools terraced in millimetres, like a model of a watershed left out in the rain. The corners carry cave popcorn, knuckle-bone clusters of it. The colours are the room's own — bone-white, amber, rust-orange, and here and there a faint verdigris where the copper lines contribute. The room is being painted by its materials, in the colours of its materials.

Some formations echo the machinery. Along the brine main, stone has accumulated in pipe-shaped runs — a second plumbing of flowstone hanging beneath the iron, the room growing a ghost diagram of its own infrastructure. The pressure gauge on the discharge line has been entombed to its rim; its face is a frosted window now, but when the pump starts you can see the needle tremble behind the mineral crust, the room's pulse readable only through stone.

Water arrives three ways. It seeps through the walls, the slow continuous bleed. It condenses on the cold pipework and gathers into pearls that migrate along the undersides — some of them rolling against the slope, as if drawn toward the brine line, clustering where the frost feathers grow. And every few hours the plant above defrosts: somewhere in the dark a valve opens and the downpipe speaks — a gush, a bucket thrown into a lake — and a pulse of warmer water hits the sump and raises a brief mist, a small cloud that hangs a moment before the cold takes it back.

The sump is a square pit in the floor, a metre and a half deep, black and still. When it is calm it mirrors everything: the lamp, the pipework, the hanging field of stalactites, doubled. The room has two ceilings then, and the cave hangs in both.

The cycle is the room's heartbeat. The float rises. The contactor clunks. No. 1 pump — the duty pump, crusted to the colour of the walls — takes up the load with a low hum that climbs through the plinth and into the pipes and out into the stone; the soda-straws shiver and tinkle; the rising main gulps, swallows, settles. The water falls. The pump sucks air with a rattling glug and stops. The metal ticks as it cools. No. 2, the standby, sits clean and paint-readable a metre away, because it rarely runs.

Then the long quiet. The room's baseline is near-silence — dead acoustics, concrete and water swallowing every echo an arm's length from its source. What remains is the drips. Each stalactite tip grows a bead, and each bead is a lens: the ceiling carries a field of small inverted lamps, trembling. The drop sags, falls, and strikes the sump with a note that depends on the depth of the water — as the level changes, the room is tuned; it is an instrument played by the pump. And when the pump starts, the pressure wave ticks through the structure and the whole ceiling sheds its drops in a volley — the room rains, briefly, in rhythm with the machine.

Light is thin. The caged bulkhead on the east wall gives a low amber pool; its filament is on borrowed time — it dims to a coal, recovers, hums its small ballast whine. From the vent shaft high in the corner, a blade of pale daylight falls through the grate above and lands on the floor in a coin; the coin moves across the concrete as the day does whatever it does on the surface, and where it rests there is a thumbprint of green — algae, the room's only vegetation, sparse as a signature. Along the base of the seep wall, a faint ochre bloom of iron bacteria marks the bleed's tide line, no wider than a finger. The panel carries a red and a green indicator, the only saturated colours in the room, doubled in the sump. The west end dissolves; the wall is not visible; the dark there is a presence, and the room feels larger than it is.

The cold is the point of the place. The room is cold because the cold store works; the brine main is the coldest thing in it, and where its lagging has failed, the air itself has frozen onto it — hoarfrost feathers, beards of ice that grow over days and then shrink again, sublimating into the dry air, and re-grow when the compressors above work harder. Frost is the room's weather. Metal is at skin-sticking cold. The air tastes of stone, of faint ozone, of the clean sharp ghost of old refrigerant. There is no animal life — nothing that scuttles, nothing that has ever disturbed a drip with a tongue. The damp itself is mineral.

The rules are simple, and they are in conflict. The room is a machine for moving water out. The water's errand is to build. Every litre the pump lifts leaves its mineral behind, and so the room is being dried and constructed at the same time — kept functional, and converted, and the conversion is one-way. The growths are cumulative; the pump's rhythm sets their tempo; the stone grows toward the water, and the water, lifted out, becomes the stone. The cave forming here is made of the machine. Peel a stalactite and the concrete's recipe is inside it.

The newest feature is a crack that has only just begun to weep, near the west end, where the dark is. Its stalactite is a nub no bigger than a fingertip — soft, glistening, extruding so slowly that watching it is like watching the hour hand of a clock. The lamp dims; the room holds; a bead swells at the nub's tip, lenses the amber light, lets it go. The drop falls, rings; the sump takes it; the mirror-floor closes over the ripple; the pump waits. The builders did not draw this. The room is drawing it, one drop at a time.
