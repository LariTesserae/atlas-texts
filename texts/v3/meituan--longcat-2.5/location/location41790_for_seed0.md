The land tore open here. Not long ago — the wound is still fresh, the edges still sharp, the ground still warm from the tearing. This is a rift valley, low and broad, sunk below the surrounding country, and it is still being made.

The valley runs roughly north-south, perhaps twenty miles long and half as wide. The rims rise on either side like the lips of a wound — broken basalt cliffs, fresh and black, with columns of cooling lava still visible in the cross-section, and shelves of older rock above them, and scrub and grass on the high ground beyond. The floor is chaos: shattered rock, collapsed lava tubes, fields of obsidian shards that catch the dim light and throw it back in slivers, and everywhere the hiss and bubble of water meeting heat.

The water comes from below. Underground rivers — ancient, vast, carrying the rainwater of the surrounding highlands through fissures and caves deep in the earth — surface here in scalding springs, run a little way across hot rock, and sink again through new fissures. The springs are everywhere: pools of clear, steaming water, their bottoms crusted with mineral deposits in layers of orange and green and white, and sometimes a pale blue, and sometimes a deep red. Some springs are fresh and cold — surface water that has found a shallow path — and these are the only places where you can drink without boiling the minerals out first. The hot pools smell of sulfur, of rotten eggs, of something metallic and sharp, and the smell clings to your clothes and hair and skin. The steam rises in columns, and where the columns meet the cooler air above, they spread into a perpetual haze that hangs over the valley like a lid, a ceiling of warm, mineral-laden cloud that never clears, never rains properly, just exhales moisture and sulfur and heat.

Moisture is everywhere and nowhere. The air is thick with it — warm, wet, heavy on your skin, beading on your forehead and the backs of your hands. Condensation beads on every surface: on the rock, on the leaves, on your hair and clothes, on the obsidian shards, which are always wet and slick. Occasional rain falls through the haze — warm, mineral-smelling, slightly acidic, tasting of metal and sulfur on your tongue — and runs off in temporary streams that carve tiny channels in the soft ash and pumice before vanishing into fissures. The ground is damp, the air is damp, you are damp. But there are also dry places: raised shelves of pumice where the wind keeps the steam away, and the rock is powder-dry and warm, and the dust rises in small, lazy clouds when you step on it, and it tastes of chalk and ash. The dryness is local, temporary, an eddy in the general wetness.

The light is the first thing that unsettles you. The sky, what you can see of it through the steam and haze, is the color of old brass — sulfur-yellow, ash-gray, a dull smolder that never quite brightens to day. The sun is a pale smear, sometimes visible at noon as a brighter patch in the murk, but its light is filtered, diffused, reduced to a dim, warm twilight that flattens everything and makes distances hard to judge. And below the rims, the vegetation makes it darker still.

The plants here are dense beyond belief. They have colonized every surface, every crack, every warm, wet niche. Near the springs, thick mats of moss — some as deep as your knee — cover the rock, and they are warm, heated from below by the earth, and they smell of damp and growth and something sharp and chemical. Ferns with fronds as long as your arm rise from the moss, their leaves dark green, almost black in the dimness, and their fiddleheads coated with a fine, rust-colored fuzz. Higher up, on the drier shelves, a low, tough scrub clings to the pumice — woody, small-leaved, adapted to the mineral soil and the heat, with flowers that are small and pale and almost invisible. And in the lowest, wettest parts, where the underground rivers surface and the canopy closes overhead, the vegetation becomes a jungle.

The canopy is the strangest thing. It is not made of trees — there are no trees here, not proper trees — but of a crawling, interlocking mat of vines and creepers and broad-leaved plants that have grown over the vents and the pools and each other, forming a roof of vegetation ten, fifteen, twenty feet above the ground. The leaves are enormous — some as big as doors, waxy and dark, with veins that stand out like ribs, and edges that are slightly toothed, and surfaces that are always wet with condensation. They interlock, overlapping, and beneath them the light fades to a deep green-gray, a perpetual dusk. In places it is nearly black. You navigate by touch, by sound, by the temperature of the air on your skin — the warmth of a nearby vent, the coolness of a dry shelf, the damp chill of a shaded corner.

The warmth is constant and enveloping. The air is warm — not hot, but warm, like standing near a fire, like the air in a greenhouse, like the breath of something large. Near the vents it becomes hot, scalding, and you learn to read the ground before you step: the color of the mineral stains, the smell of the sulfur, the intensity of the hiss. The heat rises in columns that you can feel on your face before you see the vent — a sudden increase in temperature, a dryness in the air, a smell of sulfur and hot stone. Some vents are new — opened in the night, the ground still cracked around them, the rock fresh and sharp, the steam jetting out with a sound like a kettle boiling dry, and the mineral stains still bright and wet. Some have been breathing for years and have built cones of sinter, pale and delicate as cake frosting, layered with orange and green and white mineral blooms, and the steam escapes from them in thin, constant streams that smell of sulfur and something else, something sharp and metallic, and the cones are warm to the touch and slightly damp and covered with a fine, mineral fuzz. Some vents have built towers — tall, thin spires of sinter, ten or fifteen feet high, hollow, with the steam whistling through them in a high, thin tone that cuts through the general roar. Some have built arches, and bridges, and lattices of mineral, delicate as lace, that crumble at a touch.

The sound is overwhelming. It is not one sound but thousands, layered and constant, a wall of noise that you feel in your chest, in your teeth, in your bones. The steam hisses — a constant, breathing hiss from every vent and pool, rising and falling with the pressure of the earth. The mud pots gurgle and burst — thick, heavy sounds, like something choking, like a throat clearing, like a bubble of mud breaking the surface and collapsing. The underground rivers roar through their fissures — a subterranean thunder that you feel through the soles of your feet, a vibration that never stops, that you stop hearing after a while but that never actually stops. And over and through all of it, the life.

Insects. Clouds of them. Midges and flies and things with iridescent wings and things with wings like torn silk and things that are not insects at all but something else, something that hums and pulses and fills the air in such density that you breathe them, that they coat your teeth and tongue, that they mat in your hair and eyebrows and the corners of your eyes. Their combined buzzing is a physical pressure, a sound you feel in your sinuses, a vibration that never stops, that rises and falls in waves as the swarm moves. They rise in columns from the warm pools, they swarm around the vents, they cloud the air in the canopy until the dim light is reduced further, until you are standing in a warm, dark, buzzing twilight, and the swarm moves around you like a living thing, like a current, like a river of wings.

Birds — or bird-like things — call from the canopy. Their voices are sharp and repetitive, a dozen different calls overlapping: a high, piercing whistle that rises at the end; a low, guttural croak that sounds like a stone dropping into water; a rapid, staccato rattle like a stick dragged along a fence; a long, descending wail that fades into the hiss of the steam. They never stop. The calls come from everywhere and nowhere, and you never see the birds — just a flash of movement in the leaves, a shape against the dim sky, a shadow that is gone before you can focus on it. Sometimes the calls synchronize — a wave of sound that sweeps across the canopy, bird to bird, and then dies away, and then builds again. Sometimes a single call cuts through the noise — a high, clear note that seems to come from everywhere at once, and for a moment the other sounds fall away, and then the roar returns.

Amphibians. Frogs of a dozen sizes, from thumbnail to dinner-plate, chorus from every pool. Their calls are pulsing, rhythmic, rising and falling in waves — a deep, resonant boom that you feel in your chest; a high, metallic trill that sounds like a finger running around the rim of a glass; a low, grinding croak that sounds like a stone being dragged across stone; a rapid, clicking call like a pebble tapping on a drum. The chorus is synchronized in places, waves of sound that sweep across the valley and die away, then build again, and the different species call in different rhythms, overlapping, interlocking, so that the sound is almost musical, almost like a composition, almost like something intentional. In the darkest pools, the frogs glow — faint bioluminescence in blues and greens, pulsing in time with their calls, so that the pools seem to breathe with light, and the light pulses in waves across the valley, and for a moment you can see the shapes of the frogs — pale, translucent, their bones visible, their eyes dark pits — and then the light fades, and the dark returns.

And something large moves through the undergrowth. You never see it — just a continuous crash at the edge of hearing, a sound like a body pushing through dense vegetation, always moving, always just out of sight. Sometimes it stops, and the silence is worse than the noise — the insects go quiet, the frogs fall silent, the birds stop calling, and the only sound is the hiss of the steam and the distant roar of the rivers. Sometimes it calls — a low, resonant sound, more felt than heard, that vibrates in your chest and makes the steam shiver and the pools ripple. You learn to read the small sounds around it: the sudden silence of the insects, the shift in the frog chorus, the way the birds stop calling and then start again, higher and faster. Then the crash moves on, and the noise returns, and the valley breathes again.

The fauna is not just numerous but strange. Many of the creatures here have adapted to the dimness. Some are pale — white, cream, pale pink — with eyes that are just light-sensitive patches, or no eyes at all, and they navigate by smell and touch and the temperature of the air. Some glow — faint bioluminescence in blues and greens, pulsing in the darkest corners, on the backs of beetles, in the mouths of frogs, along the stems of certain fungi. Some are transparent — you can see through their skin to the organs beneath, the slow pulse of the heart, the movement of the gut, the dark line of the spine. The insects are drawn to the heat and the moisture, and they are everywhere, in every layer of the vegetation, in the water, in the air, in the soil. Predators follow — things that eat the insects, things that eat the things that eat the insects, and the food web is dense and tangled, and it is loud. The ground is alive with movement — beetles, centipedes, things that skitter and burrow and crawl over each other in layers, and the soil itself seems to move, to breathe, to shift. The water is thick with larvae, with tiny crustaceans, with translucent fish that navigate by feel in the hot, mineral water, their bodies pale and their eyes dark pits. Everything is eating, breeding, dying, decomposing, and being eaten again, at a rate that makes the air smell of growth and rot and sulfur all at once, that makes the ground soft and warm and alive under your feet.

The vegetation is equally dense and strange. The plants here have adapted to heat, to dimness, to the mineral-rich water. Some are pale, almost white, with leaves that are thin and translucent, and they grow in the dimmest corners, where the light is just enough to keep them alive, and their roots reach toward the warmth of the rock. Others are dark red or purple, pigments that capture what little light filters through, and they grow in the slightly brighter places, near the rims, near the edges of the canopy, and their leaves are thick and waxy and always wet. The fungi are enormous — shelves and brackets and fruiting bodies that emerge overnight, some as big as umbrellas, some that dissolve into black liquid within hours, some that glow with a faint, sickly light, and some that are hard as wood and last for years, growing in layers, in rings, in shelves that jut from the rock like steps. The mosses are thick enough to swallow your foot, and they are warm — heated from below by the earth — and they smell of damp and growth and something sharp and chemical, and they are home to countless tiny creatures, mites and springtails and things that are too small to see. The vines and creepers form a tangled understory, and above them the canopy blocks what little light remains, and in the darkest, warmest corners, there are plants that seem to feed on the heat itself — or on the chemicals in the steam — their leaves coated with mineral deposits, their roots reaching toward the vents, their flowers small and pale and almost invisible, and their pollen glowing faintly in the dark.

The place is unstable. New vents open. Old ones seal. The ground cracks and shifts. A hot pool that was safe yesterday may be boiling today, or may have drained through a new fissure, leaving a crust of minerals and dead insects and the bones of small animals. The underground rivers change course, surfacing in new places, sinking in old ones, and the vegetation follows — colonizing the new warm ground, dying back where the heat has gone. The mineral towers crumble and are rebuilt. The sinter cones grow and collapse. The whole valley feels like it is still being born — or still being digested — by the earth. The steam is not constant; it pulses, it shifts, it rises and falls with pressures you cannot see. Sometimes the haze thickens until you can barely see your hand in front of your face, and the sound is muffled, and the world shrinks to the warmth on your skin and the hiss in your ears, and you are alone in a warm, dark, breathing cave. Sometimes the haze thins, and you can see the rims of the valley, and the dim sky, and the distant steam columns, and the valley feels vast and open and exposed, and you can see the layers of the vegetation, the canopy and the understory and the ground cover, and the pools and the vents and the mineral towers, and the whole place is a single, complex, living system, and it is beautiful and terrible and alive.

And there are people. Or something like people. They live at the margins — on the rims, in the drier, cooler high ground, in caves in the basalt cliffs, in shelters of woven matting and hide that they build and abandon and build again. They come down into the valley to hunt, to gather, to harvest the minerals and the fungi and the strange plants. They use stone tools — sharp obsidian flakes, wooden spears, baskets woven from tough vine, scraps of hide from the animals they kill, and pigment made from the mineral deposits, which they smear on their skin and their tools and the rocks at the valley's edge. They have no permanent structures — just the shelters, just the fire-pits ringed with stones, just the caches of tools hidden in cracks in the rock, just the marks they leave on the stone to show which vents are safe and which are new and dangerous. They navigate the valley by sound and by heat, by the smell of sulfur, by the color of the mineral stains. They know which vents are safe and which are new and dangerous. They know the calls of the birds and the frogs, and they use them to orient themselves — a particular frog call means water, a particular bird call means the rim is near, a particular insect hum means a hot pool is close. They are adapted, in their way, to the dark and the noise and the heat — their eyes are large, their hearing is sharp, their skin is dark and tough — or they are desperate, or both. Their presence is faint — a snapped twig, a discarded obsidian flake, a smear of mineral paint on a rock, the distant sound of a bone whistle cutting through the roar, a smell of smoke that is not from a vent, a footprint in the warm ash. They do not stay long. The valley is too loud, too hot, too alive. They come, they take what they need, they leave.

The whole place has a quality of strangeness, of wrongness, that settles into your bones. It is too alive, too loud, too hot, too dark. The life here is not the life of the surface world — it is denser, faster, more frantic, more violent. Everything is competing for the heat, for the moisture, for the light. The decay is as rapid as the growth. The whole valley is a single, breathing, hissing, shrieking organism, and it is still growing, still changing, still tearing itself open in places. It feels less like a place and more like a process — the earth digesting itself, life colonizing the wound, and the wound still bleeding heat and steam and mineral water.

And sometimes, in the deepest dark, in the warmest pools, in the thickest part of the canopy, you hear something that might be a voice. Or the steam might shape itself into something that might be a face. Or the bioluminescence might pulse in a pattern that might be a word. You tell yourself it is the heat, the noise, the exhaustion. You tell yourself it is the valley playing tricks. But you do not stay to find out. You leave while you still can, and behind you the valley hisses and roars and pulses with light, and it does not notice that you are gone.
