The plateau is above the world. Not metaphorically — actually above it, high enough that the air is thin and the horizon curves and the sky is a deep, hard blue that fades to black at the zenith. Below, somewhere in the haze, there are forests or grasslands or whatever green things grow at lower elevations. Up here, there is nothing green. There is nothing alive at all, except possibly the builders, and they are invisible.

Everything is built. From horizon to horizon, the plateau is covered in fitted stone — a continuous fabric of walls, channels, cisterns, terraces, platforms, and covered ways, all built from a pale, hard limestone that breaks in clean fractures and takes a sharp edge. The stone is almost white, with a faint cream or gray undertone. When it's dry, it's matte and slightly rough. When it's wet, it darkens to a warm ivory and becomes smooth and slightly reflective. The construction is dense — there are no gaps, no empty spaces, no courtyards or plazas. Every surface abuts another. The walls are shoulder-high or chest-high, just enough to divide the space into compartments. The channels are narrow, cut into the tops of walls or sunk into the floors, just wide enough for a finger of water. The cisterns are rectangular pits lined with fitted stone, covered with flat slabs that fit so tightly you can't slip a blade between them. The terraces step down the gentle slope of the plateau in irregular tiers, each one a flat stone floor slightly tilted toward a drain. The covered ways are passages roofed with stone slabs, just high enough to walk through upright. Everything is fitted without mortar — dry stone, every joint tight, every surface leveled by hand.

The stone is cold. Even in the bright sun, it stays cool to the touch, because the air is thin and the wind moves across it and the stone radiates its heat quickly. In the shade, it's cold enough to hurt. At night, it's freezing. The cold is part of the design. The stone is cold so that water will condense on it.

The water comes from the air. There is almost no rain up here — the plateau is above the rain line, in a zone where clouds thin to fog and fog thins to moisture that clings to cold surfaces. Every night, the stone cools. Every dawn, the air moves across it, and water condenses on every exposed face. The construction is designed to catch this water and nothing else. The walls are grooved and dimpled, their surfaces textured with thousands of tiny channels that guide the condensation downward. The terraces are slightly roughened, their stone faces angled to present the maximum surface to the moving air. The channels collect the water that runs off every surface and carry it, thin and clear, toward the cisterns. The covered ways are roofed to keep the condensation from evaporating before it can be collected. The cisterns are covered to keep the stored water from evaporating. Every drop is accounted for.

During the day, the water is visible everywhere — a faint sheen on the stone, a thin line of moisture in the channels, a darkening of the stone where water has recently run. The channels whisper. The cisterns tick as drops fall from the undersides of the cover slabs into the water below. The sound is very quiet — a faint, continuous dripping and trickling, like a distant fountain heard through a wall. The wind moves through the passages and over the walls, but the dense construction breaks it into small, separate gusts that make no single coherent sound. The silence is profound. There are no birds, no insects, no rustling leaves, no animal footsteps. The only sounds are water and wind and stone.

There is no life here. No plants — the plateau is above the line where anything green can grow, and in any case there is no soil, only stone. No animals — there is nothing to eat, nothing to shelter in, no reason for anything to come here. The construction is biologically dead. The only living things are the builders, if they are still here — and they may or may not be. There are signs of recent work everywhere: fresh tool marks on a newly fitted stone, a pile of chippings beside a half-finished wall, a channel that has been cleared of mineral deposit within the last few days. But the workers themselves are invisible. They work slowly, alone or in pairs, and they are spread across the vast plateau so thin that you could walk for days without seeing one. Or they are gone, and the signs of recent work are older than they look — the mineral deposition preserves tool marks, makes fresh cuts look ancient and ancient cuts look fresh. The place works either way. The water condenses, the channels carry it, the cisterns fill. The machine runs itself.

The stone is growing. This is the strange thing, the thing that takes a while to notice. The water that condenses on the limestone is saturated with dissolved mineral — it comes from fog that has passed over mineral deposits below, or from the stone itself, which is porous and slowly dissolving and recrystallizing. When the water evaporates, it leaves behind a thin film of new mineral. When it runs into the channels and cisterns, it deposits mineral on the channel floors and cistern walls. The stone is accreting, growing, slowly thickening. The channels get narrower over decades. The cisterns get shallower. The walls get slightly thicker at their bases. The terraces rise imperceptibly. The construction is simultaneously being built by hand and by mineral deposition, and the two processes are indistinguishable. A wall that was built by hand a century ago has grown another finger's width of stone since then. A channel that was cut to a certain depth is now half-filled with crystalline deposit. The place is alive in a mineral sense — it is growing, slowly, patiently, in the same way a cave grows.

The light is intense. The plateau is high enough that the air is thin and dry, and the sun strikes with little filtering. The white stone reflects the light, so the whole place seems to glow — a bright, even luminosity that comes from everywhere and nowhere. The shadows are sharp and dark, the contrast extreme. At midday, the stone is almost too bright to look at directly. At dawn and dusk, the light slants across the textured surfaces and every groove and dimple casts a tiny shadow, so the whole plateau seems to be covered in a fine, dark stippling. The sky is a deep blue during the day, fading to black at the zenith, and at night the stars are brilliant and close. The temperature drops sharply at night — the stone radiates its heat quickly, and the air is too thin to hold it. The stone becomes cold enough to condense water from the air even without a drop in humidity. This is when the water harvesting is most active — the long, cold nights, when the entire plateau sweats.

The scale is regional. The plateau extends to the horizon in every direction, covered in the same dense, fitted construction. From a high point — a terrace built up above the general level, perhaps for viewing or for catching more wind — you can see the pattern of the construction extending for miles. The walls and channels make a kind of text, a writing in stone that covers the entire visible world. The pattern is not regular — it follows the contours of the land, the direction of the wind, the path of the water — but it is dense and continuous. There are no landmarks in the ordinary sense, because everything is made and everything is similar. You navigate by the shape of a particular wall, the angle of a particular channel, the feel of a particular passage. The place is a labyrinth, but a labyrinth with no center, no exit, no monster — just the endless, patient geometry of water collection.

The age of the construction is hard to determine. Some of it is clearly ancient — walls that have grown thick with mineral deposit, channels that have been recut so many times they are trenches, terraces that have risen several feet above their original level. Some of it is clearly recent — fresh tool marks, new stone that hasn't yet darkened or developed the patina of age. But the two are indistinguishable in style, because the methods haven't changed. The same hand tools — stone hammers, bone chisels, wooden wedges, abrasive sand — have been used for as long as anyone can remember. The construction is all of a piece, a single continuous project that has been going on for centuries and will continue for centuries more, because the water never stops condensing and the stone never stops growing and the work is never done.

There is something unsettling about the place. It is the totality of it — the fact that there is no natural ground, no untouched surface, no place where the world is not shaped by hand or by mineral deposit. It is the silence — the profound, almost total absence of sound, broken only by water and wind. It is the brightness — the even, shadowless luminosity that makes the place feel like a dream or a hallucination. It is the absence of life — the knowledge that nothing lives here, that this is a place for water and stone and nothing else. And it is the patience — the sense that the place is in no hurry, that it has been here for centuries and will be here for centuries more, that it is growing and being built and being used in a cycle that is so slow it is almost static. The place is a machine for harvesting water, or for some purpose that has been forgotten — but whatever the purpose, the machine is still running, and it will keep running long after the last person who knows its name is gone.
