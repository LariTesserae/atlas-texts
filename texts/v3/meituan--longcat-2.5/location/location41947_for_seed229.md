# The Chime Flats

A basin on a high plateau, three kilometers across, ringed by the rounded, weathered remains of an ancient crater. The rim is low — a few hundred meters — and soft-shouldered, eroded into saddles and gullies that speak of epochs. It has been here longer than the things that live on it. It is settled. It is in balance. It is also, at this moment, cracking.

The floor is a dry pan of pale mineral crust — white-gold, faintly iridescent, broken into plates and polygons by the slow heave and settle of the ground beneath. No water. No stream cuts the rim. No pool has gathered in the lowest fold of the basin for longer than the oldest pan-walker can remember, and they remember a long time. The crust is a deposit — salt, alkali, something else — laid down over millennia by the vents, by the haze, by the patient chemistry of the place. It is hard. It is brittle. It rings faintly underfoot, like a bell struck once and left to hum.

From every crack, from every plate-boundary, from every fumarole margin, grow the chime-reeds. They are silicate organisms — not plants, though they perform the office of plants. They rise in fans and blades, translucent, opalescent, some as tall as a person, some clustered low in dense mats that crust over and fuse into sheets of living glass. They are lush in the way a reef is lush — crowded, competitive, layered, a forest of mineral and light. They grow visibly, slowly, and as they grow they sing: a constant crystalline creaking, a high thin ringing, a chime that rises and falls with the wind and the geothermal pulses and the pressure of their own expansion. It is loud. It is everywhere. It is the voice of the place, and it never stops. Visitors cover their ears and then, after some hours, hear it as silence — the way one hears a sea.

The light is dim. A permanent haze of mineral vapor hangs in the basin, thick enough to soften all edges, to make the far rim a pale ghost, to turn the reed-stands into smudges of opalescence. Above the haze, a high overcast of pale gray, unbroken. The sun is a brighter patch, a diffuse glow, never a disc. Shadows are soft, directionless, and the reeds catch what light there is and hold it — faintly phosphorescent, faintly luminous, so that the dimmer hours are not dark but merely deeper. The whole basin is lit like a room with the curtains drawn: present, muted, intimate, enclosed. The warmth comes from below. Fumaroles, scattered across the pan, hiss and roar, venting hot gas through cracks in the crust. Some are old, sealed, crusted over with mineral deposits that the reeds have colonized into strange hybrid forms. Others are new, raw, the ground around them still warm and discolored, the air above them shimmering and thick with smell — sulfur, metal, something sharper and more electric. The vents open and close on timescales of days and weeks. The ground shifts. The pan cracks anew. The chime-reeds colonize the fresh mineral surfaces within hours, sending out filaments of glass that harden into blades, that ring as they grow, that join the chorus.

The fauna is abundant but sparse in distribution — spread thin across the basin, territorial, migratory in slow cycles that follow the vents and the reed-growth. The pan-walkers are the most visible: six-legged, low-slung beasts with broad, flat feet that distribute their weight on the crust, so that they move across the plates without breaking through. Their carapaces are mineral-shelled, grown from the same silicate chemistry as the reeds, and they are often overgrown with lichen-like growths that they scrape off against the reed-stalks — a mutualism, perhaps, or a parasitism, or simply a fact of the place. They graze on the low mats of reed-growth, grinding the silicate with broad flat teeth, digesting the organic fraction, excreting the mineral as fine dust that settles and becomes new substrate. They are slow. They are ancient. They have been here as long as the reeds, and their shells carry growth-rings like trees, and the old ones are so overgrown with reed and lichen that they look like moving hillocks, like fragments of the landscape that have decided to walk.

There are swarms of pan-springers — small, hard-shelled insects that leap from plate to plate on spring-loaded mineral legs, and they are the base of a food chain that supports larger, rarer things. A stalker, perhaps, with a long still body and many jointed legs, that moves between the reed-stands like a shadow, that freezes for hours and then strikes with a speed that makes the springers explode from the crust in all directions. A gas-bag floater that drifts above the reeds on warm vent-air, trailing tendrils, grazing on the springers in swarms, its body a translucent sac of membrane and mineral ribbing, its voice a low constant drone that harmonizes with the reeds. Something else, too — the basin has room for strangeness, and the far pan, near the raw vents, is thick with things that have not been named, that move in ways that suggest they are still deciding what they are.

The built is scattered, faint. On the southern rim, a settlement of a dozen structures, built from blocks of the pale mineral crust, roofed with pan-walker hides or with dense mats of reed-fiber woven and hardened into something like ceramic. The structures are low, wind-scoured, ancient and recent at once — rebuilt and rebuilt again as the vents shift, as the crust subsides, as the reeds colonize the walls and have to be cut back. There are mine-workings: pits and trenches where the mineral crust is quarried, or where the dense mats of old-growth reed are harvested, or where the mineral shells of dead pan-walkers are gathered and worked. There is a single larger structure on the highest point of the rim — a tower, a marker, a watch-post, a shrine. Its purpose is unclear. It might be very old. It might be older than the settlement. It might be older than the basin. It is built from a different stone, darker, and it rings differently when the wind hits it — a lower note, a hum, a drone that you feel in your chest before you hear it with your ears.

The technology is primitive, but it has a specific focus. Stone tools, reed-fiber cordage, mineral-shell blades, simple lever and wedge — and also resonators. Wind-harps strung between reed-stalks. Hollow chambers dug into the crust and roofed with hide, designed to catch and amplify the chime of the reeds. Tuning forks the size of a person, carved from single crystals, that are struck and left to ring, that are used to test the mood of the basin, to call the pan-walkers, to warn of a vent opening. The inhabitants, if they are here, have built a technology of sound and mineral, of listening and resonance. They read the chime of the reeds as weather, as warning, as language. They read the vents as omens, as calendars, as gods. They know the place is alive. They do not say so.

The turbulence is the defining character. The place is in constant, slow crisis. New vents roar open and the ground cracks and the reeds colonize and the pan-walkers migrate to follow the fresh growth. Old vents seal and the crust subsides and the reed-stands topple and the sound changes. The light is never the same twice — the haze thickens and thins with the venting, and the reeds glow brighter or dimmer in response, and the whole basin seems to breathe. The place is established — ancient, eroded, in balance — and it is also in a state of chaotic becoming. It has always been thus. The balance is the turbulence. The erosion is the growth. The stillness is the sound. The place is not decaying; it is composing. It is not dying; it is rehearsing.

The weirdness accumulates in the details. The chime-reeds grow toward something — not the sun, which they cannot see clearly, but toward each other, toward the vents, toward the center of the basin, so that the stands are not random but patterned, arranged in arcs and spirals that suggest intention, or respiration, or the growth-rings of something vast. The pan-walkers migrate in routes that are too precise, too regular, too ancient — they walk the same paths their ancestors walked, and the paths are not the easiest routes but the ones that make the reeds ring in certain ways, that resonate with the tower on the rim, that keep the whole basin in tune. The vents pulse in rhythms that suggest speech, or digestion, or dreams. The haze thickens and thins in patterns that might be random, might be responsive, might be communicative. The whole basin might be a single organism, or a single process, or a single thought, or a single instrument being played by something that has not yet arrived. The inhabitants, if they are here, know this. They have always known. They tend the reeds and tune the harps and read the vents and wait. They do not speak of it, or they speak of it constantly, or they have no concept of it at all — but they know, the way you know your own body, the way you know a song you have heard since before you were born.

The place does not care. The place is busy. The place is growing. The place is singing. And if you stand in the center of the pan, on the cracked white-gold crust, surrounded by the ringing of a million glass blades, with the haze thick around you and the vents roaring in the distance and the pan-walkers moving slow as hills across the far plates and the tower on the rim humming its low note into your chest — if you stand there long enough, you will begin to hear it. Not the sound. The pattern. The intention. The thing the place is saying, has always been saying, will say until the vents go cold and the reeds stop ringing and the crust finally, after all these epochs, goes quiet. You will hear it, and you will not be able to unhear it, and you will understand that you have not come to a place. You have come to a voice. And it has noticed you. And it is still singing.
