# The Warming Dark

The warmth comes up through the soles of your boots first. Steady, patient, like standing on the flank of something sleeping. The ground is warm — not hot, never hot enough to burn, but warm the way a body is warm, a low persistent heat that doesn't rise or fall because there is no sun to rise or fall for. The sky is a deep reddish-brown, the color of old iron, of dried blood, of rust spreading across a forgotten tool. It presses down from above, thick and close, and gives nothing back. You can see, after your eyes adjust. Dimly. In shades of rust and shadow and black. The light comes from everywhere and nowhere, scattered through an atmosphere that has never known a clear day.

The rock under your feet is raw. No soil, no sand, no dust — those things take time, and this world has not had time. The surface is freshly extruded basalt and obsidian, still carrying the shapes of their liquid birth. Ripples frozen mid-flow. Bubbles caught in the act of escaping. The edges are sharp enough to cut. Nothing has been weathered. Nothing has been smoothed. The land has not yet learned to be old.

You are high — absurdly high, kilometers above any baseline that might exist, on a plateau that is still rising. The elevation is raw too, lumpy and undecided, mountains that are not yet mountains, just swellings in the crust that haven't committed to the form. The air is thin but warm, heated from below, and it carries sound poorly. The silence is profound. You can hear your own blood. You can hear the faint, distant hiss of a gas vent somewhere to the west, and beneath that, below the threshold of hearing, a low continuous rumble that you feel in your teeth more than your ears. The ground is moving. Slowly, constantly, like breathing.

---

The plateau extends in all directions, featureless for kilometers, then suddenly not.

A field of basalt columns rises to the east — hexagonal pillars, some as tall as trees, some fallen and broken, arranged in a rough circle that might be natural or might not. To the north, a plain of black obsidian gleams dully in the dim light, its surface a frozen ocean of glass. To the south, sulfur flats spread in bands of yellow and pale green, warm to the touch, smelling of sharp minerals. And scattered across all of it, visible from a distance as dark shapes against the dark ground, standing stones. Single pillars of rough rock, some no taller than a person, some twice that. They don't form a pattern you can read. They don't point anywhere obvious. They're just there — old beyond reckoning, already half-absorbed by the landscape that is still, even now, growing around them.

The ground is not stable. It trembles constantly, a gentle persistent vibration that you stop noticing after a while, like the hum of a city. But sometimes it intensifies. Sometimes the ground cracks, and a new fissure opens, exhaling warm gas and the smell of minerals. Sometimes a section of the plateau rises by a meter over the course of an hour, and the rock groans and splits. Sometimes a hill that was there yesterday is gone today, collapsed into a new basin. The landscape is being created and destroyed in real-time, on a timescale of hours and days. Nothing is permanent except the warmth and the dark.

In sheltered places — in the lee of rock formations, in cracks and crevices — pale branching structures grow. They look like coral, or like fungi, or like frost on a window. They're made of mineral deposits, precipitated from the warm gas that seeps through the rock. They grow by accretion, molecule by molecule, over years. They crumble when touched. They're not alive, but they grow, and they spread, and they die back when the gas flow changes. They're the closest thing this world has to an ecology — a system of chemical flows and deposits and dissolutions, self-sustaining, self-replicating in a crude way, responding to stimuli. Not life, but life-like. Pre-life, perhaps. Or post-life. Or something else entirely.

In the deepest shadows, where the rock is thinnest, a faint light rises from below. Not sunlight — the light comes from the warm interior, filtered through translucent rock, a deep amber glow like the light through a thumb held over a candle. The glow pulses slowly, in sync with the tremors. In the darkest areas, the glow is enough to see by — a dim, warm, amber twilight that comes from the ground itself. Standing in it, you cast no shadow. The light is too diffuse, too omnidirectional. You are illuminated from below, and your face is strange to you.

---

The standing stones are not the only structures. There are low walls, chest-high, made of stacked slabs, forming rough enclosures that might have been shelters or might have been pens or might have been something else entirely. There are cairns, piles of black stone, some no larger than a person, some larger. There are lines of stones, short paths, leading nowhere obvious. All of it is ancient. All of it is made of the local rock. All of it is slowly being absorbed — the ground rising around the walls, the stones themselves crumbling and being replaced by mineral accretions that mimic their shapes. Who built them? When? There's no one now. The structures are the only evidence that someone was here, or something, and the evidence is being erased.

There are no animals. No plants. No birds in the dark sky. No insects in the warm air. The silence is not the silence of a place where life is hiding — it's the silence of a place where life has never been, or has not yet been. The mineral growths are the closest thing, and they are not life. The world is too young, or too old, or too something, for life. It is a mineral world, a world of rock and gas and warmth and darkness, and it is complete without life.

The air is dry. Breathing it is like breathing dust and heat. Your lips crack. Your skin dries. Your body craves water constantly, a deep animal craving that has no answer here. But the warmth is constant, so you never feel cold. The cold is the one thing this world has never learned.

The sky is textured. Layers of cloud or haze, dark reddish-brown, with occasional lighter patches where something is happening — maybe lightning, maybe just thicker and thinner haze. No stars. No moon. No sun. The sky is a ceiling, close and heavy. Sometimes the lighter patches pulse faintly, and the whole sky brightens by a shade, and the shadows on the ground shift, and you realize that the light is alive too, that it breathes with the ground.

The horizon is visible but not clearly. The darkness and the haze blur the distance. You can see shapes at the horizon — maybe mountains, maybe clouds, maybe nothing. The plateau seems to extend forever, or to end just beyond what you can see. The scale is impossible to judge. The dim light compresses distances, makes everything feel closer than it is. A feature that looks an hour away might be six hours. A feature that looks six hours away might be a day.

Time is hard to tell. There's no sun, no day or night. The sky is always the same deep reddish-brown. The only way to tell time is by the tremors, which come and go in cycles that don't match any clock. And by your own body, which gets tired, hungry, thirsty. But the thirst is the worst.

---

The ground is alive with small details. Cracks that glow faintly from within. Vents that hiss warm gas, then fall silent, then hiss again. Patches of mineral growth, pale against the dark rock. Areas where the rock is still soft enough to take a footprint, and the footprint slowly fills in as the rock flows back, hours later, like a wound healing. Areas where the rock is hard and sharp and unforgiving, and a fall would open your skin to the bone.

The obsidian plain is the most dangerous. The glass is razor-sharp, and the dim light hides the edges. Walking on it is like walking on a field of knives. The surface is a frozen ocean — ripples, waves, bubbles, all caught in the act of becoming and stopped. In the darkest areas, the obsidian is translucent enough to show the amber glow from below, and the whole plain seems to be lit from within, a vast dim lamp.

The basalt columns are the most beautiful. Hexagonal pillars, some as tall as trees, some taller, rising from the ground in clusters and rows and rough circles. Some are still connected at the top, forming arches and bridges. Some have fallen, forming fields of broken pillars, like a ruined temple, like a graveyard of giants. The columns are warm, and in the darkest areas they glow faintly from within, and the whole field seems to be breathing.

The sulfur flats are the most alien. Bands of yellow and pale green and white and orange, layered like sedimentary rock but made of pure mineral, deposited by gas vents over millennia. The flats are warm, and they smell of sulfur and sharp chemicals, and the thin air carries the smell poorly but it's there, at the edge of perception, a constant reminder that this world is not for you.

The standing stones are the most mysterious. They're made of the local rock, roughly shaped, standing upright. Some are single, some are in small groups, some are in lines. They're old — older than the current landscape, which means they survived the last major reshaping. How? No one knows. Maybe they were built after the last reshaping. Maybe they were built by something that's gone. Maybe they built themselves, mineral deposits that grew in upright forms and were later abandoned by the processes that made them. They don't form a pattern you can read. They don't point anywhere obvious. They're just there, and they're watching, and they're waiting, and they're being absorbed.

---

This is a world that hasn't decided what it wants to be. The geology is raw and undecided. The rocks are still figuring out their forms. The mineral growths are experiments in structure. The built-looking formations are the world's first attempts at architecture, made by no one. The standing stones are questions the world is asking itself, in a language of rock and gas and warmth and darkness.

The rules are simple: the ground is warm. The sky is dark. The land is still forming. The only constant is change. Nothing is permanent. The structures that seem oldest are already being absorbed. The landscape that seems stable is already moving. The world is not finished, and it may never be finished. It is a world in the process of becoming, and you are standing on it while it dreams.

And the dreams are slow, and they are warm, and they are dark, and they are not for you. You are a visitor in a place that has no use for visitors, a witness to a world that is not yet a world — a brief warm thing standing on the back of something that is still sleeping, still becoming, still dreaming its slow mineral dreams of rock and gas and darkness and warmth and time.
