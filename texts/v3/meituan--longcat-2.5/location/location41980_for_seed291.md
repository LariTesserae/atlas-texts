The Vethra River comes down from the cold north through a mountain gorge and, where the valley floor widens into a low basin, lets go of its load. The delta it has built is young — a few thousand years of sediment, which is nothing. The ground is raw alluvium: silt, sand, and fine gravel, layered and cross-bedded by the river's restlessness, still compacting, still settling, still deciding what it wants to be. There is no bedrock within reach. Channels braid and rejoin in patterns that change with each flood. Islands emerge — a bar of gravel after the spring melt, a mound of silt after an autumn storm — and may persist for a decade or be gone by the following thaw. The edges of the delta feather into marsh and shallow lagoon. The whole thing is embryonic, provisional, still arriving. The river is the builder and it has not yet settled on a design.

The city of Sorva occupies the higher bars and islands of the central delta. It is dense in the way a reef is dense — every surface colonized, every gap filled, no empty space. Buildings press against each other, separated by narrow canals and passages that are sometimes dry and sometimes flooded. Construction is layered vertically: the lower floors, often damp or submerged, are used for storage or industry or are simply sealed and abandoned; the living and working spaces are above; and above those, new construction is always beginning. Cranes are as common as chimneys. Scaffolding clings to every facade. The city is being continuously assembled and disassembled. Nothing is finished. Nothing is entirely demolished. A building may be rebuilt three times on the same foundation, each iteration different, the old materials incorporated into the new.

The foundations are the city's response to the shifting ground. Buildings are set on screw-piles — massive iron screws turned deep into the delta sediment — that can be adjusted as the land settles or rises. Others sit on timber cribs filled with stone, shimmed and reshimmed over the years. Some structures are on floating platforms, moored to piles, rising and falling with the water. The city has learned to move with the land, and this has produced architecture that is strange: buildings that lean at angles that would be catastrophic elsewhere but are stable here because they are braced against each other; structures cantilevered over channels on adjustable iron arms; entire blocks that are theoretically movable, though in practice they are never moved.

The industry is organized around the river's sediment. The Vethra carries down from the mountains a fine, pale-gray clay — plastic when wet, hard as stone when dry, useful for ceramics and sealing and a dozen chemical processes. Mills and foundries line the main channels. The works are mechanized and scaled: steam-powered mills, electric furnaces, conveyor systems, rail lines on raised embankments. Smoke and steam rise from the industrial quarter, but the mist absorbs much of it. The air smells of wet clay and hot metal and something faintly sulfurous. The industry runs continuously, but the sound is muffled — the mist and the water and the moss absorb the noise. The city is busy but quiet. You hear the clatter of a crane's block, the hiss of steam, the call of a bird, the lapping of water against a pile. The overall effect is of a place that is working hard but not raising its voice.

The vegetation is lush in the manner of cold, wet places — the green of moss and fern rather than the green of leaves. Mosses carpet every surface: stone, timber, iron, roof, pipe, cable. They are thick and soft and vividly green, growing with extraordinary speed in the damp cold air. Ferns crowd the gaps between buildings, the gutters, the drainpipes, the spaces where walls meet the ground. Stunted birch and willow find purchase in the thinnest accumulation of silt and grow in dense thickets in every open space. The vegetation is packed in with everything else. It grows on the cranes, on the bridges, on the moored boats. It half-chokes the narrower canals. The city has not fought this; it has incorporated it. Some factories have living walls of moss that filter the exhaust; some canals are managed as much for their vegetation as for their navigability. The boundary between the built and the grown is not a line but a zone of interpenetration, several meters deep in places.

The fauna is abundant. The delta is a major stop on a migratory bird route, and in season the sky is full of waterfowl — ducks, geese, waders, terns — nesting on the islands and the rooftops. Fish run in the channels: salmon in the cold months, smaller species in the warm. Insects swarm in the brief summer, thick enough to cloud the air near the marshes. The cold slows everything but there is a great deal of life. Rats and mink move through the lower levels of the city. Bats roost in the abandoned upper floors. Herons stand motionless on the canal edges, ignored by the passersby. The delta is loud with birdcall at dawn and dusk, but the mist absorbs it, and the overall impression is of a place teeming with life that is nonetheless quiet.

The light is normal — overcast but not dark, the sun a diffuse brightness behind the cloud layer. The mist rises from the water and hangs in the air, softening edges, muting colors. The palette is gray and green and brown: the gray of the sediment and the clay, the green of the moss and ferns, the brown of the timber and the water. The city does not look old, exactly, because it is always being rebuilt, but it looks layered — a palimpsest of construction and demolition and reconstruction, the old never fully erased, the new always beginning.

The strangeness is in the details. The clay the city processes is not quite like other clays. It is extraordinarily plastic when wet — it can be molded by hand into forms of great delicacy — and it dries to a hardness that borders on stone. Some of the older buildings incorporate it as a structural material, and it has weathered into shapes that look almost organic, like bone or coral. The vegetation has adapted to the clay-rich environment in unexpected ways: certain mosses concentrate the mineral and take on a faint bluish cast; certain ferns grow in the steam vents of the industrial quarter, thriving in the warmth and the moisture, their fronds a lighter green than their relatives elsewhere. The constant rebuilding has produced architectural forms that are genuinely unfamiliar — buildings that are half-timber and half-concrete and half-clay, fused together in strata; structures that have been added to so many times they resemble geological formations more than buildings. And the delta itself is strange: channels that shift and sometimes, during flood events, briefly reverse their flow; islands that appear overnight and are colonized by vegetation within a week; patches of ground that are warm to the touch even in the coldest months, as if the sediment were still generating heat as it compacted.

The turbulence is the constant. The river shifts. The city rebuilds. The vegetation grows and is cleared and grows back. The fauna migrates through. The industry runs day and night. The delta is an anthill of activity, but the activity is quiet — the sound absorbed by the mist and the moss and the water and the cold air. The place is in a state of continuous becoming. It is not settled. It is not finished. It is not still. It is a city on a river that is still deciding where to go, built on land that is still deciding what to be, and it has the quality of something that is not yet complete — something that is, in the deepest sense, alive.
