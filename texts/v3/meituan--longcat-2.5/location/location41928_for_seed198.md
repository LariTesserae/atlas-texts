They call it the Weep.

Officially it is Vael, the Cauldron Basin Administrative Territory — a name that survives only on customs manifests and the brass plates of the fog-harvest bureaus. Everyone who lives there calls it the Weep, and after your first hour inside it you understand why: water leaves the place constantly, from every surface, and the place is never entirely dry, and neither are you.

You arrive from the north pass in the hour before the false dawn, when the fog is thickest, and the first thing you lose is the mountains. The road descends in switchbacks cut through mortared terrace walls, and the mist takes the sound of your engine, your footsteps, the creak of your pack, and folds them away, until the loudest thing in the world is water: drips from the parapet, a gutter running somewhere beneath the road, the soft lap of a canal you cannot yet see. Then the fog thins the way a curtain thins, and Vael is below you — a bowl of dim green-gold under a lid of cloud, every surface alive with wet.

The Cauldron is a high geothermal basin, ringed on all sides by bare peaks two and a half kilometers up. The peaks are the only naked rock you will see. Everything inside the rim is made. Long ago — the records disagree on how long, and the city's historians enjoy the disagreement — the basin floor was cased: terraced, channeled, mortared, faced. There is no soil in Vael except what sits in troughs and pots. There is no ground in the lowland sense; there are floors, many of them, one above another, and beneath them the shallow aquifer, and beneath that the hot seams. Dig a meter anywhere inside the city and water greys in, sullen and warm. The valley is a vessel, and the vessel is made.

The first rule of the place is that the water moves. The springs migrate along their fault-seams, decade by decade, sometimes season by season. A cellar that was dry for a generation begins to weep. A fountain that has run for a century goes silent, and its basin becomes a planter. The ground is restless; the city answers. Buildings stand on jacking screws and are re-leveled by crews with felt mallets and chalk marks. Canals are re-routed, barges re-moor, and the maps in the water-keepers' office are dated by the week. Flooding is not a disaster in Vael. It is weather. The only true disasters are the ones you failed to expect, and the city, after long practice, expects almost everything.

From the rim you can take it in at once, and it is worth taking in: six or seven kilometers of dense, dripping construction, terrace leaning on terrace, district on district, the whole bowl packed so full that it reads less like a city than like one organism that has grown in place for centuries. On the eastern slope, the Foundry Terraces run warm with kilns, brine boilers, glassworks and forges — the only part of the city where your breath does not mist, and the only part with insects. Below them the Ropewalks, long arched alleys where cable is walked out under sooty lamps, the air thick with hemp and tar. Above the basin's heart rise the Drips, the residential stacks, where every balcony is a moss garden and every eave is a waterfall in all but the driest week of the year. At the center sits the Whisper Exchange under its iron-and-glass canopy, gone black with age and green with oxide, and beneath everything the Warrens: warm, dark, sweet-smelling fungal cellars where brine-soaked timber grows the city's leather and much of its food. And over all of it, the cranes. The city has more cranes than birds, and the cranes are never still.

Nothing here is natural substrate, and nothing here is finished. Streets at the lower levels are canals, brick-lined and barge-wide; streets at the upper levels are paved, but the paving is laid over warm discharge pipes, so the frost never takes and the moss grows between the setts all year. Every facade is faced with glazed tile, which sheds water and takes a patina; every roof is in use — moss lawns, sedge meadows, fog-net racks, apiaries, cisterns, pigeon-less dovecotes converted to pump housing. Skybridges stitch the towers at four, five, six floors up. Pipes and cables run along every face of every building, lagged in rope and felt, so that from across the basin the walls look wrapped rather than built. Timber is scarce and precious — imported down the pass at great cost — so wood is for boat ribs, loom shuttles, pattern frames and coffins. Stone is cheap. Stone is the present. The city even quarries its own walls: the great retaining terraces weep from a hundred seam-spouts, and the mineral crusts that form there — banded ochre, rust, cream — are harvested, dressed, and sold as building stone. The slow quarry, the masons call it. The walls grow; the city grows them.

Light is the city's second preoccupation, because light is scarce. The cloud deck sits on the rim like a lid, and under it the day is a brightening and dimming of pearl-grey, diffuse, shadowless. At street level it is perpetual dusk. So the lamps are everywhere: electric, fed by the turbines in the deep levels that run on the basin's own heat, glowing amber in glass globes, doubled and tripled in wet stone and canal water. Windows steam and glow. Late boats drift through the lower canals with single lamps, like slow embers. On the Foundry slopes the stacks throw a dull red up into the fog, and whole streets of cloud are lit from below, so that some nights the ceiling of the valley is a warm dark orange and the streets beneath it are teal and shadow. Direct sun is a rumor. On the rare days the deck breaks — perhaps a dozen a year — the city stops. People climb to the roofs. Children who have never seen their own shadows stand in the Bright and cry, or laugh, or sleep, overwhelmed. It is a holiday, every time. It is always disorienting.

Sound behaves strangely here, and so the city has built its life around the strangeness. Fog eats noise. The dense green eats it too — moss and fern and felt are the best absorbents known, and the city is upholstered in them. The result is the Hush: a background so low that conversation across a room arrives softened, that a crowd sounds like wind in leaves, that the loudest thing in the Exchange is the rustle of chalk and paper tickets. Commerce there is conducted in near-silence, by hand-signs and written chits and token trays; auctioneers use a silent hand-chant, fingers flickering like a conductor's. Locals lean close to speak. Voices are for friends. Loud is rude, and loud is also dangerous, because the ground is listening — or rather, the people are listening to the ground: gas seeps hiss before a blowout, and a spring about to shift ticks in its channel. Listening is a civic skill. The permitted loud things are few: the felt mallets of the levelling crews, the electric whine of the cranes (dampened, always dampened), the shift-horn at dawn and dusk — two low notes — and the water-keepers' bell, a deep bronze note that travels wrong in fog and seems to arrive from everywhere at once.

The green is property, and property is green. Every wall garden, every trough of liverworts, every rooftop meadow of sedge is owned, leased, assessed, and argued over. The vegetation is lush beyond belief — moss in thirty kinds, ferns cascading from every ledge, algae panels farmed for oil and pigment, the Warrens' fungi in their ranked dark — and it is almost entirely silent green, spore green. No flowers to speak of, no pollinators to speak of. The city's birds are the eave-swifts, small and dark, nesting under bridges, and their thin needle-call is the only birdsong in the basin. Cats exist, a few, pampered and legendary. Insects live only in the Foundry Terraces and the rooftop hives above them, and there are eels in the warm canals, tended by their keepers like livestock, caught rarely and eaten rarely and prized always. The deep levels have rats, the stories say. The deep levels have many things, the stories say. Almost nobody goes to check.

The people move like weather. At shift change the stairs and bridges fill with a river of wool, felt and oilskin — rust reds, indigos, ochres, all dark with damp — and the river moves quietly, soft-soled, breathing mist. The city works in three tides: the foundries never stop, but the crews change; the fog-nets are tensioned and read by keepers who read mesh the way sailors read sails; the markets assemble at dusk and dissolve before dawn, stalls unfolding from bridges, awnings rigged between cranes. Nothing is built to last in the lowland sense; everything is built to be replaced, adjusted, re-levelled, re-faced. Scaffolding is not a temporary condition here. It is an architectural style. A district can change its trade in a season, its roofline in a week. Maps are dated by the week. The city is never the same city twice — and it has been that way for centuries. The only permanence is the rate of change.

And yet it is in balance. That is the thing you feel after the first day, when the damp stops being a complaint. Everything is weathered, yes — but weathered the way a river stone is weathered: settled, rounded, in its own state. Erosion is not decay in Vael. It is deposition. The mineral crusts, the moss, the patina on the tile, the slow accretion of the slow quarry — the city is accumulating itself as fast as it wears away, and it has been doing so long enough that the two processes are married. The city has achieved its weather.

Stand at the deepest hour on a skybridge over the lower canal, and you will see the whole thing at once. A crane's lamp swings across the fog, slow, patient, feeling for its load. Below, a night barge slides past with one ember of light, its wake clicking softly against the mooring posts. Water drips from the bridge arch in a rhythm like a clock with no hands. Somewhere a felt mallet taps, tap, taps. The fog presses close, warm, smelling of moss and wet stone and faint sulfur, and the underside of the cloud deck is the color of old brass. Nothing here is wild. Nothing here is finished. The Weep weeps, as it has wept for longer than anyone remembers, and it is, on the whole, content.
