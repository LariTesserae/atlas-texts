The basin is old. Not old in the way of mountains, which are old because they have endured, but old in the way of a wound that has healed into a scar — the land here was opened, and then it closed, and what remains is the memory of that opening, softened and overgrown and saturated. It sits low, below the surrounding country, so water gathers here the way attention gathers in a quiet room. There is no outlet that behaves like an outlet. The water comes in from the hills on the west and the north, and it stays.

The ground is peat. In places it is shallow, a skin over clay; in places it is deep, ten meters or more, an accumulated sponge of dead vegetation compressed into something that is neither soil nor stone but has the patience of both. The peat is dark, the color of strong tea, and it weeps when you step on it. Water rises into your footprints before your next footfall. The whole basin is slowly exhaling.

The vegetation is lush in the way of a place that has given up on being anything else. Sphagnum moss in hummocks and hollows, green and russet and gold, so dense that it forms a continuous carpet that swallows sound and light. Sedges with their sharp leaves, rushes in clumps, cotton-grass with its white heads bobbing in the least breath of wind. Low shrubs — bog rosemary, crowberry, dwarf birch stunted into creeping forms by the wet and the wind. Scattered trees, pine or spruce or something like them, but they don't grow tall; they grow slowly, and their roots are shallow, and they lean at angles that suggest they have been listening to something for a long time and have begun to tilt toward it.

There are no animals to speak of. No birds call. No insects hum. The stillness is not the stillness of death but the stillness of a place that has decided, collectively, to be quiet. Occasionally there is a track in the peat — the three-toed print of something large, or the delicate stitching of something small — but the tracks don't lead anywhere. They stop, or they loop, or they double back in ways that suggest the animal was as confused as you are.

The water is the dominant presence. It is everywhere near the surface, and it moves. Not dramatically — there are no rapids, no waterfalls — but it moves with a kind of slow insistence. Channels braid and rejoin across the basin, and they are old channels, incised into the peat over centuries, so that the water follows paths that were decided long ago. But the water also does things that water should not do. In some places it flows uphill, slowly, imperceptibly, rising from a lower pool to a higher one over the course of hours. In some places it climbs the inside of a hollow tree trunk and emerges from a knot hole three meters up, falling in a thin thread to a pool below. In some places it is warm — not hot, but warm, as if it has been somewhere deep and has returned with a memory of heat.

And the water carries things. Not sediment, though it carries that too. It carries impressions. If you hold your hand in a certain pool — and you will know which pool, because the water in it is a degree or two colder than the surrounding water, and it seems to pull the heat from your skin — you may see something. Not a vision, exactly. More like the memory of a place that isn't this one. A flash of a different sky. The shape of a building that was never built here. The sound of voices speaking a language that has no name.

This is the nature of the basin: it is a place where the membrane between what is and what might be has worn thin. Not torn — worn, the way a stone is worn by water, over a long time, until it is smooth and translucent. The wearing is not uniform. There are places where the membrane is thicker, and the world behaves as the world should — the water flows downhill, the light falls from the sun, time moves at the pace it has always moved. And there are places where it is thin, and the world becomes suggestible.

In the thin places, time pools. A drop of water falls from a ledge and hangs in the air, trembling, for minutes or hours. A leaf detaches from a branch and descends so slowly that you can watch it for a quarter of an hour and not see it move. Conversely, in other thin places, things accelerate — a fallen branch crumbles to fragments in a day, a pool evaporates in an afternoon, a seed germinates and grows and flowers and dies in the span of a breath. The thin places are not marked. They do not glow or hum or announce themselves. You find them by the way your watch stops, or the way your shadow falls in a direction the light doesn't justify, or the way a path you have walked before is suddenly longer or shorter than you remember.

Space, too, is unreliable. The basin is not large — you can see across it, from one side to the other, on the rare days when the mist lifts — but it is not the size it appears to be. A walk that should take an hour takes a day, or a walk that should take a day takes an hour, and there is no way to predict which. The landmarks shift. A stand of stunted pines that you passed on your left appears on your right, and then behind you, and then not at all. The far edge of the basin, which looks like a low ridge covered in the same moss and sedge as the rest of the place, recedes as you approach it, and the light behind it is not the light of the sky but something else, something older and more diffuse, as if the sun that illuminates the far edge is not the sun you know.

The sound of the basin is loud. This is not because anything in it is loud — there are no animals, no rushing water, no wind strong enough to howl — but because the sound carries. The air is dense with moisture, and moisture is a better medium for sound than air, and the basin is shaped like a bowl, and the bowl is lined with peat, which is soft and absorbent, except where it is not. The result is an acoustic that is strange and specific. A footstep on the peat, which should be a soft sound, arrives at your ears a moment after you make it, as if it has traveled a long distance. The drip of water from a branch, which should be a small sound, arrives from the wrong direction, or from several directions at once. And underneath these sounds, there is a hum — low, constant, felt in the sternum more than heard — that seems to come from the peat itself, or from the water in the peat, or from the space beneath the water. The hum has a structure. It rises and falls in patterns that are almost musical, almost linguistic, as if the basin were trying to say something and had forgotten the words but remembered the rhythm.

In certain places, the hum becomes audible. Not louder — clearer. It resolves into something like a voice, or a chord, or the sound of a vast machine operating at the threshold of perception. These places are the thinnest places. If you stand in one long enough, you may begin to understand what the voice is saying, not in words but in something prior to words — a meaning that arrives directly, without the mediation of language. The meaning is usually simple, and usually about water, or time, or the nature of the basin itself. It is not always benevolent. It is not always comprehensible. It is always old.

The light in the basin is dim. The sky is almost always overcast, and the cloud is low, and the mist is frequent, and the vegetation is dense enough to filter what light does arrive. The light that reaches the ground is diffuse, sourceless, the color of old pewter or of water that has been standing in a dark place for a long time. It does not cast sharp shadows. It pools in hollows and drains from hummocks, so that the basin appears to be lit from below, or from within, rather than from above. At the edges of the thin places, the light behaves oddly — it thickens, or thins, or moves in slow currents like a liquid, and objects in it appear to be at different distances than they are, or to be made of different materials, or to be slightly transparent, so that you can see the peat through them, or the water through the peat.

There are structures in the basin. Not built structures — there is nothing built here, no roads, no buildings, no tools, no markers — but structures nonetheless, formations that have the quality of design. In the deepest part of the peat, where the water is stagnant and the vegetation is thickest, there are accretions of mineral that grow in geometric patterns — hexagonal plates, spiral columns, lattices of such delicacy that they appear to have been etched rather than grown. They are not metal, though they have a metallic sheen. They are not crystal, though they are translucent. They are not alive, though they grow, slowly, over centuries, adding layer to layer in a process that resembles nothing so much as deposition, or crystallization, or the slow accretion of a thought. They are found in clusters, and the clusters are arranged in patterns that suggest function — channels, nodes, junctions — and the water flows through them, and the hum is loudest near them, and the thin places cluster around them like iron filings around a magnet.

These formations are the closest thing the basin has to a purpose. They suggest that the place is doing something — processing, computing, remembering — and that the water, the peat, the light, the thin places, the hum, are all part of that doing. The purpose is not legible. It may not be a purpose in any human sense. It may be that the basin is simply the residue of something that happened here long ago, something that operated on physical laws that are no longer in force, and that the formations are the fossilized machinery of that operation, still running, still processing, still remembering, long after the operator has gone. Or it may be that the basin is alive in a way that has nothing to do with biology, and the formations are its organs, and the hum is its voice, and the thin places are the places where its attention is focused. There is no way to know. The basin does not explain itself.

The rules of the basin are these:

Water flows toward the lowest point, except where it flows toward the oldest point, or the thinnest point, or the point where the hum is loudest.

Time moves forward, except in the pools, where it moves sideways, or backward, or in circles, or not at all.

Distance is a measure of effort, not of space.

Sound is a measure of age, not of loudness — the oldest sounds arrive last.

The light is the memory of a sun that may not be the sun that currently shines.

Nothing is built, but everything is structured.

The plants are the basin's way of being patient.

The animals are rare because they have learned, or because they have forgotten, or because the basin has no use for them.

The basin does not want anything. The basin does not not-want anything. The basin is.

If you stay long enough, you will begin to hear the hum as language. If you hear it as language, you will begin to understand it. If you understand it, you will not leave. Not because you cannot, but because the leaving will seem, by then, like a kind of forgetting, and you will have become, without noticing, the kind of creature that does not forget.
