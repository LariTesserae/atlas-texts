# The Throat

It begins as a crack.

The plateau—what locals call the Weighing Stone, though no one lives near enough to call it anything regularly—has been eroding for four hundred million years. Once a mountain range of impossible altitude, it has been ground down to a vast, pale, nearly-flat expanse of gneiss and quartzite, so ancient that its topography has achieved a kind of terminal simplicity. Rivers don't cross it. Soil barely exists. Wind moves across it with nothing to interrupt it, and the plateau's surface has been polished to a matte, bone-dry finish that reflects the sun like old porcelain.

And then, without warning, the crack.

It's perhaps two meters wide at its narrowest, an irregular linear fracture running fifty meters across the plateau floor. You could step over it. You could miss it in tall grass—except there is no tall grass here. You would notice it only because the air above it moves differently, warm and wet, bending light like a mirage in reverse. The crack breathes.

At its widest point—where a section of the plateau roof has collapsed inward—the opening widens to a throat: a vertical shaft perhaps thirty meters across, and deep. The walls descend through every layer of the plateau's ancient geology, striated bands of metamorphic rock laid down in seas that existed before vertebrates, each layer slightly different in color, a slow rainbow of deep time. Water has long since finished its work here; the walls are smooth, worn to a finish that feels organic. And at the bottom—forty meters below the plateau surface—there is the basin.

---

## The Basin

It is small. That is the first thing. You can see all of it from any point.

Perhaps two hundred meters across, irregularly circular, the basin occupies the full width of the cavern floor. The ceiling above it is not rock but sky—the collapsed throat opens upward, and beyond it, visible as a ragged ellipse of white, is the plateau sky. Light enters from above like water poured from a jug: a single column of illumination that moves with the sun, and at the edges of the basin, everything is in shadow, or at least in the warm brown twilight of reflected light from the walls.

The basin is warm. Not hot—warm. Thirty-two, thirty-four degrees Celsius. The heat comes from below: the same geothermal fracture that opened the throat and collapsed the roof continues downward, and the basin floor is a lattice of warm stone, threaded with narrow channels where mineral water rises and spreads. The water is not hot enough to steam. It is body temperature. It pools in shallow depressions across the basin floor—dozens of them, hundreds, ranging from the size of a palm to the size of a room—and between them, the stone is damp, perpetually damp, a skin of moisture that never fully dries because the air itself is saturated.

The air. It is heavy. It presses against the eardrums on the walk down the throat wall's interior path—a path that switchbacks along the smooth rock, worn into existence by whatever has been coming and going here for longer than the path has existed. The air is thick with oxygen from the vegetation, thick with humidity, thick with the mineral tang of warm water on warm stone. It smells like a greenhouse left open overnight, like a riverbank in summer, like the inside of a body.

---

## The Green

The vegetation does not grow across the basin floor. It grows *everywhere*, in every direction, at every scale.

Mosses first—thick, velvet mosses in perhaps forty distinct species, covering every surface that receives even indirect light, a carpet of green so complete that the stone beneath it is theoretical, known only by the way your foot sinks into it. The mosses give way to ferns at the base of the walls, great arching ferns with fronds the length of a human body, their fiddleheads still coiled like sleeping fists in the permanent warmth. Above them, climbing the walls, are vines—thick, woody lianas that have found purchase in every crack the ancient erosion left behind, draping downward in loops and curtains, some reaching the basin floor, others stopping halfway, their leaves clustering at the ends like green chandeliers.

And in the pools. The pools are not water and stone. They are water and *life*. Floating ferns, tiny and perfect, cover the smaller pools entirely—a skin of green so dense it looks like solid ground until you step on it and it gives way. Larger pools hold water lilies, but not any water lily that exists in field guides: they have broad, flat leaves a meter across, deep green above and the color of old blood beneath, and their flowers are the size of a human head, white and open even at night, releasing a scent that is sweet in a way that borders on rot—a fruit-sweet, fermentation-sweet, the smell of something that wants to be eaten.

Trees grow here. Not large ones—the light is too diffuse, the space too enclosed—but there are trees. A species of fig, perhaps, or something like a fig, with pale bark and small, dense leaves, their roots gripping the wall or plunging into the moss floor, their branches spreading horizontally to catch what light falls from the throat above. They are thirty, forty in number, scattered across the basin with the irregular spacing of old growth, and their roots have broken and lifted sections of the warm stone, creating hollows beneath the canopy where the air is even warmer, even wetter, and the moss grows to ankle depth.

The vegetation is not arranged in zones. It is not organized by moisture or light or soil depth. It is everywhere at once, layered and overlapping, each species growing wherever it has managed to establish, the whole basin a single tangled, continuous green that seems less like an ecosystem and more like a single organism that has decided to express itself as many different forms.

---

## The Noise

It is loud. Not loud like a storm—loud like a body full of noise, loud like standing inside a mechanical clock, loud like a room where every surface is vibrating.

The water is the foundation of it. The geothermal channels beneath the basin floor are narrow and constricted, and the warm water moving through them produces a constant, low, subterranean rumble—not a sound you hear so much as a sound you feel in your sternum, a continuous bass note that never quite resolves. Above this, the pools themselves speak: water trickling from one pool to another through moss-choked channels, water dripping from the fern fronds in the permanent condensation that forms at their tips, water falling from the throat opening above when the intermittent rains of the plateau reach the crack and funnel down into the basin in sudden, heavy sheets that strike the pools and the canopy and the moss and produce a roar that fills the entire space for minutes at a time before subsiding.

And the creatures. The creatures are overwhelming.

---

## The Animals

Bats. The basin holds bats in numbers that make the air thick. They roost in the upper walls, in every crevice and overhang, in clusters that hang like fruit, and they emerge at dusk—or what passes for dusk in the basin, the dimming of the light column as the sun passes the throat's edge—in a stream that takes an hour to fully exit. The sound of their departure is a continuous, chattering, clicking roar, thousands of voices in frequencies that range from audible squeaking to a subsonic pulse you feel in your teeth. They fill the air above the basin in a column of movement so dense it seems impossible, and they do not leave the basin—they circle, they wheel, they dive, they feed on the insects that rise from the vegetation in clouds at the same hour, and the noise of their feeding, the snap of wings and the clicking of echolocation, is a physical presence in the air.

Insects. The insect population of the basin is beyond counting. Dragonflies with bodies the length of a finger hover above the pools in groups of hundreds, their wings producing a continuous, high, electric whine. Beetles—great, slow, iridescent beetles the size of a thumb—crawl across the moss in visible waves, consuming the constant rain of detritus from the canopy above. Mosquitoes rise from the pools in columns, and above them, the dragonflies, and above the dragonflies, the bats, and above the bats, swallows—small, dark, impossibly fast swallows that nest in the throat wall and dive through the bat stream without collision, feeding on the insects that the bats disturb but do not catch.

There are frogs. There are always frogs in a place like this, and these are not small frogs. They sit on the lily pads with their throats pulsing, calling in a chorus that is not loud individually but is deafening in aggregate—a continuous, rolling, basso-profundo *ronk-ronk-ronk* that fills the lower register of the basin's soundscape and vibrates the surface of the pools in visible concentric rings. Some are the size of a fist. A few, the old ones, are the size of a human head, and they sit motionless for hours, their eyes at the waterline, waiting.

And there are things that are not frogs, not bats, not insects. Things that move in the peripheral vision. Things that the light does not catch correctly. The basin is warm and wet and old, and it has been sealed in its current form for longer than it is comfortable to consider, and the life within it has had time to become something that is not quite what life outside the crack would recognize. There are creatures in the pools that have no names. There are shapes in the moss that resolve into something and then don't. There is a particular quality to the movement at the edge of the fern curtains, a suggestion of something large enough to push the fronds aside, that recurs often enough to be a pattern but never resolves into a sighting.

## The Remains

The basin has been found before.

In the warm stone of the basin floor, near the largest pool—a pool perhaps twenty meters across, deep enough that its center is black and still—there are structures. Not built *on* the floor. Built *into* it. The stone has been cut, or grown, or convinced, into shapes that are not natural: a ring of low pillars, each perhaps a meter high, arranged in a pattern that suggests a perimeter but does not complete itself. The pillars are the same material as the basin floor, indistinguishable in color and texture, and they are ancient—worn by the same processes that wore the walls, their edges rounded, their surfaces colonized by the same mosses and ferns that cover everything else.

At the center of the incomplete ring, the pool's bottom is not stone. It is something else—a surface that is darker, smoother, and when the light column strikes it at the right angle, it reflects with a quality that stone and water do not possess. A mirror finish. A computed finish. Looking into it, you see the sky through the throat, but the sky is not the sky as it is now. The reflection shows a different configuration of clouds, or perhaps a different time of day, or perhaps something that is not sky at all but a similar-shaped brightness that has been substituted.

The pillars are not the only marks. On the throat walls, at the level of the basin floor, there are faint lines incised into the smooth rock—geometric, precise, running in parallel sets that intersect at angles that suggest a language or a notation but resolve into nothing legible. The lines are filled with a material that is not the rock's natural mineral deposit. They glow faintly, not with light, but with warmth—the same body-temperature warmth as the water, the same warmth as the air, and they have been producing it for as long as anyone has been coming here, which is longer than the oldest records of the plateau's exploration.

There is a single structure that is clearly built, not grown or cut. It sits on a ledge at the base of the throat wall, a platform perhaps three meters square, made of a material that is not stone, not metal, not ceramic—a dark, slightly translucent substance that seems to absorb the light that falls on it and return nothing. On the platform, there is an object: a shape that suggests a seated figure, or a console, or a seed, or a wound. It is approximately the size of a human torso. Its surface is perfectly smooth and perfectly featureless, and it is warm to the touch—warmer than the air, warmer than the water, a temperature that is not quite comfortable to hold your hand against for long.

The object does nothing observable. It has been sitting on the platform for at least several thousand years, based on the wear patterns in the stone beneath it. It does not move. It does not make sound. It does not respond to touch, or to the presence of living things, or to the cycles of the basin. But the bats do not roost on the ledge above it. The insects do not crawl across the platform. The moss stops at its edge, as if the stone beneath it is not stone at all but something the moss recognizes as not-ground.

---

## The Rain

The plateau above is dry. The basin is not.

The intermittent moisture that sustains the basin comes from two sources: the geothermal water rising through the floor, and the sky through the throat. When it rains on the plateau—which happens perhaps twenty times a year, in sudden, heavy, brief storms—the water finds the crack immediately. It funnels down the throat in a column that is not a waterfall but a *wall* of water, a solid sheet that strikes the basin floor and fills every pool to overflowing in minutes. The sound is total. The basin becomes a drum, every surface vibrating, the air itself seeming to compress and expand with the impact.

The water does not stay. The basin floor is porous, the geothermal channels beneath it drinking the excess, and within an hour of the rain's stopping, the pools have returned to their normal levels, the channels are running with the combined rain-and-spring water, and the moss has absorbed what it can. The humidity spikes to a point where breathing feels like drinking, and for a period of perhaps two hours after each rain, the basin's life enters a state of visible exaltation: the frogs call louder, the insects swarm thicker, the bats emerge in daylight to feed on the explosion of flying things, and the vegetation seems to *move*, the fronds and vines shifting in the heavy air as if responding to a stimulus that is not wind.

This is the rhythm of the place. The long, warm, humid stillness between rains. The brief, violent inundation. The exaltation. The settling. The moss grows. The ferns unfurl. The bats return to their crevices. The water finds its level. The object on the platform continues to be warm and silent and featureless.

---

## What It Is

The basin is old. The plateau is older. The structures are older than both, or they are the same age as the rock, or they are not old at all but have been made to appear so by something that operates on a timescale that makes the distinction meaningless.

The basin is a place that should not exist on a plateau that has been eroding for four hundred million years. It is a pocket of sustained warmth and moisture and life in a landscape that has otherwise achieved a terminal dryness. It is maintained—by the geothermal fracture, by the structures in the floor, by the object on the platform, by something—and the maintenance has been running for long enough that the life within it has become something that is not quite the life that exists outside.

It is hidden. It is small. It is loud and warm and green and full of creatures, and it is not on any map, and the plateau above it is so featureless that the crack is invisible until you are standing at its edge, and even then, it is only a crack, only a line in the stone, only a breath of warm air rising from somewhere below.

You would not know. You would not know until you climbed down.
