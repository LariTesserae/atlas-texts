# The Hollow of Angev

---

The mountain is young. You can feel it in the edges — no rounded shoulders, no patient smoothing. The stone rises in blades and wedges, in fracture planes that catch the light like the inside of a split geode. It is called Angev, and it is barely a million years old, which is nothing. It still remembers being deep. The pressure hasn't finished leaving its body.

On the north-east face, where the rock folds inward like a half-closed hand, there is a hollow. From below — and there is no below, only cloud — you would never see it. The overhang above it juts out like a brow. The approach is a series of ledges and chimneys that seem accidental until you notice the rails, the anchored cables, the subtle resurfacing of stone where footholds have been encouraged. The hollow opens to the sky but not to view. It is a room. A room the size of a town.

---

## The Room

The hollow is perhaps four hundred metres across at its widest, tapering to a narrow slot at its eastern end where water has been finding its way through the rock for decades. The ceiling is a single unbroken arch of dark grey stone, veined with quartz that runs through it in thick, irregular cords. The floor is not flat — it slopes gently toward the centre, where a shallow basin collects everything the mountain weeps.

And the mountain weeps constantly.

Water moves through Angev in ways that don't map onto ordinary hydrology. It doesn't simply seep from above; it emerges from the walls at specific points, from pores in the stone that seem almost designed — round, regular, spaced at intervals that suggest intention. The water is cold and tastes of minerals in a combination that no one has fully catalogued. It runs down the walls in thin sheets and threads, gathering in the basin, which has no visible outlet. The water simply leaves, downward, through a bed of smooth gravel that appears to descend further than any sounding line has yet reached.

The air is wet. Not raining, not misted, but saturated — the humidity sits at the edge of condensation, so that every surface carries a fine film of moisture. Your skin is always damp. Metal tarnishes quickly. The stone itself seems to sweat. In the colder hours — and all hours are cold here, a settled 1–3°C that never quite freezes — the moisture thickens on the ceiling into droplets that grow, detach, and fall with soft, irregular percussion. The sound is the hollow's heartbeat. It never stops.

## The Settlement

They call it simply the Hollow, or sometimes Angev-Town, though it has never held more than two thousand people. The construction is dense — that is the only word — packed into the available space with the organic logic of a thing that has grown rather than been planned. But it was planned. You can see it in the infrastructure: the water channels that run in clean lines along every wall, the cable trays, the ventilation shafts that have been drilled through the rock at angles that suggest they follow natural fracture systems but with a precision that nature doesn't bother with.

The buildings are grown. That is the simplest explanation, though it is not quite accurate. The original settlers — engineers, geologists, a few physicists who had been working on lattice-growth problems — discovered that the stone of Angev responds to certain stimuli by reorganising its crystalline structure. Not dissolving, not eroding, but *rearranging*. Given the right chemical bath, the right frequency of vibration, the right seed-crystal template, the rock will slowly, patiently, build itself into new shapes. Walls. Floors. Load-bearing arches. It takes weeks. The results are seamless — the grown structures are continuous with the parent stone, identical in composition, though their internal grain is subtly different. The new stone is slightly darker, slightly denser, and it carries faint patterns that look like growth rings, or circuit traces, depending on the light.

Most of the town is built from this grown stone. The oldest structures, near the western wall, have been growing for thirty years and have developed a kind of patina — not erosion, exactly, but a softening of the sharpest edges, a rounding that suggests the stone is still slowly settling into its final form. Newer construction near the eastern slot is still angular, still precise, still smelling faintly of the chemical baths used to seed it.

Between the buildings, the space is organised but not regular. Streets — passages — follow the natural topography of the hollow floor, curving around the central basin, climbing the lower slopes of the walls via ramps and staircases that have been coaxed out of the stone. There are bridges across the deeper channels where water runs. There are platforms that jut from the walls at the level where the ceiling begins to curve, connected by narrow walkways that make the most of the vertical space. The town is three-dimensional in a way that ground-based settlements never are — it uses the walls, the ceiling, the full volume of the hollow.

## The Light

The opening above is irregular — a long, rough ellipse that runs from west to east, wider in the middle. It admits normal light, daylight, the full spectrum. At noon, when the sun is high enough, a column of light enters and moves across the floor like a slow searchlight, illuminating first the western wall, then the central basin, then the eastern slot. The effect is not dramatic — the light is diffused by the moisture in the air, and the quartz veins in the ceiling scatter it — but it is the town's clock. The light-column's movement is how people tell the hour. When it reaches the eastern wall, the day is ending. When it disappears, the hollow enters a long twilight that is not quite dark — the stone itself seems to retain some of the light, or the quartz veins luminesce faintly, or perhaps it is simply that the wet surfaces reflect what little ambient light remains from the sky.

There is no artificial lighting in the town. None is needed. The grown stone, it turns out, has interesting optical properties. The growth-ring patterns are slightly phosphorescent — not bright, but enough to navigate by in the deepest hours. And the water itself seems to carry light, or to generate it in some subtle way; the basin at the centre glows faintly on clear nights, a pale blue-green that has never been satisfactorily explained. The physicists have theories. The residents do not concern themselves with theories. They simply live in the dim, watery luminescence and find it sufficient.

## The Sound

The hollow is mostly quiet. The water is the loudest thing — the constant, soft dripping from the ceiling, the trickle of sheets running down the walls, the occasional heavier splash where a channel overflows. The wind does not enter; the overhang and the shape of the opening deflect it. What reaches the town is a low, steady hum, almost below hearing, that seems to come from the mountain itself — perhaps the movement of deep water through fractures, perhaps the slow crystalline adjustment of the stone, perhaps something else entirely.

Voices carry oddly. The curved walls and the water-film on every surface create a complex acoustic environment where sound seems to come from everywhere and nowhere. Conversations in the central area are private unless you raise your voice; sound seems to be absorbed by the wet stone. But a word spoken near the eastern slot will carry clearly to the western platforms, as if the shape of the hollow funnels it. Residents have learned to use this — certain passages serve as natural announcement systems, and the town's informal communication network relies on where you stand to speak as much as what you say.

There are other sounds. A deep, intermittent groaning from the rock, felt more than heard, that occurs every few hours — the mountain settling, the physicists say, thermal contraction as the temperature drops toward its nighttime minimum. A high, thin whistling from the narrowest part of the eastern slot, where wind does enter, though no one can explain how it gets through the overhang. And, rarely, a sharp crack like a gunshot, followed by a brief patter of falling fragments — a piece of the ceiling detaching, a small rockfall from the upper ledges. These events are common enough that no one startles at them anymore. The stone is young. It is still finding its shape.

## The Ecology

There is no plant life. None. The moisture is constant, the light is adequate for at least part of the day, but nothing grows. The stone of Angev is chemically hostile to organic colonisation — the water that seeps through it carries compounds that inhibit cellular division, a property that the original geologists noted and the residents have learned to treat as a blessing. No mould, no moss, no slow creep of lichen across the walls. The town stays clean. What would take centuries of scrubbing elsewhere takes nothing here.

But there is life, of a sort. In the central basin, in the water that is never still, there are colonies of microorganisms that the biologists have classified and reclassified and finally given up on. They are not bacteria, not archaea, not anything that fits the existing taxonomy. They seem to derive energy from the mineral content of the water and the faint thermal gradient between the deep earth and the surface, and they form complex, filamentous mats that drift in the current like underwater clouds. They are colourless, or perhaps a very pale grey. They are the base of a food web that has exactly one other trophic level: small, translucent crustaceans — or crustacean-like organisms — that filter the filaments from the water and are, in turn, eaten by nothing in the hollow. They simply live and die and accumulate in the gravel at the bottom of the basin, their tiny shells contributing to the smooth, pale sediment that the water drains through.

Above, on the outer ledges and in the crevices of the overhang, there are birds. Two species, both small, both grey-brown, both largely silent. They nest in the rock and fly out to hunt in the cloud layer, returning with insects or something like insects that exist in the cold, wet air at this altitude. They are rarely seen inside the hollow — the opening is too small and too exposed for comfortable flight — but their calls, thin and high, occasionally echo down from above, distorted by the stone into something that sounds almost mechanical.

## The Infrastructure

The town runs on water. This is not metaphor. The same seepage that weeps from the walls is channelled, collected, and put to work. The water flows through turbines — small, efficient, grown into the stone channels with the same lattice-growth technology that built the walls — that generate the settlement's modest electrical needs. The water is also the heating system: it emerges from the rock at 4°C, and the difference between that and the ambient air temperature, managed through a network of heat-exchange surfaces, keeps the inhabited spaces just above freezing. It is not warm. No one expects warmth. But it is survivable, and the technology that manages it is elegant in a way that makes the physicists quietly proud.

The more interesting infrastructure is the growth system itself. The original seed-baths and vibration arrays have been refined over decades into something that looks, to the untrained eye, like a series of shallow pools set into the floor at various points around the hollow. The pools contain solutions — clear, faintly coloured — and the seed-crystals, which look like small, dark stones. When a new structure is needed, the residents prepare a template (a physical model, usually carved from wood or printed from a digital design), suspend it in the pool, and wait. Over days, the stone grows toward the template, slowly accreting, following the seed-crystal's lattice direction. The template is removed once the structure has sufficient integrity. The results are not perfectly precise — there is always some deviation, some organic irregularity — but the deviations are small, and the residents have learned to design with them in mind, to treat the growth process as a collaboration rather than a command.

No one fully understands why it works. The original research suggested that the stone of Angev has a crystalline structure that can be "persuaded" to extend itself under the right conditions — that the boundary between existing stone and new growth is not a boundary at all but a continuous process, like the growth of a crystal in a supersaturated solution, just vastly slower and more controlled. But the physics has proven stubborn. The lattice-growth process involves quantum effects at the boundary layer — coherence, entanglement, something that shouldn't persist at room temperature in a macroscopic system. The physicists have published papers. The papers have been largely ignored or dismissed. The residents continue to grow their buildings.

## The People

Two thousand, more or less. They are the descendants of the original settlers and a slow trickle of newcomers — scientists, mostly, and a few who are simply drawn to the place without being able to say why. They are quiet people, suited to the acoustics. They move through the town with the unhurried patience of those who live in a place that changes on geological timescales. They maintain the channels, monitor the growth pools, repair the turbines, study the water, the stone, the organisms in the basin. They have a government of sorts — a council of senior researchers and long-term residents — but it is a government that concerns itself mostly with water allocation and building permits, which is to say, with the rhythm of growth.

They do not leave often. The descent from the hollow is difficult and dangerous, a full day of climbing and scrambling to reach the cloud layer, and there is nothing below the cloud layer except more mountain, and below that, eventually, the lowlands, which the residents regard with a mixture of nostalgia and impatience. They go down once a year, in a group, to trade — the town produces data, mostly, and a few mineral specimens of unusual quality — and to bring back supplies that cannot be grown or manufactured on site. The rest of the year, they are here, in the wet, in the cold, in the dim phosphorescence of the stone, listening to the water.

## The Feeling of the Place

It is not hostile. That is the surprising thing. A cold, wet, plantless stone room at high altitude, inhabited by people who grow their buildings and drink mineral water and listen to the mountain groan — it should feel like exile. It doesn't. It feels like a held breath. Like a place that is waiting for something, or that has already found it.

The wetness is the key. It should be oppressive, but it isn't. The constant moisture creates a kind of softness, a blurring of edges — the stone gleams, the light diffuses, sounds are absorbed and redirected. The cold is present but not punishing; it is the cold of a cellar, of deep water, of the space beneath a glacier. It slows you down. It makes you aware of your body, of the heat you are generating, of the small act of warmth that it is to be alive in a place like this.

And the growth — the knowledge that the town is still growing, that the stone is still slowly building itself into new shapes, that the hollow is not a finished thing but a process — gives the place a quality of becoming. It is not a ruin. It is not a monument. It is a conversation between human intention and mineral patience, and it has been going on for thirty years, and it will go on for longer than anyone now living.

The mountain is young. The town is younger. The water has been here longest of all, moving through the dark, finding its way to the walls, weeping out into the light and the cold air, falling into the basin, disappearing into the deep. It is the mountain's blood, or its breath, or its thoughts — no one has decided. It simply moves, and the town moves with it, and the sound of it is the sound of the place continuing.

*At night, when the light-column is gone and the hollow glows faintly blue-green from the basin, the water dripping from the ceiling sounds almost like speech. The residents say this is only the acoustics. The residents say many things. But they stay.*
