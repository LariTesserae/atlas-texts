# The Holt

The basin is a shallow dish, perhaps two kilometers across, ringed by low hills that keep the wind out and the mist in. The air is cool and damp—never wet, never dry. Condensation beads on every surface and runs in thin threads down bark and iron. Rain comes in soft, intermittent spells, never heavy, just enough to keep the ground dark and the moss thick and the air smelling of wet stone and resin. A small river—really a wide stream—enters through a gap in the eastern rim, wanders across the basin in slow, braided channels, pools in hollows, and drains away through a crack in the western rim. There are puddles everywhere. The soil is dark and compressible, like a sponge.

The basin is a grove. Not a forest—a grove. Forty or fifty massive trees, each the size of a cathedral, spaced well apart with open ground between them. You can stand at the foot of one and see the next across a clearing of moss and fern and low, dark ground-cover, and the next beyond that, and the next, each trunk a separate presence in the mist. They are a species that matches no catalog—something between a banyan and an oak and a pipe organ. Their trunks are thick and fluted, ribbed with buttress roots that rise like walls and fall away like drapery. The bark is dark, damp, deeply furrowed, and it weeps a thin resin that smells of turpentine and wet stone. The canopy is high and broken—not a continuous ceiling but a series of great, irregular crowns that let through slanted columns of light. The trees are old. They have been here for centuries and have reached their size. They are not growing much anymore. They are in their state.

Between and among the trees, the settlement. It is not built near the trees or around them—it is built into them and from them. The oldest structures are grown: living wood trained, grafted, hollowed into walls, floors, chambers, towers. Some buildings are simply hollowed trunks, three or four stories of interior space carved from the heartwood, with windows cut through the bark and stairs spiraling around the inner wall. Others are built from the trees' own substance—planks and beams of dense, resinous wood, darkened with age, fitted with joinery that has no nails. The roofs are layers of bark shingles, or sometimes a living canopy of intertwined branches that sheds water and repairs itself.

Later structures—and there are later structures, generations of them—incorporate industrial materials. Iron, steel, brass, copper, glass, ceramic, concrete. Pipes run along the ground and up the trunks. Cables hang between the crowns. There are engines in the roots—small, sealed, humming things that pump water or air or something else through the system. There are vents in the bark that release steam on a regular cycle, a soft white exhalation that drifts up through the canopy. There are rails, narrow-gauge, that run between the trees on low trestles of wood and iron, and small carts that move along them, powered by a quiet electric motor or a clockwork. The carts carry blocks of resin, bundles of fiber, containers of water, pieces of machinery.

The industrial infrastructure and the trees are not separate. They are fused. A pipe emerges from a trunk like a branch. A gear is embedded in the bark, half-covered by new growth, still turning. A chimney rises from a hollow trunk and vents through the crown, so the tree appears to smoke. A bridge between two trees is a steel truss overgrown with living wood, the metal and the grain fused so that you cannot tell where one ends and the other begins. The machinery is old and well-maintained, but it is also old in the sense that it has been here long enough to have been absorbed. The settlement is not a town with trees in it. It is a single organism of wood and metal, plant and machine, that has grown together over centuries and reached a kind of equilibrium.

The engines run on the trees. This is the quiet strangeness of the place. The root-engines are fed a slow syrup tapped from the trunks—a concentrated resin-sap that the trees produce in quantity, perhaps because they have been bred or grafted to produce it, perhaps because the engines and the trees have co-evolved. The syrup ferments in sealed tanks, and the fermentation gas drives the engines, and the engines pump water and turn gears and generate a low electric current. The trees feed the machines; the machines tend the trees. The settlement is a closed loop, or nearly closed. It has been running this way for so long that the distinction between the organic and the mechanical has become academic.

The settlement is quiet. There are few animals. No birds in the canopy—the crowns are too high and too sparse, and the resin or the steam discourages them. A few insects: small black beetles in the bark, pale moths that rest on the damp wood, a thin population of midges over the stream. Some small mammals in the undergrowth—a dark-furred vole-like creature, a shrew, a few others, all quiet, all nocturnal. No large animals. The people are present but not numerous, and they move softly. They tend the engines and the trees with equal care, and they speak rarely, and their voices are low. The place is not silent, but it is quiet.

The sounds: water, always. The stream, the drip of condensation from leaf to leaf, the seep of resin, the soft hiss of steam. The creak of wood—not the loud crack of a tree under stress, but the slow, small sounds of a living thing adjusting to its weight. The hum of engines, low and steady, felt more than heard. The occasional clang of metal on metal, distant, from somewhere in the settlement. The wind in the canopy, a dry rustle, because the leaves are small and tough and numerous, not broad and wet. Mostly quiet, though. The dominant sound is the sound of water and the sound of slow processes.

The light is normal daylight, but filtered. The canopy breaks it into columns and patches. The mist diffuses it. The overall effect is a soft, even light with occasional bright shafts where the sun finds a gap. In the morning, the mist is thick and the light is pearly. At midday, the light is clearer, and you can see the structure of the crowns. In the evening, the light goes amber and then grey, and the settlement's own lights come on—small, warm points of electric light in the trunks and along the rails, a constellation at ground level and in the lower branches.

The processes are active but not urgent. Water is pumped from the stream up into the trunks, through pipes, to tanks and boilers in the root-engines. Steam is generated, used, vented. Condensation is collected from the canopy and channeled into cisterns. Resin is tapped from the trunks and collected in small cups, then carried away on the carts. The trees are pruned, grafted, hollowed, reinforced—not heavily, but carefully, a continuous maintenance. The rails are inspected, the engines are oiled, the pipes are cleared. There is a daily cycle: the engines run faster in the cool of the morning, slower in the heat of the afternoon, and the steam vents on a schedule that the settlement's inhabitants could recite but rarely need to. There is a seasonal cycle: the resin flows heavily in spring and fall, thinly in summer and winter, and the settlement's work follows the flow. There is a longer cycle: the trees grow, slowly, and the buildings are adjusted, slowly, and the settlement creeps outward at the edges, one new trunk-hollow every few decades, one new bridge every few generations. The place is in motion, but the motion is slow and steady and self-sustaining.

The history is implied in the structure. This was once an industrial town, built in a grove. The founders used the trees as foundations and scaffolding, and then as building material, and then as partners. Over generations, the town grew into the trees and the trees grew into the town, and the distinction was lost. The original industry—resin-processing, perhaps, or the manufacture of some wood-derived chemical—is still running, but it has been absorbed into the life of the grove. The settlement is not a ruin and not a utopia. It is a place that has been working for a long time and has worn into its final form.

The rules are simple. The trees are the structure. The machinery is the metabolism. The water is the blood. The people are the tenders, or the symbionts, or the guests. The place is in balance. It is not growing and not declining. It is maintaining. It is a steady state. The boundary between living and built has dissolved, and the settlement is a single thing, a wood-and-iron organism, and it has been this way so long that no one remembers it being otherwise.

At midday, a shaft of light finds a gap in the canopy and falls on a bridge of wood and iron. Steam rises through the light from a vent in a trunk. Water drips from a leaf to a pipe to a cistern. An engine hums in a root. A cart moves along a rail, carrying blocks of resin to a hollow where the resin will be boiled down into syrup. The whole basin is quiet and working and old and complete. It has been this way for centuries. It will be this way for centuries more.
