# The Weeping Plateau

From the air — if anything flew here, which nothing does — it would look like a scar. A continental mass of rock thrust upward so recently, geologically speaking, that the stone hasn't yet learned to be old. The peaks are knife-edged, the ridgelines unsoftened, the cirques sharp as chiseled bowls. Glaciers cling to the highest spines not as ancient ice but as young ice, ice that formed in the uplift's shadow and hasn't finished deciding what shape it wants. Below the snowline, the rock is raw and fresh-fractured, and from every face, every crack, every tilted slab, water weeps.

Not streams. Not rivers. Weeping. The water emerges from the rock itself — from pores too fine to see, from the stress-relieved joints of recently exhumed granite, from the condensation that forms on cold stone in an atmosphere so saturated it cannot hold another molecule. It beads on vertical surfaces and runs in thin, continuous films. It collects in the angles of boulders and spills over in threads no wider than yarn. It pools in depressions that have no outlet and, because the air drinks constantly, never overflows. Every surface is wet. Every stone wears a skin of water that renews itself moment by moment, the old film draining away as the new one condenses, a perpetual motion of moisture that never arrives anywhere and never leaves.

The sound is enormous. It is the sound of water — not falling, not rushing, but *existing* in quantity. The hiss of condensation on cold rock, the tick of droplets striking pools, the low murmur of seep-lines threading down a thousand kilometers of cliff face. Layered over this is the industrial drone: the continuous, unvarying thrum of engines, the rhythmic exhalation of compressors, the low-frequency vibration of turbines spinning at constant speed. The sound does not fluctuate. It has no crescendos, no lulls. It is a single, sustained chord of water and machinery, loud enough to press against the eardrums, loud enough that conversation requires shouting, loud enough that silence becomes a memory rather than a presence. And beneath even this, occasionally, the deep *crack* of ice adjusting to its own weight — sharp, irregular, but somehow never surprising, because it has always happened at exactly this frequency and always will.

---

The vegetation is moss. Primarily moss. Vast, deep, saturated mats of it, species that have no names because no botanist has ever classified them — or perhaps species that were engineered and then abandoned to their own logic. It covers the plateau in a continuous carpet of green so deep it is nearly black, so thick it compresses underfoot into something like wet felt, two, three, sometimes five meters deep. In the hollows where water pools longest, it grows in hummocks and pillows, forming a terrain of soft, rounded domes that shift slightly when stepped on, water welling up between them. Higher on the rock, where the gradient is too steep for deep accumulation, it forms thin, dense crusts — bright emerald, almost fluorescent against the grey stone — that grip the surface with a tenacity that suggests the moss was designed to hold the mountain together.

There are lichens too, and a kind of low, creeping sedge that grows in dense tufts no taller than a fist, its roots interwoven with the moss into a single mat. In the sheltered lee of the industrial structures, where waste heat keeps the rock slightly warmer, the vegetation grows thicker, lusher, almost obscenely green, pressing against pipe-joints and foundation slabs with a slow, blind insistence. But it never spreads beyond the plateau. It never colonizes the peaks. Its boundaries are exact, as if drawn with a ruler — which they were, or are, or will be, because the machines maintain them.

There are no animals. No birds cross the sky. No insects rise from the moss. No fish move in the pools. The water is sterile. The air carries no pollen, no spores that drift on wind currents, no living thing in transit from one place to another. The absence is not a silence — the sound is too loud for silence — but a *stillness* within the sound, a sense that nothing is watching, nothing is moving with intention, nothing is hunting or fleeing or building. The moss grows. The water flows. The engines turn. And that is all.

---

The city — if it is a city — is not a dense thing. It is a *distribution*. A network of structures spread across the plateau in a pattern that follows the water: along seep-lines, around the largest pools, at the bases of cliffs where condensation is heaviest. The buildings are low, utilitarian, built from the same grey stone as the plateau itself, as if quarried from the bedrock and set back down in different configurations. They have no ornament. Their walls are thick, their windows narrow and deeply recessed, their roofs flat and collecting water in shallow sheets that drain through pipes into the general system.

Between the buildings run pipes. Not hidden, not buried — *exposed*, raised on stone pilings, sometimes a meter in diameter, sometimes a cluster of smaller conduits bundled together, crossing the landscape in straight lines that ignore the terrain, bridging gullies on simple stone arches, cutting through moss hummocks with the moss growing right up to the pipe-walls and even onto them, a green tide against metal. The pipes are always wet. Water condenses on their outer surfaces and runs down to the ground, feeding the moss at their bases, which grows thicker there, as if grateful.

The engines are in the lowest buildings, the ones built into natural depressions where water would otherwise pool to problematic depths. They are large — not the size of buildings but the size of *cathedrals* — and they run continuously. Their exhaust is steam, white and voluminous, rising from vents and stacks in plumes that do not waver, do not shift, do not change density or direction. The wind is constant here, blowing at a steady velocity from the northwest, and it carries the steam away in a long, unbroken ribbon that dissipates into the already-saturated air. The steam plumes are landmarks. They are visible from kilometers away, white columns against grey rock and green moss, and they never vary. A traveler — if there were travelers — could navigate by them, because they are always in the same place, always the same shape, always rising at the same rate.

There are generators. There are condensers — enormous radiator-like structures, finned and wet, pulling moisture from the air with a sound like continuous, gentle rainfall. There are settling tanks, filtration beds, pressure vessels, valve stations, transformer yards where electricity hums at a frequency that makes the teeth ache. The infrastructure is extensive. It covers the plateau in a web, a circuit, a nervous system of stone and metal and water and current. And it is all running. All of it. Every pump, every turbine, every compressor, every condenser, every valve, every transformer. Running at exactly the speed it was designed to run at, moving exactly the volume it was designed to move, producing exactly the output it was designed to produce.

---

This is the thing about the plateau that takes time to notice. At first, it seems alive. The water moves, the moss grows, the engines turn, the steam rises. Everything is in motion. Everything is active. But after an hour, or a day, or a week, you realize that nothing is *changing*. The water level in every pool is exactly the same. The moss has not grown visibly thicker. The steam plumes have not shifted. The sound has not varied. The engines are not accelerating or decelerating. The system is in perfect equilibrium — not a static equilibrium, not a frozen thing, but a *dynamic* equilibrium so precise that the net change at every point is zero. Water enters the pools at exactly the rate it leaves. The moss grows at exactly the rate it dies and is decomposed. The engines consume fuel at exactly the rate it is supplied. The steam rises at exactly the rate the condensers return it. The temperature is constant. The humidity is constant. The wind speed is constant. The light — flat, cold, normal, filtered through a high overcast that never breaks — is constant.

It is not that time has stopped. It is that the system has achieved a state in which time has no visible effect. The plateau is a machine for maintaining itself, and it is working perfectly. It has been working perfectly for — how long? The moss is deep, but the moss grows slowly in the cold. The stone is sharp, but the stone is young. The engines are not worn. The pipes are not corroded. Perhaps it has been running for decades. Perhaps for centuries. Perhaps the engines do not wear because they were built not to, because the equilibrium includes the maintenance of the engines themselves, because the system is closed and self-repairing and has been since the moment it was activated.

There are no people. This becomes clear slowly. There are no paths worn in the moss, no handholds on the rocks, no refuse, no markings, no signs of habitation beyond the structures themselves. The buildings are not entered. The engines are not tended. The valves are not adjusted. The system tends itself, or is tended by something that does not leave traces, or does not need to be tended at all. The city is a city without citizens. The industry is industry without workers. The infrastructure is infrastructure without purpose beyond its own continuation.

And yet it is not ruined. It is not abandoned in the way that implies desertion. It is *occupied* by its own operation. The plateau is inhabited by the process it performs — a process that may be water management, or climate control, or atmospheric processing, or something else entirely, something that has no name because no one is left to name it, something that consumes and produces and cycles and maintains without end and without change.

---

At night — if night comes; the light is so flat and constant that day and night blur into a single grey continuum — the condensers glow faintly with waste heat, and the steam plumes become visible as darker columns against the slightly darker sky. The moss is black. The water is black. The rock is black. The only light is the faint orange shimmer above the engine buildings and the occasional blue-white flicker of electrical discharge in the transformer yards, steady as a heartbeat, regular as a metronome.

The sound at night is the same as the sound during the day. The water does not quiet. The engines do not slow. The wind does not drop. The plateau does not sleep. It does not dream. It does not age. It is not alive, but it is not dead. It is not growing, but it is not decaying. It is not changing, but it is not still. It is a process frozen at the moment of its own perfect execution, a machine that has become a landscape, a landscape that has become a machine, and it will be exactly like this tomorrow, and the day after, and the year after, and the century after, because that is what it does: it continues, exactly as it is, forever, in the cold, in the wet, in the loud, in the green, in the grey, in the vast, in the empty, in the perfect equilibrium of its own endless operation.
