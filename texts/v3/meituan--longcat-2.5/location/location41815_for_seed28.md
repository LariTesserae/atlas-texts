# Kharn — the House of Bone

The Pan above has no water. It is a salt desert below the level of the surrounding plains, the lowest country for a hundred leagues, and it has not seen rain in living memory. No river has ever crossed it. The salt is the corpse of an old sea, ground white and blinding, and on still days the horizon shakes with heat. Travelers cross it at dawn and dusk and speak little, because the white throws the sun back into their eyes.

Beneath the Pan, in a hollow of stone that the salt seals away from the sky, lies the House.

The House is the skeleton of something vast—a god or a beast, the priests disagree, and both may be right. It fossilized before the Pan was dry, and its bones have gone to warm, slightly translucent stone, honey-colored at the edges where they thin. Its highest ribs arch out of the salt like the nave of a ruined cathedral, and from between them, on still nights, a green-gold light leaks upward. Caravanners camp far from those ribs. They call the light a ghost, a will-o'-the-wisp, a curse. They are half right.

The city of Kharn is built inside the ribcage. The city took the god's name, as a child takes a parent's.

---

You enter through the Sternum Gate, a sinkhole between two ribs, and descend the Spine Steps—a causeway along the fossilized spine, worn by ten thousand years of feet into a groove as smooth as a river stone. The air grows warm. Not hot, not yet, but warm as a body is warm. The bone under your hand is warm. This is the first thing newcomers notice, and they do not stop noticing it.

The second thing is the sound.

The House breathes. Gas seeps from deep in the earth through the beast's throat—a vent the size of a city gate—and the wind of the Pan finds its way in through cracks in the skull and between the ribs. The ribs are flutes. The chest is a drum. The result is the Lament: a low, enormous chord that never stops, that rises and falls with the surface wind and the deep gas, that rattles the teeth in your skull and hums in the bones of your forearm. In the Cavity—the great void of the chest—it is deafening. Not loud like a forge or a storm. Loud like standing inside a bell that is always, always being struck. You do not hear the Lament with your ears, the Kharni say. You hear it with your ribs.

Speech is impossible in the Cavity. In the outer districts, people speak in shouts against the hum. In the deep districts they do not speak at all. They use drum-speech—tapping patterns on the bone—and the bone-wire: a language of taps that travels through the skeleton itself, faster and farther than any voice. The whole House is a instrument and a telegraph. The Listeners sit at the Ear, a spur of fossilized bone near the skull, and hear messages, footfalls, distant quarries, tremors, and things they do not discuss. There are echoes that return from nowhere. There are taps that answer taps no one made. The Kharni say the bone remembers, and that the dead are tapped into the wire and still send the occasional message. Children play games with the echo-answers. The priests do not.

There is one place of silence: the Quiet, a sanctum in the skull lined with baffles of woven salt-reed and soft stone. Silence in Kharn is not an absence. It is a presence, thick and listening. Silence is for the dying, for the priests, and for criminals. The Kharni are deaf from birth or they are mad; there is no in-between, they say, and they are only half joking.

---

The Cavity is the heart of the city and it is mostly empty. A volume like an inverted cathedral, a hollow so vast that lantern-moss on its far walls is only a smear of green. Most of the House is uninhabited. The Kharni cling to the ribs like barnacles—dwellings carved into the rib faces, terraces and balconies and rope bridges spanning the Open, the emptiness between. The bridges are the city's streets. Below them: nothing, and the Lament, and drifting clouds of moths. The roar-mills stand on the widest ribs, their sails turned by the wind that howls between the bones, grinding cap-fungus into flour. The Ceiling district hangs inverted from the upper ribs—moth-keepers and echo-listeners, people who want to be closest to the god's voice. Their gardens hang in baskets. Their children learn to climb before they learn to walk.

Rib Row is where most Kharni live: a vertical slum carved into the inner curve of the ribs, layer upon layer, so that a single rib holds a hundred families and the smoke of a hundred lamps. The Pelvis is the bowl of the body, and it is the green heart of the city—terraced gardens, the Breath Market, the great fungus farms, the goat pens. The Throat is where the gas vents, and there the breath-forges burn blue and copper is worked with hand tools. The Skull is the temple and the observatory, and in its eye-socket—the Eye—a shaft of true daylight falls at noon, and the dust in it shines like a solid thing. The Heart lies beyond the Skull, in the deep bone, and no one goes there. The Heart-wardens see to that.

The Deep Hollows are the limb-bones, tunnels running down into the dark, where the oldest bone is quarried and where, the Kharni say, the bone-worm lives—a thing pale and long and patient that eats the god from within. Quarriers leave it offerings. The god does not mind, they say. The god is tolerant.

---

Nothing in Kharn is built; it is carved. The bone-stone is warm and slightly greasy to the touch, and it takes the chisel beautifully. Salt-brick is cast in the Pelvis from Pan salt and binders of fungus-paste. Rope is sinew and vine. Metal is copper from the surface mines, bronze for tools, and meteoric iron for the few blades that matter. There are no wheels in Kharn. There are no roads—only steps, ladders, pulleys, and the long climb of the Spine Steps. Everything heavy moves on rope and muscle. Everything is worn smooth. The steps are grooves. The doorframes are polished by shoulders. The bones themselves have weathered into soft curves, and the city has weathered with them, so that after ten thousand years it is impossible to say where the god ends and the city begins. The Kharni say the House has settled into itself, that it is in balance, that it tolerates them.

The moss is the light. Lantern-moss—grave-light, god-glow—grows in thick carpets on every rib, cultivated over millennia into strains that glow green, gold, pale blue. It is the city's sun. It is harvested under strict rules; a family holds a light-debt and a light-credit, and to take moss without permission is to steal from the god's own skin. The moss has a strange property: it brightens when the Lament peaks. On nights when the wind rises and the ribs sing, the whole Cavity flares green, and the Kharni say the moss is singing back. The gas-flames at the Throat burn blue and steady. Tallow lamps burn in the deep districts. Between the moss and the flame, the Cavity is never dark. The only darkness in the House is the Deep Hollows and the Quiet, and both are holy.

The vegetation is lush beyond reason for a place with no water. The Green Curtain hangs from the ribs in waterfall cascades of moss and fern. Rib-vines climb in spirals. Rib-cap fungi spread in shelves the size of doorways, and their spores hang in the warm air like dust, like snow that is alive. The plants drink vapor—the warm breath of the House, the moisture that rises from the Throat and condenses in films on the cool bone. No plant in Kharn has ever drunk a drop of free water. The old songs speak of the Wet, of rain and rivers and the god's blood, which once filled the House to the brim. The priests say the Wet is a lie, or a memory, or a promise. No one has seen standing water. The Kharni bathe in dust and moss-rub, and their skin smells of warm stone and smoke and fungus and incense.

The moths are the cattle of the air. Moss-moths, big as a fist, are kept in jars and on leashes and in the hanging gardens, and they are the reason the moss grows so thick—the Kharni tend the moths, the moths tend the moss, the moss tends the people. Grubs are the meat: marrow-grubs, fat and white, harvested from the old marrow cavities by the marrow-men, who drill carefully and sing apologies. Moss-goats give milk. Rib-runners—blind, fast lizards—are hunted on the ribs, and their skins make the drumheads. Grief-birds nest in the Skull and cry like children, and to kill one is a hanging crime. There are few other animals. The Kharni say the House is the only animal that matters, and it is enough.

---

Time in Kharn is measured in Breaths—the pressure cycles of the House, each one a few hours long, rising and falling like a slow tide. The Breath Market opens on the low breath and closes on the high. The Lung-Moods are the god's weather: days when the Throat runs hot and the forges roar, days when the gas runs thin and the lamps gutter and the city goes quiet and careful. Once a year comes the Turning, when the Heart—which has not beaten in any memory—beats once, and the whole House shakes, and every Kharni lies down on the bone and listens, and the priests weep. Twice in recorded history the Heart has beaten twice in a year. Both times, they say, the city changed.

The House dreams. On Still-Days, when a tremor runs through the skeleton and the Lament shifts its key, the Kharni stop their work and lie still and listen. The god is dreaming, they say, and it is bad luck to make noise in a dream. The mirrors in the Skull are polished obsidian, and they show things a moment behind—the Eye-priests use them for divination, and they say the lag is the god's attention, trailing.

There are rules, because the House is alive and a living thing has rules. Do not cut the quick-bone—the warm seams near the Heart. Do not carry flame into the Deep Hollows. Do not tap the wire in the god's own rhythm; that is how the dead answer, and you may not like what answers. Do not speak in the Quiet. Do not cross the Open alone. Do not harvest moss above your debt. Do not ask about the Wet. Outsiders are brought down the Spine Steps blindfolded and introduced to the Lament slowly, or they go mad. The dead are tapped into the bone, and their names are sent along the wire, and sometimes, for years after, a tap comes back that matches their name. The Kharni do not call this ghosts. They call it the bone remembering.

---

On still nights, out on the Pan, you can see the ribs arching out of the salt like the ribs of a drowned ship, and the green light leaking between them, and the Lament is audible for miles—a low hum under the silence of the desert, a sound like the earth itself is breathing. The caravans give it a wide berth. They tell stories: a ghost, a curse, a city inside a monster, a god that is dead and will never die.

They are half right. The god is dead. The god is home.
