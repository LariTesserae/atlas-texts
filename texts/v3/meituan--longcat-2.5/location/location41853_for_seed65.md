The wind arrives before you do.

It comes from the west, as it has always come from the west, and it does not stop. It does not gust or lull or shift. It presses against you at a steady, tremendous force—a wall of moving air that makes the first hours on the Height a matter of physical negotiation. You lean into it. You breathe in shallow gasps. The skin of your face is pulled taut and cold. After a while, your body learns to angle itself, to present a narrower profile, to move in the wind's language rather than against it.

The sound is beyond loud. It is a continuous roar, a single unbroken note that contains all frequencies, that vibrates in your chest and your skull and your teeth. You cannot hear your own footsteps. You cannot hear your own thoughts—or rather, you can, but only as a thin, distant thread beneath the overwhelming presence of the wind's voice. After days, you stop trying to hear anything else. The sound becomes a kind of silence. A baseline. The condition of being here.

---

The light is the opposite of the sound. Where the sound is total, the light is precise—sharp, almost surgical. The sun hangs in a sky that darkens toward the zenith, from pale blue at the horizon to a deep violet overhead, and at the highest point, nearly black. The sun itself is a white disk, unfiltered by the thin atmosphere, and it casts shadows that are absolute. No diffusion, no softening—just bright surfaces and dark surfaces, with a line between them as sharp as a blade. The shadows are blue, not black, because the sky itself is a source of light, and they are cold. Standing in shadow on the Height is like standing in a different place entirely.

The ground is gravel and stone, packed so hard by the constant press of wind that it rings faintly underfoot—a dull, ceramic note. In places, the wind has sorted the gravel by size, carrying away the finer particles and leaving behind a lag deposit of larger stones that fit together like a mosaic, each one polished on its western face. In other places, the wind has scoured down to bedrock, and the rock is dark and shiny, grooved and fluted by grit carried at speeds that strip flesh from bone in minutes. The grooves all run west to east, parallel, like the lines of a vast musical score.

There is no soil. Or rather, there is soil, but it is thin and young and confined to the lee of rocks, to depressions, to the few places where the wind's force is slightly reduced. It is enough for lichens, for mosses, for the small, dense, ancient plants that grow here—cushion plants that hug the ground, that present no surface to the wind, that grow so slowly that a plant the size of a fist might be a century old. Their leaves are thick and waxy, gray-green or rust-red, and they are covered in a fine down that traps moisture from the air. They are the primary water-capture system of the Height, extracting dew from the wind, holding it in their tissues through the dry hours, releasing it slowly through the day.

The vegetation is sparse. You can walk for hours and see nothing but gravel and rock and the occasional dark patch of lichen. Then you come to a low ridge, and in its lee, there is a dense mat of moss, and small white flowers, and a pool of water that condensed overnight and has not yet evaporated. The flowers are tiny, almost invisible, but they are bright, and they are open to the sun, and they are gone by afternoon—withered, curled, already returning to the moss from which they came.

---

Water is everywhere and nowhere. It is in the air—the wind carries moisture from somewhere to the west, from a sea or a lake that exists beyond the horizon, beyond the ring of peaks that encloses the Height—and this moisture condenses on every surface overnight, so that the rocks are wet in the morning and dry by midday. It is in the low places—small pools that form in wind-scoured depressions, that are replenished by condensation and by the occasional meltwater stream from snowfields that persist in the highest, coldest hollows. It is not in the sky. It does not rain here, or rarely, or only in a fine mist that the wind tears apart before it reaches the ground.

The streams are brief, urgent things. They emerge from gravel seeps, run for a few hundred meters over stone, and disappear—sinking into the gravel, evaporating, or simply running out of gradient and spreading into thin, temporary sheets that freeze overnight and vanish by noon. They are cold, barely above freezing, and they carry a fine load of silt that gives them a milky, opalescent quality. Around them, the lichens grow thicker, darker, and there are rushes—thin, wiry, pressed flat by the wind—that mark the water's path.

---

The fauna is abundant. You do not see it at first—the wind hides it, the light hides it, the vastness of the landscape hides it. But if you stay, if you learn to look, you begin to see the signs. The burrows. The droppings. The tracks in the gravel. The dark shapes that move at the edge of vision, low and fast, pressed against the ground.

The grazers are the most visible. They are broad, heavy-bodied animals, built low and wide, with thick coats of coarse hair that the wind cannot lift. Their legs are short and powerful, their feet broad and splayed, and they move with a strange, sliding gait—leaning into the wind, letting it push them forward, so that they seem to flow across the gravel like water. They are not fast, exactly, but they are relentless. They travel westward, always westward, because the wind pushes them and they have stopped fighting it. They eat the lichens, the mosses, the cushion plants, scraping them from the rock with broad, flat teeth, and they are numerous—herds that stretch across the plain, a dark river of bodies flowing toward a horizon they will never reach.

They have no predators, or few. The wind is predator enough. The cold is predator enough. The thin air, the bright light, the constant, unrelenting exposure. They die of exposure, of starvation, of the simple accumulated wear of living in a place that does not want them. Their bones are scattered across the Height, white and clean, polished by the wind, and they are used by the burrowers as shelter, as landmarks, as architecture.

The birds are the most striking animals, because they fly, because they ride the wind as naturally as the grazers walk the ground. They are large, with long, narrow wings, and they barely flap—they hold their wings rigid and let the wind carry them, and they turn and wheel and dive with a precision that suggests they are not fighting the wind but using it, that they understand it in a way that nothing else on the Height does. They feed on insects—small, hard-shelled insects that are blown across the plateau in clouds, that are so numerous that they form a kind of haze in the air, that coat every surface with a thin layer of chitinous bodies. The birds fly through these clouds with their beaks open, straining the insects from the air, and they never land. Or rarely. They roost on cliff faces, on the lee side of rock formations, on the few vertical surfaces that the wind cannot reach.

The burrowers are the most numerous. They are beneath the surface, in a network of tunnels that extends across the entire Height—a hidden city, a submerged world. You cannot see them, but you can hear them—a faint, continuous rustling, a sound like dry leaves, that comes from below the ground. They are small, rodent-like, with broad heads and powerful forelimbs, and they eat the roots of the cushion plants, and the lichens, and each other, and they are so numerous that their tunnels have weakened the ground in places, so that it collapses underfoot, so that you must be careful where you walk. The people have learned to read the ground, to recognize the places where the burrowers are close to the surface, where the gravel is loose and gives way, where the earth is hollow.

---

The rock formations are the oldest things on the Height. They are the products of long erosion—wind and grit working on stone for millennia, for longer than memory, for longer than the people have been here. They have been carved into shapes that seem impossible, that seem intentional—arches, spires, flutes, hollows, chambers. Some of them are small, no larger than a person. Others are vast, the size of hills, their surfaces grooved and ridged like the skin of some enormous creature.

They are resonant. The wind passes through them, over them, around them, and they sing—a low, continuous drone, punctuated by higher notes, by whistles, by moans. The sound is not musical, exactly, but it is structured, patterned, and it changes with the wind's direction and speed, which means it changes constantly, which means it is never the same twice, even though the wind itself never changes. The people have learned to read these sounds, to interpret them, to find meaning in the variations. They say the rocks are speaking. They say the rocks are remembering. They say the rocks are the Height's memory, its record of all the winds that have ever blown.

In the chambers of the largest formations, the wind is quieter. Not silent—never silent—but reduced, muffled, a distant roar rather than an overwhelming presence. The people seek these places out. They press their ears to the stone and listen. They say they can hear the wind's history, its deep past, its intentions. They say the rocks are older than the wind, that they were here before the wind began, that they will be here after it stops. They say the wind is young, and the rocks are old, and the difference between them is the difference between a breath and a bone.

---

The people are few. They are nomadic, following the herds, following the birds, following the water. They have no structures—nothing built, nothing permanent, nothing that would catch the wind or hold the cold. They carry everything they own on their backs, or on the backs of animals that are not quite domesticated, that are more like companions, that come and go as they please. Their tools are simple—stone blades, bone needles, woven fabrics made from the hair of the grazers, ropes made from sinew and plant fiber. They do not build fires, or rarely—there is no fuel, nothing to burn—and they eat their food raw, or dried, or fermented in skins that they carry with them.

They are small, and lean, and their faces are wrapped in layers of woven fabric against the wind. Only their eyes are visible, and their eyes are narrow, squinting against the light, adapted to the constant glare. They do not speak, or rarely—the wind makes speech impossible—and they communicate with their hands, with gestures that are precise and economical, that can be read at a distance, that are visible even in the flat light of the Height. They have a language of touch, too, for the times when they are close—a press of the hand, a tap on the shoulder, a complex grammar of contact that conveys what the wind would steal.

They have been here for a long time. How long, they do not know—they have no history, no stories of arrival, no myths of origin. They have always been here, as far as they are concerned, and they will always be here, and the Height is the world, and the world is the Height. They do not travel beyond the plateau—there is nowhere to go, no reason to go, no way to survive the descent through the passes that ring the Height, where the wind is even stronger, where the cold is even deeper, where the air is too thin to breathe.

They do not conceive of change. Not because they are incapable of it, but because there is nothing to change into. The Height is complete. It has reached its final form. The wind blows, the animals move, the people follow, and this is not a cycle, not a circle, not a return—it is a line, straight and level, extending infinitely in both directions, and the people are on it, and they cannot step off, and they do not want to.

They have a word for the Height. It is not a name, exactly—it is more like a description, a phrase that means something like "the place where the wind lives" or "the place where the light is sharp" or "the place where the animals walk." The phrase is long, and it is spoken rarely, and it is always spoken with the hands as well as the voice, and it is never spoken in the wind's full force, but only in the shelter of the rocks, in the chambers where the sound is muffled, where the wind's voice is reduced to a distant murmur.

They have a word for themselves. It means something like "the people who follow" or "the people who listen" or "the people who are carried." They do not have a word for "home." They do not have a word for "future." They have a word for "now," and it is the same word they use for "always," and the same word they use for "here."

---

The Height does not change. This is the most important thing about it. The wind blows, and has always blown, and will always blow. The animals move in their patterns, and have always moved in their patterns, and will always move in their patterns. The people follow, and have always followed, and will always follow. There is no variation, no season, no cycle—or rather, the cycle is so large, so slow, that it is invisible to anything that lives here. The Height is in equilibrium. It has reached its final form. It is finished.

The lichens grow, but so slowly that their growth is indistinguishable from stillness. The rocks erode, but so slowly that their erosion is indistinguishable from permanence. The people are born, and live, and die, but their lives are so similar, so patterned, so determined by the wind and the light and the animals, that their births and deaths are indistinguishable from the landscape's own rhythms. They are part of the equilibrium. They are part of the stillness. They are part of the wind.

This is not a place that is dying, or that is being born. It is a place that is complete. It is a place that has become exactly what it is, and it will remain exactly what it is, until the wind stops. And the wind will never stop. The wind is the Height, and the Height is the wind, and everything else—the animals, the people, the lichens, the rocks—is just a variation on the wind's theme, a temporary shape that the wind has taken, a form that will persist as long as the wind persists, and no longer.

---

At night, the stars are close. The atmosphere is so thin, so clear, that the sky is not a dome but a depth—an infinite, depthless black, crowded with points of light that do not twinkle, that burn steady and cold. The Milky Way is a bright band, a river of light, and it casts shadows—faint, blue-gray shadows, visible on the gravel, on the rocks, on the faces of the sleeping animals. The people do not sleep deeply. They do not sleep at all, some nights—they sit in the shelter of the rocks, wrapped in their woven fabrics, and they watch the sky, and they listen to the wind, and they wait for nothing, because there is nothing to wait for.

In the morning, the sun rises, and the light is immediate, total, blinding. The shadows snap into place. The rocks begin to sing. The animals rise, and shake themselves, and begin to move westward. The people rise, and fold their fabrics, and shoulder their burdens, and follow. The wind blows. The Height is exactly as it was yesterday, exactly as it will be tomorrow, exactly as it has always been.

The wind blows.
