The basin sits low in the land, a saucer worn down over time beyond reckoning. You come to it by a gentle descent, the ground sloping away around you until you're inside it, and the rim is a low line behind you—soft, rounded, the last suggestion of a wall. From the rim you can see the whole floor: a few kilometers across, maybe less, a mosaic of shallow pools and damp flats and low hummocks where vegetation has taken hold. The far rim is visible from almost everywhere, a low hazy line against the sky, and the feeling is of a contained place, a room open to the sky, a grove-or-grove scale, a single feature with boundaries.

The basin has been here long enough to have worn itself smooth. The walls are not cliffs but slopes, rounded and gentle, and they weep water—thin threads and seeps that stain the earth dark and feed the pools below. The walls are made of a soft, fine-grained stone, or something like stone, and the water has worked it into smooth curves and shallow grooves, and it has stained it in bands of color: rust, ochre, dark green, black. The floor is nearly flat, the product of eons of erosion, of water and time and the slow patience of both. It has reached its final form, or near enough. The forces that shaped it have finished their work, and what's left is this: a shallow saucer of water and life, settled into its oldest stable state.

The water is everywhere. It sits in the pools, it saturates the flats, it rises to meet any shovel. The pools are not separate but connected, a mosaic of water and damp earth, and the water level is constant—not rising, not falling, held in place by something. The water is clear in some places, cloudy in others, and in the cloudy places it's full of life: tiny translucent creatures that turn the water into something that seems to breathe, that seems to be more life than water. The water is warm, or warmish, and it steams faintly in the bright light, and it smells of minerals and life and something else—something faint and sweet, like a flower you can't name. The air is so full of moisture that it condenses on everything: on leaves, on stone, on your skin, on the strange small artifacts that stand here and there across the floor. The warmth is constant, a greenhouse warmth, and the whole place feels like something breathing.

The light is bright and diffuse, scattered by the wet air into an even glow that makes everything visible and nothing harsh. Shadows are soft, colors are vivid—the green of the leaves, the blue or silver of the pools, the pale of the creatures in the water, the strange colors of the artifacts. The brightness is almost too much; the pools glitter, the leaves shine, the air itself seems to glow. You squint even under cover, even in the soft shadow of a hummock. The light comes from above, from a sky that's always somewhat hazy, always somewhat bright, and it fills the basin evenly, without direction, without source, like light from everywhere at once. It makes the colors more vivid than they should be, and it makes the place feel like something seen through water, or through glass, or through something else.

The vegetation is lush where it grows. The hummocks are crowned with broad-leaved things—strange trees or massive ferns or something in between—with leaves that drip and shine and turn slowly to follow the light. The leaves are broad and waxy, or covered in fine hairs that hold water, and they are a green so deep it's almost black in the shadows, so bright it's almost yellow in the light. Some of the leaves are fringed, or serrated, or covered in patterns: veins that branch and rebranch, or spots, or stripes. The vegetation grows in clumps, scattered across the basin floor with much open space between—a grove here, a single tree there, a patch of dense growth around a pool, and then a stretch of open water or bare damp earth. The overall effect is of lushness balanced with emptiness, of green islands in a sea of water and light. The roots of the vegetation are in the shallow water, or in the saturated earth, and they spread across the surface, and they are part of the mosaic, part of the pattern of water and life. The vegetation has a smell—green, sweet, faintly spicy—and it's stronger after the rain, or when the wind blows, or when you brush against the leaves.

The fauna is overwhelming. The pools are alive with tiny creatures—translucent, pale, countless—turning the water into something that seems to breathe, that seems to be more life than water. They are small, most of them, barely visible, and they move in swarms that shift and swirl and are gone. Some of them are segmented, some are winged, some are many-legged, and some are shapes that don't have names. The air is full of small flying things, soft-winged, silent or nearly so, that drift and swirl and are gone. The ground is a carpet of small life: insects, larvae, tiny crustaceans, things that scuttle and burrow and are gone before you can focus on them. The biomass is immense, the numbers beyond counting. You can stand in one place and watch the water teem, the air teem, the ground teem, and feel the weight of life pressing in from all sides.

But the place is mostly quiet. The creatures don't call, or their calls are too faint, or they move through water and air without sound. What you hear is the faint plip of water, the soft drip from leaves, the occasional rustle of something moving through a pool, the whisper of the wind across the flats. The silence is not absence but presence—the life is there, teeming, but it is quiet life, and the quiet is a quality of the place, a rule of the world here. Sound doesn't carry, or it's absorbed, or the creatures have learned not to make it. You can speak in a normal voice and it sounds loud, intrusive, wrong. You find yourself whispering, then not speaking at all.

Scattered across the basin floor are a few small structures and artifacts. They are clearly artificial, clearly old, clearly not made by any current hand. Some are partially submerged in the pools, some are overgrown with vegetation, some stand on the damp flats like small monuments. Their forms are simple, geometric—a sphere, a cylinder, a lattice, a shape that doesn't have a name—but the materials are wrong. Not metal, not stone, not ceramic, not anything with a clear name. They seem to be made of something that is not quite matter, or that is matter in a state you don't recognize. They are smooth, or faceted, or textured in ways that don't match their forms, and they are warm or cold to the touch, or they hum at a frequency you can feel but not hear. They are worn, or they're made of something that doesn't wear, or they're wearing in a way that isn't erosion—slowly, strangely, becoming something else.

They seem to be functioning. Maybe the water level near them is slightly different, or the light, or the density of life. Maybe the creatures avoid them, or are drawn to them, or move around them in patterns. Maybe the air near them is warmer, or brighter, or thicker. Maybe they emit something—a faint hum, a faint glow, a faint smell, a faint pressure. Their purpose is unclear. They might be maintaining the basin: keeping the water level constant, keeping the climate stable, keeping the ecosystem in balance. They might be leftover from something that built the basin, or shaped it, or uses it. They might be old beyond reckoning, and still running, and not going to stop. They are few, scattered, balanced with the emptiness. You might walk for an hour and see only one, or pass several in a small area and then none for hours. They are part of the place, as much as the pools or the hummocks, and they are strange, and they are old, and they are not understood.

The basin is active. The ecosystem is in motion—the creatures feed and are fed upon, the water rises and falls in small increments, the vegetation grows and dies and grows, the weather cycles through its patterns. You can see it if you watch: the swarms shift, the pools breathe, the leaves turn, the artifacts hum. The processes are active, but they are quiet and slow and steady, and they don't disturb the overall feeling of deep time. The place is not static, but it is settled, and the activity is the activity of something that has been running for a long time and will continue to run.

The rules of the basin: the water is always near the surface. The life is mostly silent. The light is diffuse and bright. The artifacts are old and functioning and not understood. The basin is bounded but not closed—you can leave, but you might find it hard to find again, or you might find that time has passed differently, or you might find that you don't want to leave. The place is self-contained, complete, balanced, ancient. The erosion is complete; the land is at its final form. The warmth and wetness are constant; the climate is stable, held in place. The life is overwhelming but not aggressive; it's just there, teeming, quiet. The place feels like it's been waiting, or like it's maintaining itself, or like it's a kind of garden or preserve, or like it's the inside of something, or like it's the bottom of something that used to be larger.

You stay for a day, or a week, or longer. Time moves strangely in the basin. The light doesn't change much—it's always bright, always diffuse, always the same—and the warmth and wetness are constant, and the life teems on, and it's hard to tell if hours have passed or days. You sleep when you're tired, eat what you can find, drink the warm mineral water from the pools. You watch the swarms shift, the pools breathe, the leaves turn, the artifacts hum. You whisper, then stop whispering, then just listen to the water and the wind and the faint rustle of small things.

You feel the weight of the place, the age of it, the settledness, the quiet. You feel like you're inside something, or at the bottom of something, or in a place that has been worn down to its essential form and is now just being, just continuing, just teeming quietly in the bright warm wet light. You feel like the basin is aware of you, or at least responsive to you—the life shifts around you, the water ripples where you step, the artifacts hum louder or softer as you approach. You feel like you're part of the place now, another small thing in the water, another small thing in the air, another small thing on the ground.

You can leave. The rim is low, the descent is gentle, the way out is there. But you might not. You might stay, and watch, and listen, and become part of the quiet life of the basin. You might stay until you forget there was ever anywhere else, until the basin is the whole world, until you're just another small thing teeming silently in the bright warm wet light.

The basin is a saucer in the earth, worn down to its final form, filled with water and life and light, bounded but not closed, ancient but not dead, strange but not hostile. It's a place that has been waiting, or maintaining itself, or being something that doesn't have a name. It's a place that is enough, that is complete, that is itself. And you can leave, but you might not, and that's the rule of the basin, the rule of the world here: you can leave, but you might not.
