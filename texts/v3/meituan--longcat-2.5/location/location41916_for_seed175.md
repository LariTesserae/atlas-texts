The path up is the last rough thing. It ends at a rim of dark stone, weathered to a soft profile, and beyond it the land opens into a bowl.

Not a vast bowl—you can see all of it from where you stand. Two hundred meters across, perhaps less. The rim is uneven, lower on the eastern side where a channel has been worn through, higher on the west where the wall rises another ten or fifteen meters above the floor. The bowl is not deep. It holds you gently.

The stone is dark. Volcanic, perhaps, or something older—a dense, fine-grained rock that weathers to a smooth, almost waxy surface. It is wet. Not flooded, not pooled deeply, but everywhere damp, everywhere shedding a thin film of moisture. The walls weep. Water emerges from the rock face in dozens of places, not as springs exactly, but as a general seepage, a slow perspiration that moves down the stone in dark tracks. The tracks are old. They have stained the rock in vertical streaks of ochre and rust and a deep, almost black green.

The air is warm. Warmer than the elevation should allow. It sits in the bowl like something poured. There is no wind. The moisture in the air is not fog, exactly, but a kind of thickening—the distance to the far wall is slightly softened, slightly dimmed. Light comes from above, normal light, but it arrives filtered, as if through a thin membrane of cloud or vapor that never quite becomes either.

---

The floor of the bowl is not flat. It rises and falls in gentle undulations, shallow depressions that hold water—not pools, but sheets, perhaps a centimeter deep, perfectly still. The water is clear but carries a faint mineral tint, something between pale green and pale amber. Where it is shallowest, the stone beneath is visible: dark, worn smooth, marked with fine grooves and channels.

And here is the first strange thing. The channels are too regular.

They run from the base of the walls, where the seepage is strongest, down toward the center of the bowl. They are narrow—a few centimeters wide, a few centimeters deep—and they branch and rejoin in a way that suggests design. Not the chaotic branching of natural drainage, but something closer to a network, a system. The water moves through them, slowly, feeding the shallow sheets. But the channels themselves look carved. Or worn by centuries of use. Or grown.

They are not carved. The stone is too hard, the walls too smooth, the edges too rounded for tool marks. They look as though they were always there, or as though the water itself, over a very long time, persuaded the stone to take this shape. The distinction between erosion and intention has become irrelevant.

---

Scattered through the bowl—sparsely, with much open stone between them—are patches of vegetation. Mosses first, in thick, saturated mats of deep green, growing in the channels and along the base of the walls where the seepage is constant. The moss is lush, almost obscenely so, rising in soft, wet cushions that yield underfoot. Ferns emerge from cracks in the stone—small, delicate things, their fronds heavy with moisture, curling slightly at the tips. In the most sheltered hollows, where the walls curve inward, there are low shrubs with small, waxy leaves, and one or two stunted trees—not pines, something broader-leaved, something that suggests a warmer climate than this elevation should support. Their bark is dark and wet, furred with moss at the base.

The vegetation is intense where it grows. It is also sparse. Between the green patches, the stone is bare—wet, dark, marked with its channels and grooves, slightly warm to the touch. You can walk across it. You can sit on it. It is not hostile. It is simply open.

---

There are no animals.

This is not immediately apparent. The lushness, the water, the warmth—all of it suggests life, and you find yourself listening for birdsong, for insects, for the small rustle of something in the undergrowth. But there is nothing. No birds. No insects. No lizards on the warm stone. No frogs in the shallow pools. No fish. No worms in the wet soil that collects in cracks. The silence is not the silence of a place where animals are hiding. It is the silence of a place where they are absent.

The quiet is not empty. It is full of small, wet sounds: the drip of condensation from a fern frond, the almost imperceptible trickle of water in a channel, the soft, continuous seep of moisture from the rock face. These sounds are so quiet that they seem to come from inside your own hearing, not from the landscape. The absence of animal sound makes them louder by contrast, but they remain at the threshold of perception. You are always almost not hearing them.

---

There are stones that look like tools.

This is the second strange thing. A stone near the eastern wall, half-buried in moss, that has the size and shape of a grinding stone—a shallow depression worn into its upper surface, smooth as clay. Another, near the center of the bowl, that sits in a depression of exactly its shape, rounded on one end, flattened on the other, the size of a hand. A third, near the northern wall, that is split along a clean line, as if struck, and the two halves sit where they fell, worn by water but still sharp enough to cut.

They are not tools. They are stones. The stone of the bowl is prone to fracturing in this way, or to wearing into these shapes, or to collecting in these hollows. There is no reason to think they were placed. But you pick one up and it fits your hand. You set it down and it sits in its depression. You do not know what this means.

The channels, the basins, the worn stones—all of it has the feeling of a place that was once used. Not built. Not developed. But used. Worked. Someone sat here, perhaps, and ground something in these stones, and poured water through these channels, and the stone remembers. Or the stone did this itself, slowly, over a time that makes human use irrelevant. Or both.

---

The rim on the eastern side is lower, and the channel there is wider—a groove in the stone, perhaps a meter across, that leads out of the bowl and down the slope beyond. The water that exits here is slightly more than the seepage that enters. The balance is not perfect. The bowl is slowly draining, or slowly filling, or has been doing one or the other for longer than anyone can say. The dynamic is slow. The change, if it is happening, happens across centuries.

The temperature is the third strange thing, or perhaps the first. The stone is warm. Not hot—not geothermal, not volcanic. But warm, like skin, like something alive. The water is warm. The air is warm. The warmth is not aggressive; it does not oppress. It simply is. It makes the bowl feel held, enclosed, sheltered from something that exists beyond the rim. The world outside is cooler. The world outside is where wind lives, and animals, and the ordinary processes of weather. The bowl is different.

You do not know why there are no animals. Perhaps the warmth is wrong for them, or the water carries something they cannot tolerate, or the silence itself—the deep, continuous, unbroken silence—is a kind of wall. Perhaps they have never found the place. Perhaps they found it and left.

The vegetation does not seem to suffer from the absence. The mosses are thick. The ferns are healthy. The shrubs fruit, occasionally—small, dark berries that fall into the shallow water and are slowly worn away. There are no seeds scattered by birds, no pollen carried by insects. The plants reproduce slowly, vegetatively, by spreading and dividing, or not at all. The ones that are here have been here for a long time.

---

The light changes slowly. The thin membrane of cloud or vapor above the bowl thickens and thins, and the quality of the light shifts—brighter, then softer, then slightly dimmer. These changes take hours. They are not weather. They are something else, something about the way the air sits in the bowl, the way the moisture condenses and disperses.

You can stay here for a long time. The warmth, the quiet, the wet stone, the green—it is not uncomfortable. It is not restful, exactly. It is something else. It is the feeling of being in a place that is not waiting for you, but is not indifferent to you either. A place that is simply continuing, slowly, in its own state, and has been for longer than you can imagine, and will be after you leave.

The stone is warm. The water moves, barely. The moss grows, imperceptibly. The silence holds.
