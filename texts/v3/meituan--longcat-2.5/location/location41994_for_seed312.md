The plateau is still rising. Not quickly — the uplift is measured in hand-spans per century — but the rock remembers every pulse. The eastern escarpment is a fresh wound in the earth, a fault scarp of grey and rust-colored stone that shears upward for three hundred meters and then, instead of cresting, keeps going, rolling into a high plain that stretches to every horizon. From the top of the scarp you can watch weather systems form in the distance: clouds building over the interior, storms that will take days to arrive, wind that carries the smell of rain long before the rain itself reaches the stone.

The rock is young. That is the first thing you understand. The cliff face is clean-fractured, its edges still sharp enough to cut hide. Boulders the size of houses rest at its base, newly shed, their surfaces unweathered and pale. The strata are exposed in horizontal bands like pages, and in the lower layers you can find shells — coiled ammonites, ribbed bivalves, the imprints of creatures that lived in warm shallow seas. The plateau was seabed once. It rose in pulses over millions of years, and the most recent pulse is still settling. On cold mornings the cliff face pops and cracks as frost melts in its fractures, and stones fall without warning. The people build their lowest structures behind thick, unglamorous walls that are not dwellings but deflectors — barriers against rockfall that cost more labor than any house and are rebuilt every generation.

Water is everywhere and nowhere. The plateau is too cold for rain most of the year. Precipitation comes as snow in the brief winter, and the rest of the year water arrives as melt, as seep, as condensation. The city sits at the foot of the escarpment where a dozen snow patches persist through the summer, feeding a network of stone channels that carry meltwater down through the settlement in narrow, precise aqueducts. The channels are the city's true architecture, older than the buildings, maintained by a guild of water-keepers whose knowledge of flow rates and seasonal variation is passed down through generations. Every house has a cistern. Every terrace has a drainage channel. The sound of water is constant but quiet — a thin, precise murmur that never rises above a whisper. In the lowest quarters, where the rock seeps, walls are damp to the touch year-round, and a pale mineral bloom grows on the stone, which the people scrape off and use to treat hides.

The city climbs the lower slopes of the escarpment in tiers. It is built of stone — not cut stone, for there is little metal here, but shaped stone, gathered stone, stone that has been fitted and refitted until the walls stand without mortar. The buildings are low, thick-walled, their roofs flat and layered with bundled grass. They are packed close, sharing walls, climbing over one another in a dense fabric that from a distance looks like the rock itself has been coaxed into geometry. Streets are narrow, stepped, sometimes tunneled through the lower floors of buildings. There are no grand avenues. The city's scale is measured not in width but in depth — in the number of rooms, the layers of occupation, the terraces stacked one above another up the slope. From the escarpment above, the city is a pattern of rectangles, a tessellation of stone and shadow, and the smoke of dung-fires rises from a thousand small hearths in thin, vertical threads that barely bend in the still air.

At the center, where the escarpment curves inward and provides shelter from the worst of the wind, is the old city — the part built highest on the slope, where the rock is oldest and most settled. Here the buildings are carved directly into the cliff face, rooms hollowed from the stone by hand over centuries. The rock is soft enough to work with stone tools but hard enough to hold its shape, a fine-grained sedimentary layer that splits cleanly along bedding planes. Each room is a single generation's work. A family begins with one chamber, then adds another, then another, tunneling deeper into the cliff. After five hundred years the old city is a lattice of rooms, passages, and terraces, some lit by shafts cut to the surface, others windowless and lit only by seal-oil lamps. The air inside is cold and still and smells of stone and rendered fat and the faint, sweet smoke of burning dung.

In the walls of the oldest rooms, the fossil shells are everywhere. The people who carved these chambers worked around them, leaving ammonites exposed like medallions in the stone, building them into doorframes and niches. Some are the size of a hand. Others are the size of a cartwheel, their chambers still lined with crystal. The oldest families keep a particular fossil — a small, perfect bivalve — above the doorway of the innermost room, a marker of continuity, a claim that the family has been here since the cliff was young. The children who grow up in these rooms play with fossil shells, skipping them across the floor, trading them like coins. They do not think of the sea. The sea is too large an idea for a room that has only ever known stone.

The plateau above the city is grassland. Not rich grass — the cold and the thin soil keep everything low and tough — but enough. Ichu grass grows in dense clumps, pale gold in summer, grey in winter. Between the clumps, cushion plants press themselves against the ground, and lichens paint the rocks in patches of orange and green. There are no trees. The people import wood from the lowlands via trade routes that follow the river canyons, and it is precious — used only for roof beams, for the handles of tools, for the frames of the litters that carry the dead to the burial terraces. Everything else is stone, bone, dried dung, and grass.

The herds are the reason for the city. The plateau supports vast numbers of a camelid-like animal — smaller than the lowland llamas, with dense copper-colored fleece and broad, padded feet that can cross scree without injury. They graze on the ichu grass, their heads moving in a steady rhythm, their breath steaming in the cold air. A large herd is five hundred animals, and there are dozens of herds on the plateau, each belonging to a particular lineage of the city, each moving through a seasonal circuit of grazing grounds that has been established over centuries. On a clear day you can see them from the escarpment as copper-colored patches moving across the pale grassland, and the sound of their calls — a low, nasal honking — carries across the plateau in a continuous, soft murmur. The herds are guarded by large, aggressive dogs and by herders who carry slings and know every rock and fold of the grassland.

The predators are fewer but present. A large cat — heavy-shouldered, pale grey, with a wide skull and short, powerful forelimbs — hunts the plateau's margins, taking the young and the weak from the herds. The herders know its habits, its territories, the places where it prefers to wait. They have a grudging respect for it. The cat is not hunted; it is managed, kept away from the herds by noise and fire, and in return it keeps the herd populations from growing beyond what the grassland can support. The balance is old and precise. The city's elders say that the cat was here before the people, and that the people learned to herd by watching it hunt.

The birds are more numerous. Large raptors — condor-like, with wingspans wider than a man is tall — circle the escarpment on thermals, rising in the morning as the sun warms the rock and descending in the evening to roost on narrow ledges. The cliff face is a vertical habitat, full of crevices and ledges, and it supports a dense population of smaller birds — brown and grey species that nest in holes and feed on the insects that gather around the snow patches. At dawn the cliff is loud with birdsong, a brief, intense chorus that fades as the sun rises and the air warms. By midday the plateau is quiet again.

The sky is enormous. At this elevation the atmosphere is thin, and the light is clear and fierce. The sun is blinding on bare rock. Shadows are sharp-edged and absolute. The plateau has its own weather — winds that sweep across the grassland from the interior, storms that build in the afternoon and dissipate by evening, fog that pools in the basins and burns off by mid-morning. Rain is rare but when it comes it is cold and vertical, falling without wind, drumming on the flat roofs and turning the streets to streams for an hour before the stone drinks it up. Snow is more common — a dusting that melts in a day, or a storm that lasts a week and buries the lower terraces. The people read the sky constantly. They know the smell of snow, the color of approaching fog, the particular quality of light that means the wind will shift by evening.

Sound dies quickly here. The thin air does not carry it, and the open plateau absorbs it. The loudest constant presence is the wind — a low, steady hum that rises and falls with the passage of weather systems. The loudest single events are rockfalls from the escarpment, which arrive without warning and end just as quickly, a crash and then silence. The city itself is quiet. Voices carry only a short distance. The water channels murmur. The animals call. But the city does not roar. There are no carts, no machines, no metal on metal. The loudest human sounds are the percussion of stone on stone — the building, the repair, the slow, patient work of maintaining the city against the cliff and the weather.

The pace of life is set by the stone and the herds and the seasons. A new room is carved in a decade. A terrace wall takes a lifetime. The water channels are rebuilt over generations, each section the work of a different hand, each repair visible as a slight change in the color or texture of the stone. The city changes at the speed of geology, or nearly. A man begins a wall; his grandchildren finish it. A woman begins a room; her great-grandchildren expand it. The plateau itself is still rising, imperceptibly, and the city rises with it, its terraces creeping upward, its channels extending to reach new snow patches as the old ones retreat. Everything is built for duration — not for a single life but for centuries. The people think in terms of generations. They do not say "I will build this." They say "I will begin this."

The dead are buried in terraces cut into the cliff above the city, in chambers that are sealed with stone and marked with a single fossil shell. The terraces are old — older than the living city — and they are full. The ancestors are everywhere, in the walls, under the floors, above the roofs. The city is a conversation between the living and the dead, conducted in stone, over centuries, in a language that changes so slowly that no single generation can hear the change.

At night the city is a field of small fires. The dung-fires burn in every hearth, and the light leaks from doorways and windows in thin, warm lines. The streets are dark and cold. The stars are brilliant — more stars than the lowlands ever see, the Milky Way a solid arc of light across the sky. The wind is steady. The herds are silent. The cliff is a darker darkness against the sky. Somewhere a dog barks, and another answers, and then the silence returns. The city sleeps in its stone, and the plateau rises, and the snow patches glimmer in the starlight, and the water moves through the channels with a sound like breathing.
