**Vhul-Keth, the Weeping Span**

You stand on a high plateau of young stone, on a world that has never known a friendly sun. The cold is absolute — it bites exposed skin in seconds, makes your lungs ache, turns every breath into a cloud of vapor that freezes and falls as a fine, glittering dust. Yet the air is wet. Saturated, heavy, condensing on every surface, filling your nose and mouth with the taste of cold water and minerals. Water is everywhere. It weeps from the cliff faces in thin, glassy sheets. It drips from the underside of every ledge in a constant, rhythmic patter. It runs in channels carved into the stone, in pipes and conduits, in the gutters of the city. It gathers in pools — still, black, mirror-bright — that reflect the faint glow of the creatures and the city's lights. The rock itself is wet, as if the plateau is a sponge that has been squeezed and is still releasing its stored moisture. You cannot escape it. Your clothes are soaked in minutes. Your skin is never dry. The water gets into everything — your boots, your pack, your hair, your mouth. It is cold, mineral, slightly salty, slightly metallic. It tastes of stone.

The stone is new. You can feel it in the sharpness of the fracture edges, in the unweathered crystal faces, in the faint warmth that rises from the deeper seams. This is raw geology — a vast igneous intrusion, or a block of crust thrust up and still cooling from its own formation. The surface is a chaos of polygonal columns, tilted terraces, and deep fissures, all of it still settling, still cracking, still creaking as it contracts. The fractures are filled with water — not rainwater, but water exsolved from the cooling rock, mineral-rich and briny, kept liquid by dissolved salts and by the heat that bleeds from below. In the deepest seams, the water is warm — almost hot — and the rock there is still plastic, still flowing, still crystallizing in slow motion. You can see it in the curved, flowing forms of the deepest exposures, in the way the crystal faces bend and fold like frozen smoke. The plateau is a body with a fever, and the water is its sweat.

The city is grown from the rock. It is not built in any conventional sense; it is extruded, crystallized, self-assembled. The builders — whoever they were, whatever they were — seeded the plateau with programmatic matter, with templates and catalysts, and the rock has been coaxed into becoming architecture. The structures are dark: basalt-black, iron-black, the black of wet slate and polished obsidian. They are also wet — the same water that films the rock films the walls, drips from the eaves, runs in channels carved into the facades. The city is dense. Towers lean against towers, bridges arc between them at every level, pipes and conduits thread through the gaps, platforms and balconies project and overhang until the air between the buildings is a tangled web. It is layered: you can see a dozen levels of construction in any cross-section, older strata of growth buried beneath newer, the whole thing a palimpsest of accretion. And it is large: the city covers the plateau from edge to edge, a sprawling, continuous fabric of construction that extends past the horizon in every direction, broken only by the deeper fissures and the occasional open pool. There are spires that rise like frozen flames, terraces that step down like amphitheaters, warrens that burrow into the rock like the nests of some vast, mineral insect. There are reservoirs and cisterns, pipes and aqueducts, chimneys and vents, all of it wet, all of it dark, all of it humming with a faint, subsonic vibration that you feel in your teeth.

The fauna are overwhelming. They are everywhere — in the air, on the walls, in the water, in the fissures. They are small, most of them: a few millimeters to a few centimeters, though some are larger. Their bodies are chitinous or crystalline, pale or translucent, and they move in clouds, in sheets, in rivers. The air is thick with them — a constant drift of tiny forms, a snow of living things, a fog of wings and antennae. They coat the walls in living tiles, they hang from the undersides of ledges in curtains, they swarm around the warm seeps in columns that rise like smoke. Their numbers are beyond counting; they are a biomass that rivals the city itself. And they are loud. Their wings beat in a constant haze of high-frequency sound, their legs click on the rock, their mouthparts scrape and chitter, and the combined effect is a roar — a dense, layered, unending sound that fills the air and vibrates in your chest. It is the sound of the city, the sound of the plateau, the sound of the world. You cannot hear yourself think. You must shout to be heard, and even then your voice is swallowed by the roar.

The light is very low. The sky is black — not the black of a moonless night on a living world, but the black of deep space, of a world with a thin atmosphere and no sun to speak of. The star — if there is one — is a dim ember, a red dwarf or a distant sun, and it casts no shadows, only a faint, diffuse glow on the horizon that might be aurora or might be the last memory of light. The city is dark, but not lightless. The creatures glow: cold bioluminescence, blue and green and white, pulsing in the swarms, tracing the outlines of the buildings in living points of light. The water glows too, faintly, with the light of chemiluminescent reactions or with the reflection of the creatures' glow. The rock glows in the warm seams, a dull red or orange, and the city's machinery — where it is exposed — glows with waste heat or with indicator lights that are not meant for human eyes. The overall effect is a darkness that is textured, veined, and alive with points and threads of cold light. You can see enough to navigate — the bioluminescence is sufficient for that — but the darkness presses in from every side, and the lights are islands in a black sea.

The ecology is simple and strange. There are no plants. There is no sun to drive photosynthesis, and the rock is too young, too raw, too mineral to support anything like a plant. The base of the food web is chemical: the water, mineral-rich and warm from the rock's heat, carries dissolved energy — hydrogen, hydrogen sulfide, methane, the exhalations of the cooling deep. The creatures feed on this, directly or indirectly. Some of them are filter-feeders, straining the water for its dissolved chemistry. Some of them are grazers, scraping the rock for its mineral films and the bacteria-analogues that colonize it. Some of them are predators, hunting the others. The entire ecosystem is built on chemosynthesis and mineral metabolism, and it is as alien as the world itself. The creatures are not biological in any familiar sense: their shells are mineral, their fluids are brines or liquid metals, their metabolisms are slow and cold. They are part animal, part crystal, part machine. Their shed shells accumulate as a sediment — a white or pale gray drift that fills the gutters and the low places, that is crunched underfoot, that is incorporated into the city's growth. The sediment is deep in some places — a meter, two meters — a record of countless generations of creatures.

The city is part of this ecology. It is grown from the same rock, fed by the same water, warmed by the same heat. The creatures live in it, on it, through it. They nest in its gaps, they feed on its surfaces, they die and their bodies become part of its material. The city is alive, in a sense — it grows, it heals, it responds to the creatures and to the rock. The programmatic matter that it is made of is not so different from the creatures' own mineral shells; the boundary between the city and the fauna is blurred. Perhaps the city is a creature, or the creatures are the city's cells, or the distinction is meaningless. The city's growth is slow and patient: a new wall extrudes over weeks, a new bridge crystallizes over months, a new tower rises over years. It does not tear down. Old structures are buried, incorporated, built over. The city is a palimpsest, a record of its own growth, a body that remembers its own history in its strata.

The builders are gone, or they are changed, or they are so other that they cannot be recognized. There are signs of them everywhere — in the city's layout, in the programmatic matter, in the creatures themselves. Perhaps the creatures are their descendants, or their tools, or their art. Perhaps the city is a monument, a machine, a garden, a farm, a laboratory, a tomb. Perhaps the builders are still here, but they are no longer biological — they have uploaded themselves into the city's systems, or they have become crystalline, or they have merged with the programmatic matter. They are the hum in the walls, the slow pulse of the growth, the patterns in the bioluminescence. They are the city's mind, if it has one. Or perhaps there is no mind, only the slow, patient, mineral processes of growth and decay. You may never know. The city does not explain itself.

The dynamics are slow. The rock cools over millennia; the city grows over decades; the water seeps and freezes in cycles of years; the creatures live and die in seasons that are measured in months or years. The city is patient. It does not change quickly, and neither do its inhabitants — if inhabitants there are. If there are minds in Vhul-Keth, they are minds that think in centuries, that plan in geological time, that move with the slow certainty of growing crystals. The place is temporary on a geological timescale — a warm, wet, living bubble in a cold, dark, mineral world. The rock will cool. The water will freeze. The warm seeps will shrink. The creatures will retreat or die. The city will slow and stop. But that will take millennia, and for now, the place is alive and growing and loud.

The rules of the world are the rules of cold, wet, dark, and stone. Water is liquid where the rock is warm, and ice where it is cold. The creatures follow the warmth, the water, the chemistry. The city follows the rock, growing where the rock is ripe, dormant where it is spent. The light is life: the creatures make their own, and they gather where it is made. The sound is constant: it is the sound of the world being born, of the rock cooling and the water flowing and the creatures swarming and the city growing. The sound is also a sense — the creatures use it to navigate, to find the warm seeps, to find each other, to warn of danger. The city uses it too, perhaps, to sense its own growth, to coordinate its slow, distributed intelligence. The darkness is not empty; it is full of information, full of sound, full of the faint, cold light of life and machinery.

You are small here. The cold is absolute, the wet is total, the dark is complete, the sound is overwhelming, and the city and the creatures and the rock are vast. You are a brief, warm, dry, quiet, lightless thing in a place that has none of those qualities. And yet — the city is beautiful. The bioluminescence is beautiful. The wet black stone is beautiful. The roar is beautiful. The sheer, patient, mineral life of the place is beautiful. You stand on the plateau, and the water weeps around you, and the creatures swarm, and the city grows, and the rock creaks, and the sound fills your skull, and you are alive in Vhul-Keth, the Weeping Span, the city on the cold plateau where the stone is young and the water is warm and the dark is full of wings.
