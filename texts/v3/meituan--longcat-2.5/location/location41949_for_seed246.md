# The Hollow City

A high plateau, ancient beyond reckoning. The stone here is old—not old like mountains are old, but old like the bottom of the sea is old, because that is what it once was. Continental plates carried it up, inch by inch, over hundreds of millions of years, and now it sits at an elevation where the air is thin and cold and the wind never really stops. But the elevation did not come with sharpness. Whatever peaks it might once have had were worn away so long ago that the land has forgotten how to be steep. It is flat. Not perfectly flat—there are gentle swells, shallow basins, the memory of ancient folds—but flat enough that you can see the curvature of the world at the horizon.

The flatness is the first thing that makes this place strange. The second is what grows on it.

---

Rising from the stone, in clusters and scatterings across the plateau, are formations that look like buildings. Columns, walls, arches, chambers. They are made of tufa—calcium carbonate deposited by mineral-rich water over spans of time that make human civilization look like a brief accident. The process is simple: water seeps through the stone, dissolves calcium, reaches the surface, loses carbon dioxide to the air, and leaves the mineral behind. A film. A crust. A slow accretion. Over ten thousand years, a centimeter. Over a million years, a meter. The formations here are tens of meters tall in places, which means they have been growing since before the ice ages, since before the genus *Homo*, since before most of the species alive today had any ancestors at all.

They are not perfectly architectural. They lean. They bulge. They have the organic irregularity of things grown rather than built. But the overall effect is unmistakable—a settlement. Streets of columns run between blocks of walls. Open plazas of flat stone are enclosed on three sides by curving, chambered structures. In the largest cluster, near the center of the plateau, the formations are dense enough to feel like a town center—a maze of passages and rooms where the wind finds a dozen different notes to play at once. Further out, the clusters thin. A colonnade of pillars standing alone in an empty quarter. A single arch, door-sized, leading nowhere. A ring of walls open to the sky, like a amphitheater for an audience of stone.

The layout has a kind of logic, but it is the logic of water, not of planning. The formations grow where the water flows, and the water flows in channels worn into the flat stone over the same immense timespan. The channels branch and reconnect, and so do the formations. The result looks almost like a street grid—but a street grid designed by a river, or by a root system, or by something that thought in centuries rather than days.

---

The water that built them is still here, still working. It comes from the sky—not often, but persistently. Occasional rain, more often mist and condensation. Clouds sit at this elevation, and when they touch the stone they give up their moisture. The formations themselves are spongy with it. Water drips from their undersides, runs down their sides in thin sheets, pools at their bases in shallow basins worn into the stone. The basins are smooth and rounded, some no larger than a cupped hand, others broad enough to wade across. They never overflow. The water seeps away as fast as it arrives, draining through cracks in the stone to some deeper system, some aquifer or underground river that will eventually carry it to the lowlands far below.

The air is damp. Not wet—there is no standing water in any quantity, no streams, no lakes—but damp in the way that a cave is damp, or a bog. Your skin feels it. Your clothes feel it. The moss is saturated, heavy with moisture, and when you touch it the water runs out like from a sponge. The stone is dark with it, almost black in the sheltered places, and pale where the wind has dried it. The formations themselves are streaked with mineral stains—cream and rust and pale green—where the water has carried different dissolved minerals to the surface over different centuries.

---

But the real sound is the wind.

At this elevation, on this flatness, the wind has nowhere to go but across and through. It howls over the open stone, a low continuous roar that you feel in your chest before you hear it with your ears. And when it reaches the formations, it finds every opening, every channel, every hollow chamber. It whistles through gaps between columns. It hums in the larger chambers like breath across the mouth of a bottle. It moans in the deeper hollows, a low frequency that you sense more than hear. The formations are acoustic instruments, and the wind plays them constantly.

The sound is loud—not chaotic, but layered, a constant drone shot through with higher whistles and occasional deep resonant booms when the wind shifts and finds a new chamber to fill. In the densest cluster of formations, the sound is almost overwhelming. It is not like standing in a city. It is like standing inside an organ, or inside the chest of something enormous that is breathing. The stone vibrates. The air vibrates. You can feel the sound in your teeth, in your bones, in the hollow spaces behind your eyes.

And the wind is not the only musician. The water drips—drip, drip, drip—a constant percussion in the sheltered places. Ice forms in the night, and in the morning it cracks with a sound like a gunshot, sharp and sudden, followed by the tinkle of fragments falling. The freeze-thaw cycle is the engine of erosion here, working at the stone with infinite patience, and it has been working so long that the stone has given up. It is smooth. Rounded. Worn to its final form. But it still sings, occasionally, as the temperature changes and the rock contracts or expands—a low, almost subsonic groan that you feel in your sternum.

---

The cold is pervasive. Temperatures hover near freezing for most of the year, dropping far below in the long nights. In the brief summer, the sun—sharp and intense at this altitude—can warm the stone to something almost comfortable, and the moss wakes into vivid, almost aggressive green. But the warmth never lasts. By evening the cold is back, and the water in the basins has frozen again, and the sound of cracking ice echoes across the plateau.

The vegetation is lush in the way that a bog is lush—not tall, not diverse, but dense, vivid, persistent. The damp hollows are filled with moss, thick mats that soften the stone and turn the basins into velvet pools of green. Lichens crust every surface in patches of orange and grey and pale, almost luminous green. In the most sheltered spots, small cushion plants huddle against the stone, their leaves tight and waxy against the cold. A few hardy grasses find purchase in cracks, and in the brief summer they send up tiny flowers—white, yellow, pale purple—that last only a few days before the cold returns.

The vegetation has been growing here for as long as the formations have, and it will continue long after whatever is reading this is gone. It grows slowly—millimeters per year—but it does not stop. It creeps across the stone, fills every damp hollow, softens every sharp edge. In a few thousand years, the formations will be furred with it, their architectural lines softened into something more organic, more like a reef than a ruin. In a few million years, they will be gone entirely, worn down to the same flatness as everything else, and the moss will cover the stone like a blanket, and the place will be only a plateau again.

---

Fauna is rare. A few insects—springtails, mites, a species of darkling beetle—live in the moss, moving slowly in the cold. Birds pass through occasionally, riding the wind, but they do not stay long. There is nothing here for them—no trees, no abundant insects, no shelter from the wind except the formations themselves, and the formations are too exposed, too resonant, too loud. Sometimes a fox or a small cat will cross the plateau, following some ancient route between basins, but they move quickly and do not linger. The place is too open, too loud, too strange.

At night, if you stay (and few do), the silence between sounds is profound. The wind drops, sometimes, in the small hours, and then you can hear the water dripping, the ice settling, the faint creak of the stone as it cools. And sometimes, in that near-silence, you can hear something else—a low, rhythmic pulse, like a heartbeat, that seems to come from the stone itself. It is probably just the wind, finding some new channel, some new chamber, some new way to make the place speak. But it is easy to imagine that the plateau is alive, that the formations are its bones, that the water is its blood, and that it is dreaming, very slowly, of the sea it came from.

---

The light is clear and sharp at this altitude. On clear days, the sun is intense and the shadows are dark, and the pale tufa seems to glow, almost luminous against the dark stone. The colors are vivid—the green of the moss, the orange of the lichen, the cream and grey and rust of the formations. On overcast days, the light is diffuse and even, and the formations seem to absorb it, to hold it, to glow with a faint inner light. When clouds roll in—which they do often—they do not just bring moisture; they bring a quality of silence and enclosure. The wind drops. The sound changes from a roar to a whisper. The formations emerge from and dissolve into the mist, and for a little while the place feels smaller, more intimate, almost gentle.

But the wind always returns.

---

The weirdness of the Hollow City is not in anything supernatural. It is in the timescale. The formations are so old that they have outlasted the processes that might have explained them. The water chemistry that built them has shifted slightly over the millennia; the rate of accretion has slowed. They are, in a sense, dying—not quickly, but inevitably. The wind and the freeze-thaw cycle erode them at the same time as the water builds them, and eventually the erosion will win. The formations are in their old age, their final form, and they will be gone in another million years or so, worn down to the same flatness as everything else on the plateau.

Until then, they stand. They resonate. They drip. They grow, almost imperceptibly. The moss spreads. The lichen brightens and fades. The wind plays its endless, complex music. And the place remains—ancient, empty, loud, and strange—a city built by no one, inhabited by almost nothing, older than most of the mountains it once might have rivaled, and still, in its slow, cold, patient way, alive.
