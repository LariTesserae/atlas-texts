# The Foundries of Hollowmar

The descent ends not in darkness but in a dull red pulse, like the cave is breathing.

Hollowmar is a town built into the throat of something that should not exist — a geological wound, a basalt chimney that opened barely forty years ago when the deep drilling crews hit a pocket of superheated gas and brought the mountain's blood to the surface. The rock here is young. It hasn't learned to be smooth. Every wall is glassy and fractured, sharp as broken teeth, veined with minerals that cooled too fast to crystallize properly — they sit in the stone like frozen smoke, like the memory of heat. Touch a wall and your fingerprint comes away dusty with something that glitters faintly in the dim light.

The town clings to the interior of the chimney in stacked, cantilevered layers, descending into the red glow below and climbing toward a distant circle of daylight far above — a long way up, but close enough that the thermals pull at your clothes and carry the faint smell of rain down into the depths. The architecture is industrial salvage: iron walkways bolted directly into fresh basalt, corrugated metal sheets riveted over ledges, pipe networks that run everywhere, carrying steam and coolant and something thicker that condenses in the cooler upper reaches and drips steadily onto the levels below. The water here is not a river or a lake — it is condensation. It beads on every cool surface, runs in threads down the walls, pools in the sagging canvas awnings that shelter the upper workshops. Intermittent rain falls from the opening above, warm and smelling of the surface world, and when it comes the whole chimney fills with mist that takes hours to settle, during which time every surface weeps and the iron walkways become treacherous with rust-water.

The heat is the reason for everything. It rises from below — from the magma chamber the drillers cracked open, still cooling after millennia, still willing to warm the world above it. The foundries are built directly over the hottest vents, their furnaces fed by the earth itself, requiring no fuel but the planet's slow exhalation. The forge-halls glow at the bottom of the shaft, and their light — orange, arterial — is the primary illumination for the entire settlement. Above them, the residential tiers are lit by bare electric bulbs on fraying cables, and above those, near the rim, by whatever daylight filters down through the haze. The result is a town that exists in permanent dusk, its lowest levels bathed in furnace-light, its highest levels in grey-green shade, everything in between in the amber half-light of a hundred naked filaments.

There are no animals. The heat drove them out years ago, or the toxic compounds that seep from the mineral vents — sulfurous, metallic, sharp on the tongue — poisoned the upper reaches where they might have nested. No birds circle the opening above. No insects drone in the warm air. But life persists in its most patient forms: pale, fat fungi that fruit on the damp canvas awnings in shelves like ears, colorless as candle wax; thin films of thermophilic algae that coat the warmest pipes in slippery green; a moss that grows only where condensation drips steadily, forming dense dark cushions on the upper ledges that smell of wet stone and something faintly chemical. The people of Hollowmar harvest these — the fungi for food, the moss for insulation, the algae for processing into a bitter paste that protects the skin from the mineral vapors.

The sound of the town is low and constant: the deep thrum of the forge-bellows, the tick and ping of cooling metal, the steady drip of condensation, and beneath everything, a subsonic vibration that you feel in your teeth rather than hear — the mountain, still settling. Occasionally a pipe bursts and steam screams through a residential tier for minutes before someone reaches the valve. Occasionally a section of fresh rock shears away from the wall with a sound like a gunshot, and everyone stops and waits to see if anything else is coming down. These events are part of the rhythm. The town is young and the rock is young and neither has learned stability.

The people live in the middle tiers, where the temperature is merely unbearable rather than lethal. They sleep in the cooler hours — not because it ever truly cools, but because the foundries reduce their output and the red glow dims to embers, and the air drops from *wet furnace* to merely *sauna*. They work in the hot hours, tending the machinery that processes the mineral-rich slurry pumped up from the depths. The machinery is old — older than the town, older than the drilling — salvaged from surface factories and adapted to the heat, running on steam and electricity in equal measure, covered in grease and mineral deposits and the patient green film of the algae that no one has the heart to fully scrub away.

And at the very bottom, where the rock is too hot to touch and the air shimmers, the forge-masters tend the primary vents with long iron poles, and the light is so red and so deep that they appear as silhouettes even at noon, figures from a older world, working at the mouth of something that predates the town, predates the drills, predates the human need to build and dig and burn — something that was here first and will be here after, patient and hot and entirely indifferent to the small, dense, dripping, glowing thing that clings to its throat.
