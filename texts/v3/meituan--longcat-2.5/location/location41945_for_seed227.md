The stream is small enough to step across in most places, but it has cut a tight canyon through dark stone. The walls rise steep and jointed on either side, the water runs fast and cold over worn cobbles, and the air smells of wet rock and vegetation. You follow the stream downhill until the canyon opens slightly and the water gathers itself and drops—not far, perhaps the height of two people—into a pool below. Behind the falling water, there is a dark space. You climb around the pool's edge, through the spray, and step behind the curtain.

The sound changes immediately. Outside, the waterfall is a hiss and a crash, noticeable but not overwhelming. Inside the chamber, the same water is a sustained roar. The sound fills the space completely—it presses against the walls, the ceiling, your chest. You feel it in your teeth and your sternum. It is not a noise you hear so much as a pressure you inhabit. You cannot hear your own footsteps. You cannot hear your own breathing. If you speak, you hear only the vibration in your skull, not the sound in the air.

The chamber is a single room, perhaps twenty meters across, with a ceiling that rises into darkness above the reach of the light. The floor is bare stone, worn smooth by millennia of water, sloping gently toward the pool where the waterfall lands. The pool is deep and dark, fed by the falls, drained by a crack in the floor that gurgles with outflow. The water in the pool is black except where the falls strike—a circle of white foam and concentric ripples that spread and dissolve.

Most of the chamber is empty. The volume is vast compared to what fills it. The floor is bare. The walls are bare. The ceiling is bare. The presences—the waterfall, the pool, the light from the entrance—are clustered at one end, and the rest is void. Dark, cold, empty air extends back into a space you cannot see the end of. You know it continues because the sound of dripping echoes from somewhere in the darkness, and because the air moves—a constant draft, pulled by the falls toward the interior. But your eyes fail before they find a far wall. The chamber is a room built for nothing, or for something that left long ago.

The light enters only through the waterfall's curtain and the passage you came through. It is diffuse, green-gray, filtered through water and forest canopy. Near the entrance, it is enough to see by—dim, but sufficient. A few paces in, it is twilight. Further, it is darkness. The darkness is not an absence but a presence—it has weight, it presses against your face, it absorbs the sound of the falls and seems to hold it at bay. At the rear of the chamber, the darkness is complete. You cannot tell if the space continues for meters or tens of meters. You cannot tell if there are walls, or formations, or nothing at all.

The walls near the entrance are lush. Moss grows thick as a fist, a deep green so dark it is almost black, weeping water, soft as foam. Ferns grow from every crack and ledge—small, delicate, their fronds reaching toward the light, some trailing water like green hair. Liverworts form dark green crusts on the wettest stone. A film of algae coats the rock where the spray reaches, bright emerald where the light hits it directly, darker in shadow. The vegetation forms a narrow band, perhaps two meters deep, a green halo around the entrance. Within that band, the life is dense, almost tropical in its intensity—a pocket of lushness sustained by the constant moisture and the diffused light. Then it stops. Further in, the walls are bare—wet, dark stone, smooth and water-worn, with occasional streaks of mineral: ochre, white, a faint green where moisture seeps and evaporates. The transition is abrupt. The plants need light; the light stops; the plants stop.

Condensation beads on every surface. The cold stone draws water from the air, and the water drips—slowly, steadily, from the ceiling, from the walls, from the tips of ferns. Each drip strikes stone or pool with a sharp, clear note that pierces the roar for an instant before being swallowed. The drips are the chamber's clock, counting time in falling water.

The air is cold. Not the cold of wind, but the cold of deep stone and snow-melt water. It is a cold that has mass, that has been stored in the rock over centuries. The stone is cold to the touch. The water in the pool is snow-cold. Even in summer, the chamber holds the temperature of deep winter. Your fingers numb quickly if you touch the walls. Your face feels the cold as a steady pressure. The waterfall is the only thing that moves, and its movement is constant but its volume changes—slowly, with the seasons, with the weather, over days and weeks.

Life is sparse but present. A spider on the wall near the light, motionless, its web beaded with moisture. Moths clustered in a crevice, their wings dusted with spray. A salamander—pale, almost translucent, eyeless-looking—under a wet ledge near the pool, motionless, perhaps waiting for something to fall into the water. Bats, small and quick, in the upper darkness—you glimpse them only when they cross the light from the entrance, dark shapes against the gray, gone before you can focus. In the pool, small fish—dark, holding against the current near the edges, their mouths working. Bird droppings near the entrance, white streaks on the stone, perhaps from swallows or dippers fishing the stream above. The place is tenanted, but thinly. Everything that lives here is at the margins, near the light, near the water, near the entrance. The interior is too dark, too cold, too empty.

The human traces are near the entrance, on the dryest ground, where the light is best. A low wall of stacked stone—perhaps waist-high, perhaps less—forming a rough semicircle, open toward the chamber's interior. The stones are dark with moisture and age, fitted without mortar, some fallen, some tilted. Behind the wall, the ground is stained black—a fire, long ago, that cracked the stone and left a thin lens of ash and charcoal, now washed and scattered. A few flakes of worked stone lie near the fire—dark chert, sharp-edged, some with retouched edges. A bone, perhaps, too far gone to identify—a splinter, a fragment. On the wall above the stone semicircle, at chest height, a series of vertical lines cut into the stone—a tally, a count, perhaps thirty marks, their edges softened by moisture and mineral deposits. Some are older than others; some are cut deeper, some are faint. Other marks are visible nearby—a spiral, a hand outline, a shape that might be a fish or a bird. The marks are old. Whoever made them came here once, or came here many times over many years, and then stopped.

The chamber changes slowly. In winter, the waterfall shrinks to a trickle and ice forms on the walls—clear, blue-white, building in layers, thickening the stone, changing the acoustics. The roar becomes hollow, echoing, brittle. In spring, meltwater swells the flow and the roar returns, and the ice breaks and crashes into the pool. The moss grows, imperceptibly, extending its margin by fractions each year. The drips in the ceiling shift their paths over decades, cutting new channels in the stone. The pool's level rises and falls with the seasons. The stone wall settles and slumps. The marks on the wall weather and fade. Nothing is quick here. The chamber has been this way for thousands of years, and it will remain this way for thousands more, except for the slow work of water and cold and time.

The rules of the place are simple:

- The water always flows, but its voice changes with the seasons.
- The light stops at the threshold; beyond, there is only dark.
- The sound fills the chamber and dominates all other senses.
- The cold is constant. The rock remembers winter.
- Life clings to the entrance, where light and water meet.
- The interior is empty—a void that holds only echo and cold.
- Time moves in seasons and centuries, not days.
- Human presence is temporary and leaves only faint traces.

The chamber is a room that water built for itself, and everything else—the moss, the ferns, the bats, the fish, the humans—are visitors, tolerated at the margins. The waterfall is the host. The darkness is the guest that never leaves. The sound is the voice of the place, and it has been speaking for longer than there have been ears to hear it.
