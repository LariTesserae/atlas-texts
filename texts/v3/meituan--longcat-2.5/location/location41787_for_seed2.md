# The Yards

The floor of the ocean is metal.

Not rock dressed in metal — *metal*. Hexagonal plating, each plate forty meters across, welded at the seams with beads still bright and unoxidized, laid over a substructure of I-beams and cross-bracing that descends into darkness below. Drainage channels cut geometric trenches between sections. Access hatches sit flush with the surface, their handles untouched by corrosion. Everything is young. Nothing here has had time to age.

The water above is cool and clear, fifty to eighty meters deep across most of the basin, more in the service trenches, less over the raised platforms where the great structures stand. Sunlight reaches the floor in pale green columns that shift with the weather above — this sea is open to the sky, or so close to open that the distinction hardly matters. The surface is real, and weather crosses it. Storms push cold surges downward along the towers. Rain darkens the upper layers and sends freshwater lenses sliding across the salt. On clear days the light is almost ordinary, almost like a natural ocean, and you can see for kilometers across the empty water to the next structure standing alone on the horizon.

They are spaced apart — the refineries, the pump stations, the processing towers, the pipe lattices — separated by whole kilometers of open water and bare metal floor. The scale is such that each one is its own neighborhood, its own weather system, its own horizon. Between them: nothing but water and the faint geometric lines of the plating below. The emptiness is enormous. You can hang in the water at mid-depth and see no structure in any direction, only the faint blue gradient of distance and the pale floor beneath, and yet the sound never stops — the pumps are everywhere, a subsonic throb that travels through the water for dozens of kilometers, a bass note felt in the chest before it's heard.

The largest of the structures is a refining tower called the Spine. It rises from a raised platform the size of a city, its base a forest of intake pipes and outflow channels, its midsection a dense lattice of heat exchangers and separation columns wrapped in catwalks and running lights, its top disappearing into the haze of the upper water column where the light goes white and diffuse. Water moves through it constantly — drawn in at the base, pushed through the works, expelled at various depths through pipes the diameter of train tunnels. The outflow is warmer than the ambient water by two or three degrees, and around each outflow pipe a zone of warmth spreads through the metal and the water, a thermal island in the cool sea. Life clusters there.

It is abundant, the life. Not lush — the vegetation is sparse, stubborn, still colonizing — but the animals have arrived in force. Silver fish school in the spaces between heat exchangers, turning as one through the lattice like birds through a forest canopy. They move between structures in migrations that follow the currents the pumps create, whole rivers of flow that run through the open water in channels you can feel but not see. Around the warm outflows, anemones spread across the plating in pale constellations, and small octopuses hunt between them, and crabs pick their way along weld seams. Tube worms coat the lower pipes in white and orange colonies that sway in the manufactured current. Strands of kelp — not forests, not yet, but patches, holdfasts gripping the pipeline supports — lean into the flow and release long ribbons of brown-green that twist and reach toward the light. Algae films the upper surfaces of everything, a thin green skin that the fish graze.

The sound is constant and loud. The pumps are the bass line, always. Water rushing through pipes the size of tunnels is a mid-range roar that comes and goes as systems cycle. Metal expands and contracts with the temperature of the water, groaning at the joints, ticking as it cools. Pressure releases — sudden, sharp bangs that send silver bubbles racing upward in startled columns. Somewhere, always, a vent is opening or closing, and the water shivers. The sound bounces off the metal floor and the metal towers and comes back layered, a dense industrial chord that never resolves.

The currents are complex and managed. The pumps move water in patterns that shift with the operations of the Yards — a strong outflow here, an intake there, a circulation loop running through a pipe lattice for kilometers before releasing at a different depth. The fish know these currents. The kelp knows them. The thermal zones migrate as systems come online and offline, and the life follows the warmth, the flow, the food. It is an ecosystem built entirely on industrial rhythm, and it works. Things eat and are eaten. Larvae settle on fresh plating and grow. Predators patrol the edges of the light. The whole thing is in motion, always, a biological pace layered over the mechanical one, both of them fast, both of them relentless.

And it is all so *new*. The paint on the Spine is still bright yellow and white. The weld marks are clean and regular. The access hatches have serial numbers stamped into them that you can read. There is no silt, no rust blooms, no natural sediment — the floor is swept clean by the currents, or by maintenance systems, or simply by the fact that nothing natural has had time to accumulate. The sharp edges of the hexagonal plates are still sharp. The corners of the structures are still corners. The place looks like it was completed last year, or last month, or this morning — a vast, shallow, cool ocean laid down on a metal floor and filled with water and switched on, and now running, running, running, loud and bright and empty and full of life that has already, already, made it home.
