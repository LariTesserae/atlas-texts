**The Weeping Level**

The sky is a broth — not cloud, not haze, not quite either, but a warm thick medium of steam and spores and the volatile breath of everything that grows here, so that the sun is only a brighter bruise somewhere overhead, a pale copper smear that throws no shadows. Light arrives from everywhere at once, the color of weak tea, of old amber, of the inside of a shell. It is always this light. It is never darker and it is never brighter. Even at what might be noon the world sits in a warm twilight, and the heat — the heat is not from the sun. The heat rises from the ground.

The ground is young. That is the first thing to understand, and it explains almost everything else. This is a landscape not worn into shape but being built into shape; the word for what is happening here is not erosion but accretion. The basin is low and broad — broad enough that the far edge is only a rumor, a slightly darker band in the haze — a fresh bowl of soft gray-cream silt and sinter laid down by water that has been drawing dissolved stone out of the earth and laying it down again, patiently, in rimstone dams and terraced pools and low mounded hummocks. The hummocks are the land's first attempts at elevation, embryonic hills that have not yet decided how tall to be. The sinter is new — cream and apricot and pale blue-gray, still soft enough to mark, still growing. The rims of the pools are thin and slightly ragged, the way a scab looks before it thickens. There are no rivers here, because rivers take time to carve and time has not yet been given; instead the water spreads in a single vast sheet, barely deeper than a hand in most places, mirror-still where it is warmest, dimpled and bubbling where springs breathe up from below. The water level has not changed. The same leaves have been floating in the same positions for as long as anything can remember.

Every surface weeps. The air is so saturated that water condenses on the underside of every leaf and falls again in a slow, irregular patter — not rain, but the world sweating. The sinter mounds and hummocks weep too, exuding opalescent threads of mineral water that run down their flanks and gather in the pools below, so that the whole landscape seems to be weeping warm mineral tears, and the tears are building it even as they fall. The vegetation is a mat, a layered squishy carpet of sedge and moss and liverwort over the water, broken here and there by stands of reed and by low, leathery shrubs whose broad dark leaves hold pools of their own and spill them over the edges. Algae paint the shallows in films of green and rust-orange. Fungi come up in the dim — pale cups, bracket shelves, soft blooms of mycelium — and some of them carry a faint cold light of their own, a blue-green glimmer that means the twilight is never quite dark enough to be dark. The mats are stitched together underground by a single continuous mycelial network, so that the whole marsh is, in a sense, one organism wearing many green wigs. Everything drips. Everything is warm. The mud between the mats is warm to the touch, and it is alive with bubbles.

The sound is loud and it is constant. A roar-hum of insect wings fills the air from ankle-height to well above the head — midges in their thousands rising in columns, water-striders stippling the shallows, beetles with shells crusted pale as old shells from the mineral water they drink, dragonflies the span of a hand holding station over the pools. Under that, the frogs: a wall of frog-call, a continuous braided chorus that never peaks and never troughs, so many throats that individual calls cannot be sorted out. Under that, the water — bubbling, dripping, the squelch of mud, the small splashes of things sliding off mats, the rustle of reeds. It is loud enough that a voice would have to rise to be heard, and it never stops, and it never changes in volume, and after a while you stop hearing it as sound and start hearing it as the tone of the place itself, the note the world is holding.

And the animals — the animals are overwhelming. The warm shallows are thick with tadpoles, a living paste of them, and with small fish that flicker like thrown coins. Salamanders the length of an arm move through the mats with the slow grace of eels, their skin translucent enough to show the dark thread of the spine. Waders pick their way along the hummocks on stilt-legs, their beaks adapted to whatever it is they probe for in the warm mud. In the air there are more wings than there are gaps between wings. In the mud there are burrows, and in the burrows there are things. In the water there are larvae, and on the larvae there are smaller things. The density of life is such that the world feels less like a place with animals in it and more like a single body with a very large number of moving parts. Nothing here is rare. Everything is everywhere, in numbers that do not fluctuate.

That is the strangest thing, and it takes a while to notice: nothing changes. The water level is fixed. The steam columns rise and are replaced by identical steam columns. The chorus holds its amplitude. The heat does not break and does not build. The sinter grows, but so slowly that growth and stasis are the same thing. Nothing is born faster than something else dies; nothing accumulates; nothing wears away. The equilibrium is perfect and it is held — not by anything visible, not by any rule that can be named, but held the way a held breath is held, except that this breath has been held for a very long time. The flies rise, and fall, and rise again, and the total number of flies is the same. The pools overflow their rims at the same height they have always overflowed. The far edge of the basin is lost in the haze, and it has always been lost in the haze, and the haze has always been the same thickness.

You stand on a hummock and look out across the Level — the sheet-water catching the copper smear of the sky, the terraced pools stepping down in their cream and apricot bands, the mats of vegetation floating like rafts, the columns of steam, the clouds of wings — and you understand that you are looking at a world that is still becoming, and that has decided, for now, to be exactly this. A nursery. A broth. A warm, dim, loud, weeping nursery for everything that lives here, and the nursery is full, and the door is closed, and the temperature is perfect, and it has been perfect for a very long time, and there is no reason to believe it will ever be otherwise.
