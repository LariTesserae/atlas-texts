The basin is young. Its rim is fresh stone — basalt, obsidian, pumice, tuff — still showing the stripes, bubbles, and flow-lines of its making. The cliffs are sharp-edged, the talus angular, the channels steep-sided and rock-bottomed. There is little soil. The land has the quality of something recently emerged: raw, unweathered, precise, as if the earth had only just finished shaping it and had not yet begun to wear it down. The basin is low, a bowl in the land, a crater, a sag, a fold. The rim rises on all sides — not high, but enough. Enough to trap what is inside. Enough to make the basin a room. From the rim, you see the whole of it at once: the town, the water, the steam, the haze. The whole place is a single feature, a single view, a single object. It is small. It is bounded. It is complete.

The town fills the floor of the basin, but thinly. It is not a city. It is a hamlet, a settlement, a works. The buildings are of brick and dark stone, with roofs of slate and corrugated iron. They stand apart from one another, separated by channels of water, by pools, by yards of mud and gravel, by steaming vents. The density is low. There is much space between presences. A building, then a gap. A chimney, then a pool. The town breathes. The spaces are as important as the buildings — the channels, the pools, the open ground. The infrastructure is dense: pipes, wires, channels, rails, cables — but the buildings are sparse. A single road climbs the rim, switchbacking up the sharp rock, the only way in and out.

The air is hot and wet. The heat is not dry; it is the heat of a bathhouse, a laundry, a greenhouse, a stomach. It presses on the skin. The humidity is near saturation. Every surface sweats. Metal rusts quickly. Wood swells. Stone darkens. Condensation runs down walls, drips from eaves, beads on leaves, pools on every horizontal surface. The atmosphere is a soup of steam and smoke and haze, and the sun is only a dim disc behind it — a pale coin, a smear, a memory of light. There are no shadows. The light is diffuse, warm, dim. The town's electric lights glow amber and green through the haze, and their light is caught by the steam and scattered into a general glow. They are always on, day and night, because the dimness is constant. Day and night are nearly the same.

The water is everywhere. It rises from the ground in hot springs — some boiling, some merely warm, some barely hotter than blood. It flows in channels, natural and cut, that thread the town like veins. It collects in pools of all sizes, their surfaces filmed with minerals, algae, oil-rainbows. It falls as warm rain, frequent, almost hot, that hisses on hot metal and raises more steam. It condenses on every cool surface and runs back down. The water cycle is short and tight: sky to ground to spring to channel to pool to steam to sky. Nothing leaves the basin. The basin is a closed system. The water is the blood of the place.

The heat comes from below and from the machines. The earth's heat rises through cracks — fumaroles, hot springs, warm ground. The machines add their own: boilers, furnaces, engines, the waste heat of industry. The town is built around a geothermal field, and the industry is built around the town. Steam drives turbines. Turbines generate electricity. Electricity lights the town, powers pumps, runs machinery. The machines are iron and brass, old but maintained, their valves oiled, their pistons steady. They run at a constant speed. They have always run at a constant speed.

The sound is constant and loud. A low hum under everything — the turbines, the pumps, the boilers. Above that: the hiss of steam vents, the clank of metal, the rhythmic thump of pistons, the rattle of chains, the splash and gurgle of water. Above that: the buzz of insects, the cries of birds, the squeal of rats. The sound is a wall, a blanket. It does not vary. It is the town's heartbeat, and it never changes. You stop hearing it after a while, or rather you hear nothing else. The silence of the outside world becomes unimaginable.

The fauna is abundant. Insects rise in clouds from the warm water — midges, mosquitoes, flies, beetles, dragonflies. They are food for swallows and swifts that nest under the eaves and in the chimneys, their flight constant, their cries sharp. Crows and ravens pick at the refuse. Rats move in the walls, the channels, the pools' edges, swimming in the warm water, their fur wet and sleek. Bats take the insects at dusk — or what passes for dusk, the slight dimming when the machines cycle. In the warm pools: fish, crustaceans, amphibians, their movements slow in the heat. In the mud: worms, larvae, beetles. The fauna is stable. The populations do not grow or shrink. The swallows return to the same nests. The rats are neither more nor fewer than last year. The insects hatch at the same rate they die. The abundance is constant.

The vegetation is sparse. Reeds and rushes at the water's edge, thin and yellow-green. Mosses on the wet stone, dark green to black. Lichens on the dry rock of the rim, pale circles. A few hardy shrubs in the gravel, their leaves small and waxy. Thermophilic algae paint the hot pools in bands of green, yellow, orange. No trees inside the basin. Maybe one or two at the rim, stunted, wind-shaped. The ground is mostly bare: mud, clay, ash, gravel, paved stone, brick. The green is only at edges, in cracks, in water. The town does not garden. It does not farm. It takes its food from outside, and what grows here grows by accident.

The town's work is extraction and transformation. The geothermal field provides steam, heat, minerals. The water carries dissolved salts, sulfur, silica, metals. The factories evaporate the water in great shallow pans, harvesting salt, sulfur, borax, other minerals. They tan leather in hot mineral baths. They dye cloth in vats of hot liquor. They brew and distill. They fire bricks and ceramics in kilns fed by the earth's heat. They smelt and work metal. The products leave the basin on the road that climbs the rim. The raw materials enter the same way. The town is a throat, swallowing and excreting. The work is constant. The pans never cool. The vats never empty. The kilns never go out. The machines never stop.

The people are part of the machine. They tend the boilers, the pans, the vats, the kilns. They monitor the steam pressure, the water flow, the temperature. They keep everything at its set point. The equilibrium is their work. They do not innovate. They do not expand. They maintain. The town has been this size for generations. The machines have run at this speed for generations. The water has flowed at this rate for generations. The heat has been this heat for generations.

This is the rule of the place: nothing changes. The equilibrium is frozen. Not frozen in ice but frozen in balance — a steady state that consumes all disturbance and returns to itself. If a vent clogs, the pressure rises elsewhere; if a channel silts up, the water finds another path; if a machine slows, the others compensate. The system is homeostatic. The town is an organism of brick and iron and steam, and it maintains its own conditions. It has no history because it has no change. It has no future because it has no change. It is a snapshot of industry at perfect equilibrium, a moment that has lasted for generations and will last for generations more.

And it is not unhappy. The people do not dream of elsewhere. The heat is their heat. The noise is their noise. The dim glow is their light. The insects are their insects. The town is the world, and the world is the town. The sharp rim of the basin is the edge of existence. Beyond it, there is nothing, or nothing that matters. The town breathes, steams, hums, and remains.
