# The Underweald

The fissure opens and the world changes. You step through into a space that has never seen rain.

The air is cool — a steady, mineral cool, the temperature of deep stone. It is also extraordinarily dry. Not the dry of desert wind, but the dry of sealed spaces, of places where water has never existed in any form. Your skin notices first: the tightness across your knuckles, the catch in your throat. The air smells of hot rock and warm metal and something else — a dry, resinous, almost chemical organic scent, like heated epoxy and old paper.

The cavern is vast. The word "cavern" is insufficient — this is an underground region, a geography. The ceiling is lost in a warm haze somewhere above. The floor extends outward past the reach of sight, broken by formations, by clusters of construction, by open stretches of bare stone and growth. The scale is regional. You are standing in a valley-sized space, and there are others like it, connected by passages, by shafts, by corridors of raw rock.

The rock is young. This is the first thing you notice about the terrain: it is not weathered. The walls are fresh fractures, sharp-edged, crystalline, with none of the rounded softness that water or time imposes. Mineral veins run through the stone in bright, unoxidized lines — sulfides, salts, compounds that would have dissolved or degraded long ago in any wet world. The floor is a mosaic of broken stone and growth, with debris from recent collapses, from ongoing fracturing. The geology here is not settled. It is still happening. You can see new surfaces — stone that was inside the earth yesterday and is now exposed, raw, uncolonized. The cavern is still forming.

And where it has had time — where the stone has been exposed for weeks, for months — the growth has come.

It is not green. There is no chlorophyll here, no water-based photosynthesis, no plant life in any surface-world sense. What covers the surfaces is a biology built on a different chemistry entirely: silicate processing, sulfur cycling, the direct extraction of energy from mineral reactions and from the heat of the earth. The organisms grow as crusts, as mats, as sheets, as columns, as hanging curtains of dry, fibrous material. Their colors are mineral — gray-white, ochre, rust, a pale metallic sheen. They look like lichen reimagined by a geologist, like coral grown from ceramic, like fungus that has forgotten what moisture is.

The growth is the substrate. It carpets the floor in undulating mats that crunch underfoot with a dry, brittle sound. It climbs the walls in vertical gardens of crystalline formations, each one a slow accretion of mineral and organism, layer upon layer. It hangs from the ceiling in curtains and veils that shift in the air currents with a soft rustle, like paper, like cloth. In some places it has grown into structures that are almost architectural — arches, buttresses, columns — as if the organisms are trying to become the cavern's own anatomy.

This is the material of the world. The growth processes the rock, draws what it needs from it, and leaves behind a modified stone — a biological-mineral hybrid that is harder than the original rock, more complex, patterned with the history of its growth. The cavern is not just rock with life on it. The cavern and the life are the same thing, in the process of becoming each other.

And the growth is aware. Not conscious — nothing so dramatic — but responsive. It grows toward heat. It thickens around warmth: around machinery, around vents, around the bodies of animals. Walk across a mat and it will slowly, over hours, adjust its growth pattern to account for your passage — not retreating, but rerouting, sending new filaments along the path of your footsteps, as if the pressure and the warmth have told it something useful. The city has learned to use this. Paths are worn through the growth not by clearing it but by walking it — the organism incorporates the traffic, reinforces the route, builds up along the lines of movement until the path becomes a raised ridge, a natural road. The city's infrastructure and the growth's architecture are continuous. A wall is a place where the growth was encouraged to grow thick. A road is a place where it was encouraged to grow flat. A building is a place where it was encouraged to grow up.

The city is spread out. Districts of construction are separated by open stretches — voids of bare stone and growth-mat, places where nothing has been built, where the cavern is still just cavern. You can stand in one district and see the next as a distant cluster, a faint glow, a suggestion of geometry. The density is low. There is space here. Emptiness is part of the design, or part of the process — the city is not filling the cavern; it is growing into it, slowly, leaving room for what is not yet built.

And much of it is not yet built. The city has a frontier quality. Some districts are finished — dense with structures, with infrastructure, with the hum of industry. Others are half-built: frameworks of metal and growth-hybrid, open to the air, machinery parked and waiting. Others are just cleared: raw stone floors, exposed walls, the growth scraped back to make room for what comes next. The city is always in the middle of building itself. You can see the stages, laid out across the cavern like a diagram of construction: raw rock, cleared rock, prepared ground, foundation, framework, finished structure. The frontier moves outward, and the growth follows behind it, colonizing the new surfaces, beginning the slow transformation.

The industry is real and constant. Factories — low, sprawling complexes of metal and growth-hybrid — process the organisms: harvest, refine, extract, recombine. The machinery runs on geothermal heat, on chemical gradients, on the energy of the earth. Exhaust rises in thin streams from vents and stacks — not smoke, but warm gas, chemical effluent that the organisms nearby seem to welcome, their growth thickening around the outlets, feeding on the waste. The city's exhaust is the ecosystem's nutrient. The loop is closed. There is no pollution because there is no outside — everything is consumed, processed, incorporated back into the growth, back into the stone.

The city also digs. Excavation is continuous — machinery carving new space, opening new caverns, extending the frontier. The rock is raw and fresh where the diggers have been. The growth follows, colonizing new surfaces, beginning the slow process of transformation. The city follows the growth. The whole world is expanding, and the expansion is the industry.

The light is normal. That is the unexpected thing. It is not the dim red of emergency lighting or the harsh glare of industrial floodlights. It is warm, diffuse, slightly amber, filling the space without a single obvious source. Some of it comes from the growth — certain mats, certain structures carry a bioluminescence that is warm and steady, like sunlight through honey, like the glow of a hearth. Some of it comes from the industrial infrastructure: lamps on machinery, on towers, on the underside of overhangs, a grid of small warm lights that recede into the distance. And some of it comes from above — shafts and fissures in the cavern ceiling where the surface world leaks through, pale columns of natural light that catch the dust and make it visible, a slow, constant snow of mineral particles drifting down through the beams.

The sound is quiet. The vastness absorbs it. The growth absorbs it — those dry mats and curtains are acoustic dampeners, breaking up sound, swallowing echoes. You hear your own footsteps, the crunch of the growth underfoot. You hear the distant, muffled thrum of machinery, felt in the stone more than heard, a vibration that is part of the world's background hum. You hear the air moving through the hanging curtains, a soft rustle like paper, like wings. And sometimes, from somewhere in the distance, a single sound from the fauna — a click, a low chirp, a soft mechanical-sounding call — that hangs in the air for a moment and then is gone.

The animals are abundant. They are everywhere, but they are built for quiet. Flying things — membrane-winged, dry-winged, creatures with wings like leaves of thin leather — roost in the hanging curtains, hanging upside down in clusters, their bodies the color of the growth so they are nearly invisible until they move. They fly without sound, their wings making no beat, no flutter — they move through the air like they are part of it. Crawling things — multi-legged, armored, soft-footed — move across the mats with a ceaseless, quiet rustle, processing the growth as they go, leaving trails of disturbed crust behind them. Things that burrow into the growth itself, that live within it, that are part of its ecology — you see the evidence of them in the disturbed mats, in the small openings and tunnels, but rarely the animals themselves. They are present. They are numerous. They are silent.

Some of them are partly mineral. You see this when one crosses a patch of raw stone: a sheen on its carapace, a crystalline edge to a limb, as if the boundary between animal and growth is not fixed. The city's biologists have opinions about this. The city's engineers have applications for it. The animals themselves have no opinion. They simply live here, in the way that works.

The temperature is cool and constant. The geothermal vents and the industrial processes keep it from being cold, but there is no sun, no variation, no seasons. It is always the same: cool, dry, still. The organisms do not mind. They have never known water. Their chemistry runs on sulfur, on silicate, on the direct processing of mineral energy. They do not drink. They do not transpire. They do not wilt. They are not plants. They are something else — a biology that found a different way, that built a world without water and made it total.

And the world is still becoming. This is the rule of the place: it is not finished. The caverns are still forming — fresh fractures, new surfaces, stone that was inside the earth yesterday. The city is still building — new structures rising, new infrastructure being laid, the industrial complex expanding into virgin cavern. The ecosystem is still establishing — new growth colonizing new stone, new animals appearing, new relationships forming, the loop between industry and biology tightening. Nothing here is complete. Everything is in the process of becoming.

The city does not sit on the land. The land is not a substrate for the city. The rock, the growth, the machinery, the life — they are all part of the same process, the same ongoing act of creation. The cavern is not a container. The city is not a structure. The world is not a place. It is a process, and you are standing inside it, and it is still happening.
