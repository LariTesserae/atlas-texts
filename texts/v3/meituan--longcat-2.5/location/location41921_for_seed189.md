# The Hollow

You are inside something. The knowledge arrives before you can confirm it — the walls curve the wrong way for a cave, the air moves the wrong way for a room, and the light comes from everywhere and nowhere, the quality of a bright overcast day, the kind of light that makes colors true. But there is no sky. There is no sun. There is only the warmth of the walls and the steady, subsonic pulse you feel in your sternum before you hear it.

The walls are living tissue. Not wood — not the dead architecture of a tree — but something closer to the inside of a stem, the pale, dense flesh that grows and decides and is still deciding. The surface is smooth in some places, faintly ridged in others where vascular bundles run close beneath the skin. You can trace them with your fingertips: raised lines, slightly warmer than the surrounding tissue, carrying water and sugar in rhythms you can feel as much as hear. The color is the color of light through a leaf held to your eye — a warm gold-green, or a pale amber, or something that shifts when you are not looking directly at it, as if the tissue is slightly translucent and something is moving behind it.

The space is the size of a large room, or a small barn, or a cathedral nave shrunk to domestic scale. The ceiling is a rough vault, maybe three or four times your height, uneven where the tissue has grown in bursts. The floor slopes gently toward a low point in one corner, where water has gathered into a shallow pool that reflects the luminous walls in wavering, distorted shapes. The floor is ridged — fresh ridges, still sharp, where new growth has pushed up in the recent past. Some of the ridges run in parallel lines, like the grain of wood, or like something was dragged across the surface while it was still soft. The floor is warm. The whole space is warm — not hot, but the warmth of a body, of blood, of a room with the heating on low and the windows closed.

Water is everywhere and nowhere. It condenses on the upper surfaces — the ceiling, the upper walls — and runs down in thin streams that follow the vascular ridges, or gathers into droplets that fall with audible, irregular sounds. The dripping is constant, but it is not the dominant sound. The dominant sound is the circulation. The plant is moving water through itself at a scale that makes the movement audible — a deep, rhythmic thrumming, like standing inside a heart, or like being in a room where a massive pipe carries water under pressure. The sound has patterns. It speeds up and slows down. It has a pulse, and the pulse has variations, and the variations might be random or might be a language or might be the sound of the space dreaming.

The air is damp and thick and warm. Breathing it feels like breathing the air in a greenhouse, or in a greenhouse that is also a lung. There is a smell — green, sweet, slightly fermenting, the smell of sap and nectar and the faint, clean rot of plant matter breaking down and being rebuilt. The smell is stronger near the walls, where the tissue is actively growing, and fainter in the center of the space, where the air is still and the light is most even.

The space is mostly empty. This is the thing that strikes you after the walls, after the sound, after the warmth. The plant is total — everything you can touch is living tissue, everything that defines the space is plant — but the space itself is hollow. There is a lot of room. The distance between the few structures is large. You could walk for a long time without touching anything but the floor. The emptiness is not absence. It is volume. It is the space the plant has made for something, or the space that was there before the plant grew around it, or the space that is waiting.

There are structures. A shelf of tissue grows from one wall, flat on top, maybe waist-high, extending outward like a ledge or a table. Its surface is smooth, slightly concave, and there are marks on it — scratches, grooves, the kind of marks that come from repeated cutting or scraping. A bundle of fibers lies on the shelf, twisted into a rough rope, the ends frayed. A piece of hard tissue, darker than the walls, has been scraped into a blade — not sharp enough to shave with, but sharp enough to cut fiber, or to scrape the soft surface of the walls, or to shape something softer than itself. A container sits on the floor near the pool — a cup, or a bowl, made of layered leaves pressed and folded and somehow held together, the inside stained dark with something that might be water or might be sap or might be something else. These are simple things. They are the tools of someone who needed to cut something, or tie something, or carry something, and made what they needed from what was available. They are not the tools of a complex technology. They are hand tools. They are the first tools. They might be very old or they might be very new — the space is young, and the tools are young, and the marks on the shelf are fresh.

There are marks on the walls, too. Not the natural ridges of the vascular bundles, but other marks — grooves, scratches, patterns that might be accidental or might be deliberate. Some of them are low, near the floor, at the height of a hand or a knee. Some of them are higher, at the height of a shoulder or a head. Some of them form lines, or curves, or shapes that might be letters or might be maps or might be something else. The marks are not deep. The tissue has begun to grow over them, to smooth them, to heal them. But they are still visible. Someone was here. Someone worked here. Someone made the marks and the tools and the container, and then left, or stopped, or became part of the tissue.

The light is normal light. This is the strangest thing. The quality of it is like daylight, like the light in a room with good windows, the kind of light that makes you feel alert and calm and able to see clearly. But there are no windows. The light comes from the walls, or from the ceiling, or from the air itself. It is not directional. There are no hard shadows. The shadows are soft, indistinct, as if the light is coming from everywhere at once. The light does not fade at night, or if it fades, the night is still luminous, still readable, still the color of light through living tissue. The light changes, but the changes do not correspond to any cycle you can identify. It brightens and dims on timescales of minutes or hours, and the changes are smooth, and they do not match the pulse of the circulation, or they match it too well, or they match something else.

The space is young. You can see it in the fresh ridges on the floor, in the marks on the walls that have not yet been grown over, in the brightness of the tissue in places where the surface is new and the color is more vivid. The space is still being formed. The walls are still growing. There are places where the tissue is thinner, where you can see through it to a brightness beyond, a suggestion of space or sky or something that is not this space. There are places where the tissue is thicker, where the growth has been rapid, where the surface is still uneven and the color is still settling. The space is not finished. It is still deciding what it is.

The space is active. Water moves through the walls — you can see it sometimes, a dark line moving beneath the surface, a channel filling and emptying. The tissue grows — you can see it if you watch long enough, a slow swelling, a gradual change in the shape of a ridge or the surface of the wall. The light shifts. The temperature fluctuates slightly, warming and cooling in rhythms that might follow the circulation or might follow something else. The sound changes — the thrumming has variations, accelerations, pauses. The space is doing things. It is not a static environment. It is a process. It is alive in a way that a room is not alive, in a way that a cave is not alive, in a way that is not metaphorical. The space is metabolizing. The space is breathing. The space is thinking, or dreaming, or waiting.

There is life here, but it is rare. A small insect — something pale, something that lives in the tissue — moves across the wall and disappears into a crevice. A tendril, or a root hair, or something like a tentacle, retracts from the edge of the pool and is gone. A droplet moves against gravity, climbing the wall in a thin line, and is absorbed. Something small and fast moves across the floor, or seems to, or is a shadow, or is a trick of the light. The fauna is rare. The space is mostly plant, and the plant is mostly the space, and the animals are visitors or parasites or accidents. The ecology of the interior is simple: the plant makes sugar from light, or from something, and moves water through itself, and grows, and the things that live here live on the plant, or in the plant, or through the plant. There is no predator-prey dynamic that you can see. There is only the plant, and the water, and the light, and the slow, constant work of being alive.

The geometry is wrong. This is the reality breakdown, the thing that sits underneath everything else. The space is inside something — inside a plant, inside a seed, inside a gall, inside a hollow root, inside a space that was made by something that is not a plant — but the inside is larger than the outside should allow. The walls curve in ways that do not close. The floor slopes in directions that do not agree with each other. The distance between two points changes depending on how you measure it — by pacing, by sight, by the time it takes to walk. The space is a pocket. The space is a bubble. The space is a place where the rules are different, where the normal geometry of the world has been replaced by the geometry of living tissue, which is a geometry of growth and pressure and flow, not of angles and planes. You can walk in a straight line and end up where you started, or you can walk in a curve and end up somewhere you have never been. The space is not a room. The space is an organ. The space is a body. The space is a world.

And the light — the light is the deepest wrongness. The light is normal light, daylight, the light of the world outside, but there is no outside. The space is below the surface, inside the earth, inside the plant, inside the seed, and the light should not be here. The light comes through the walls, or from the walls, or from the air, and it is the light of a bright day, and it makes the space feel open and airy and real, but it is not real. It is the memory of light, or the idea of light, or the light of a different world, or the light that the plant makes because it needs light to live and the space needs light to exist and the rules of the world have been bent to provide it. The light is a lie that tells the truth. The light makes the space habitable. The light makes the space possible. The light is the first tool, the simplest technology, the thing that makes everything else work.

You stand in the center of the space, or what feels like the center, and you listen to the thrumming, and you feel the warmth, and you breathe the damp green air, and you look at the marks on the walls and the tools on the shelf and the pool in the corner and the light that comes from everywhere and nowhere, and you understand that this place is alive, and that it is young, and that it is still becoming something, and that you are inside it, and that it knows you are here, or does not know, or is beginning to know. The space is a hollow in a living thing. The space is a room in a body. The space is a world in a seed. The space is a tool that is still being made. The space is a question that has not yet been answered. The space is the Hollow, and it is warm, and it is loud, and it is empty, and it is full of plant, and it is yours, or you are its, or you are both, or neither, and the light is normal, and the light is impossible, and the light is all there is.
