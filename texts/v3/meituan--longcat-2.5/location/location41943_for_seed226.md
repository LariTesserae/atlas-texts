# The Valley of the Singing Ice

The glacier sings. It is not a voice that words could hold—it is a constant low hum, a drone you feel in your sternum before you hear it with your ears, the sound of a million tons of ice shifting on bedrock a few centimeters a day. Sometimes it booms: a crack like a mountain breaking, when a serac calves and crashes down onto the valley floor, and the echo rolls back and forth between the walls until it fades into the hum again. The people of the valley have lived with this sound for three generations. They no longer hear it. It is the silence of their world.

The valley is new. The ice withdrew—is withdrawing—and revealed it. The rock is raw: sharp-edged, unweathered, scratched with parallel grooves where the glacier dragged its teeth across it. Boulders the size of houses sit alone on the flat floor, dropped by the ice and left behind. The ground is ground stone and gravel, saturated with meltwater. Dig anywhere and water wells up, milky turquoise, thick with rock flour. It braids across the valley in streams that split and rejoin, that shift their courses after every storm, that have not yet decided where they want to go.

The valley is small. Two kilometers long, perhaps, and four hundred meters across. The glacier blocks the upper end—a wall of blue-white, fifty meters high, its base fringed with debris and meltwater caves. The sides are steep walls of raw rock, with snow in the cracks and small waterfalls that appear and disappear with the weather. The lower end is closed by a moraine, a ridge of till, with a stream cutting through a narrow gap. It is a room. A bowl. A place with edges. You can see the whole of it from almost anywhere, and the glacier is always in view.

The town has no name that outsiders use. The people call it simply the Settlement, or the Valley, as if there were no other. It is spread across the valley floor, on the slightly higher ground of an old lateral moraine. Perhaps eighty buildings, perhaps a hundred. They are low—one story, or two—with walls of dry-laid stone and roofs of turf. The turf is green with moss, deliberately planted or simply inevitable; from a distance, the buildings are lumps in the moss, indistinguishable from the hillside until you are close. The buildings are separated by open ground: gardens of sedge and moss, channels of running water, yards of stamped earth and gravel. The town is not dense. You can see across it. You can see the glacier from almost every doorway.

The streets follow the water. Channels—some cut by the people, some inherited from the meltwater—run between the buildings, and small bridges cross them: stone slabs, wooden planks, sometimes a single carved beam. Water wheels turn in the channels. They are horizontal wheels, simple, wooden, powered by the current; they drive trip hammers and grinding stones and polishing wheels in the workshops. The wheels creak. The hammers clang. The grinding stones rumble. These sounds mix with the water and the wind and the glacier's hum, and the town is never quiet.

At the center of the town is a natural amphitheater: a semicircular outcrop of rock, thirty meters across, that catches and holds sound. A person standing at the center can speak normally and be heard clearly at the edges. The rock reflects sound in a way that seems almost deliberate—a whisper at the center carries to the rim, and a sound at the rim focuses back to the center. The people gather here for meetings, for judgments, for stories, for music. Visitors often speak in whispers, or shout without meaning to, unnerved by the way their voices return to them. The people are used to it. They know that privacy is a lowland luxury. In the Valley, there are no secrets, only things not yet said loudly enough.

The valley is cool and wet. The temperature hovers around freezing at night and rises to perhaps ten degrees in the day. Rain is frequent—or rather, water is frequent: rain, drizzle, mist, condensation. Every surface is wet. The rock sweats. Water beads on the stone walls and runs down in thin sheets. The air is thick with moisture, and the smell is of wet stone, moss, and cold—a clean, mineral smell, with a faint sulfur note that comes from the warm spots.

The light is bright. The valley is high—above the tree line, or just at it. The air is thin and clear, and the ice reflects sunlight. Even on overcast days, the valley is luminous, the mist diffusing the light so that shadows are soft and colors are vivid: the turquoise of the water, the emerald and rust of the moss, the gray of the rock, the blue-white of the ice, the green of the turf roofs. At noon, when the sun is overhead, the valley is almost too bright to look at. At dusk, the ice glows pink and gold, and the mist turns the color of milk.

The vegetation is lush where there is water. Mosses form thick carpets—emerald, chartreuse, rust-red, almost black in the deepest wet. Lichens cover the rocks: cup lichens, beard lichens, crust lichens in orange and gray and green. Sedges and grasses in the marshes. Small flowers in the short summer: saxifrage, mountain avens, gentian, a tiny pink cushion plant that blooms for two weeks and then vanishes. Dwarf willows and birches in the sheltered spots, never taller than a person, their branches twisted by the wind. The vegetation is a sponge, a knee-deep layer of green that holds the water and softens the rock and makes the valley feel inhabited even where there are no people.

The animals are few. A species of snow finch nests in the rock walls and sings in the early morning—a thin, high song that is almost lost in the glacier's hum. A pipit runs in the moss. A raptor—a falcon, or perhaps a small eagle—circles overhead on the thermals, hunting the finches. Insects are more numerous: midges in the marshes, a few butterflies in the summer, beetles in the moss. A hardy mammal—a fox, or a mountain goat—is seen occasionally at the valley edges, passing through. Small fish live in the deeper pools, pale and cold-adapted. The people keep a few goats or sheep for milk and wool, but the valley cannot support many, and the animals are as scattered as the people.

The people are settlers. They came here three generations ago, when the ice was closer and the valley was smaller. They followed the retreating ice, or they were driven here, or they came seeking something—the stone, the solitude, the Voice. They build slowly. A house takes years: the stone is quarried and shaped with stone and copper tools, the walls are raised a course at a time, the turf is cut and layered on the roof. The town grows by a building or two a generation. The people think in long timescales. They carve stone that will outlast them by millennia. They mark the glacier's edge each year with a carved stone—a line of stones that shows the retreat, a calendar of ice, a record of the valley's growth. The oldest stones are nearest the current ice edge; the newest are further down the valley. The line is a history and a promise.

The people's craft is stone. The valley's rock is fine-grained and fresh, and it carves well. They make tools, vessels, ornaments, and trade them with lowland peoples for metal, for grain, for oil. Their carvings are distinctive: spirals, or the shapes of the valley's animals, or abstract patterns derived from the glacial striations. The water wheels power their forges and their polishing wheels, and the sound of the hammers is part of the town's voice.

The people's relationship with the ice is spiritual. The ice is alive, they believe, or it is a god, or it is the body of an ancestor. It is retreating, and they are following it. They make offerings at the edge: carved stones, or water, or milk, or the first flowers of summer. They have myths about the ice advancing and retreating—a cycle of ages, a breathing of the world. The Voice is its speech. They do not fear it. They listen. When the ice booms, they pause in their work and turn toward it, and some of them raise their hands, or bow their heads, or speak a word that is not quite a prayer.

The dead are given to the ice. When someone dies, their body is carried to the edge and placed in a meltwater cave, or laid on the rock at the base of the wall, for the glacier to take. The people believe that the dead become part of the ice, and that when the ice calves, the dead are released into the water, and the water carries them down the valley, through the gap in the moraine, and out into the world. The dead are not buried in the ground—the ground is too raw, too stony, too wet. They are given to the ice, and the ice gives them to the water, and the water gives them to the sky.

The valley has warm spots. The ground is hot in places—not enough to burn, but enough to keep the snow off in winter, enough to make the moss grow thick and bright green. Steam rises from cracks in the rock. There are pools of warm water, edged with orange and yellow minerals, where the people bathe and wash wool and keep their animals warm. The warm spots are respected, even sacred. The sulfur smell is strongest here, and the people believe that the warm spots are the ice's breath, or the earth's blood, or the place where the dead emerge from the water and rise into the air. A young person's coming-of-age ritual is to spend a night alone at a warm spot, listening to the steam and the glacier, and to return with a story or a song or a carving.

The moss grows in spirals around certain rocks. This is unusual. Elsewhere the moss grows in mats or cushions, but around these rocks it forms clockwise or counterclockwise spirals, as if following an invisible pattern. The spirals are subtle—you have to look for them—but once seen, they are unmistakable. The people have myths about this: the rocks are marked by the dead, or the moss is the ice's writing, or the spirals are the paths that the water takes underground. Some of the spiral rocks are used as landmarks, or as meeting places, or as the sites of offerings. A few of the town's carvings reproduce the spiral pattern, and it has become a kind of signature.

The valley is loud. The glacier hums and booms. The water rushes and drips. The wind funnels through the gap in the moraine and sings in the rock walls. The water wheels creak. The hammers clang. The people speak, sing, shout, argue, tell stories. The acoustics carry everything. There is no silence. The people sleep with the noise, work with the noise, think with the noise. A visitor finds it exhausting—the lack of quiet, the constant presence of sound. The people find silence—the silence of the lowlands, the silence of a closed room—unnerving. They say that silence is the sound of the world holding its breath, and that it means something is about to happen.

The valley is bounded. The only way out is through the gap in the moraine, and the path beyond is steep and dangerous, a scramble down a scree slope to a lower valley where there are trees and other people. The people do not leave often. They are not trapped—they could leave if they wanted—but the valley is their world. They know every rock, every pool, every warm spot. They have names for the boulders, for the channels, for the bends in the stream, for the spiral rocks. The valley is small enough to be known completely, and they know it completely.

The land is raw and dangerous. The rock is sharp. The ground is unstable—there are pockets of quicksand-like till, hidden pools, sudden drops. The ice calves unpredictably. The weather changes fast: a clear morning can become a whiteout by afternoon, and the wind can rise from nothing to a gale in an hour. The people are careful. They know the safe paths. They have rules: do not walk on the ice alone; do not dig in certain places; do not build below the old ice line; do not ignore the sound of the glacier changing pitch; do not go out in a whiteout without a rope. The rules are old, and they are kept.

The town is temporary, in a sense. The ice will continue to retreat, or it will advance again. The valley will change. The water will find new channels. The town will move, or it will be abandoned, or it will be buried. The people know this. They build anyway. They carve their stones. They mark the ice edge. They tell their stories. They live in the emerging land, at the edge of the ice, in the loud wet bright cold valley, and they are patient. They are the people of the new land, the people of the raw rock and the singing ice, and they will be here when the ice is gone, and they will be here when it returns.
