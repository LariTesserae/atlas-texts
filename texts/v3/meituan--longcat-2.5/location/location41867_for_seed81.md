The plain begins at the road's end, or what passes for a road — a compressed track of sinter gravel that holds the heat of the day long into the dim hours. You walk out of the last fold of high ground and the world opens into a basin so wide the far rim is only a darker line against a darker sky. The ground underfoot is sinter: layered, chalk-pale, faintly warm even through boot soles. It cracks in polygonal plates where it has cooled and contracted, and between the plates, thin threads of vapour rise. The air smells of sulfur and wet stone and something metallic, like coins held in a damp palm.

You do not so much see the city as detect it — a denser texture on the basin floor, a cluster of verticals where the plain had offered only horizontals. It sits in the lowest part of the depression, where the geothermal water tables rise to within a meter of the surface. From a distance it looks like a growth, a pale excrescence on the basin floor, and this is not entirely wrong.

**The city is called Vehl**, though the name is older than the current occupation and no one is certain what it meant originally. It is compact — perhaps two kilometers across at its widest — and dense in a way that the surrounding emptiness makes almost violent. Buildings press against buildings. Structures have been built on top of older structures, or carved into the flanks of sinter terraces that predate them, or grown around industrial works that predated both. The architecture is not a style but a accumulation: cast sinter blocks, imported timber long since mineral-encrusted into stone-like rigidity, sheet metal gone to rust-lace, pipework that has become load-bearing. Everything is damp. Everything weeps.

The water is the reason and the medium. It rises hot from the basin's geothermal plumbing — not boiling at the surface, but close, pressurized, saturated with dissolved silica and salts to the point of viscosity. It moves through the city in open channels, in pipes, in the very walls of certain districts where porous sinter acts as a wick. Wherever it meets a cooler surface it deposits its load: fine layers of pale mineral that build, over months and years, into crusts, curtains, rimstone dams, flowstone sheets. The city is being slowly, constantly grown over. In some alleys the accretion is so thick that the original walls are entombed, and the alley has become a corridor of flowstone with a street-level groove worn into it by foot traffic. In other places the deposits have been chipped away, or diverted, or harvested — the sinter is a material, after all, and the city both fights and farms its own burial.

**The industrial works** occupy the eastern quarter, where the ground is hottest and the water rises fastest. This is the reason Vehl exists. Great condensation towers stand in rows — not for cooling, but for capture: the hot mineral-laden steam is drawn up through packed beds of scrap sinter and cooled, and the water condenses out, and the minerals deposit, and the process repeats. The towers are forty meters tall, built of brick and iron and sheathed in accretion that has been partially trimmed into angular, almost architectural forms. Between them run heat exchangers the size of buildings, engine halls with roofs that pulse faintly with the rhythm of compressors, and pipe runs that cross the district at height, draped with mineral curtains where valves weep. The power is geothermal — steam-driven turbines, old but maintained, electrifying the grid that lights the city's dim hours and runs the pumps and the crushers and the brine evaporation works. The factories process the harvested sinter into refractory blocks, into mineral salts, into a fine pale powder that is packed into sealed containers and carried away on the occasional transport that crawls out across the plain on the sinter road. What the powder is for, and where it goes, is not discussed by the workers. It is enough that it leaves.

The city's water is managed in zones. The hottest channels run through the industrial quarter and are not for human contact. Cooler distributaries branch west and north into the residential districts, where the water is still warm enough to keep the streets free of the mineral crust's sharpest edges — the constant damp softens the sinter, rounds it, makes it walkable. In the oldest quarter, near the basin's lowest point, the water has been pooled into a series of stepped basins, each one a different temperature, each rimmed with sinter dams that have grown into organic, flowing shapes — like frozen waterfalls, like the interiors of shells. These pools are the city's center in a way the industrial works are not. People soak in the cooler ones. The hottest are used for cooking, for washing, for dissolving mineral crusts from tools. Steam rises from them constantly, and in the dim light they glow faintly with the heat of the water.

**The light is the strangest thing.** The basin sits at high elevation, and the sky above it is often clear, but the city itself lives in a permanent twilight. The steam and haze rising from the geothermal field meet the cooler air of the altitude and condense into a layer that sits at the level of the towers' mid-heights, diffusing everything. Direct sun is rare. Instead there is a silvery-grey luminosity that comes from everywhere and nowhere, that casts no shadows, that makes the sinter glow like old bone. At certain hours, when the wind shifts and the haze thins momentarily, the light breaks into strange effects: halos around the sun, light pillars rising from the vents, brief rainbows in the steam. The inhabitants measure time by these events. The industrial district has artificial light — electric lamps on tall poles, harsh and white — but the residential quarters mostly do not, and the old quarter is lit only by the glow of the pools and the faint phosphorescence of certain mineral deposits that hold the day's heat and release it as a dull, warm light through the dim hours. The overall effect is of a city underwater, or underground, or existing in the last hour of a dusk that never quite ends.

**Sound is sparse.** The dominant noise is the hiss of steam — not loud, but omnipresent, a white-noise wash that fills the basin. Under it, the dripping of condensate from every overhang, every pipe, every sinter ledge. The occasional deep clang of a heat exchanger cycling. The distant, irregular percussion of the crusher in the mineral works. The creak of sinter cooling, which sounds almost organic — a slow, fibrous cracking, like ice on a lake but warmer. Voices carry strangely in the haze, muffled and directionless. The city is not quiet in the sense of silence, but it is quiet in the sense that nothing breaks the steam-hiss except other small sounds, and the overall impression is of a place holding its breath.

**Beyond the city**, the plain extends. This is the parameter that defines everything: the emptiness. The sinter crust runs to the horizon in every direction, broken only by the occasional fumarole cone — a low mound of pale mineral with a vent at its summit, breathing steam — and by the scattered remnants of older workings. There are the remains of extraction sites, perhaps centuries old, where sinter was quarried or where wells were sunk into the geothermal plumbing. These are slowly being reclaimed by accretion; in another few hundred years they will be indistinguishable from natural formations. There are a few standing structures on the plain — watchtowers, shrines, relay stations for the road — but they are kilometers apart, and between them there is nothing. No vegetation. No soil. No animal trails. The sinter plain is too hot, too mineral, too unstable to support anything but the simplest chemistry. The sky above it is empty of birds except in the rare moments when something — a high-flying insect, a wind-borne spore — crosses from one distant thermal to another. The sense of space is immense and uncanny. The city is the only concentration of matter and effort for fifty kilometers in any direction, and it shows. People who go out onto the plain — to service a fumarole, to inspect a relay, to walk the road — speak of the silence and the flatness and the way the horizon seems to curve up at the edges, as if the basin were a bowl and they were walking toward its rim.

**Life is marginal.** In the hottest pools, where the water is nearly at boiling and the mineral load is highest, there are mats — not algae, not anything with a name in the old taxonomies, but thin films of thermophilic organisms that tolerate what nothing else can. They are orange and grey and faintly iridescent, and they grow in the pools' centers where the water is too hot for even the mineral-tolerant invertebrates that live at the margins. These invertebrates — small, pale, many-legged things that resemble neither insects nor crustaceans clearly — are the only fauna most inhabitants ever see. They cluster at the edges of the cooler pools, in the damp sinter crevices, feeding on the microbial films. Occasionally something larger moves: a skittering form the size of a cat, mineral-dusted, that may be a predator or a scavenger or something else entirely. It is seen rarely and described inconsistently. The inhabitants do not concern themselves with it. There is no vegetation in the ordinary sense — no plants, no fungi visible to the naked eye, no mosses even in the dampest, coolest corners. The world is mineral and microbial and otherwise empty.

**The rules of Vehl** are the rules of the accretion. Nothing is permanent, but nothing is lost. The city is constantly being buried and constantly being excavated, and the balance between these processes is the city's true governance. New construction happens on the upper terraces, where the sinter is cooler and the deposits build more slowly. Old structures sink — not dramatically, but over decades — as the ground rises around them. The founders' quarter, at the basin's lowest point, is now two stories below the current street level, accessible by stairways cut through the sinter that were once open to the sky. The inhabitants have made a peace with this. They build in materials that can be sacrificed — timber, soft stone, metal that will rust and be replaced — and they maintain the critical infrastructure, the pipes and channels and pumps, with a vigilance that borders on religious observance. A blocked channel in Vehl is not an inconvenience; it is a flood, a burial, a slow catastrophe. The water must be kept moving, kept warm, kept from pooling where it will deposit too fast and entomb a district.

There are places in the city where the accretion has been allowed to proceed unchecked — deliberately, as experiment or offering or simply because the district was abandoned. These zones are strange and beautiful: caverns of flowstone where streets once ran, curtains of sinter in pale bands of white and ochre and rust-red, rimstone pools that have merged into tiered lakes, all of it damp and warm and faintly luminous with condensate. The inhabitants call them the Grown Places and avoid them, not from fear but from a kind of respect. They are the city's future, or its past, or its subconscious. Children dare each other to enter. Sometimes they do not return for hours, and when they do they are mineral-dusted and silent and will not say what they saw.

**What is strange** — truly strange, beyond the strangeness of any settlement — is the sense that the city is not merely in the geothermal system but part of it. The pipes and channels and pumps are a circulatory system; the condensation towers are organs; the accretion is metabolism. The inhabitants are cells in a body that is slow and warm and largely unconscious. They maintain the works not because they choose to but because they are the works' maintenance mechanism, the way the body's cells maintain the body. This is not a metaphor the inhabitants would use. They speak of the works as machines, as infrastructure, as the reason for Vehl's existence. But out on the plain, in the silence and the flatness, with the steam-hiss fading behind you and the sinter cracking under your boots, you can feel it: the basin is alive in some mineral, thermal, unhurried sense, and the city is its concentrated thought, its one dense place of memory and intention, growing and being grown, building and being buried, warm and wet and dim and old, the only thing for fifty kilometers that has a name.
