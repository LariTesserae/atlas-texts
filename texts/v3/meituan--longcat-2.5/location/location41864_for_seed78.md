The plateau is called the Carpet by the people who live on it, and the name is accurate and insufficient. It is a continent of low, dense vegetation—mosses, lichens, liverworts, cushion plants, scale-leaved shrubs, stunted conifers that grow sideways along the ground rather than up—stretched across a recently uplifted plain at the roof of the world. The mat is the color of deep water and old iron: dark greens, olive, rust-brown, black. In the dim light it reads as a single dark fabric, unbroken, rolling to every horizon. There is no bare ground. The mat covers the soil, the rock, the cliffs, the ruins, the bones. It is the substrate, the architecture, the building material, the food, the medicine, the god.

The plateau is high. The air is thin and cold. The temperature hovers around freezing for most of the year, and in the long winter it drops so far below that the mat stiffens and the streams freeze and the people huddle in their stone shelters and burn the dried stems of cushion plants and wait. The cold is not a presence; it is an absence, a withdrawal, a slowing. The people move slowly. The animals move slowly. The mat grows slowly—a millimeter a year, perhaps less. Everything on the plateau is in no hurry.

The light is dim. A permanent ceiling of cloud—thick, low, gray-white, turbulent—presses down on the plateau. The sun is a brightening somewhere above the cloud, never a disk, never a direct beam. The light is diffuse, blue-gray, shadowless. It is like being inside a room with the curtains drawn. At the darkest time of year the cloud thickens and the world goes to a deep gray, and then to a gray so dark it is almost black, but never fully black—there is always some light, from the clouds themselves, from the faint bioluminescence of the lichens, from the mat where it is thickest and oldest. The people do not have a word for night. They have a word for the time when the gray is thin and the world is lighter, and a word for the time when the gray is thick and the world is darker, and they measure their days by the quality of the gray.

Water is everywhere and nowhere. It falls as rain perhaps a hundred days a year—not heavy rain, but a persistent, fine, cold drizzle that soaks the mat and runs off in streams and fills the pools in the depressions. The streams are loud and numerous, pouring down the sharp valleys, over the fresh rock, in waterfalls and rapids that echo off the cliffs. There are warm pools too, heated by geothermal vents far below—places where the mat is thin and the rock is bare and the water steams in the cold air. The people use these pools. They wash in them. They soak their joints in them. They believe the warm pools are the places where the plateau breathes. But most of the water is in the air—the clouds, the fog, the condensation that forms on every surface and drips from the mat in a constant, soft percussion. The mat is always damp. You can wring water from it. Your clothing is always wet. The sound of dripping is the background of everything.

The smell is of earth and water and decay and something sharp and resinous—the cushion plants have leaves like scales and they exude a bitter oil that gets into everything, your hands, your clothing, your food. The streams smell of stone and cold. The warm pools smell of sulfur and something older, something mineral and deep. The shelters smell of smoke and fur and unwashed bodies and the sweet rot of the mat that roofs them. The air itself smells of nothing—it is too cold and too thin to carry much scent, except when the wind shifts and brings the smell of the mat or the water or the animals in a sudden, overwhelming rush.

The sound is the defining quality of the plateau. It is loud. The wind howls across the open mat—there are no trees to break it, no hills to shelter behind, only the low, dense fabric of the vegetation, and the wind roars over it like a river over a flat stone. The wind carries every other sound with it: the streams, the birds, the animals, the creak and settle of the mat, the rumble of the plateau still rising, the crack of rocks settling, the hiss of steam from a vent. The birds are loudest—there are thousands of them, small dark fast-flying things that nest in the mat and call to each other across the vastness, their voices carrying in the thin air and echoing off the cliffs. The mammals are quieter but constant—the scraping of hooves on rock, the rustle of bodies moving through the mat, the grunts and barks of alarm. The people are quiet. They have learned to move without sound, to speak in low voices, to listen. But they cannot escape the sound of the plateau. It is always there. It is the sound of the mat and the wind and the water and the life, and it is loud.

The density of things is very low. You can stand on the mat and see nothing but mat in every direction—no trees, no rocks, no animals, no structures. The horizon is a line where the dark mat meets the lighter sky. The distance is immense. The plateau is thousands of kilometers across—a continent, a world. The people do not know its full extent. They know the region around their shelters, the paths between the warm pools, the places where the herds graze, the places where the birds nest. Beyond that there are stories—of a forest of stone, of a lake that never freezes, of a place where the mat is white instead of black, of a place where the plateau ends and the world falls away. These stories may be true. No one has gone to see and returned. The plateau has its own weather—clouds form over the eastern edge, rain falls in the south, fog rolls in from the west, and the wind is different in every region. There are places on the plateau where the mat is meters deep and places where it is a thin crust on bare rock. There are places where the streams converge into rivers and places where the water disappears into the ground and does not return. There are places where the geothermal vents are so numerous that the air is warm and wet and the mat grows in strange, lush forms. The people know a fraction of this. They know their own region, and they know the stories, and they know that the plateau is larger than they can comprehend.

The features are scattered and faint. There are rock outcrops—dark stone rising a few meters above the mat, shaped by frost and wind into sharp, angular forms. There are cliffs—the edges of the plateau, or the walls of valleys, vertical and fresh and cracked. There are valleys—V-shaped, steep-sided, cut by the streams, their floors hidden by the mat and the fog. There are geothermal vents—places where the ground is warm and the mat is thin and the rock is bare and the steam rises in columns that the wind tears apart. These features are separated by vast stretches of empty mat. You could walk for days and see nothing but the mat and the sky and the sound. The emptiness is profound. It is not barren—it is full of life, but the life is low and uniform and spread thin. The density of discrete objects is near zero. The space between presences is vast.

And yet the fauna is abundant. There are animals everywhere—in the mat, under the mat, above the mat. Small mammals—rodent-like things with thick fur and short ears and slow movements—burrow in the mat and emerge to feed on the plants. Larger mammals—herd animals with shaggy coats and broad hooves—graze on the cushion plants and move across the plateau in slow migrations, following the growth of the mat and the melting of the snow. Predators follow them—large birds with dark plumage and wide wingspans that circle above the plateau on the thermals from the geothermal vents, and smaller, mammalian predators that hunt in the mat. Insects are numerous—they live in the mat, they emerge in clouds when the temperature rises slightly, they are food for the birds and the small mammals. The animals are not visible most of the time—they are hidden in the mat, or they are far away—but they are there, and their signs are everywhere: tracks in the soft mat, droppings, feathers, bones, the sound of movement in the vegetation, the sudden eruption of birds from the mat as you approach.

The people are scattered and few. They live in small groups—a dozen individuals, perhaps two dozen—in shelters made of stone and the wood of the stunted conifers, roofed with thick mats of vegetation. The shelters are low and dark and warm. They are built against rock outcrops, or in the lee of cliffs, or in shallow depressions in the mat. They are smoky inside—the fires burn constantly, fed by dried cushion-plant stems and the bones of old trees. The people are stocky and slow-moving, with thick frames and small extremities and a tolerance for cold that would kill a lowlander. They wear layers of fur and woven plant fiber. They carry stone tools—scrapers, blades, points—and a few metal objects, copper or bronze, passed down through generations and used with great care. They make baskets from the stems of the cushion plants, rope from the fibers of the mat, clothing from the fur of the mammals. They build cairns to mark paths across the featureless mat—stacks of stones that rise slightly above the vegetation, visible from a distance. The cairns are old. Some have fallen. Some have been rebuilt. They are the only marks of human presence on the plateau, and they are faint.

There are ruins too. Not many, and not large, but they are there—a wall of fitted stone half-sunk in the mat, a circle of standing stones on a ridge, a pit lined with clay that holds water long after the rain has passed. The people do not build like this anymore. They do not know how. They find these places and use them and wonder about them, and the old people tell stories about the people who built them—people who were taller, or smarter, or more numerous, or who came from somewhere else, or who were the first, or who were not people at all. The stories contradict each other. The ruins are older than the stories. The mat grows over them slowly, and in a few hundred years they will be gone, and the plateau will be only mat and sky and sound.

The people follow the herds. They move across the plateau in a slow circuit, from the warm pools to the grazing grounds to the nesting cliffs and back again. They hunt the mammals with throwing sticks and stone-tipped spears. They gather the plants—the cushion plants are edible, and the lichens, and the inner bark of the stunted conifers. They fish in the streams with baskets and nets woven from plant fiber. They use the mat for everything—for bedding, for roofing, for insulation, for medicine. They know the mat intimately. They know which plants grow where, which plants are edible, which plants are medicinal, which plants are dangerous. They know the moods of the plateau—where the fog is thickest, where the wind is strongest, where the ground is unstable, where the warm pools are hottest. They have no written language. They have stories—long, detailed, precise stories about the plateau and its features and its moods—and they tell these stories to their children, and the children tell them to their children, and the stories are the map.

The plateau is young. It was not here—or it was not this high—a few thousand years ago. The rock is fresh, the features are sharp, the erosion has barely begun. The cliffs are vertical, the valleys are V-shaped, the ridges are knife-edged. The plateau is still rising—slowly, imperceptibly, but measurably. The old people say that in their grandparents' time the warm pools were lower, and the streams were shorter, and the cliffs were smaller. The instruments of the people—simple things, marked sticks, strings of beads, notches on stone—show that the land is higher than it was. The streams are cutting down. The cliffs are cracking. The geothermal vents are shifting. The landscape is still being born.

And it is active. The weather cycles—clouds form over the eastern edge, rain falls, the streams rise, the fog rolls in from the west, the wind shifts and the temperature drops and the mat stiffens and the animals move to the lee of the cliffs. The biological pace is constant—the mat grows, the animals move, the predators hunt, the plants flower and set seed in the brief warm periods, the insects emerge and are eaten and emerge again. The geological processes are slow but present—the plateau rises, the rocks fall, the vents hiss, the earth trembles. The people move with the herds, they build and rebuild their shelters, they tell stories, they die and are buried in the mat. The plateau is a living system, and it is in constant motion.

The weirdness is in the mat. It is not quite a normal ecosystem. The mat is too dense, too uniform, too interconnected. It behaves like a single organism in some ways—it responds to damage by growing faster, it shares nutrients between distant parts, it produces chemicals that affect the animals that eat it. The people say the mat is aware, that it responds to their presence, that it moves toward them when they sleep. This is probably superstition. But the mat does glow faintly in the dark—bioluminescence from certain lichens, or from the mat itself—and the glow is not random. It forms patterns, slowly shifting, like something breathing, like something dreaming. And the mat does produce sounds—a low hum, below the threshold of hearing, that you feel in your chest rather than hear with your ears, a sound that rises and falls with the wind and the water and the life. And the animals behave strangely around the mat—they avoid certain areas, they circle certain spots, they refuse to eat certain plants, and the people have learned to read these signs and to avoid those areas too. The plateau is not a normal place. It is unusual. It is not quite of this world.
