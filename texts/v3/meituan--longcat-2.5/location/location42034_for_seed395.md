**The Salwood**

A name for a place that has no people to give it one. But stand on the rim of the low, warm basin and look out over the white expanse, and the name will come to you anyway. The Salwood. Not a wood of trees, but a wood of salt and living mat — a vast, low, packed, biological city of evaporite and turf, a continental pan that has become a reef, a reef that has become a settlement, a settlement that is still being born.

---

The basin is young. The rim is a low escarpment of raw, unweathered rock, the color of old bone, the edge of a plateau that has only recently begun to shed its sediment into the depression below. The depression itself is an evaporite — a salt pan in the earliest stage of its life. The salt is still coming into being. In the lowest places the crust is soft, plastic, still precipitating from a brine that rises from below and evaporates above. The ground is not yet fully ground. It is pre-formation, embryonic, raw. You can watch it grow: faint white filaments pushing up through the crust, the surface dimpling as gas escapes, the crust cracking and healing in the same hour. The basin is still deciding what it will be. The rim is still eroding into it. The salt is still rising. Everything is under construction, and the construction crew is the place itself.

And on this young salt, the mat has begun.

---

The mat is the vegetation of the Salwood, though it is not a plant. It is a continuous, packed, layered biological crust — a turf of halophytic organisms that metabolize the salt itself. From the rim it looks like a white-green carpet, a vast low prairie of pale, fleshy growth, an unbroken sheet of life that fills the basin from wall to wall. Up close it is a city.

The mat rises in mounds and ridges, in towers and spires of crystallized biomass and salt. The towers are the mat's own architecture — places where the organisms have trapped salt, where the salt has crystallized and pushed the living layer upward, where the mat has grown around its own waste. They are the color of old ivory, of bone, of dried salt. They are porous, pitted, honeycombed with chambers and galleries. Some are a meter tall, some three or four meters, and in the oldest districts they cluster like a skyline — a dense, packed, layered construction of living salt. The towers are the city's high-rises, its cathedrals, its landmark districts. Each one is different: some are squat and broad, some are slender and fluted, some lean together like old friends, some stand alone like sentinels. Their surfaces are ridged and banded, recording the growth of years, the wet years and the dry, the years of high humidity and the years of drought. A tower is a history book. A district is a library.

Between the towers run the avenues — low, winding channels where the nightly brine flows and where the grazers move. The avenues are the mat's streets, its infrastructure, its vascular system. They carry the moisture that the mat pulls from the air; they carry the nutrients; they carry the slow pulse of the living crust. Some are broad and straight, some are narrow and meandering, some branch and rejoin like a delta. They are never empty. There is always something moving in them — a herd of grazers, a column of burrowers, a predator on patrol, a drift of salt dust, a thin film of brine catching the light.

The mat is layered. The top layer is alive — a few centimeters of pale, gelatinous, salt-saturated tissue, the color of a brine shrimp, of a lichen, of a mushroom's underside. It is soft, yielding, springy underfoot. It smells sharp and mineral and organic, like a tide pool, like a salt marsh, like a mushroom cellar. Below that is the dead layer — a thick, compacted stratum of old mat, of salt, of the cast-off skeletons of the organisms, the color of chalk, of old paper, of dried mud. It is hard, crunchy, a substrate that the living layer is always growing over and the burrowers are always tunneling through. Below that is the salt — the raw, young evaporite, the base of the whole city, still crystallizing, still rising, still pushing the mat up as it grows. The whole thing is a three-layer cake of life and death and mineral, and it is growing, always growing, the living layer creeping forward over the bare salt at the edges, the dead layer thickening, the salt rising to meet it.

The mat is packed. There is no bare ground. The living layer is continuous, a single unbroken sheet of biomass that covers the basin floor from rim to rim. The towers are packed together. The avenues are packed with grazers. The burrows are packed with fauna. The whole place is saturated with life, with structure, with the slow constant work of growth and crystallization and tunneling and grazing. It is a city without a builder, a settlement without settlers, a reef without a reef-builder — or rather, the reef-builder is the reef itself, the mat, the collective, the biological whole. The city is the organism. The organism is the city.

And the mat is lush. It is warm, wet in its own way, productive. The basin is low and warm, and the air is heavy with the moisture that the salt pulls from it. Every night the temperature drops, the relative humidity rises, and the hygroscopic salts in the mat absorb the moisture from the air, forming a brine that the organisms drink. The mat swells. It softens. It becomes slick and glistening. The avenues fill with a thin film of brine. The chambers drip. The towers weep faint trails of moisture down their flanks. By day the sun is hot, the air is dry, and the brine evaporates, and the salt recrystallizes, and the mat shrinks and hardens and cracks. But the organisms are waiting, dormant, and the cycle begins again. It is a daily pulse, a breath, a slow heartbeat. The mat is always either swelling or shrinking, always either drinking or waiting. It is a living thing on a continental scale, and its metabolism is the metabolism of the whole basin.

---

The fauna of the Salwood are abundant. They are the citizens of the living city, the inquilines of the mat, the grazers and burrowers and predators that make the city their own.

The grazers are the most visible. They are small, pale, padded-footed creatures — mammals, mostly, rodent-like, hare-like, the color of the salt, the color of the mat. They move in herds along the avenues, cropping the living layer with their broad flat teeth, their lips and noses protected by thick keratinous plates. They are silent, or nearly so — a soft pad of feet, a faint rustle of mat, the occasional low throaty call that carries only a few meters in the heavy air. They are the city's livestock, its foragers, its constant quiet presence. They are never far from the towers, which give them shade and shelter. They are the reason the mat is cropped, the reason the avenues are clear, the reason the city has streets. A herd will work an avenue for a day, cropping it down to the dead layer, then move on, and the living layer will creep back within a week. The grazers are the city's lawnmowers, its pruners, its gardeners. They keep the avenues open. They keep the mat young.

The burrowers are the city's engineers. They are the creatures that tunnel through the mat and the salt, that excavate the chambers and galleries, that aerate the crust, that create the voids in which the moisture condenses. They are small, many-legged, armored — crabs, insects, the salt-white analogues of earthworms and termites. They work in the dark, in the wet, in the salt. They are the reason the towers are porous, the reason the avenues are connected, the reason the city has a subsurface, a foundation, a hidden infrastructure. They are the city's miners, its plumbers, its unseen workforce. When you walk on the mat you feel them beneath your feet — a faint constant tremor, the soft collapse of a gallery, the rustle of a thousand small bodies moving in the dark. They are the most numerous animals in the Salwood, and the least seen. They are the city's subconscious, its underground mind, its buried hands.

The predators are the city's police, its regulators, its necessary violence. They are the salt foxes, the pan snakes, the white hawks that hover on the thermal low. They are pale, fast, patient. They hunt the grazers and the burrowers and each other. They are the reason the grazers are silent, the reason the burrowers are hidden, the reason the city is quiet. A predator's strike is a sudden sharp sound — the snap of a jaw, the rustle of a body, the brief high cry of a kill. Then silence again. The predators keep the city in balance, keep the grazers moving, keep the burrowers working, keep the mat from being overcropped. They are the city's rhythm, its pulse, its active edge. A salt fox will patrol the same district for days, learning the avenues, learning the herds, learning the towers. It knows the city better than any grazer. It is the city's memory, its attention, its sharp tooth.

And there are the others — the filterers that strain the nightly brine for microorganisms, the scavengers that eat the dead mat and the fallen towers, the hoverers that drift on the warm air, the skitterers that live on the surface of the mat and never touch the ground. The city is full of them, packed with them, saturated with them. Every niche is filled. Every resource is used. The mat feeds the grazers, the grazers feed the predators, the predators feed the scavengers, the scavengers feed the mat. The salt is the skeleton, the mat is the flesh, the fauna are the motion. It is a complete ecosystem, a closed loop, a living city that has no waste. Even the towers, when they fall, are eaten. Even the salt, when it dissolves, is drunk. Nothing is lost. Everything is passed along.

---

The atmosphere of the Salwood is warm, still, and heavy. The basin is low, and the air is dense, and the salt haze hangs in it like a veil. The haze is fine salt dust, kicked up by the nightly winds and the daily thermals, suspended in the air, scattering the light. The sky is pale, a washed-out blue, a brassy white near the horizon. The sun is bright, but the haze softens it, diffuses it, makes it a warm even glow. The shadows are short and soft. The light is normal — not dim, not blinding — just the clear warm light of a dry, low, salt-hazed world. The air tastes of salt, of minerals, of a faint metallic bitterness. It coats the tongue. It dries the lips. It is the taste of the place itself.

The basin has its own weather. The scale is vast, and the vastness makes its own physics. By day the salt and the mat heat the air, and the air rises, and a thermal low forms over the center of the basin. The low draws air in from the rim, from the plateau, from the distant cooler highlands. The air moves slowly, heavily, a warm dry wind that hisses softly over the mat. It carries the salt dust. It carries the smell of the mat. It shapes the city: it piles the loose salt into low dunes, it sculpts the towers into streamlined forms, it clears the avenues of debris. By night the thermal low reverses. The salt and the mat cool, and the air sinks, and a high forms over the center. The wind dies. The air is still. The moisture condenses. The mat drinks. The cycle turns. It is a daily weather, a breath of wind and a long exhalation of stillness. The city has its own climate, its own wind patterns, its own nutrient cycles. The center of the basin is different from the edge. The old districts are different from the new. The city has neighborhoods, quarters, each with its own character, its own fauna, its own towers.

---

And the sounds. The Salwood is quiet. The vastness absorbs sound. The soft mat muffles it. The haze diffuses it. The only sounds are the soft ones — the crackle of salt crystallizing, the rustle of the mat swelling, the pad of grazer feet, the drip of condensation in a chamber, the low hum of insects, the occasional hiss of the wind. A vast living stillness. A city that breathes but does not speak. A place where the loudest sound is the sound of your own footsteps, and even that is soft, swallowed, gone. Stand still in an avenue and you can hear the mat: a faint, constant, granular sound, like sand settling, like a distant fire, like the slow grinding of a thousand tiny teeth. It is the sound of the city eating itself and growing. It is the sound of the salt rising. It is the sound of the place becoming.

---

The light at night is brilliant. No water vapor, no haze, no light. The stars are sharp. The Milky Way is a bright river. The moon is a white coin on the white salt. The towers cast long, sharp shadows. The mat glows faintly with its own slow metabolism — a pale luminescence, the color of a foxfire, of a glow-worm, of a brine shrimp's glow. It is not bright, but it is there, a living light in the living city. The predators' eyes shine in it. The grazers move in it, silent, pale, ghostly. The city is never dark. It is always faintly lit — by the stars, by the moon, by itself.

---

The rules of the Salwood are the rules of salt and life. There is no free water. The water is in the salt, in the air, in the mat, in the brine. The organisms use hygroscopic salts — calcium chloride, magnesium chloride, the bitter salts of the evaporite — to pull moisture from the air. The salt is both the problem and the solution: it is the desert that the life must survive, and it is the sponge that holds the moisture that the life needs. The mat builds the ground as it lives. It traps the salt, it precipitates it, it pushes it up into towers. The ground is growing. The city is growing. The basin is filling. The salt is rising. Everything is emerging, becoming, coming into being. It is a young world, a raw world, a world that is still under construction — and the construction crew is the mat, the fauna, the salt itself.

And it is vast. You can walk for days and never leave it. The rim is a faint line on the horizon, a low pale escarpment that never gets closer. The towers repeat. The avenues repeat. The herds repeat. The predators repeat. The scale is continental. The scale is planetary. The scale is its own physics. The mat is so vast that it has its own climate, its own winds, its own nutrient cycles. It is a world in itself — a planet of salt and life, a single continuous packed living city that covers a continent.

This is the Salwood. A low, warm, dry, vast, packed, emerging, biological city of salt and turf and fauna. A place where the ground is alive, where the city is a reef, where the desert is a garden, where the silence is full of soft constant living sound. A place that is not weird, not magical, not impossible — just an extreme, an exaggeration, a scaling-up of the real. A salt flat that became a reef. A reef that became a city. A city that is still being born.
