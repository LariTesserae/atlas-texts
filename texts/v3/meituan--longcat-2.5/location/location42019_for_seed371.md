# The Throat

The mountain is young and this wound in it is younger — twelve, maybe fifteen years since the summit blew out its plug and the peak decided to breathe. You cross a ridge of bare, rust-red rock, no soil anywhere, scree sharp as slag under your boots. The ground is warm in patches. Then the ridge ends, and the sound arrives before the sight: a roar so total it has weight, a pressure against the chest, the way a factory floor feels from the floor itself.

Nothing was built here. That is the first strange thing, and then you see the second.

---

### The Pit

It is a shaft, roughly elliptical, a hundred and eighty meters across at the lip, walled in fresh-broken rock that still shows glassy fracture faces — no rounding, no weathering, no soil. Everything is sharp. The walls drop two hundred meters into fog, terraced with mineral ledges, fluted with vertical chimneys, overhung in places by lips of glazed sinter that jut like congealed slag. The bottom is never fully visible; it hides behind layers of steam.

Above, the opening is a jagged oval of deep, thin sky. The sun, when it comes at all, enters only near midday as a single narrow blade that sweeps the east wall for perhaps twenty minutes and then is gone. The rest of the time the pit is dark — not night-dark, which has stars, but a textured mineral dark, shapes known by the heat on your face and by sound bouncing off them. The walls sweat. Everything glistens in gradations of black.

It is room-sized, cathedral-sized. From the entry ledge you can take the whole of it in at a glance. Distance is readable. The fog hides the floor so it feels larger than it is, but it is a single chamber, a single feature — a world with clear boundaries.

---

### The Works

The floor is a field of pools, and the pools are machines with no builders.

Some boil in slow, heavy chugs — each bubble the size of a room surfacing and bursting with a rhythm like a freight engine climbing a grade. Stand near one and you feel the piston-stroke in your teeth. Others are still and bright: acid chartreuse, iron orange, the color of old copper. They connect through flumes and channels that clog with mineral crust, back up, and then tear themselves clear with a crump you hear through the ground, sending a rusty flood wallowing across the floor before it drains away through holes you cannot see. Nothing here keeps its level. Water finds its level, then changes its level.

Geysers — dozens — erupt on schedules that are not schedules: twenty minutes, six hours, a jet that lasted three days and then died overnight, its cone already crumbling. A waterfall threads over the far lip, white and thin, running for hours after snowmelt or one of the intermittent rains that spill over the rim, then stopping altogether, leaving the rock dark and weeping. At night the ceiling sweats in earnest, and the condensate falls in thousands of drops — scalding and cold mixed — ticking and plinking against the sinter like a foundry raining bolts.

Between floods the floor is merely damp. Then it is drowned. Then damp again. The whole place cycles wet and dry, never committing.

---

### The Garden

The vegetation arrives as a shock, because the sound told you this was a factory and the green tells you it is a swamp.

The upper walls, within the reach of spray and of that brief noon blade, are upholstered. Moss in cushions a foot deep, moss in hanging curtains, moss stalactites, liverworts like spilled paint, filmy ferns trembling in the steam-drafts. It springs back when pressed. It holds water like a sponge and releases it for hours. The lushness is layered, near-touching: live moss over dead moss over peat over bacterial film over mineral crust — a ten-centimeter cross-section shows six colored strata, cream at the bottom where it is hottest, then sulfur yellow, rust orange, jade, leaf green, and finally the dark wet green of the living skin.

At midday, when the sun blade crosses the east wall, the garden ignites. The moss goes incandescent green. The air in the beam is a slow snow of spores — gold dust hanging, turning. The steam itself seems to glow. The whole pit appears to inhale. Then the blade passes and the dark returns, and your eyes re-learn the black, and the garden is only a smell again: wet wool, crushed green, sulfur underneath.

Below the garden line there is no green — only microbes, only color without leaves. The floor belongs to bacteria and to mineral.

---

### Sound

This is the loudest place on the mountain, and the mountain is the loudest thing for fifty kilometers.

The main plume rises from the vent field in the center, a column of steam going straight up the throat of the shaft, and its roar is broadband, bottomless, felt in the sternum more than heard. Under it, the pit has voices: fumaroles tuned to notes — one a high whine, one a low huff, one a sustained shriek like a valve stuck open. Pools chug their piston rhythm. Water hammers bang in constrictions, sudden as an anvil dropped in an adjoining room. Cooling rock ticks and cracks, a sporadic rifle-shot. Rimstone dams collapse with a deep crump and then the hiss of the flood behind it. Wind through the skylight produces a low organ note that swells and fades with the weather above, so the pit's pitch changes with the sky.

The loudness is physical. You shout into cupped hands and your own words arrive muffled. After an hour your ears ring in the relative quiet of the rim, where the roar is only a roar and no longer a pressure. Nothing with ordinary ears could live here. Nothing with ears does.

---

### Air and Breath

The pit breathes. Cold air pours over the low western lip, slides down the wall, warms over the floor, and rises in the central column — a slow lung with a twenty-four-hour rhythm, the mist streams reversing as the organ note swells. On the ledges away from the vents the air is cool, cellar-cool, hand-numbing; breath fogs. Ten meters away a pool boils and the radiant heat crisps your hair. The gradients are violent over a single meter.

The fog is stratified — layers of different temperatures, different wetness, sliding past each other, so you stand in cool gray mist and watch a white column tower out of it ten meters off. The smell is sulfur, hot stone, wet green, and something metallic, a taste like a coin on the tongue.

And the pit is electrified, after a fashion. The dry upper plume carries mineral dust, and the dust carries charge. On still nights the rim rocks crawl with blue sparks; the plume crackles and snaps, and for an instant the terraces are frozen in a strobe of actinic white, every sharp edge lit, before the dark takes them back. The hair on your arms rises before each discharge. Engines without engineers. Electrification without wires.

---

### What Lives, What Doesn't

There are no animals. No insects in the air — the air carries spores instead of wings. No birds over the lip; nothing sings against the roar because nothing could be heard, and nothing tries. No tracks mark the mud; the only prints are water's. Even the midges that cloud every other mountain water cannot manage the chemistry, the heat, the noise.

So the green grows and is not eaten. The food web is truncated at the bottom: producers and decomposers only. Moss dies and lies down under living moss; fungi thread the peat; bacteria build the terraces grain by grain, depositing at a rate that looks industrial. Everything above the microbial line is a monoculture of the uncropped. The only mouths in this place are vents.

Spores arrive constantly on the wind — the pit is colonized by whatever the mountain blows in — and most die, and some don't. That is the entire drama of arrival.

---

### Time

Nothing here is older than the pit. The oldest terrace may be eight years; its rims are already sharp, already banded, already crumbling. Terraces grow centimeters in a season and vanish in an afternoon when their dams burst. A vent opens overnight; a wall sloughs; the floor reconfigures. The pit is being built and unbuilt continuously, and it has not yet decided what it is. The rocks still remember being deep. The fracture faces are glassy, unweathered, as if the mountain has only just finished breaking.

At night, from the entry ledge, the skylight is a field of hard bright stars in thin air, and below it the pit is absolute dark, and the roar does not change — the place does not sleep. You navigate by the heat on your face and by the drip of the ceiling and by the crackle before the sparks.

Nothing here is impossible. There is no magic in it. It is only geology at full throttle, chemistry scaled up, water self-organizing into pools and flues, the mountain's own works running in the dark — a factory that was never built, producing only itself, witnessed only by moss and steam.
