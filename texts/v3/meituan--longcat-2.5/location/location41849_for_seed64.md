# The Engine Garden

You find it through a crack in the summit stone — a passage just wide enough to squeeze through, carved by no hand, leading down and then up and then *in*. The air changes immediately: cooler, thicker, carrying a smell like a workshop floor after rain. Ozone. Hot metal. Something green and urgent underneath.

Then the passage opens, and you are inside the room.

---

It is not large. Perhaps twenty paces across, twelve high, roughly circular. You could hold it in your mind the way you hold a closet or a well-room. But it is *packed* — every surface occupied, every volume filled, a saturation so complete that the room feels less like a space with things in it and more like a solid that has been carved into the shape of a hollow.

The walls are stone, but you can barely see them. They are furred with growth — dense, low, moss-like carpets in deep greens and blue-greens and near-blacks, shot through with filaments that catch what little light exists and hold it a moment too long, as if reluctant to let it go. The moss is not moss. Up close it has the structure of ferns, of liverworts, of something between a lichen and a coral — tiny fronds, tiny cups, tiny spiraling horns, all overlapping, all interlocked, all *dry* to the touch. They do not use water. Their sap, where you break a stem, is thin and pale and smells of ketones. They drink from the air — from the oil-mist, from the solvent vapor, from the hot exhaust breath of the machines.

Because the machines are everywhere.

They rise from the floor in clusters like iron trees — pistons, flywheels, gear-trains, pipe-runs, all riveted and flanged and ancient in a way that suggests they were old before the stone around them was laid. They are running. All of them. A slow, massive rhythm — *chunk, chunk, chunk* — from the largest piston, a cylinder as wide as your torso, stroking up and down with the patience of geology. Smaller mechanisms race and chatter around it: escapements clicking, camshafts turning, a turbine somewhere behind the far wall spinning so fast it is a continuous whine rather than a motion. The sound is enormous. Not loud the way an explosion is loud, but loud the way a cathedral is loud — it fills every corner, it presses on your chest, it makes your teeth hum. You cannot hear yourself swallow. You cannot hear your own heartbeat. The room *roars* with a sound that has no peak and no silence, a continuous industrial ocean.

And the plants grow through it all.

Vines — if they are vines — have wrapped the piston housings. Root-masses have infiltrated the pipe-joints, not clogging them but *sealing* them, extruding a dark resin that is better than any gasket. Fronds poke through the spokes of flywheels. A dense mat of something like liverwort has colonized the top of a generator housing, and it is *warm* there, warm from the coils beneath, and the liverwort has turned a deep, bruised purple in response, and it has begun to flower — tiny, glassy, colorless blossoms that open and close in time with the vibration, a mechanical pollination. You watch one open. You watch it close. It will do this forever. It has done this forever.

There is no dust. There is no rust. The brass fittings are dull but not corroded. The steel is dark but not flaking. The stone under your feet is dry and clean. The room is in equilibrium — perfect, total, eternal equilibrium. The machines produce. The plants consume. The plants seal and lubricate and filter. The machines run cooler, run smoother, run forever because of the plants. Nothing wears out. Nothing grows beyond its bounds. Nothing dies. The moss has reached the walls and stopped. The vines have reached the ceiling and stopped. The piston has stroked its stroke and will stroke it again, the same stroke, the same distance, the same force, tomorrow and tomorrow and tomorrow. The turbine spins. The escapement clicks. The glassy flowers open and close. The room breathes — in through the exhaust vents, out through the intake grilles, a slow industrial respiration that moves the air just enough to keep the solvent vapor at exactly the concentration the plants need and the machines need and the stone needs.

The light comes from everywhere and nowhere. It is dim — you could not read by it, but you can see the shapes of things, the gleam of oil on metal, the wet shine of fronds. Some of it is phosphorescence from the mosses themselves, a faint blue-green glow that clings to the walls like lichen-light. Some of it is the dull red of a bank of heating elements behind a grille, pulsing slowly, *chunk-chunk-chunk*, in time with the great piston. Some of it — the thinnest, coldest, most precious — comes from a crack in the ceiling, a fissure no wider than your finger, through which you can see a single star. The star does not move. The room does not turn. The crack is always there, always pointing at the same patch of sky, and the star is always in it, and its light falls on the same patch of moss, and the moss has grown a pale ring around the spot, a halo of bleached green, and it will never grow beyond that ring because the star will never move.

You sit down. There is a place to sit — a flat stone, warm from a pipe running beneath it, clear of growth, as if it has been kept clear. You rest your back against a machine housing and feel the vibration enter your bones, *chunk-chunk-chunk*, and you understand that the vibration is not a byproduct. It is the point. The vibration is what keeps the plants alive — the mechanical shaking that releases their spores, that pumps their sap, that tells their cells when to divide and when to stop. The machines are not merely tolerated by the garden. The machines are the garden's heart, its lungs, its sun. And the garden is the machines' blood, their breath, their purpose. They are one organism now, one system, one continuous body of metal and cellulose and oil and stone, and it has achieved what all living things seek and none achieve: it has stopped changing.

Outside, the mountain falls away into cold and dark and thin air. The wind screams past the summit. The star watches. The room does not care. The room is complete. The piston strokes. The turbine spins. The flowers open. The flowers close. The moss glows. The oil drips. The pipe breathes. The stone holds. And you are inside it now, and you can feel the equilibrium pressing against you like a hand, gentle and absolute, asking you without words to stop moving, to stop changing, to take your place among the machines and the mosses and the never-blinking star, and become part of the stillness.
