# The Stillworks

You cross the last scree slope — gray, loud underfoot, bitterly cold — and then the ground stops being ground.

The mat begins without a border. Scree sinks into a cushion of sponge two meters deep, blue-green and beaded with water, and your footsteps make no sound. Not quiet: nothing. The sump takes sound the way it takes everything else — into itself.

This is the plateau: high, wind-hollowed, cold most of the year, and entirely covered. No bare rock, no soil you could call soil. The mat *is* the substrate. It has been here so long that it has planed the land's roughness flat; the hills that remain are compost domes, soft as shoulders, centuries deep. The world and its carpet are in balance — the carpet grew over the world until the world stopped meaning anything underneath.

It is layered, like a factory arranged in storeys. Below, the **sump**: dead-and-living sponge, and warm. Decomposition heat. Stand still and the cushion is faintly warm against your boots — a slow compost fever that keeps the plumbing from freezing. Press it and the smell rises: greenhouse, pharmacy, something petroleum-sweet. Above that, the **pipework**: hollow stems with knuckles and valves of translucent tissue, and you can see the fluids move inside them — milky, amber, clear — in slow pulses. Peristaltic constrictions along each stem are pumps made of flesh. This layer is the refinery's pipe rack, grown. Then the **canopy**: broad waxy leaves, dark as liver, angled to the thin sun; each one a drip-channel, condensation rolling in beads along its ribs to fall with a faint *tick* into the dark below. Under the canopy the light comes down green-brown and underwater-soft.

Water here comes from the air. The sky is fog for weeks at a stretch, and the mat harvests it — leaf angles, sponge pores. Occasional rain arrives as soft mist and vanishes into the cushion with a sound like breathing. Meltwater runs only in the **milkveins**, channels the mat excretes mineral slurry into; the water is white, opaque, faintly sweet, and it resists freezing because the mat has distilled antifreeze into it — alcohols and esters, manufactured to keep its own fluids moving at −10°. Pools gather in sump hollows, surface skinned with fiber, and beneath the skin the water is the temperature of a warm room. There are sink pockets too: places where the sponge dips toward old drain channels. They look solid. They give way.

Everything the mat does is scaled production, and that is the uncanny thing — there is industry here, but nothing was ever built. In the **resin fields**, hectares of low stems weep raw amber through pores; the pools are slow as honey and harden over seasons into a glass that traps whatever falls in. **Oil seeps** shimmer; the solvent air above them can make you dizzy by midday. The **chimneys** — towers of compacted fiber, ten and twenty meters tall, venting warm alcohol-laden breath — are a distillation works running continuously, and the exhaust is why the plateau air smells drinkable-odd, faintly intoxicating if you stand downwind of a vent. Antifreeze, resins, oils, fibers, sugars: tonnes per hectare per season, all of it alive, all of it self-repairing. And nothing dead persists. The mat digests its own shed parts within weeks — bark-sheets, molted stem segments, spent spore bodies — drawn back into the sump. The only old things are the **amber tombs**: resin so deep and ancient it has buried earlier generations of the mat itself, layer on layer, a fossil record written in its own product.

The upper canopy is dry, and it clicks. Leaves rub; static builds. At dusk the discharge begins — blue points of corona on every leaf tip, sparks crawling along the stem-lines, silent except for a tickle in the teeth and hair rising from your scalp. The pulses are structured. Slow waves of discharge travel the stem-grid in repeating motifs — not language, but not random either. The mat's signaling, or its dreaming. On still nights the whole plateau glitters faintly, a field of blue pinpricks, breathing.

And it is never still, the mat, in the ways that matter. Overnight, a hectare of sporing bodies erupts from the sump — pale stalks a meter tall in a dozen hours. At midday they open and release a tide of spores that hangs like smoke in the dead air. By dusk they have collapsed into mulch, and the mulch is being drawn back into the sponge as you watch, a slow inward creep. Resin channels reroute: a pipe blackens, seals, and a new bulge opens meters away in the night. A chimney grows a hand's width in a season. Breaches heal in days — a sink pocket collapses, and within the week the mat has bridged it with woven fiber, already warm, already pumping. Creation and destruction at speed, and all of it silent. A boiling pot in a vacuum. Time here is layered: fast chemistry on a patient body; the surface churns; the structure endures.

There are almost no animals. The mat *is* the ecosystem; animals are exceptions, brief and rare. The **glass moth**: transparent wings, lives its whole life in the canopy drinking condensation from leaf ribs, never descends — and is sometimes entombed mid-gesture, so the amber holds hundreds of them. The **vein-worm**: pale, blind, swims the milkveins filtering mineral snow; you might find one curled in a shallow pool like a dropped thread. Birds cross high above the plateau and do not land. The mat does not tolerate large animals. It entangles, it entombs, it floods burrows with milk, it thickens the air with solvent until the intruder leaves or becomes a nutrient pocket — a mound of darker green within days, a resin plug if it was big enough. This is part of why the silence has no voices in it. The silence is partly the mat's own exclusion.

From a dome top, the plateau extends past the ridge line, feature after feature. The **Sponge Flats**, where the sump is deepest and the surface is a taut skin over black water. The **Pipewood**, where the stems stand so dense the light comes through in green bars. The **Chimney Steppe** on the horizon, its fiber towers venting, warm columns shimmering — a refinery skyline seen from far off, the only city this world has ever had, and it is grown. The **Milkvein delta**, braiding white across the sump. The **Cold Bogs**, where the water shows through in mirrors between floating fiber skins. At the plateau's edge the mat meets the scree of the peaks and patiently consumes it, a front of sponge advancing a hand's width a year — the mountain has been shrinking into the mat for longer than there have been names for either.

Frost silvers the canopy most mornings; each leaf wears a rim of ice that melts into the drip-channels by noon. The light is high-altitude, clear, slightly blue; the sheen of the leaves turns it metallic; spore tides make halos around the sun. Breath hangs. Eyelashes crust.

The rules of the place are simple. Everything is the mat — ground, water, air chemistry, the warmth underfoot, the smell, the silence; all of it is the organism's body or its product. Nothing dead persists; resin is the mat's memory, not its grave. Sound dies here: the sponge eats it, the canopy is a continuous skin and the wind passes over it without entering, and the loudest things you will hear are the tick of condensate, your own heartbeat, and a creak in the mat that sits below hearing, felt in the sternum. The mat is indifferent. It will not chase you and it will not notice you; it will, without malice, entomb you if you lie still long enough, because resin flows toward heat and carbon. Keep moving. Avoid the sink pockets. Do not sleep on the warm sump — the warm sump is where the mat digsests.

At night you stand on it in the dark: the blue pinpricks breathing over the canopy, the chimneys breathing warmer, the silence pressing on your eardrums like depth. A place that is quietly, constantly making and unmaking itself. The only factory in the world — and it is alive, and it does not know you are there.
