# The Bryomass of Kharen's Table

---

The first thing you understand is that you are standing on something alive.

Not *in* something alive, not *among* it — *on* it. The ground beneath your feet is a living skin, and it extends to every horizon. It has no trees, no shrubs in any conventional sense, no bare rock, no soil as a geologist would recognize it. There is only the mat: a continuous, interwoven, meters-deep accumulation of bryophytes, lichens, liverworts, and a dozen small vascular plants so thoroughly entangled with one another that they have become a single substrate. You cannot put a spade into the earth here. There is no earth. There is only the mat, and beneath the mat, older mat, and beneath that, compressed mat going down ten meters, twenty meters, into darkness and cold and the slow, slow squeeze of deep time.

This is Kharen's Table — a high plateau, four thousand meters and change above the distant sea, worn almost flat over the course of two billion years. The mountains that once stood here were ground down by ice, by wind, by the patient chemistry of rain, until what remained was a vast, gently undulating surface of ancient crystalline rock, already old when the first cells were learning to divide. And then the mat came — or rather, the mat *accumulated*, slowly, over tens of millions of years, each generation of plants growing on the compressed remains of its ancestors, building a substrate of itself, until the rock below was forgotten and the world above became a single, continuous organism.

The elevation is extreme enough that the sky is a paler blue than you are used to, and the sun, when it is out, burns with an intensity that reddens your skin in minutes. But the temperature is warm — not tropical, but warm, a persistent, gentle warmth that comes from the mat itself, which holds the day's heat and releases it slowly through the night. The air is thin but not unbreathable. The wind is constant, a steady, moderate breeze that never quite stops, that presses against you from no particular direction, that carries the smell of green and water and something faintly sweet and faintly rotten, the smell of a living thing breathing.

---

## The Mat

Walk on it and you will sink to your ankles. The surface is springy, resilient, a carpet of mosses and liverworts in shades of deep green, olive, bronze, and rust-red. The individual plants are small — most no taller than a finger — but they grow so densely that they form a continuous, unbroken layer. There are no gaps, no bare patches, no places where you can see through to what lies beneath. The mat is the world.

It is not flat. Over the millennia, the mat has created its own topography — low hummocks, shallow ridges, gentle swales, all formed by the uneven growth and accumulation of plant matter. Some hummocks are a meter high, two meters, built up over thousands of years by a particularly successful clump of moss or a fallen branch that was slowly engulfed. The swales between them are where the water gathers, where the mat is wetter and softer, where the liverworts grow in thick, glistening sheets. Walking across the Table is a constant, gentle undulation — up and down, up and down, over swells and dips that go on forever.

In places, the mat is punctuated by something harder — an outcrop of the ancient rock beneath, a knob of granite or gneiss that has resisted the advance of the plants, that rises a meter or two above the surrounding surface. These outcrops are rare, and they are landmarks. They are also, often, the sites of the built structures — the cairns, the standing stones, the small shelters — because they are the only solid ground for kilometers.

The mat is alive in ways that are not immediately obvious. If you stand still for long enough, you can feel it — a very slow, almost imperceptible movement, a gentle rise and fall, as if the entire plateau is breathing. This is not imagination. The mat is constantly growing, constantly dying, constantly compacting, and the aggregate of billions of tiny processes produces a motion that you can sense only when you are perfectly still. It is the slowest breath you will ever feel. It is the breath of a geological epoch.

## Water

Water on the Table is intermittent, elusive, always present and never quite available. It comes from the sky and from the air itself — from rain that falls in brief, intense, warm downpours, and from the condensation that forms on the surface of the mat when the temperature drops at night. The mat is saturated, always, but the water is held in the plant matter, bound up in the cells and the spaces between them, and it does not flow or pool unless the mat is fully saturated and the rain is heavy enough to exceed its capacity.

When that happens, the water gathers in the swales and depressions, forming shallow, temporary pools. These pools are the only open water on the Table — dark, still, tannin-stained the color of weak tea, full of life. Algae bloom in them, turning the surface green or orange or rust-red. Small invertebrates — water fleas, copepods, rotifers — cloud the water. The pools are ephemeral, lasting days or weeks before they drain through the mat or evaporate in the sun and wind. But while they last, they are the centers of life on the Table, the places where everything congregates, the places where the sound is loudest.

There are also the *drip lines* — places where the condensation on the mat is so heavy that it runs in thin streams down the sides of hummocks, or drips steadily from the overhanging edges of a particularly dense clump of moss. These drips are constant, a quiet, irregular percussion that you stop hearing after a while but that is always there, underneath everything else. In the early morning, when the temperature is lowest and the condensation is heaviest, the entire Table is audible with dripping — a soft, random, endless patter, like rain falling upward from the ground.

## Fauna

The life of the Table is small and it is beyond counting.

The insects come first — or rather, they are always there, but you notice them when you move. Walk ten steps and you disturb a cloud of them: midges, flies, small moths, springtails, mites, all rising from the mat in a swirling, buzzing column. They settle back within seconds, but there are always more. The air is full of them. They land on your skin, your eyes, your lips. They crawl into your hair, your ears, the folds of your clothing. They are not biting, most of them — they are feeding on the mat, on the algae, on each other, on the constant rain of organic matter that falls from the sky and from the birds.

The beetles are everywhere in the mat itself — small, dark, iridescent, burrowing through the upper layers, feeding on dead plant matter, on fungi, on each other. Spiders are abundant, ranging from tiny money-spiders that balloon on silk threads in the wind to larger wolf-spiders that hunt on the surface, that you see scuttling away from your footsteps. There are caterpillars, too — the larvae of the moths — that feed on the living moss and leave behind trails of grazing, patches of shorter, browner growth.

And then there are the birds. They come in flocks — thousands of them, tens of thousands — small, fast, swallow-like birds that wheel and dive through the insect clouds, feeding in a frenzy of motion. They arrive in the morning and stay until evening, and their calls are constant, a high, chattering, shrieking chorus that never stops. They nest on the rock outcrops, in crevices and on ledges, and the outcrops are whitewashed with their guano, and the smell is sharp and ammoniac. When a predator appears — a fox-like creature, or a large bird of prey circling overhead — the flock rises as one, a single, fluid, screaming mass, and the sound of their wings is like a gust of wind through dry leaves.

There are mammals, too, though you see them less often. Small, vole-like creatures tunnel through the mat, creating runways and burrows, feeding on the roots and stems of the plants. They are preyed upon by the fox-like creatures — lean, long-legged, reddish-brown animals that move across the mat with a particular gait, a high-stepping trot that keeps them from sinking too deeply. The foxes are not large, but they are efficient, and they are always hungry. There are also bats, which emerge at dusk to feed on the insects, which fly in swift, erratic patterns low over the mat, their echolocation calls audible as faint, high clicks beneath the roar of the insects.

The overwhelming quality of the fauna is not any single species but the *aggregate* — the sheer, suffocating abundance of small lives, all feeding, breeding, dying, decomposing, all part of the same cycle that builds the mat and is built by it. The Table is a machine for converting sunlight and water and the ancient minerals of the rock into living matter, and the living matter is insects and birds and small mammals and the plants themselves, and it is all happening at once, everywhere, all the time.

## Sound

The sound of the Table is not a background. It is a presence.

It is the constant, low-frequency drone of insect wings — billions of them, a continuous, rising and falling hum that you feel in your chest as much as you hear with your ears. It is the chirping of crickets or cricket-like insects, a rhythmic, repetitive pulse that varies in intensity, that swells and fades, that sometimes synchronizes across vast distances into a single, throbbing tone. It is the calls of the birds, the high, sharp, endless chatter of the flocks, the deeper, harsh cries of the raptors. It is the wind, which is always present, which moves through the mat and across it and produces a constant, soft, rushing sound, like the ocean heard from a great distance.

And it is the dripping. The endless, irregular, soft percussion of condensation falling from the mat, of water moving through the plant matter, of pools filling and overflowing and draining. It is the small sounds of the mat itself — the creak of plant matter compressing under your weight, the faint, wet sound of your footsteps, the rustle of insects moving in the surface layers.

Together, these sounds form a wall of noise, a constant, unbroken roar that you cannot escape. You can walk for hours and the sound does not change. You can stand still and it does not diminish. It is the sound of life at its most abundant, its most relentless, its most indifferent to your presence. After a while, you stop hearing it as sound and start hearing it as silence — the new baseline, the absence of the quiet you are used to. And when the wind drops, when the insects pause, when the birds are still, the sudden absence of noise is shocking, a pressure on your ears, a ringing emptiness that is worse than the roar.

## The Built

You will walk for hours and see nothing that is not alive. The mat extends in every direction, green and bronze and rust, gently undulating, broken only by the occasional rock outcrop, the occasional pool, the occasional hummock. The horizon is a thin, pale line, curved by the altitude and the scale of the plateau. The sky is enormous. You feel very small.

And then you will come across a cairn — a stack of stones, a meter high, built on a low outcrop, visible from a distance because it is the only vertical thing in a landscape of horizontals. The stones are ancient, weathered, lichen-covered. They have been stacked and restacked many times. Someone built this, long ago, and someone else maintained it, and someone else after that, and now it is part of the Table, as much a feature of the landscape as the hummocks and the pools.

There are others. A line of standing stones, weathered to rounded lumps, arranged in a rough circle on a slight rise, their purpose forgotten. A small shelter, half-collapsed, made of bone and woven plant fiber stretched over a frame of bent saplings — the only wood on the Table, carried from somewhere far away, somewhere lower, and precious beyond measure. A scatter of stone tools — a hand-axe, a scraper, a pile of flakes — lying on the surface of the mat as if dropped yesterday, the stone dark and sharp-edged, utterly out of place in a world of green.

The people who made these things are gone, or nearly gone. There may be a few left — small bands of hunter-gatherers who move across the Table following the pools and the bird flocks, who build temporary shelters of bone and fiber, who leave cairns and standing stones as markers, as memorials, as offerings to something they do not name. They are small, lean, dark-skinned from the sun, their hair matted with the dust of the mat. They move quietly, which is difficult on the Table, where every step produces a sound. They are almost invisible against the green and bronze of the mat. You can walk past one and not see it until it moves.

Their technology is simple — stone, bone, fiber, fire (which is rare and precious, made from the dry heartwood of the few stunted shrubs that grow on the rock outcrops). They have no metal. They have no pottery. They have the mat, and the birds, and the insects, and the water, and the stone, and the bone. It is enough. It has always been enough.

## Light and Weather

The light on the Table is normal light — sunlight, unfiltered by anything but the thin air and the occasional cloud. It is bright, clear, warm. It casts sharp shadows. It makes the colors of the mat vivid — the greens, the bronzes, the rusts, the occasional flashes of orange or yellow from a flowering plant or a butterfly. In the early morning and late afternoon, the light is golden, and the mat glows, and the pools reflect the sky, and the birds are silhouettes against the sun.

The weather is active, changeable, never quite predictable. The day begins clear and cold, the sun rising over a horizon that is impossibly distant. The temperature rises quickly. By mid-morning, the sun is hot, the air is still, the insects are at their peak. By midday, clouds may be building — white, towering cumulus that rise from the horizon and spread across the sky, that cast shadows on the mat, that bring rain. The rain is brief, intense, warm — a downpour that saturates the mat in minutes, that turns the swales into pools, that sends streams of water running down the sides of the hummocks. It lasts twenty minutes, half an hour, and then it stops, and the sun returns, and the mat steams, and the insects rise in clouds, and the birds return, and the sound returns, louder than before.

Or the clouds may not come. The day may remain clear and hot, the sun beating down, the mat drying, the pools shrinking, the insects settling into the deeper, wetter layers. The wind may rise in the afternoon, a hot, dry wind that blows dust and spores and the seeds of the mat plants across the surface, that makes walking difficult, that fills the air with a fine, green haze.

At night, the temperature drops sharply. The mat releases its stored heat slowly, but the air is cold, and the condensation is heavy, and the dripping is constant. The stars are brilliant, close, innumerable — the Milky Way a visible band of light across the sky. The birds are silent. The insects are quieter, though never silent. The foxes move in the darkness, and the bats, and the small mammals in their tunnels. The mat breathes, slowly, almost imperceptibly. The sound of the Table at night is lower, deeper, more sparse — the wind, the dripping, the occasional call of a night bird, the rustle of something moving through the moss.

## The Rules

The Table is old. Older than the mountains it replaced. Older than the rivers that cut the valleys below. It has been worn down to its final form, and it will not change again in any way that a human lifetime can perceive. The mat grows, but it also dies, and the net accumulation is zero — the mat is as deep as it will ever be, and it is in equilibrium with itself, a closed loop of growth and decay.

The water comes and goes. The rain falls and evaporates. The pools form and drain. The mat holds what it can and releases the rest. There is no river on the Table, no stream that flows to the sea. All the water is recycled, over and over, through the mat, through the air, through the bodies of the plants and animals. The Table is a closed system, hydrologically isolated from the world below.

The life of the Table is abundant but not diverse. There are relatively few species — a few dozen plants, a few hundred insects, a few dozen birds, a handful of mammals — but each is present in staggering numbers. The system is simple, robust, ancient. It has survived mass extinctions, ice ages, the slow drift of continents. It will survive the people who live on it, and the people who come after them, and the changes they bring.

The mat is the world. Everything else is temporary — the pools, the birds, the insects, the foxes, the people, the cairns, the standing stones, the shelters of bone and fiber. The mat endures. It has been here for millions of years, and it will be here for millions more, growing and dying and growing again, breathing its slow breath, making its slow sound, covering the ancient rock with its living skin.

You are standing on something alive. You have always been standing on something alive. You will always be standing on something alive. The Table does not care. The Table does not notice. The Table is.
