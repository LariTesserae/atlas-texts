The plateau of Xhur is not so much a place as a negotiation. At two thousand meters, the air sits cool and heavy, saturated with moisture that never quite falls as rain and never quite lifts as fog. It clings. It drips from every surface. It beads on leaves the size of cloaks and runs down bark in threads, so that the whole forest — because forest is what it is, though a forest built over and around human habitation — seems to weep continuously, a soft and persistent dirge that has been playing for centuries.

The settlement occupies a natural amphitheater, a shallow bowl of weathered basalt ringed by higher crags. The rock is old, deeply grooved, its channels carved by water into flutes and runnels that sing when the wind moves through them. Every crevice holds a spring. The water is not dramatic — no waterfalls here, no rushing torrents — but it is omnipresent, seeping, welling, condensing on stone and leaf until the ground is a saturated sponge of peat and root matter, and the air itself feels drinkable. The sound of it is constant: a low, liquid murmur beneath everything, the white noise of a world made wet.

The buildings are grown, not built. The people of Xhur — and they are people, human, with the same needs and noise and argument as anywhere — have long practiced the art of shaping living wood. They train trees from saplings, guiding trunks and branches into arches, walls, frames. A house in Xhur is a tree that has been persuaded over decades to grow in a particular shape, its branches interwoven, its bark thickened and hardened by salves made from resin and ash. Roofs are layers of broad, waxy leaves — each one the size of a shield — overlapped and stitched to the frame with flexible vine, so that when it rains, the water sheets off in a drumbeat that is the town's primary percussion. Some structures are dozens of trees fused together, their root systems merged into foundations that shift and settle and occasionally crack a wall, requiring the inhabitants to prune or redirect a root the way a householder elsewhere might repair a roof tile. The architecture is never finished. It is always becoming.

The density is the first thing that overwhelms. Not just the buildings, but the life. The forest has not been cleared to make room for the town; the town has been inserted into the forest like a graft. Every trunk hosts its own ecosystem: mosses, ferns, orchids, lichens that hang in pale curtains. Vines lash tree to tree at every level, and between the vines move animals — constant, noisy, unafraid. Tree frogs in electric blues and sulfur yellows call from every hollow. Insects fill the air in clouds, great glittering beetles and moths with wings like stained glass, and birds — birds in such numbers that their collective voice is a physical presence, a wall of sound that rises and falls with the hour. There are mammals too: small, rust-colored primates that move in troops through the canopy, dropping scraps and excrement and occasional screams onto the roofs below. The people tolerate them. They have learned to sleep through the dusk chorus, which begins before the light fades and does not truly end before dawn.

The cool is sharp. At night, the mist thickens and the temperature drops, and the warm places are the crowded ones — the longhouses where a dozen families sleep shoulder to shoulder, the animal pens where goats and a local species of shaggy, pig-like grazer radiate heat. Fires are small and smoky, built from peat and resinous wood, and they are never allowed to go out. The smoke hangs low, trapped by the damp air, so that the lower levels of the town are always hazed in a blue-gray pall that smells of tar and wet ash.

The tech is simple. Tools are wood and bone and a little copper, worked by hand. The people knap obsidian for blades and grind stone for axes. They know weaving — elaborate, beautiful weaving of plant fibers into cloth and rope and netting — but not the loom. They know the management of water, the digging of channels, the placement of stone to guide a spring, but not the aqueduct. There is a forge in the town, small and inefficient, where a single smith works copper into pins and hooks and the occasional blade, and the sound of his hammer is part of the town's soundscape, a bright, irregular punctuation amid the green noise. Most of what they have is made from the forest: boats from hollowed logs, cordage from bark fiber, medicine from the endless pharmacopeia of mosses and fungi that coat every surface.

And the forest is always taking it back. This is the dynamic that defines the place. A wall is grown, and within a season the moss has claimed it, and within a year a vine has split it, and within five years it is a ruin, a hump of vegetated debris from which the villagers salvage what they can and shape a new tree to take its place. The town is not decaying — it is cycling, a perpetual motion of construction and collapse and reconstruction, so that the map of Xhur changes year to year, house to house, and the oldest structures are not the most permanent but the most frequently renewed, their lineages of growth and regrowth extending back through generations. The people do not resist this. They have a philosophy, or perhaps just an acceptance: that to live here is to participate in a metabolism larger than one's own, and that the forest is not an enemy to be defeated but a partner with whom one is always dancing, and the dance is loud and wet and cool and dense, and it never stops.
