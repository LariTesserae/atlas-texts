The water has no shore. It simply is — a single planetary skin of it, deep and cool and darkening toward the bottom, where light goes to die and pressure becomes the dominant fact of existence. You could call it an ocean, but that word implies edges, and there are none. The surface, seen from a great height, would look like a continuous polished basalt mirror, interrupted only by the slow, pale smears of storms and the faint greenish haze of whatever sparse life has managed to take hold.

Beneath, the floor is young. Not old-young, not worn-down-young, but genuinely, geologically young — raw basalt still cooling from its own birth, fresh fractures exposing glassy black interiors, mineral veins so new they haven't yet oxidized into color. There is no sediment worth the name. No ancient mud, no compressed history. The bottom is a construction site: lava lobes pushing upward like slow fists, vent fields exhaling superheated chemical breath, and the constant, subsonic groaning of a lithosphere that hasn't decided what shape it wants to be. This is a world still coming into itself, and the water above it is less a feature than a witness — present, enormous, but somehow provisional, as if the planet has not yet chosen between being stone or sea.

And yet it is *high*. The entire body of water sits at an elevation that would, on any familiar world, be considered extreme — as though the ocean had been lifted and set down on a continental shelf the size of a hemisphere, then the shelf removed and the water simply stayed, held in place by something other than gravity alone. The atmosphere above is thin, cold, and sharply lit by a sun that seems too close and too white. The pressure at the surface is lower than it should be. The water should be boiling off, and in places it is — great slow geysers of vapor rising from thermal vents, carrying minerals and heat into the sky, where they condense and fall back as warm, metallic rain. The sound of this is constant and enormous: a low, rolling thunder that never quite arrives, the roar of water being torn apart and reassembled on a scale no storm could match.

The life here is not animal. It is not even properly plant. It is film and stain — bacterial mats clinging to the few rocks that have cooled enough to host them, spreading in thin, iridescent sheets across the black stone, feeding on chemical gradients and the faint, filtered light that penetrates the upper hundred meters. They are sparse, patchy, easily overlooked. They do not move. They do not swim. They simply grow, slowly, in the cracks where heat and water and minerals meet. The color they add to the world is minimal: a faint, oily rainbow sheen, a slight greening of the water near the vents. Otherwise, the sea is empty. No fish, no krill, no whales, no drifting colonies of anything. The water is clear to a degree that feels unnatural, and the only motion in it is the slow, convection-driven circulation of the deep and the occasional, violent updraft from below.

But the emptiness is not quite emptiness. The water is full of something too small to see — particles, or machines, or something in between. They are not alive, but they are not inert either. They drift, they cluster, they respond to temperature and pressure and the chemical signatures of the vents in ways that suggest purpose. They are building. Not structures you could walk through — there is nothing built here, no artifacts, no monuments, no ruins — but they are building the *geology*. They seed the cooling rock with crystalline lattices that shouldn't form at these temperatures. They precipitate minerals in patterns that look almost like circuitry, or like language, or like the slow, frozen record of a computation no one is running anymore. They are the reason the water stays where it is, the reason the floor is rising, the reason this world is still coming into being. They are the invisible workforce of a planet that is constructing itself, and they have been at it for a very long time.

The storms are the loudest thing. They form without warning, spinning up from the temperature differential between the warm vents and the cold upper water, and they are not like terrestrial storms — they are vertical, columnar, reaching from the seafloor to the stratosphere in a single, unbroken helix of vapor and suspended minerals. Inside them, the water moves at speeds that strip rock bare and send boulders tumbling like pebbles. The sound is indescribable: not a crash, but a continuous, modulated shriek, like metal being bent and torn and welded back together, like a forge the size of a continent operating at full capacity. The storms last for hours, or days, or weeks, and they leave behind them a changed seafloor — new vents, new fractures, new chemical gradients, new opportunities for the sparse, patient life to spread.

There is no day and night in the usual sense. The sun is fixed, or seems to be, and the light that reaches the surface is constant, harsh, and shadowless. The only rhythm is the storms, and the slower rhythm of the geology — the pulse of the vents, the creep of the lava, the imperceptible rise of the floor. Time here is measured in the growth of crystals, in the spreading of bacterial films, in the slow, patient work of the invisible machines. It is a world without urgency, because there is no one to be urgent. It is a world without witness, because there is no one to witness it. It is simply happening, vast and loud and empty and new, a planetary-scale event with no audience, a creation story still being written in water and stone and something that might be technology or might be geology or might be a distinction that no longer matters here.
