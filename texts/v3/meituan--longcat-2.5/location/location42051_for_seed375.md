The boulder is unremarkable from the outside — a gray lump the size of a house, half-buried in scree, indistinguishable from the thousands of other lumps that litter this low, cold basin. But the boulder is hollow, and the hollow is a city.

You enter through a fracture in the shell — a crack just wide enough to admit a body, though you will not find it again on your way out. The passage beyond is short and descends slightly, and then the space opens, and you are inside.

---

The city is small. Its entire extent might fit within a cathedral, but it is not a cathedral. It is a settlement — dense, layered, organized — a construction of chambers and corridors and platforms and shafts, all grown from the interior surface of the shell. The material is a pale mineral, something between salt and chalk and ice, that has accreted in layers over something older. The laminations are visible everywhere — thin bands of white and cream and pale amber, each one a slightly different chemistry, a slightly different season of growth. The oldest layers are chalky and opaque. The newest are translucent, almost soft, still warm from the chemistry of their deposition.

The architecture is not random. It is urban in the truest sense — organized, intentional-seeming, with a logic of circulation and congregation. There is a central chamber, roughly circular, perhaps thirty feet across, with a ceiling that rises to twice your height. From this chamber, corridors radiate — not in a hub-and-spoke pattern but in a more organic, branching way, like the veins of a leaf or the bronchi of a lung. The corridors subdivide. They lead to smaller chambers, which lead to smaller corridors, which dead-end or loop back or descend into darkness. There are platforms — flat-topped accretions that rise from the floor like tables or beds or altars. There are shelves — thin, projecting ledges that ring the chambers at varying heights. There are columns — not structural, not holding anything up, simply present, like the pillars of a temple whose roof has dissolved.

The mineral grows from vapor. There is no water here — no seep, no drip, no humidity. The air is dry as bone. But the rock of the shell is porous, and it exudes a slow, mineral-laden breath that rises through the pores and deposits itself on every surface it touches. The process is ongoing. The city is still building itself. The thinnest walls are newer than you are. The thickest are older than your species. The growth is not uniform — it is faster in some places, slower in others, depending on the porosity of the shell and the chemistry of the vapor and the temperature of the rock beneath. This is why the architecture is so varied — why one chamber is a cathedral and the next is a closet, why one corridor is a grand promenade and the next is a crawlspace. The city grew where the conditions allowed, and the conditions were never the same in two places.

The light is dim and sourceless. The mineral itself seems to hold it — a faint, cool luminescence that is not quite phosphorescence, more like the memory of light stored in crystal lattices and released so slowly you cannot perceive the decay. It is enough to navigate by, barely. Shadows pool in the deeper chambers and do not dissipate. The brightest area is the central chamber, where the mineral is thinnest and the luminescence seems to concentrate, as if the city's heart is its most translucent point. The light does not flicker. It does not vary. It is as constant as the temperature, which is cool and uniform — no gradients, no warm or cold pockets, a single degree that never changes.

Sound is swallowed here. The mineral surfaces are micro-porous, almost spongy at close range, and they absorb vibration with an appetite that feels almost intentional. A footstep produces a brief, soft contact and then nothing — no echo, no resonance, no aftermath. Speech is absorbed within arm's length. A shout dies in your throat. The silence is not empty; it is full, textured, a presence in itself. You become aware of your own body's sounds — the creak of joints, the rustle of clothing, the too-loud pulse in your ears. Then even these seem to dampen, as if the space is teaching you how to be quiet. The only sound that persists is subsonic — a rhythm like breathing or tide, felt in the chest rather than heard, that is the rock beneath exhaling its mineral burden. It is slow. One cycle perhaps every thirty seconds. You do not hear it so much as feel it, a pressure that rises and falls, rises and falls, the pulse of the world that contains you.

Once in a great while — minutes apart, or hours — there is a sound. A faint *tick*, like a nail being driven into soft wood a great distance away. A hairline fracture in the mineral, propagating. A new layer setting. The city adjusting itself. Then silence again, deeper than before, as if the sound had never been.

---

The city is mostly empty. This is the first thing you notice and the last thing you forget. The architecture is dense, the construction is layered, the infrastructure is all there — the corridors, the chambers, the platforms, the shafts — but there is no one here. No inhabitants. No builders. No signs of habitation. The platforms are bare. The shelves are bare. The chambers are open and unoccupied. The corridors run from nothing to nothing, connecting empty rooms in an elaborate network of purposeful purposelessness. The city is a city without a population, a settlement without settlers, a vast investment of mineral and time in the service of no one. The emptiness is not abandonment — nothing was ever here to abandon the place. The emptiness is original. The city grew itself, for itself, and no one has ever lived in it.

Sparse life persists at the margins. In the deepest, darkest chambers — places where the mineral growth has slowed or stopped — a thin crust of lichen clings to the walls. It is not green. It is a pale, grayish-white, the same color as the mineral, and it mimics the accretion so perfectly that you cannot tell where geology ends and biology begins. It is dry, brittle, dormant. It may have been growing for centuries to reach its current size, which is no larger than a thumbnail. In the cracks and crevices, smaller things — mites, perhaps, or springtails, or something that is not quite either — move through the mineral forest like fish through a reef. They are rare. You may see one in an hour of watching. They are pale, eyeless, and they move with the slow certainty of creatures that have never known light. They do not react to your presence. They do not react to anything. They are part of the city's ecology, such as it is — the only inhabitants of a place built for no one.

In some chambers, the mineral has formed structures that resemble artifacts. A basin, smooth and oval, set into the floor. A seat, low and curved, projecting from the wall. A rack of thin, parallel spires that might be a loom or a harp or a cage. In others, the forms are more ambiguous — a cluster of needles that might be a forest or a hand or a sentence in a language of verticality. The accretion does not discriminate. It deposits itself wherever the vapor rises, and the vapor rises wherever the rock beneath is porous, and the rock is porous wherever something once passed through it — root, worm, water, time. The resemblance to artifacts is coincidental, but it is persistent. The city is full of things that look made but are grown, that look intended but are accidental, that look like evidence of a builder but are only evidence of a process.

---

The active life of the place is slow and mineral. You can see it in the thinnest chambers, where the accretion is newest: a faint haze of mineral dust hangs in the air, not falling, not rising, simply suspended, drifting in currents too slow to perceive. In the deepest silence, you can feel the subsonic pulse — the rock exhaling, the mineral depositing, the city growing. The floor is not perfectly still. It is rising, imperceptibly, the slowest elevator in the world, carrying you upward toward a ceiling that is descending to meet you. The clearance in the corridors is narrowing. The chambers are shrinking. The city is filling itself in, and it will continue until the rock is exhausted or the vapor stops rising, and then it will be solid, and then it will be a boulder, and then it will be indistinguishable from the thousands of other boulders that litter this low, cold basin.

There are rules here. The space does not want sound. The space does not want water. The space does not want rapid movement or warmth or the chemistry of decay. The space wants mineral, patience, and pressure. The space is making more of itself, and it is using the rock beneath as raw material, and it will continue until the rock is exhausted or the vapor stops rising. This is not a place that ends. It is a place that fills.

You are a guest in something that is still being born. The city is young — its oldest structures are sharp-edged and unweathered, its newest are still translucent, still soft enough to mark with a fingernail. It has not yet learned to be old. It does not know erosion. It knows only growth, layer upon layer, the slow architecture of accumulation, the patient construction of a world from vapor and silence and time.

And the city is strange — not because it is alien, but because it is familiar in the wrong way. It looks like a place where someone lives. It has the shape of intention. It has the grammar of settlement — the public and the private, the thoroughfare and the dead-end, the platform and the shelf. But there is no one here, and there never was, and the intention was never anyone's. The city is a sentence without a speaker, a home without an inhabitant, a body without a mind. It grew itself from vapor and silence and the chemistry of rock, and it will complete itself the same way, layer by layer, in the dark, in the quiet, in the cool, until it is solid and finished and empty forever.
