# The Drownings

---

The lake has no name that anyone speaks. It sits in a basin between peaks so high that the air above it is thin enough to hurt, and the water below is deep enough that its bottom has never been seen. Soundings have been attempted — weighted lines paid out from wooden boats, stones dropped from cliffs, voices called into cracks in the shore ice — and the answers disagree. In the eastern basin, the bottom seems to be nine hundred meters down. In the western trench, the line runs out and finds nothing, and the people who manage this say the rope was cut clean, not broken, not frayed, *cut*, by something in the dark that did not want to be measured.

The water is cold. Not the cold of winter ponds or mountain streams — that is a surface cold, a skin. This is a cold with mass, with ambition. It presses. It enters. It remembers the glaciers that feed it and the depth it has fallen through and the pressure of nine hundred meters of water above the dark, and it carries all of that into your body when you touch it. Within seconds your hand goes white and numb. Within minutes, you are in danger. The cold here is not a condition; it is an entity, and it is patient.

---

Below ten meters, the light changes. The surface world — the wind, the peaks, the pale sky — becomes a memory, a rumor. The water takes the blue and keeps it, filters out everything else. By twenty meters you are in a blue so deep it is almost violet, and the last shapes of the shore rocks fade into the uniform. By thirty meters, the blue is gone too, and there is only the faintest suggestion of direction, a gradient so subtle that your eyes invent patterns in it, faces and hands and the outlines of things that are not there.

And then, at forty or fifty meters depending on the slope, you reach the forest.

---

It is not kelp. The people who came here first called it kelp because they had no other word, because it rises from the bottom in long vertical lines, because it sways. But it is not kelp. The stipes are thick as a person's waist, ribbed like the stalk of something that grew in a place with no sun, pale as bone or grey as weathered wood. They rise thirty meters, forty, fifty — the tallest reaching almost to the surface, their tips lost in the blue dark above. They do not branch. They do not leaf. At the top of each stipe, the tissue flares into a broad, flat blade, translucent, reinforced with a lattice of silica that catches what little light exists and holds it, so that from below, the canopy glows with a faint, sourceless luminescence, a green-white that is not quite light and not quite color but something in between.

The blades are broad as doors. They overlap. They form a ceiling. Below that ceiling, the water is still and strange, sheltered from the currents that move through the upper water, full of the chemical smell of the forest itself — a smell like copper and wet stone and something sweeter, something almost floral, that has no business being here.

The forest grows on the slopes of the drowned basin, following the terrain down. On the gentler gradients it is dense, a jungle of stipes so thick that you cannot swim between them without pushing them aside, and they push back — not with strength, but with persistence, with the slow hydraulic pressure of their tissues, reclaiming the space you opened within seconds. On the steeper slopes, the forest thins. The holdfasts cannot grip the bare rock, and the stipes grow at angles, reaching sideways, forming strange leaning galleries, arches, tunnels. In places where the rock is vertical, there is no forest at all, only the bare stone and the bacterial mats that grow on it — pale, dusty, like a disease of the rock.

And there are no animals.

---

This is the thing that takes longest to understand, and the thing that, once understood, makes the whole place different.

There are no fish. There are no insects. There are no crustaceans, no worms, no jellyfish, no larvae, no eggs, no microscopic things darting in the water column. The water is full of plant matter — spores, fragments, the fine silicate slurry that the blades dissolve into when they die — but nothing that moves with intention. Nothing that hunts or flees or mates or dies in the animal way. The only motion is the motion of water and plant: the sway of the stipes, the drift of the spores, the slow collapse of a blade that has grown too heavy and torn free from its stipe and is sinking, turning, dissolving as it falls.

The absence is not natural. The water is cold and oxygen-rich. The forest provides structure, shelter, chemical energy in abundance. By every law that governs such places, the water should be *thick* with life — with things that graze the bacterial mats, with things that hide in the holdfasts, with things that swim between the stipes and spawn in the canopy. But there is nothing. The silence within the noise is absolute. The only sounds are the sounds of water, stone, and plant.

---

And the noise is constant.

The lake is never quiet. The water transmits every vibration with a fidelity that makes the air seem deaf by comparison. The wind on the surface is a continuous roar, even on days when the surface is calm — the sound enters the water at a distance and spreads, filling the basin with a low, rolling thunder that never quite fades. Waves break against the shore rocks and the sound is a percussion that reaches the bottom, a rhythm of impact and withdrawal. Ice calves from the glaciers that feed the lake and the sound is enormous, a crack like the world splitting, followed by a deep boom that rolls through the water for minutes, bouncing between the basin walls.

Underwater, the forest itself is loud. The stipes creak under tension — a sound like rope under strain, like the rigging of a ship. The blades slap against each other in the deeper currents, a sound like wet canvas, like hands clapping. The gas bladders that some of the stipes develop — small, clear spheres, filled with something that is not quite air — pop and crack as they are squeezed by the pressure, and each pop is sharp, distinct, a tiny percussion in the larger music. When the forest releases its spores, it does so in clouds, and the release is audible: a hiss, a sigh, a sound like a breath let out after holding it for years.

Deeper still, there are other sounds. The lake floor settles — a low, grinding rumble that comes and goes, that might be the weight of the water pressing the rock into new configurations, or might be something else. The mineral springs that feed the forest pour their water into the basin with a roar that can be heard from hundreds of meters away, a constant white noise like a waterfall heard from inside the falls. And from the deepest parts, from the trench where the bottom has never been found, there is a sound that is not geological. It is low — below the threshold of hearing, felt in the chest more than heard — and it is regular. It pulses. It has a rhythm. It might be the lake breathing. It might be the lake dreaming. It might be something else.

---

The structures are old. They are older than the oldest stories, and the stories themselves are fragmentary, contradictory, full of gaps.

Near the shore, in the shallows where the water is only five or ten meters deep, there are the remains of wooden platforms. The wood is dark and waterlogged, preserved by the cold, swollen to twice its original size, soft to the touch but still holding its shape. The platforms are built from logs lashed together with rope made from some plant fiber — the fiber is still intact in places, though it crumbles when touched. The logs are not cut; they are split, along the grain, with tools that left marks like the marks of stone axes. The platforms are rectangular, perhaps three meters by four, and they stand on legs that are sunk into the lake floor. They were dwellings, perhaps — houses on stilts, above the water — or they were piers, or they were racks for drying something. There are no remains of what they might have been used for. No bones, no tools, no pottery, no metal. Only the wood and the rope and the shape of the thing.

Further out, on a submerged ridge where the water is fifteen meters deep, there is a ring of standing stones. They are crude — unworked boulders, roughly upright, arranged in a circle perhaps twenty meters across. The stones are granite, and they are not from the ridge; the ridge is basalt, and the granite boulders must have been carried from the peaks to the north, down the slope, across the shore, and into the water. Each stone weighs hundreds of kilograms. There are eleven of them, and they are evenly spaced, and the circle is precise. Their tops are ten meters below the surface. The water around them is thick with the translucent blades of the forest, and the stones are wrapped in them, bound, as if the forest is slowly pulling them down.

There are other marks. Cairns on the lake floor, placed in lines that lead from the shore to the ridge, from the ridge to the trench. The cairns are small — three or four stones each — and they are placed at intervals of exactly fifty meters, as measured by the old system of arm-spans. Carved steps cut into a rock face on the northern shore, descending from above the waterline down into the dark; the steps continue for twenty meters below the surface and then stop, as if the carvers were interrupted, or as if they intended to continue but never did. A single large stone, perfectly spherical, two meters in diameter, resting in a depression on the lake floor at a depth of sixty meters — clearly placed there, clearly not natural, clearly moved by people who had no technology that should have allowed them to move it.

And on the walls of the trench, where the water is so deep that the pressure would crush a person, there are marks that might be carvings. They are visible only in the beam of a diving light, and they are too regular to be natural — lines, angles, shapes that might be writing or might be maps or might be something else entirely. The light does not reach far enough to see the whole of them. The marks continue down into the dark below.

---

The people who built these things are gone. There are no bones, no graves, no tools, no traces of fire or cooking or waste. Only the stones and the wood and the marks on the rock. They left no images of themselves. They left no writing that can be read. They left only the structures, and the structures suggest a people who understood the lake — its depth, its cold, its hunger, the thing that lives in it or the thing that it is — and who built things to speak to it, or to keep it back, or to mark the places where it had taken something.

The water rises. It has been rising for as long as anyone has kept track — a few centimeters per year, perhaps, as the glaciers melt or as the basin slowly subsides or as the lake itself grows. The wooden platforms were built above the waterline; they are now five meters below it. The carved steps were built from the shore; they now descend into the deep. The ring of stones was built on dry land, or on land that was briefly dry; it is now fifteen meters down. The water is still rising. The forest is still growing. The structures are still sinking. The process is slow, but it is not stopping.

---

The dynamics of the place are constant and multiple. The water rises. The forest grows and collapses in cycles of decades — a stipe lives for perhaps fifty years, growing slowly in the cold, and then it tears free or is torn free by a current or by the weight of its own blade, and it sinks, and it dissolves, and the silicate slurry settles on the lake floor, and new stipes grow from the holdfast, or from the fragments of the old ones, and the forest renews itself. The mineral springs open and close — a spring that has flowed for a century may seal itself in a season, and a new spring may open in a place where the rock was bare the day before. The currents shift with the seasons, carrying clouds of spores from one part of the lake to another, seeding new forests on bare rock, connecting the separate groves into a single system. The lake floor settles and grinds. The ice calves. The wind blows. The sound continues.

And through all of this, there are no animals. The forest grows unchecked. Nothing grazes it. Nothing prunes it. Nothing carries its spores except the water. Nothing dies in the animal way, because there are no animals to die. The only death is the plant death — the slow, cold, dissolving death of the blades and the stipes, the return of the silicate to the water, the settling of the slurry on the bottom. It is a system without predators, without scavengers, without the quick, darting, desperate motion of animal life. It is a system of water and stone and plant and ice, and it has been this way for a long time, and it will be this way for a long time more.

---

The light is not quite darkness. In the deepest parts, in the trench, it is darkness — the absolute darkness of water so deep that no light has ever reached it, the darkness that is not an absence but a presence, a substance, a pressure. But above the trench, in the forest, there is the faint bioluminescence of the canopy, the green-white glow that is not quite light, and there is the light from above — the blue that filters down from the surface, the pale suggestion of a world where the air is thin and the peaks are bare and the sky is a color that has no name. The light is enough to see by, barely, for the first thirty meters. Below that, you need a light of your own. And below that, even your light is not enough, and the dark takes it, and the sound takes over, and the pressure takes over, and the cold takes over.

---

The weirdness is in the details, and the details accumulate.

The water has a viscosity that is not quite right. It clings to surfaces — to skin, to wood, to stone — with a persistence that normal water does not have. Shapes form in it and persist for a moment too long: a bubble that should rise in a straight line instead spirals, holds its spiral, dissolves. A cloud of spores that should drift instead gathers, holds a shape — a sphere, a column, a hand — and then disperses. The water is not thick, not sticky, not strange to the touch in any way that can be named. But it behaves strangely. It remembers shapes. It holds them.

The forest's blades, when they break, do not decay. They dissolve — slowly, over months — into a fine silicate slurry that coats everything: the lake floor, the rocks, the structures, the inside of the trench. The slurry is pale, almost white, and it gives the bottom a dusty, abandoned appearance, like a room that has been closed for years. The slurry is not toxic, not to the touch, but it gets into everything. It fills the cracks in the rock. It fills the spaces between the stones of the cairns. It fills the carved steps. It is slowly filling the lake, raising the bottom, millimeter by millimeter, as the forest grows and dies and dissolves and settles.

And the sound from the trench — the low, regular pulse — it is not constant. It changes. It speeds up, slows down, stops for days or weeks, and then begins again. It has a pattern, but the pattern is too long to discern; it might be seasonal, or it might be something else. The people who have listened to it longest say that it responds to things. They say that when the water rises quickly — in the melt season, when the glaciers pour into the basin — the pulse speeds up. They say that when the forest releases its spores — in the coldest months, when the surface is frozen and the deep water is still — the pulse slows down, becomes almost gentle. They say that when someone enters the trench — when a diver descends past the two-hundred-meter mark, into the pressure and the dark — the pulse stops, and does not begin again until the diver leaves.

They say other things too. They say that the divers who go deepest hear voices. They say that the voices speak a language that is not any language, but that it is almost familiar, almost recognizable, as if it were a language that everyone knew once and has forgotten. They say that the voices ask questions, and that the divers who answer them do not come back, or come back changed, or come back unable to speak, or come back speaking only in the language of the voices, which no one else can understand.

They say that the people who built the structures did not leave. They say that the water did not rise and drown them. They say that they went down, intentionally, into the trench, into the dark, into the cold, and that they are still there, and that the pulse is their heartbeat, or their breathing, or their voice, or the sound of something they built, something that is still running, still working, still doing whatever it was built to do.

They say that the lake is not a lake. They say it is a door. They say it is a machine. They say it is a mouth.

---

The basin is large — regional, multi-feature, a geography. From the shore you cannot see the far side; the water extends to the horizon, dark and still, broken only by the occasional white of a calving glacier or the ripple of a current. The near shore is steep, rocky, bare — no soil, no plants, no life of any kind above the waterline except the lichens on the highest rocks and the thin, cold wind. The water meets the rock and continues down, and down, and down. The slopes are terraced — ancient shorelines, perhaps, or the work of the people who built the structures — and each terrace has its own forest, its own density, its own character. The upper terraces are open, sparse, the stipes scattered with much space between them. The middle terraces are dense, tangled, impassable. The lower terraces are strange — the stipes grow at angles, in spirals, in patterns that might be natural or might not be.

The trench is in the western basin, and it is the deepest part, and it is where the sound comes from, and it is where the marks on the walls are, and it is where the divers go, and it is where the divers come back from changed, or do not come back from at all.

---

The place is old. It is in its own state, weathered, in balance. The erosion has done its work — the shore rocks are smooth, the structures are worn, the forest is established, the system has reached its equilibrium. The water rises, but slowly; the forest grows, but slowly; the structures sink, but slowly. Everything is in balance, and the balance has been maintained for a long time, and it will be maintained for a long time more.

The cold is constant. The dark is constant. The sound is constant. The absence is constant. The presence — whatever it is, in the trench, in the deep, in the pulse and the voices and the thing that cuts the measuring lines — is constant.

The lake is still taking. The water rises. The forest spreads. The stones sink deeper. The slurry settles. The pulse continues. And the place waits, and watches, and remembers, in its cold, dark, patient way, for whatever comes next.
