# The Nacre

There is no land. There is no sky. There is the water, and the plain beneath it, and the dim opalescent light that fills both.

The water is deep and cold and old. It sits in a basin that has no name, in a world that has no shore. The sun is a rumor here — a faint blue-grey memory filtering down from a surface so distant it might as well be the roof of the sky. The light is the color of deep ice, of twilight seen through closed eyelids. It is not darkness, but it is not light either. It is the place where light becomes suggestion, where seeing becomes imagining.

The water is cold. Not frozen — the mineral content and the pressure keep it liquid — but cold in the way that deep water is cold: a cold with weight, with presence, with patience. It presses. It seeps. It slows everything it touches. A current's turn is a meditation. The cold is not hostile. It is simply the condition of the world, the way the world is, the way it has always been.

The water is full. Full of mineral — dissolved calcium, dissolved silica, dissolved magnesium, dissolved salts and carbonates and phosphates — and full of the suspended remains of everything that has ever lived and died in it. The water is not clear. It is milky, opalescent, a liquid shell — and it catches the dim light and holds it, so that the water itself seems to glow faintly, a soft white luminescence that comes from everywhere and nowhere. Looking into the water is like looking into the inside of an ear, or the inside of a shell, or the inside of a body.

And the plain is beneath it. The plain is the floor of this world, and it is vast — a high plateau of accumulated mineral that extends past sight in every direction. It is a submerged continental shelf that never ends, or rather, it ends only at the edges of the world, where it drops away into the abyss. Here, in the center of the plateau, the water is shallower — not shallow, but shallower — and the plain rises toward the surface in places, coming within a few hundred meters of the distant, dim, ice-blue ceiling. The sense of elevation is not of height but of proximity: the feeling that the floor is pushing up toward you, that the water column is thinner here, that the weight of the world above is almost bearable.

The plain is made. There is no bedrock. There is no natural substrate. The plain is entirely built up of the accumulated remains of shelled creatures — trillions upon trillions of them, living and dying here for longer than there have been names for time. Their shells, their scales, their calcified bones, their mineralized tissues — all of it has settled, compacted, dissolved and re-precipitated, and over eons it has formed a crust that covers everything. The crust is nacreous — layered, pearly, iridescent in places — and it is the same material that lines the inside of shells. The plain is made of shell. The plain is a shell. The world is a shell.

The plain is old. It has been eroding for so long that it is nearly flat. The ancient mountains, the ancient ridges, the ancient structures of earlier ages — they have all been worn down. The plain is the end state of erosion, the place where everything has been ground to its final, flattest form. And yet it is not featureless. Scattered across it are presences — domes, spires, pipes, arches, channels, sinkholes — the last irregularities, the last structures that have not yet been erased. They are sparse. There is much space between them. The plain is mostly empty, mostly flat, mostly smooth — a white expanse of nacreous sediment that stretches to the edge of the visible, and beyond.

The structures are the bones of the world. They rise from the plain like the remains of a civilization, or like the remains of a body. There are domes — hemispherical, smooth, pearly — that might be the skulls of vast creatures, or the bubbles of ancient gas, or simply the places where mineral happened to accumulate around a nucleus of shell. Some are the size of hills. Some are the size of rooms. Some are intact. Some are broken, their interiors exposed, and the interiors are hollow and dark and filled with slow, dense water. There are spires — tall, narrow, tapering — that rise like chimneys, like towers, like the pipes of some drowned organ. Some are straight. Some are curved. Some are branched, like coral, like antlers, like the fingers of a hand reaching up. There are arches that span channels, and the channels run with slow, dense water that carries sediment from one part of the plain to another. The channels are carved into the nacre, or built up by the nacre, or both — the distinction is not clear. There are sinkholes where the plain has collapsed into cavities below, and the sinkholes are filled with darker water, with the water of the deep places, the water that has been trapped and is only now being released.

The structures look made. They look architectural. They have the regularity of intention — the curve of a dome, the straightness of a spire, the symmetry of an arch. But they are not made by hands. They are made by chemistry, by the slow accretion of mineral around a nucleus, by the flow of water through a channel, by the collapse of a cavity and the bridging of the gap. They are made by the same processes that make shells, that make coral, that make stalactites. They are made by the water, and the water is patient, and the water has been working for longer than anything.

The fauna is abundant. The plain is alive. It is covered with creatures — not plants, never plants, but animals of every kind, animals that have made the plain their world.

There are grazers. They move across the nacreous surface on hundreds of tiny feet, or on a single muscular foot, or on a trail of cilia, scraping the mineral film that grows there — the thin layer of bacteria and mineral precipitate and dissolved organic matter that coats every surface. They leave trails behind them — grooves, furrows, patterns that look like writing, like circuitry, like the tracks of something that has somewhere to be. The trails are covered by new sediment within days, or hours, and the sediment is the same nacre as the plain, and the plain remembers nothing.

There are filter-feeders. They anchor themselves to the structures — to the domes, to the spires, to the arches — and they strain the mineral-rich water. Their bodies are pale and soft and shaped like fans, like flowers, like the ribs of umbrellas, like the gills of a mushroom. Some are the size of a hand. Some are the size of a door. Some are colonial — thousands of individuals fused into a single structure that looks like a bush, like a tree, like a coral, but is entirely animal. The filter-feeders are the most plant-like creatures on the plain, and they are not plants. They are animals that have found the plant's niche and filled it with flesh.

There are burrowers. They live in the crust itself, tunneling through the accumulated remains of their ancestors, making passages that collapse and are re-dug. Some are worm-like — long, soft, blind, pale. Some are crab-like — hard-shelled, many-legged, scuttling through the tunnels on joints of chitin. Some are bivalve-like — two shells hinged together, grinding the nacre with their bodies, slowly, slowly, making space for themselves in the rock. The burrowers are the architects of the interior, the ones who make the plain hollow, who create the cavities that become sinkholes, who weaken the structures that eventually collapse.

There are predators. They move slowly through the water — long, pale, nearly transparent, their bodies shaped like ribbons, like arrows, like the shadows of fish. Their eyes are large and dark and adapted to the dim. Their mouths are wide and lined with rows of tiny, needle-like teeth, or with comb-like structures for straining smaller prey, or with beaks for crushing shells. They hunt by vibration, by chemical trace, by the faint electrical fields of living muscle. They are patient. They can wait for days, for weeks, in the shadow of a dome, for the right vibration, the right trace, the right moment. When they strike, it is fast — a blur of pale flesh, a cloud of disturbed sediment, and then stillness again.

There are the mineral-creatures. They are the strangest. They are animals that have incorporated mineral into their bodies — not just shells, but entire structures of nacre, of calcite, of aragonite. Some look like rocks that move — slow, lumbering, patient, grazing on the mineral film. Some look like plants that are not plants — branching, fan-shaped, rooted in the sediment, their bodies half-flesh and half-crystal. Some look like nothing that has a name — lumps of mineral with a soft core, or sheets of crystal with a pulse, or lattices of calcite that grow and shed and grow again. The mineral-creatures are the boundary between the living and the dead, between the made and the grown. They are alive, but they are also stone. They will die, and when they die they will become part of the plain, and the plain will be the same as it was before they lived, and different.

The ecology of the plain runs on chemistry and death. There are no plants, no algae, no photosynthesis. The base of the food chain is the mineral itself — the dissolved calcium, the dissolved silica, the dissolved minerals that the water carries. Some creatures extract these directly, building their bodies from the water. Some creatures feed on the sediment, on the accumulated dead, on the slow rain of mineral that falls from above. Some creatures feed on each other. The web of life is simple and ancient and runs on the energy of chemistry and the energy of the dead. It is a world of eaters and eaten, of filterers and grazers and hunters and hunted, of the living and the dead and the mineral that is both.

And the dead are everywhere. The plain is made of the dead. Every grain of sediment, every layer of nacre, every structure and dome and spire — it is all the remains of creatures that lived and died here. The plain is an ossuary, a catacomb, a library of the dead. And the living walk on it, and feed on it, and incorporate it into their bodies, and when they die they add to it. The plain grows, and the plain dissolves, and the balance between growth and dissolution is the heartbeat of the world.

The world is turbulent. This is the paradox — the plain is ancient, eroded, nearly flat, settled into its final form, and yet it is never still.

The water is in constant motion. Slow, dense currents carry mineral from one place to another — eroding here, depositing there, building and unbuilding simultaneously. The currents are driven by temperature gradients, by density differences, by the slow rotation of the world, by the gas that seeps from the dissolving crust below. They are not fast, but they are relentless. They have been flowing for eons. They will flow for eons more.

There are zones of precipitation, where the water is supersaturated and mineral is actively growing. In these zones, the plain is building — new layers of nacre accreting, new structures rising, new domes forming. The growth is slow — millimeters per year, centimeters per century — but it is visible if you watch for long enough. A dome grows. A spire extends. A channel fills in, or a new channel opens. The plain is alive with growth.

There are zones of dissolution, where the water is undersaturated and the plain is actively dissolving. In these zones, the old structures crumble, the old layers are eaten away, the plain collapses into cavities and sinkholes. The dissolution is also slow, but it is also visible. A dome weakens. A spire cracks. A channel deepens, or a new sinkhole opens. The plain is alive with decay.

The two processes are always happening at once, everywhere. The plain is building and unbuilding itself, constantly. In one place, a dome grows — a slow, patient accumulation of layer upon layer of nacre, rising meter by meter over millennia. In another place, a dome collapses — the interior dissolving, the shell weakening, the whole structure giving way in a slow-motion avalanche of sediment and dust. The collapse is not fast — it takes years, decades — but it is inevitable, and it sends clouds of mineral into the water that drift and settle and become the raw material for new growth elsewhere.

There are turbidity flows — sudden, rare, catastrophic events where a large area of the plain collapses at once. The collapse sends a dense cloud of sediment racing across the plain — a submarine avalanche, a density current, a river of mud and mineral and broken shell. The flow scours the surface, burying some structures and exposing others, carving new channels and filling old ones, reshaping the plain in hours. These flows are the storms of this world — unpredictable, violent, transformative. They are followed by long periods of stillness, of slow re-growth, of the plain healing itself, of the water clearing, of the sediment settling, of the world returning to its quiet, slow, patient state.

There are seeps — places where water rises from below, warmer, more mineral-rich, more acidic, more charged with gas. Around the seeps, the plain is different: more dissolved, more porous, more alive. The seeps are oases of chemical energy, and the creatures cluster around them — filter-feeders, grazers, predators, mineral-creatures — all drawn to the richness, all feeding on the chemistry, all adding their dead to the mineral that the seeps bring up. The seeps build structures too — chimneys of precipitated mineral, mounds of accumulated sediment, terraces of layered nacre. They are the volcanoes of this world, the places where the deep earth speaks, where the world below the plain pushes up and adds to the world above.

The water itself is turbulent. The mineral content shifts — sometimes the water is thick with suspended particles, milky, opaque, the visibility dropping to meters, to centimeters, to nothing. Sometimes it clears, and the visibility extends for kilometers, and the plain seems to stretch forever, and the structures rise from the white expanse like the ruins of a city seen from a distance. The shifts are driven by currents, by temperature, by gas, by the turbidity flows, by the slow breathing of the plain itself. The water is never the same twice.

The world is mostly quiet. The sounds of the plain are muffled by the dense, cold water. There is no wind, no waves, no crash of surf. The sounds are low and slow and close: the creak of a structure settling, the soft rain of sediment falling, the distant rumble of a collapse, the click and scrape of a grazer on the nacre, the low-frequency pulse of a predator's movement, the bubbling of a seep, the slow grind of a burrower in the crust. The sound of the world is the sound of something very large and very old breathing slowly — in, out, in, out — the breath of the plain itself. If you listen for long enough, you can hear the water moving, the mineral precipitating, the crust growing and dissolving, the world becoming itself.

The plain is weird. It is not natural, and it is not artificial. It is made, but it is not made by makers. It is built, but it is not built by builders. It is the accumulated remains of life, and it looks like architecture, and it behaves like geology, and it is alive with creatures that are both animal and mineral. The boundary between the living and the dead, between the made and the grown, between the structure and the creature — all of these boundaries are blurred here.

A dome might be a structure, or it might be a creature, or it might be the remains of a creature that became a structure. A channel might be carved by water, or it might be the trail of a creature, or it might be the body of a creature that died and hollowed out. A spire might be a mineral growth, or it might be the shell of a creature that grew for a thousand years, or it might be the cast of a creature that dissolved and left its shape in nacre. The plain is a place where categories fail, where the questions *what is it?* and *who made it?* and *is it alive?* have no answers, or too many answers, or answers that change depending on how long you look.

The plain is ancient. It has been here for longer than there has been life on the surface, longer than there has been land, longer than there has been a sun to light it. It has seen the surface world rise and fall, has felt the vibrations of catastrophes far above, has received the rain of dead things from the sunlit waters and added them to itself. It is old in the way that only the deep is old — old not in years but in stillness, in patience, in the slow accumulation of layer upon layer of time.

And it is settling. The erosion is winning, slowly, imperceptibly, but winning. The high points are being worn down. The low points are being filled in. The structures are being eroded. The plain is becoming flatter, smoother, more uniform. In a million years, it will be a perfect plane — a featureless expanse of nacre under a featureless column of water, the same in every direction, the same forever. The last structures will have dissolved. The last irregularities will have been erased. The world will have achieved its final, stable, dead form.

But not yet. Now, there are still domes and spires and channels and sinkholes. Now, there are still creatures grazing and filtering and burrowing and hunting. Now, the plain is still building and unbuilding itself, still growing and dissolving, still alive in its slow, cold, quiet way. Now, the water is still thick with mineral, still milky, still glowing faintly with its own opalescent light. Now, the world is still becoming.

The plain waits. The water waits. The creatures move slowly across the nacre, leaving trails that look like writing, and the writing is covered by new sediment, and the new sediment is the writing of the future, and the future is the same as the past, and the past is the same as the present, and the present is this: a cold, dim, deep, high, ancient, turbulent, quiet, abundant, sparse, made, living plain at the bottom of a sea that has forgotten the sun.
