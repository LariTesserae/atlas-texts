The plateau is high and open and entirely made. There is no natural ground—the substrate is plate, beam, poured compound, deck and duct and conduit, layered over whatever rock or soil existed before until nothing of the original remains exposed. The construction is dense and stacked, multi-level, structures growing out of structures, a built world with no gaps except the ones that have been made for gaps. It extends to the horizon in every direction, regional in scale, a town-or-valley of continuous manufacture, repeating and varying—here a stack of platforms, there a field of antennae, there a slow extrusion of fresh material rising from a vent in the deck.

Everything is sharp. The erosion here is young, the place still holding its manufactured edges, the corners crisp, the surfaces still textured with the pattern of their making. No weathering, no rounding, no patina. The poured compound is still smooth, the cut plate still bright, the welds still holding their rippled lines. It looks less like a place that has been built and more like a place that is *being* built, continuously, the construction never quite finishing, the newest sections still slightly warm, still off-gassing a faint chemical smell that mixes with the thin cold air.

The elevation keeps it cool. The sun is very bright—high-altitude light, thin-atmosphere light, almost white, flattening and amplifying, bouncing off the pale surfaces until the whole plateau seems to glow. Shadows are sharp and short and very dark. At midday the built surfaces are hot to the touch in the sun and cold in the shade, and the temperature difference is enough to make the metal tick and sing, a slow irregular percussion, the only real music the place makes.

Water is present as damp, not rain. At night the moisture in the air condenses on every surface—a fine sheen, beads on the undersides of decks, dark patches spreading on the pale compound, the whole plateau slowly sweating. In the morning the bright sun lifts it back into the air, and the surfaces steam faintly, and by midday they are dry. This is the primary rhythm of the place: the slow breathing of moisture in and out, night to day, condensation and evaporation, the damp cycle. It keeps the air cool and smells of mineral and machine.

Vegetation is sparse and pressed into the seams. Lichen on the bright-facing surfaces—grey-green, olive, rust-orange, slow circular growths that may be the oldest living things here. Moss in the joints and channels where condensation collects, dark green, thick, holding water like a sponge. Small tough things in the gaps between plates—not quite grass, not quite weed, something that has learned to root in a millimeter of accumulated dust and condensate. No trees, no shrubs, no soil to speak of. The green is an accent, a fringe, a colonization of the margins.

Fauna is abundant. The plateau is full of animals, or animal-like things, and they are mostly quiet. Birds nest in the ductwork and under the deck overhangs—small, grey, short-winged, adapted to the thin air and the vertical spaces. They move in quick bursts and then sit still for long periods, conserving energy. Mammals live in the crawlspaces and wall cavities—small, pale, many-legged or few-legged, things that have learned to navigate the made environment, to find the warm spots near the machinery, to drink from the condensation channels. Insects cluster in the damp zones, in the moss, in the warm exhaust from the generators. The reality breakdown has made some of them strange—a bird with too many joints in its wing, a mammal that moves in a way that doesn't quite parse, an insect that seems to be made of the same compound as the deck. They are real enough. They move, eat, breed, die. But they are made, not born—extruded or condensed or generated by the same process that built the plateau. The whole place is built, and that includes the animals.

The industrial tech is present everywhere but operates at a scale and pace that makes it almost invisible. Massive flywheels turn at geological speed behind housing walls, their rotation too slow to see, only the faintest hum in the floor giving them away. Conveyors move material at walking pace or slower, a steady procession of aggregate and compound, the production line patient and unhurried. Generators and transformers fill the air with a low electric hum, felt in the teeth more than heard. Cables and conduits run along every surface, bundled, layered, a second circulatory system under the skin of the place. The electrification is total—there is no point on the plateau where you cannot feel the presence of current—but it is quiet current, steady, unobtrusive.

The density is high. Many presences per volume—structures packed near-touching, layered above and below, the animals in the walls and rafters, the insects in the damp, the condensation beading and running on every surface, the current in the cables, the slow machinery in its housings. The plateau is saturated with made things and made life, and the density gives it a kind of pressure, a sense of being inside something rather than on top of it.

And in places, the reality breaks down. Not dramatically, not all at once, but slowly, gradually, the way the condensation spreads—a zone where the geometry is wrong, where a corridor is longer on the inside than the outside, where a platform repeats when you look at it twice, where the construction seems to fold back on itself in a way that made things shouldn't. The breakdown is silent. It doesn't make sound, and it swallows sound—a room where the hum of the generators stops, where the tick of cooling metal stops, where even your own footsteps are muffled and then gone. The breakdown spreads slowly, patient, and the construction seems to heal behind it, or maybe the breakdown is part of the construction, a feature of the process, the place building and unbuilding itself in the same slow motion. The animals avoid the breakdown zones, or they don't—sometimes a bird will fly into a wrong-geometry corridor and not come out, or come out changed.

The scale is open. You can see across the plateau, the structures repeating to the horizon, the bright light flattening distance, the haze of condensation or off-gassing softening the farthest edges. It is large enough to get lost in, to walk for hours without reaching an end, the features repeating and varying, the slow machinery continuing, the damp cycle turning.

The sound of the place is mostly quiet. The thin air doesn't carry sound well. The wind is high and thin and makes a faint continuous hiss, barely audible. The machinery is slow and deep, more vibration than noise. The animals are quiet. The condensation drips, irregularly, a slow percussion. The metal ticks as it cools and warms. And underneath everything, the faint electric hum, the sound of current in the cables, a sound so constant it becomes silence. In the breakdown zones, even that stops.

The light is very bright, almost too bright, the sun reflected and amplified by the pale surfaces until the whole plateau seems to emit light. At night the condensation catches what light remains, and the plateau gleams faintly, a vast made thing breathing damp into the dark.
