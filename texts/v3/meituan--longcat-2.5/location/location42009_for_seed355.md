# The Tepidarium

The basin is a broad, shallow dish in the earth, perhaps thirty kilometers across, its rim low and eroded into rounded hills that barely interrupt the horizon. The sky above is the color of old iron — a permanent, heavy overcast that admits no direct light. What illumination exists is a diffuse, brown-gray glow, sourceless, that makes everything look like it is already underwater. Which, in a sense, it is.

The floor of the basin is wet. Not flooded — the water is too shallow for that, ankle-deep across most of it — but saturated through and through. The ground is a dark, silty mud, warm as skin, pocked with pools and seeps and the slow, blind movement of channels that braid and reunite without pattern. The heat comes from below. The basin sits on a geothermal vein, and the water is blood-warm everywhere, hotter in some places, coolest where it spreads thin across the flats. Steam rises constantly — not in columns, but in a low, pervasive haze that softens every edge and makes the air thick, almost chewable. Every surface weeps. Rock faces run with condensation. The inside of your mouth tastes of minerals.

---

In the center of the basin, the city.

It is not built of stone in the way cities are usually built. There are no cut blocks, no tool marks, no mortar, no metal. It is built of accumulated mineral — travertine, tufa, calcite, aragonite — deposited over millennia by hot springs that rise through it still. The structures are organic in form: domes that resemble the inside of a skull seen from below, arches that are really frozen cascades, columns of flowstone that branch like root systems inverted. But they are *organized*. There is a plan. A central avenue, broad and slightly curved, follows the path of a major spring line. Off it, narrower passages branch at irregular but deliberate intervals — not grid-like, but purposeful, as if the city grew around needs rather than geometries. There are open plazas: shallow, water-filled basins ringed by low walls, their bottoms paved with smooth, rippled mineral. There are chambers, some roofed with vaults of layered, porous rock, some open to the dim sky. The tallest structures rise perhaps fifteen meters — towers of accreted mineral, their surfaces pocked and honeycombed, their tops fringed with deposits that resemble nothing so much as frozen lace, or the gills of some enormous fungus.

The best analogy is a coral reef. Or the nest of a termite colony the size of a city. Or the fruiting body of a thing that grew rather than decided. The builder is gone — or dormant, or transformed into the architecture itself. Whether it was a colony organism that secreted these forms, or whether the mineral-laden water achieved some slow, crystalline intentionality, is not a question the city answers. It simply is.

---

No animals. This is the first thing you notice, though you cannot say why you notice it so quickly. There are no insects in the steam. No birds on the towers. No fish in the pools. Nothing with eyes. Nothing that moves on its own. The silence is profound — not the silence of a place where sound is absent, but the silence of a place where sound is *not yet*. The only noises are water: the drip of condensation from overhanging stone, the low gurgle of channels, the occasional soft pop of a gas bubble breaking the surface of a spring. Sometimes, beneath these, there is a deeper sound — a subsonic hum that seems to come from the ground itself, felt in the sternum more than heard, that might be geological or might be the city breathing in its sleep.

The only living things are sparse and marginal. Dark green films of thermophilic algae on the warmest rocks, spreading in slow, oily sheets. Pale bacterial mats in the shallower pools — ochre, dull green, a brownish red — that tremble slightly when the water moves over them. A few rootless, leafless growths cling to the walls of the oldest structures: things the color of old ivory, branching and rebranching in patterns that almost resemble script, that may be plants or may be mineral deposits that happen to look like plants or may be something that is neither. Nothing taller than a hand's width. Nothing that flowers or fruits or dies in any visible way. They have been here as long as the city has, and they will be here when it is gone.

---

And the strangeness.

Reality in the basin is not stable. It is not dramatically unstable — there are no lightning bolts, no floating boulders, no screaming voids — but it is *wrong* in a way that accumulates the longer you stay. Distances are inconsistent. The central plaza, which you crossed in twenty paces on the way in, takes forty minutes to cross on the way out, though you walked at the same speed. The towers cast shadows in directions that do not agree with each other. Water in some channels flows uphill — not quickly, not obviously, but steadily, persistently, as if the concept of downhill is a suggestion rather than a law. Reflections in the pools do not always match what stands above them. Sometimes they show a different arrangement of towers — taller, denser, more complete. Sometimes they show a sky.

Time moves unevenly. In some passages, condensation drips at a normal rate. In others, drops hang suspended from stone lips for minutes before falling — or fall before they have formed, striking the pool below and *then* beading on the rock above. You might walk the same street twice and find it unchanged in every respect except that the mineral growth on the walls is different — thicker in one pass, thinner in another, as if the city is slowly building itself and unbuilding itself in alternation, and you have caught it on different phases of its breath.

The steam is the worst. It does not always behave like steam. Sometimes it hangs in shapes that persist too long — that have edges, that have *interiors* — that resemble figures, or buildings, or memories of structures that are not there. They do not move. They do not dissolve on any normal timescale. They simply stand in the haze, almost legible, almost meaningful, until the slow convection of the warm air finally pulls them apart. If you walk toward them, they are always farther away than they appeared. If you walk away, they do not get smaller.

There are rooms in the city that are larger inside than outside. You can stand in the doorway and see this — the exterior wall says one thing, the interior space says another, and the discrepancy does not resolve no matter how you look at it. There are staircases that lead down into warm, shallow water and, if you follow them, lead up into the same water from below. There are chambers where the sound of dripping echoes before the drop falls, where the echo arrives from a direction the drop does not fall toward.

The city might be a memory. It might be a city that existed and exists and will exist, all at once, its temporal boundaries dissolved by the heat and the water and whatever residue the builders left behind. It might be dreaming — slowly, geologically dreaming — and the instability is the dream leaking into the world. Or it might be *still building*, still extruding itself from the warm, mineral-rich water, and what you see as decay is only construction viewed from the wrong angle, the wrong time, the wrong state of matter.

---

The basin extends beyond the city in all directions, and each direction has its own character.

To the east, the ground rises slightly into a maze of low, eroded ridges, their surfaces crusted with mineral deposits in layers — white, cream, pale orange — like the skin of something that has been dipped in wax repeatedly. The valleys between them are full of warm, shallow water, and the ridges are perforated with holes: circular, smooth-walled, ranging from a hand's width to a body's width, as if the rock was drilled by something patient and precise. The holes go down into darkness. They are all wet inside.

To the west, a broad, flat expanse of mud and scattered pools extends to the base of the rim. The pools here are larger, some tens of meters across, their surfaces covered with bacterial mats that shift color as you watch — ochre to green to rust — responding to something in the water chemistry or something in the light. There are no structures here, only the flat wet ground and the slow, braided channels that cross it.

To the north, the springs are more active. Steam rises more thickly, and the water is hotter — too hot to hold your hand in. The mineral deposits here are newer, brighter, less weathered. The ground is crusty and uneven, breaking into plates and ridges, and the water runs in quick, shallow streams that move with an unusual sense of purpose, as if going somewhere specific.

To the south, the city gives way to a zone of smaller, less defined mineral growths — lumps, mounds, suggestions of walls that never became walls. It looks like the city is slowly extending itself, or slowly forgetting how to be a city. The growth here is more chaotic, less organized, and the reality is more unstable — distances shift more noticeably, sounds arrive from wrong directions, the steam-shapes are more numerous and more detailed. If you walk far enough south, the growths stop entirely, and there is only wet ground and warm water and the dim, sourceless light.

The rim of the basin is low and broken. Beyond it, the land continues — but the basin is its own world, defined by its darkness, its heat, its wetness, its silence, and its slow, patient wrongness. Nothing that enters it leaves unchanged. Nothing that leaves it is quite the same. The city stands at its center, empty and organized and dreaming, extruding itself from the warm water one mineral layer at a time, waiting for something that may already be here, or may never come, or may be the same thing as the city itself, seen from the outside.
