The land is flat. Not gently rolling, not a plain with swells and hollows, but flat — a surface worn down over geological time to the last possible smoothness, a peneplain that has forgotten it was ever mountains. The flatness is the end of erosion: there is nothing left to erode, no slope to drive water, no rock to expose. What remains is the Sleeve.

The Sleeve is a living mat, a single contiguous organism that covers the entire basin. It is the substrate. There is no soil, no stone, no bare ground — only the Sleeve, a dense, photosynthetic skin that ranges in color from deep emerald to near-black in its older, thicker parts, to a vivid, almost luminous green where it is new. It is watertight. It has to be, because water is everywhere: warm, frequent rain falls on the basin, and the Sleeve does not absorb it. Instead, the water sheets across the surface in thin, gleaming films, pooling in the slight depressions that the Sleeve's own uneven growth creates, connecting in a web of shallow channels that shift and reform as the mat grows. The air is saturated; condensation beads on every surface and drips in a constant, gentle percussion. The temperature is warm, always warm, and the heat shimmers above the wet green.

The Sleeve is ancient. It has been growing for millions of years, and in that time it has consumed the original geology — dissolved the rock, filled the valleys, planed the hills — until the surface is a single, continuous living sheet. It is the oldest stable state of the land, the final form into which everything has settled. But it is not inert. It grows. It breathes. It creaks. If you stand still, you can feel it through your feet: a slow, rhythmic swelling, a pulse that is not quite a heartbeat, as the mat pumps water and nutrients through its tissues. If you watch, you can see it creep — a bud swelling, a filament extending, a patch of new growth brightening from olive to green over the course of an hour. It is a landscape that is simultaneously at the end of its geological life and furiously, hyperactively alive.

Scattered across the Sleeve, at wide intervals, are the structures of the hamlet of Ilde. There are perhaps eight of them, spread over a hundred meters of open green, each one a distinct presence in the emptiness. The hamlet is bounded by a ring of still growth — a shallow, circular channel where the Sleeve is shorter and denser, forming a natural wall that holds back the wild mat. Inside the ring, the ground is more level, more deliberate. The structures are grown, not built. The settlers of Ilde do not cut or assemble; they plant seeds. A seed is a small, dense object, warm to the touch, that contains instructions at the molecular level. The seeds are old — older than the hamlet, older than the settlers — inherited, traded, hoarded, each one a fragment of a technology that no one living fully understands. When pressed into the Sleeve, a seed directs the mat's growth with atomic precision, coaxing it to form walls, floors, vaults, chambers. The resulting buildings are alive — living tissue, hardened and shaped, but still part of the Sleeve. They breathe. They heal. They grow.

The largest structure is the gathering hall, a cathedral-grove. It is a single hollow vault, perhaps twenty meters tall and ten across, grown from the mat as one continuous piece. Its walls are interwoven living strands, dense and woody at the base, becoming finer and more translucent toward the crown, where they filter the daylight into a green-gold glow. The floor is a raised platform, dry, above the water film. Inside, the acoustics are strange — the vault catches sound and holds it, so a whisper at the center can be heard at the edge, and a handclap returns as a soft, sustained chord. The walls are warm, and they pulse faintly, in time with the Sleeve.

The other structures are smaller: dwellings, workshops, storage chambers, a nursery. They are arranged around a central pool, connected by bridges and causeways that the Sleeve has been asked to raise above the water. Channels radiate from the pool, managed by sluices that are themselves living valves — rings of tissue that open and close in response to the water level, or to a touch, or to a command carried through the mat. The settlement is organized, deliberate, layered. Some buildings have two or three levels, reached by ramps or by stairs that grow out of the floor and are reabsorbed when no longer needed. There are conduits — thick, ropey strands that run between buildings, carrying nutrients, water, and signals. The mat itself is a communication network; a vibration at one node is felt at all the others, and the settlers have learned to read it.

The technology of Ilde is beyond current physics. The Sleeve is not merely a biological organism; it is a quantum-coherent system, a macroscopic quantum computer woven from living tissue. It processes information, coordinates growth across kilometers, and exhibits nonlocal effects. The seeds exploit this: they do not just instruct the mat chemically, they entangle with it, so that a change in the seed is instantaneously reflected in the growth of the building, regardless of distance. Some rooms in the hamlet are "tuned" — their walls are grown to precise geometries that alter local physics. There is a chamber where time runs slightly faster, used for growing food quickly; a chamber where gravity is a little lighter, where the water climbs the walls in defiance of expectation and the children float and laugh; a chamber where sound behaves strangely, where you can hear conversations from kilometers away, or where your own voice returns to you with words you have not yet spoken. The settlers do not fully understand how these rooms work; they know the geometries, the seeds, the sequences of growth, but the underlying physics is beyond them. They are engineers of a technology they did not invent and cannot fully explain.

The fauna is abundant and loud. Insects are the base of it: beetles that click and whir, flies that form clouds, dragonflies that hover and dart, moths that beat against the translucent walls of the cathedral-grove. Ants run in trails across the Sleeve, farming the mat's secretions. Frogs croak from the pools, and small, bright birds flit between the structures, calling in sharp, repetitive phrases. There are things in the water — larvae, tiny fish, translucent worms — and things in the mat itself: burrowing creatures that you can hear moving beneath the surface, a constant rustle and click. The sound is a wall: the insect chorus, the bird calls, the frog croaks, the drip and splash of water, the creak and crack of growth, the low hum of the Sleeve (infrasound, felt more than heard), the breathing of the buildings (a slow, rhythmic expansion and contraction), the voices of the settlers, the flapping of wings, the rustle of leaves, the occasional deep, resonant tone that comes from the tuned chambers. It is loud, layered, rhythmic — a bazaar of sound.

The place is turbulent. The Sleeve grows visibly; you can watch a bridge extend itself over a channel in the course of a day, or a wall heal from a cut in an hour. Flowers bloom and collapse in minutes. The water level rises and falls with the mat's breathing, and the channels shift. Insects swarm and disperse. The weather is unstable — a sudden warm rain, then bright sun, then a gust of wind that sends the water film rippling. The settlement is constantly being adjusted: a new room here, a repaired bridge there, a channel rerouted. The settlers move through it with the ease of people who live in a place that is never finished, who expect the ground to change beneath them. It is anthill-energy, bazaar-energy — hyperactive, chaotic, but not random. There is a pattern to the turbulence, a rhythm that the settlers have learned to ride.

The weirdness is in the Sleeve's awareness. It is not intelligent in a human way, but it is responsive. It reacts to touch, to sound, to intention. If you walk across it, it knows where you are; it can thicken under your feet, or soften, or grow a handhold. If you speak to it — and the settlers do, in a language of taps and tones — it can answer, in its own way: a change in color, a shift in texture, a movement in the growth. There are thin places, where the boundary between the Sleeve and the buildings blurs, where you cannot tell where the organism ends and the architecture begins. There are ghost structures: half-grown things that were abandoned, or that grew wrong, or that are still growing, slowly, into shapes no one intended. One, near the edge of the ring, is a tower that grew in a spiral and then stopped, its top open to the rain; it hums in certain winds. There are memories in the Sleeve: if you touch certain places, you may see or hear things from the past — a fragment of a conversation, an image of a structure that no longer exists, a feeling that is not your own. The oldest memories are said to be from before the Sleeve, from the time of rock and soil; they are faint, fragmentary, and unsettling. The settlers say the Sleeve dreams, and that its dreams sometimes manifest as temporary growths: a flower that should not exist, a chamber that appears overnight and is gone by morning, a path that leads somewhere else.

The light is normal — daylight, sun, cloud — but it is filtered through humidity and the translucent walls of the buildings, so it is soft, green-gold, dappled. The air is warm and wet, thick with the smell of green growth, of rot and flowers and nectar, of insects and ozone. The heat shimmers above the water. Everything glistens. The Sleeve is deep green, almost black in shadow, bright green where new. The water is silver, reflecting the sky. The buildings glow when backlit. It is a place that is at once ancient and newborn, stable and turbulent, empty and full, quiet and loud. It is the end of the land and the beginning of something else, a flat green world that has settled into its final form and is still, furiously, becoming.
