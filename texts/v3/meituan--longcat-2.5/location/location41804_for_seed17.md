**The Cold Foundry**

A world of water. Not a world with an ocean — a world that *is* an ocean. The surface is a thin, cold skin where the water meets a pale, thin atmosphere, and below that skin there is only water, all the way down, for thousands of kilometers. No land. No ice. No islands. Just water and the rock beneath it.

The water is cold — one degree above freezing at the surface, half a degree above freezing in the depths. It's clear. Exceptionally clear. There is almost no sediment, almost no particulate matter. The light from the star above — a pale, white-yellow star — filters down through the water, diffused into a uniform, shadowless, blue-green glow. It's normal light. Like a cloudy day. Like a well-lit room. You can see for kilometers in every direction, but there's mostly nothing to see.

The seafloor is not flat. There are mountains. Vast undersea ranges, rising four, five, six kilometers from the abyssal plain. The elevation is high — the mountains are taller than any on land, but they're underwater, so no one sees them. They're young. Geologically young — maybe only two or three million years old. The rock is fresh basalt, black and glassy. The ridges are knife-edge sharp. The slopes are steep, unweathered, uneroded. There's no sediment on them. No soil. No smoothing. The terrain looks like it was made yesterday and then locked in place. The mountains are the spines of something that pushed up from below and then stopped pushing.

A few peaks break the surface. Tiny, barren, rocky islands, no more than a few hundred meters across. They're the only land on the planet. They're covered in salt spray and the bones of creatures that crawled onto them and died. No plants. No moss. No lichen. Just rock and salt and the sound of waves. The waves are small — the atmosphere is thin, the winds are weak — but they're constant. They break against the rocks with a hiss and a crash that echoes across the water. The islands are eroding, but so slowly that the erosion is imperceptible. They look like they've been there forever and will be there forever.

The ocean is vast. The scale is continental, planetary. The mountain ranges are separated by thousands of kilometers of abyssal plain — flat, featureless, covered in a thin layer of mineral dust. The water column is kilometers deep. The distances are enormous. And it's empty. Almost nothing per volume. Vast stretches of water with nothing in them. No animals. No particles. No sound sources. Just water, light, and the distant hum of machines.

But where there is life, it's abundant. The fauna clusters around the hydrothermal vents that dot the mountain slopes — hot, chemical-rich water seeping from the crust. And around the industrial installations. And in the upwelling zones. The creatures are strange. Not quite fish. Not quite anything. Some are large — whale-sized, with vast, slow-moving bodies and mouths like caves. They filter the water for the swarms of smaller creatures. The swarms are clouds of millions — tiny, bioluminescent, crackling with electricity. They move as one, like a single organism, like a cloud of insects. There are other creatures too — things with too many limbs, things with no limbs, things that are transparent, things that are made of something that isn't flesh. Some are mechanical — they have gears, pistons, wires. They're cyborgs, or they're machines that look like creatures, or they're creatures that have incorporated machine parts. The boundary between the natural and the artificial is blurred. They're bioluminescent — many of them — adding their own light to the filtered sunlight. The light is a mix of the pale blue-green from above and the flickering, pulsing colors of the creatures below.

There are no plants. No algae. No kelp. No phytoplankton. The base of the food chain is chemosynthetic — bacteria that feed on the chemicals from the vents. The creatures eat the bacteria, or eat each other, or feed on the minerals in the water. The ecosystem is strange, unusual, but stable. It has been stable for millions of years.

The sound is deafening. The water carries sound efficiently, and there's a lot of it. The hydrothermal vents roar and hiss — a constant, low-frequency rumble. The industrial installations rumble and clank — engines, drills, crushers, furnaces. The whale-sized creatures sing — deep, resonant calls that travel for thousands of kilometers, that you feel in your bones before you hear them. The swarms crackle and pop — millions of tiny electrical discharges. The rock itself creaks and groans as it adjusts to the pressure. There's no silence. The baseline is a roar. A constant, all-encompassing roar. You can't hear yourself think. You can't hear anything else over the roar. It's like being inside a machine, inside an engine, inside a world that never stops making noise. And it's structured — it has rhythms, patterns, harmonies. It's like music, or like language, or like the sound of a vast machine. The creatures contribute to it, the machines contribute to it, the vents contribute to it, the rock contributes to it. It's a symphony of noise, constant, unchanging, eternal.

The industrial installations are scattered across the seafloor. Maybe a dozen across the entire planet. They're small relative to the vastness — a few hundred meters across, at most. But they're complex. They're built of metal — dark, rust-red, covered in mineral deposits. They have engines, pipes, chimneys, drills, crushers, furnaces. They're mining the seafloor, or drilling into the crust, or processing something from the vents. They're old — ancient, maybe. But they're still running. Automated, self-maintaining, self-repairing. They've been running for millions of years, and they'll run for millions more. They're alive, or they seem alive — they pulse with heat, they breathe with currents, they grow mineral shells, they repair themselves with something that looks like scar tissue. They're loud — their machinery adds to the deafening soundscape. Their lights add to the illumination — harsh, artificial, yellow-white, cutting through the blue-green. Their heat adds to the water — warm plumes rising from their chimneys, creating local currents, attracting creatures.

The structures are the only built things on the planet. No roads. No cities. No other marks. Just these scattered installations, these isolated outposts of industry in an otherwise empty ocean. They're like stars in a dark sky — isolated points in an infinite void.

The whole system is in equilibrium. Nothing changes. The terrain is young but stable — the tectonic forces that created it have ceased, or are so slow they're imperceptible. The climate is stable — the water temperature has been the same for millions of years. The ecosystem is stable — the populations of creatures are constant, the food web is balanced. The machines run but don't degrade — they repair themselves, they replace their parts, they maintain themselves. The sound is constant. The light is constant. It's a world in perfect stasis, a snapshot that lasts forever.

The weirdness is in the details. The creatures have too many limbs, or no limbs, or they're transparent, or they're made of something that isn't flesh. The machines are built in shapes that don't make sense — curves that are too organic, angles that are too precise — or they're made of materials that shouldn't work at these pressures, or they're producing something that has no obvious purpose. The light is slightly the wrong color — too green, too blue, too uniform — and it pulses in a way that suggests something is behind it. The sound has a frequency that doesn't come from anything identifiable — a hum that's always there, a signal that might be a message or might be a machine or might be something else.

The scale is overwhelming. The ocean is so vast that you can't see the other side. The mountains are so large that they have their own weather — currents, upwellings, thermal gradients. The distances are so great that the creatures migrate for thousands of kilometers. The emptiness is so complete that the few structures, the few clusters of life, are like stars in a dark sky — isolated points in an infinite void.

It is a cold, clear, deafening, empty, vast, young, stable, industrial ocean. A world of water and rock and machines and strange life, frozen in equilibrium, loud beyond endurance, empty beyond comprehension. A world that has been running for billions of years and will run for billions more — never changing, never stopping, never silent.
