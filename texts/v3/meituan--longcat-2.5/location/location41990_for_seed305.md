The plateau has no name. It has no need of one. It is not a place that is visited; it is a place that is. It sits at the roof of the world, or what passes for the roof of this world — a vast elevated plain, young enough that its edges are still sharp, still growing, still pushing upward through the crust with the slow insistence of something that has not yet decided what shape it will take. The rock is dark, almost black, shot through with veins of something lighter that catches the light and holds it. The mountains that ring the plateau are not old mountains worn smooth by time. They are new. Their peaks are knife-edges. Their faces are fresh scars where the rock has split and not yet healed. They look like something that happened recently, geologically speaking — which, at this scale, means within the last few million years.

The plateau is large enough to have its own weather. Storms form over its center and dissipate against its edges. Winds circulate in patterns that do not match the winds of the lowlands. The air is thin, cold, and dry, but the plateau itself is damp — moisture collects in its pores, in its cracks, in the dense mat of vegetation that covers it. The moisture does not come from rain, mostly. It comes from condensation, from the meeting of cold rock and warmer air, from the slow exhalation of the vegetation itself. The plateau sweats. It is damp the way a cellar is damp, the way a lung is damp — not wet, but never dry. The dampness has a smell: mineral, sharp, like hot stone and cold iron and something organic underneath, something that might be fungi or might be something else. The smell is constant. It is the smell of the plateau's industry, the smell of its thinking.

The light is intense. The plateau sits high enough that the atmosphere is thin, that the air does little to filter the sun. The light is white, almost clinical, the light of a laboratory or an operating theater. It falls without shadow, or with shadows so sharp they look cut. The snow that lies in the sheltered places is not white but blue-white, blinding, and it reflects the light upward so that the underside of clouds, the underside of wings, the underside of leaves are all lit from below. There is no softness to the light. It is the light of a place that has not learned to be gentle. The light is also the plateau's power source. The mineral veins conduct it. The crystals focus it. The vegetation absorbs it. The whole system runs on light. At noon, the light is so intense that it is difficult to look at the ground, difficult to look at the snow, difficult to look at anything. The light presses down. It has weight.

The cold is constant. It is not the cold of winter, which comes and goes. It is the cold of altitude, of thin air, of a place that receives the sun's light but not its warmth. The cold is a presence. It is in the rock, in the water, in the air. It is in the vegetation, which has adapted to it by becoming something that is almost but not quite like the plants of lower, warmer places. The leaves are small, thick, waxy. They are dark, almost black, to absorb what little heat the sun provides. They grow close to the ground, forming mats, cushions, dense interlocking carpets that leave no bare soil visible. The vegetation is lush — there is no other word for it — but it is lush the way a coral reef is lush, or a bacterial colony, or a factory floor: dense, packed, every surface occupied, every niche filled. The vegetation is also cold to the touch. It does not warm in the sun. It stays cold, as if the cold is not just a condition but a property, as if the vegetation has incorporated the cold into itself.

And it moves. Not with the wind — though the wind moves it — but with its own slow rhythm, a pulsing, a contraction and expansion, as if the entire carpet of vegetation were breathing. The pulsing is slow, measured in minutes, not seconds. It is synchronized across vast distances. Stand on a ridge and watch the vegetation below and you will see waves of contraction pass across the plateau, slow ripples of movement that travel for miles before dissipating. The cause is not clear. It may be hydraulic — the movement of water through the dense root systems. It may be chemical — the release and absorption of gases. It may be something else. The vegetation does not explain itself. The pulsing is part of the computation. The plateau is thinking, and the vegetation is its thoughts. The thoughts are slow, vast, and patient. A single thought might take a thousand years to complete.

The fauna is overwhelming. The word is not hyperbole. There are simply too many animals. They are in the vegetation, on the vegetation, under the vegetation. They are in the air, in the water, in the rock. They are small — most of them are small — but their numbers are incomprehensible. Insects, or things like insects, cloud the air in columns that rise hundreds of feet, that cast shadows, that hum with a sound like distant machinery. The columns are not random. They rise from specific places, at specific times, in specific patterns. They look like smokestacks. They look like exhaust. The insects that form them are dark, metallic, almost mechanical. They move in synchronized waves, their wings catching the light in flashes that travel up the columns like signals. Things that are not quite birds wheel in flocks that darken the sky for minutes at a time. The flocks move in patterns that look like circuit diagrams, like flow charts, like quality control. They circle, inspect, move on. Things that are not quite mammals move through the vegetation in waves, their bodies pressing the plants down into visible paths that close behind them like water. The paths are not random. They form networks, distribution systems, logistics. The mammals are small, furred, quick. They have too many legs, or the wrong number of legs, or legs that move in ways that legs should not move. They are not aware of the patterns they form. They are simply following the logic of the system, the logic of the plateau's industry.

The fauna is loud. The combined sound of their calls, their movements, their wings, their bodies is a constant roar, a wall of sound that does not vary, that does not pause, that is as much a feature of the landscape as the rock or the light. The sound is not chaotic. It has structure. It has rhythm. It has harmonics. It sounds like machinery, like engines, like production lines. It sounds like the plateau's voice, the sound of its thinking, the sound of its industry. The sound is so constant that after a time it becomes a kind of silence, a background against which other sounds become noticeable only by their absence. A pause in the sound would be shocking. A silence would be unbearable. The plateau does not permit silence. The sound is part of the computation. The sound is part of the industry. The sound is part of the mind.

The water is intermittent. It is not absent — the plateau is damp, not dry — but it is not abundant. It comes from melting snow, from geothermal steam, from condensation on cold surfaces. It collects in pockets, in depressions, in the cracks of the rock. The pools are small, rarely more than a few meters across, and they are everywhere. They are full of life — things that swim, things that float, things that cling to the edges. The water is cold, barely above freezing, and it is clear, so clear that the life in it seems suspended in air. The pools steam slightly where they are heated from below, where the geothermal activity that shapes the plateau reaches the surface. The steam rises and condenses and falls again as a fine mist that coats everything, that keeps the vegetation damp even when it does not rain. The mist is not random. It forms patterns. It flows in channels. It circulates. It is part of the computation. It is part of the industry. It is part of the mind. The water is the plateau's nervous system, the medium through which signals travel, the medium through which the plateau knows itself.

The industry is in the rock. The young mountains are rich in minerals — not just rich, but unnaturally rich, concentrated in veins and seams and deposits that look almost manufactured. The minerals are not just present; they are growing. Crystals form in the cracks, pushing the rock apart, creating new veins, new seams, new deposits. The process is slow — it takes years for a crystal to grow a centimeter — but it is constant. The plateau is slowly filling itself with minerals, with crystals, with elements that do not occur naturally in these concentrations. The process is not biological. It is geological. But it looks industrial. The mineral veins look like circuitry. The crystal formations look like machinery. The whole plateau looks like a factory that is slowly assembling itself out of rock. The minerals are the plateau's memory, its way of storing what it has learned. The crystals are the plateau's processors, its way of thinking. The rock is the plateau's body, its way of being.

And the life is part of it. The vegetation draws minerals from the rock, concentrates them in its tissues, deposits them in its leaves and stems. When the vegetation dies — and it dies constantly, in vast quantities, because it is so dense that it is always competing with itself for light — the minerals are returned to the soil, to the water, to the rock. The fauna eats the vegetation, concentrates the minerals further, distributes them across the plateau in its movements. When the fauna dies — and it dies constantly, in vast quantities, because it is so numerous that it is always competing with itself for food — the minerals are returned to the soil, to the water, to the rock. The cycle is slow, measured in decades, in centuries, but it is constant. The plateau is slowly being refined, purified, concentrated. The life is the refinery. The geology is the factory. The whole system is an industrial process that happens to be alive.

The weirdness is pervasive. It is in the way the vegetation pulses. It is in the way the fauna moves in patterns that look like machinery. It is in the way the minerals grow in shapes that look manufactured. It is in the way the light is too bright, too even, too clinical. It is in the way the cold is intense but the vegetation is lush. It is in the way the scale is vast but the density is packed. It is in the way the industry is present but nothing is built. It is in the way the place feels like a factory and a wilderness at the same time, like a machine and an organism, like something that is alive and something that is made. The weirdness is not in any one thing. It is in the combination, in the way the things fit together, in the way the place works. The plateau is a mind, but a mind that is so vast, so slow, so alien that it is almost indistinguishable from a landscape. It thinks in minerals, in vegetation, in fauna, in light, in water, in cold. It thinks in slow, vast, geological thoughts. A single thought might take a thousand years. The creatures that live on it — the insects, the birds, the mammals, the things in the water — are not aware of this. They are too small, too fast, too brief. They are part of the thought, but they do not know it.

The slowness is the final feature. Everything here happens slowly. The mountains grow slowly. The crystals grow slowly. The vegetation spreads slowly. The fauna moves slowly, in aggregate, even though individually they are quick. The water moves slowly. The sound changes slowly. The light changes slowly, over the course of days, of seasons, of years. The only thing that happens quickly is death — the insects die in clouds, the mammals die in waves, the vegetation dies in patches — but even death is slow here, because there is so much of it, because it is constant, because it is part of the process. The plateau is patient. It has been growing for millions of years. It will continue to grow for millions more. It is not in a hurry. It is not going anywhere. It is simply becoming itself, slowly, in the bright cold light, with the sound of its own industry in its ears.
