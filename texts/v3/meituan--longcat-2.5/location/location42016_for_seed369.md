**The Weeping Bowl**

The plateau is old basalt, rounded like kneecaps under grass, and in its middle the land simply lets go — a sinkhole opened when the cavern beneath it collapsed, long before anything with hands was around to see it. The hole is the size of a cathedral laid on its side: a rough oval, a hundred and forty paces at its widest, walled on all sides by dark columnar rock that weathers into the shapes of organ pipes, bearded with moss. The few who come here call it the place where the stone sweats.

The walls weep. Not dramatically — nothing so vulgar as a waterfall, except one — but everywhere: water comes out of the basalt's seams and joints as if the rock were slowly perspiring, darkening whole faces to black-green. The moss on the walls is two fingers thick and squelches under a hand. Filmy ferns hang in green veils where the seeps are constant. Near the middle of the north wall the water gathers and descends as a sheet, three or four meters wide, a quiet continuous fall that lands on an apron of stone and spreads into the floor. The whole place is wet to the point of saturation; the air is water held in solution, warm as breath.

The floor is not land, exactly. It floats. A mat of sphagnum and sedge, moss and stolon, spans the basin from wall to wall, and it is buoyant: step on it and the whole surface dips and wobbles and sends slow waves out to the far walls, where they come back. Under the mat is brown water and the slow compost of centuries. Scattered across it are pools — lochans — open water in round and crescent shapes, some no wider than a cloak, one at the center perhaps twenty paces across and dark enough to hide its depth. Between them, hummocks of brighter green rise a foot or two, and on the hummocks sit the Bowl's presences, spaced far apart like statues in a hall: tree ferns taller than a person, their fronds a green wheel at the top of a fibrous trunk; giant rosette plants with silver-backed leaves that turn to face the diffused light; one or two stunted, moss-draped trees at the margins, bent by the rim winds into permanent bows.

The density is deliberate-feeling but isn't. There is room between things. Empty moss-carpet, empty water, and then a single plant standing in its own space. Nothing crowds. The result reads as a grove arranged by a quiet mind.

Warmth: the Bowl sits high — above the cloud line on clear days, inside it on others — but it is warm, and it stays warm. Two seeps at the eastern wall are warm to the touch, and around them the minerals have built low rims and terraces, banded rust-white and jade. The pools they feed are a milky blue-green, faintly soapy, leaving pale crusts on the moss where they overflow. A thread of steam stands above the warmest pool even at midday. Frost has never been recorded here; nothing freezes; the growing never stops.

Sound is the dominant sense, because water is everywhere and the walls make a box of it. The Weeping's sheet-fall is the loudest single voice, a continuous murmur — not loud like a river, but loud like a room full of people whispering. Under it: the hiss of a hundred small seeps, the plink of drips finding pools, the gurgle where one pool spills into its neighbor, and the mat itself, liquid and wobbling, with subterranean squelches. The basalt columns take the rim wind and hum, low, like breath across pipe mouths. And the Bowl returns sound: an echo that arrives a half-beat late and slightly lower than the voice that made it, so that a call in the Bowl comes back as an answer. A whisper at the floor reaches the rim intact; the place is shaped to carry sound upward and out.

Fauna is rare, and mostly small. A single falcon owns the airspace, circling the rim or sitting on a column stub; it stoops sometimes and the frogs flinch. A pair of frogs in the central pool. Dragonflies in season, hovering in the still air like held sparks. Midges in the mat's wettest corners. Moths at dusk, pale, tapping the water. Once in a while, at the rim's grass edge, a small deer-like animal comes down to drink at the pools — alone, wary, gone by the time the mist thickens. No fish. The water is too soft, too acidic, too still.

Human trace: on the rim, at the notch where a stream-cut stair descends to the floor, there is a flat slab with hollows worn into it — bedrock mortars, seven of them, smooth as cups, now half-filled with moss and rainwater. Generations of seed-grinding; the slab sits at the right height, the right dryness. Scattered near it are flakes of dark glassy stone, a few cores, and one finished hand-axe of black obsidian, fist-sized, still sharp, sitting on a dry hummock of moss as if it had just been set down. A low rock beside the slab carries an ochre smear, and beside that, two faded handprints, the pigment leached to a pale rust. Between the slab and the water there is a line of flat stones across the narrowest pool — not a wall, a crossing, set by hand, sunk now to their waists. No structures. Not even a fire circle, though the overhang by the stair is blackened in one patch. People came here rarely, briefly, to grind seed, to knap stone, to press ochre to the rock and leave their hands on it — and then went away again, for thousands of years.

The weirdness is cumulative, not singular. The seeps never vary; in drought years on the plateau above, when the grass goes brown and the streams dry, the walls of the Bowl weep at the same rate, fed by something deeper than the sky. The mist keeps its own hours: at dawn it lies in the floor like milk in a dish, rises by midday to the rim, and returns at dusk, entering through the notch as a single slow rope and unspooling across the pools. At dawn the pools breathe — gas comes up from the mat in big slow bubbles that break with a deep *ploomph*, and the mist above them stands up a little. Step off the mat onto a hummock and the whole surface heaves and settles over minutes, and the waves it makes ring against the walls and come back. The plants are strangers: the rosettes are silver-backed, the ferns have no lowland names, and the orchids on the warm seep's terraces bloom in midwinter. The water is faintly soapy, faintly blue.

The rules, as far as anyone has read them: water comes in at the walls and leaves through a throat — a fissure in the southeast wall, half-choked with mineral and root — and it leaves at the same rate it arrives; the Bowl neither floods nor drains. The mat builds peat downward at the pace of a hand's width in a human lifetime. The mineral drapes on the walls grow a fingernail in a year. The walls retreat at a rate too slow to see. The bowl is in balance with its own erosion, settled, long-existed, in its own state. Everything here is patient. The hand-axe on the moss has been waiting so long that the moss has begun to grow up around its edge; in another few hundred years it will be a green lump with a sharp black edge showing through. The falcon will still be here. The stone will still be sweating. The echo will still answer, half a beat late.
