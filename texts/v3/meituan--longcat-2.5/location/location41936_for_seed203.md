# The Hollow

You descend through a crack in the world. The fissure is narrow, twisting, a tight press of stone that forces you to turn sideways, to breathe shallow, to feel the weight of the earth above. Then it opens — abruptly, violently — into vastness.

You are standing on a ledge. Below you, a space that has no business existing. A cavern the size of a country. The floor is lost in a pale haze hundreds of meters down. The ceiling is lost in the same haze, hundreds of meters up. The walls are lost in the haze, kilometers away. The scale is impossible. The space is too large, too open, too *empty* to be a cave. It feels like a sky that has been inverted, a landscape turned inside out.

The light is the first thing you notice. It is everywhere — a cold, bright, directionless light, as if the air itself is luminous. It comes from the plants. Everything here is plants.

The walls are sheathed in them. The floor is built of them. The ceiling hangs with them. They glow — soft whites, pale blues, the occasional green — and their glow fills the space, bouncing off the stone, the moisture, the haze, until the whole cavern is lit like a cloudy afternoon. There are no shadows. Everything is lit from every side. The light is bright enough to read by, to work by, to get lost by. It is cold light. It does not warm. It is the light of deep water, of bioluminescence, of things that live where the sun cannot reach.

The plants are the substrate. They have replaced the rock, or digested it, or grown through it. The floor is a mat of interwoven fiber — pale, springy, faintly warm in the sense that it is alive, but cold in the sense that it is not the sun. It breathes, slowly, a vast slow respiration that moves the surface under your feet like the flank of a sleeping animal. The walls are the same — a skin of living tissue over stone, pulsing faintly with the glow.

But the plants are not a forest. They are not trees, not bushes, not a carpet. They are *architecture*. They grow as hollow structures — columns, arches, vaults, lattices, tubes, domes. The cavern is filled with a foam of plant matter, a three-dimensional web of struts and skins, with enormous voids between them. You can stand at the base of a column the width of a redwood and look up through its hollow interior to the ceiling far above. You can walk through an arch that spans a hundred meters, its surface thin as paper, its interior empty. You can crawl through a tube that runs for kilometers, its walls translucent, glowing from the other side.

The scale is impossible. The voids are continental. You can walk for days between one structure and the next, across the springy floor, under the distant glowing sky, and see nothing but emptiness and the occasional pale tower rising like a termite mound. The density of life is tiny — the place is almost all space, almost all void, with the plant matter forming a skeleton, a scaffold, a suggestion of form. The plants are the walls, the floor, the ceiling — but they are not the content. The content is the emptiness.

The place is young. The rock is sharp — fresh fractures, clean breaks, no rounding, no smoothing. The cavern feels recently opened, recently formed, still settling. The stalactites are thin and sharp and new. The floor is littered with debris from the ceiling — fallen stones, snapped growths, the remains of structures that collapsed. The plants are young too — they have not had time to settle into stable forms. They are experimental, provisional, constantly trying new shapes. And they grow *fast*. You can watch it. A tendril extends visibly, reaching. A bud swells and bursts, releasing a cloud of spores that glow faintly in the air. A column thickens, merges with its neighbor, forms a new arch. A lattice grows across a void, bridging it, and within hours it is a solid wall, and within days it is a tower, and within weeks it is a ruin, collapsed under its own weight, being digested by the next growth.

The turbulence is constant. The landscape changes on a timescale of days. Passages open and close. Towers rise and fall. The mist thickens and thins. The light shifts — some growths glow brighter, others dim, and the whole cavern pulses with slow waves of brightness. Sometimes there are events — a spore cloud erupting from a growth, filling a hollow with luminous fog. A tower collapsing, the crash echoing for minutes across the vastness. A new growth bursting through the floor, a fountain of pale fiber, building itself in hours. The place feels alive, unstable, in constant flux. You cannot map it. You can only navigate by the fixed things — the stone outcrops, the deep channels, the places where the growth will not take.

The cold is the second thing you notice. It is the cold of depth, of stone, of damp. Your breath mists. The surfaces are cold to the touch — the plants are cold-blooded, they do not generate heat. The air is still and cold and smells of wet stone and something sweet and faintly corrupt — the smell of growth, of sap, of decay. The cold slows you down. It makes you want to keep moving, to generate warmth, to not stop for too long.

The water is intermittent. It condenses on the cold surfaces — the ceiling, the columns, the walls — and drips. The sound is constant, irregular, echoing — *drip, drip, drip* — a percussion that comes from everywhere and nowhere. Sometimes the condensation is heavy enough to form mist, low-lying clouds that pool in the hollows of the floor and drift between the columns like slow rivers. Sometimes it rains — not from clouds, but from the ceiling, a fine, cold rain that falls in sheets and soaks everything. Then it stops, and the mist thins, and the drips resume. There are channels — not rivers, but *paths* — where water flows through the plant matter, carved by the plants themselves, carrying nutrients, carrying spores. You can follow a channel for kilometers, and it will lead you somewhere — to a pool, to a growth, to a hollow where the water gathers and the plants grow thick.

The fauna is rare. You see signs of it — tracks in the fiber mat, droppings, the remains of meals. But the animals themselves are scarce. Small things, mostly — skittering things in the undergrowth, pale and blind, like insects but not insects. Things that live in the channels — pale fish, translucent, glowing faintly. Things that live in the hollows — slow, fat, grazing things that move through the vegetation like ghosts, leaving tunnels behind them. Once in a while you see something larger — a shape moving in the mist, a shadow against the glow, gone before you can focus on it. The place is dominated by plants. The animals are just guests.

There are people. Or something like people. They live here — small groups, scattered across the vastness, in the shelters of the growths. They have built — small structures, faint marks, partial remains. Shelters woven from the plant fiber, or grown into the hollows of the columns, or dug into the stone where the growth will not take. They use hand tools — stone, bone, simple metal. They harvest the plants — for food, for fiber, for material. They hunt the rare animals. They leave marks — symbols on the stone, tallies, warnings. They are pale, like everything here. They are quiet — sound carries strangely in the vastness, so they move softly, speak softly. They are mobile — the landscape changes, so they do not stay in one place for long. They follow the growths, follow the water, follow the animals. They live in the hollows of the world, in the cold and the damp and the light that never changes.

The silence is the last thing you notice. The vastness swallows sound. The drip of water echoes and fades. Your footsteps are muffled by the fiber mat. Your voice is swallowed by the space. The only sounds are the ones the place makes — the creak of growing plants, the crack of a stem snapping, the rush of air through a hollow, the distant crash of a collapse. It is mostly quiet. The kind of quiet that makes you aware of your own breathing, your own heartbeat, the blood in your ears. The kind of quiet that makes you feel like you are the only thing alive in a world of stone and light and slow, vast growth.

The geometry is wrong. The arches are too thin, the spans too long, the hollows too large. The columns should not stand — they are too slender, too hollow, too lightly anchored. The vaults should not hold — they are too broad, too flat, too unsupported. The whole place feels designed, not formed — as if something built it, or grew it according to a plan, rather than letting it settle into the shapes that stone and water and time would make. The plants are not growing *on* the rock. They are growing *instead* of the rock. They are replacing it, digesting it, turning it into more of themselves. The stone is the scaffold. The plants are the building.

The rules of this place are not the rules of the surface. Light has no source. Growth has no limit. The landscape has no stability. Time is measured in growth and decay, not in days or years. The place is alive — not metaphorically, but literally. It breathes. It grows. It changes. It is a single organism, or a colony, or a process, and everything in it — the plants, the animals, the people, the water, the light — is part of it. You are part of it now. You are inside it. You are walking on its skin, breathing its air, drinking its water, eating its food. You are a cell in its body, a guest in its house, a thought in its mind.

And it is vast. So vast that you cannot see the edges. So vast that it has its own weather, its own ecology, its own time. So vast that you can walk for weeks and never see the same thing twice. So vast that you can get lost in it, truly lost, and never be found. So vast that it feels like a world, not a cave. A world inside the world. A hollow in the rock where something else is growing.
