The town sits in a shallow granite basin on a high plateau, roughly two thousand meters above the lowlands, though the lowlands are rarely visible. The basin is small — half a mile across at its widest — and the town fills it loosely, buildings scattered among open ground, paths threading between them. The basin is ringed on three sides by low cliffs, rounded and weathered, the rock folded and cracked in patterns that suggest immense age. The fourth side opens onto the plateau, which extends for some distance before dropping away into cloud.

From the plateau, the town is barely visible. The stone buildings blend with the stone of the basin. The paths are the color of the rock. The only sign of habitation is the thin column of smoke that rises from the meeting hall, or the occasional movement of a figure in the plaza. The town does not announce itself. It waits to be noticed.

The climate is cool and damp. The town sits at or near the cloud line, and fog is a regular feature of daily life — not an event, but a condition, like daylight or darkness. Mornings are foggy. The fog lifts in the afternoon, sometimes, revealing the plateau and the distant peaks. Evenings are foggy again. The fog leaves moisture on every surface. Stone is perpetually damp. Wood is perpetually damp. Metal — what little there is — rusts quickly and is used sparingly. The people have adapted to this. They build with stone and clay. They wear wool and leather. They expect the damp.

Water comes from springs in the cliff face — not dramatic springs, but seeps, places where the granite sweats. The water has been collected for centuries in a network of channels and cisterns cut directly into the bedrock. The channels are the town's most important infrastructure. They run at ground level, crossing paths, passing under doorways, feeding stone basins. The water moves slowly, continuously, with a sound like a whispered conversation. The channels are covered in moss and lichen, dark green and gray-green, and the stone beneath them is worn smooth by the flow.

The town's buildings are stone, thick-walled, small-windowed. They were built without mortar, the stones fitted together by hand and held by weight and friction. The walls are slightly out of true — not dangerously, just enough that a plumb line would show the accumulated drift of centuries. The buildings are one or two stories tall, with flat or gently sloped roofs packed with clay and gravel. Each building has a cistern or a collection system for condensation. The roofs sweat at night, and the water drips into barrels or runs down stone gutters into the channels.

There are perhaps sixty buildings, and they are not all houses. There are storehouses, workshops, a meeting hall, a structure that serves as a bathhouse (the water is cool, not hot, but people bathe in it anyway, or use it for washing). There is a building that might be a temple, or might just be a very old house that people treat with a certain formality. The buildings cluster most densely near the springs, where the ground is wettest and the stone is darkest with moss. Further from the water, the buildings thin out, and the open spaces between them grow larger. The edges of town are marked by low walls, not defensive, just boundary-marking, the way a garden has a border.

The central space of the town is an irregular plaza, paved with flat stones, sloping gently toward a central drain that feeds the main channel. The plaza is the town's public room. People meet there, or pass through it, or sit on the stone benches that line its edges. The benches are worn smooth by generations of use. There is a single tree in the plaza — a stunted, wind-shaped thing, perhaps a juniper or a similar hardy species, growing from a crack in the bedrock. It is very old. Its trunk is thick and twisted, and its branches spread horizontally, shaped by the wind. The tree is the town's clock and calendar. Its buds mark the start of the growing season. Its berries are gathered in autumn. Its shade is a place to rest in summer. The tree is not large, but it is the most significant living thing in the town.

Vegetation is otherwise sparse. Moss and lichen cover the north faces of buildings and the shaded sides of walls. There are patches of grass in the open spaces, kept short by grazing (if there are animals) or by cutting (if there aren't). The plateau above the cliffs has more vegetation — tough grasses, low scrub, the kind of plants that can handle wind and cold and thin soil. But in the basin, the green is limited to where water collects and where the stone stays damp.

Fauna is rare. Birds nest on the cliffs — a few species, perhaps some kind of falcon or hawk, perhaps smaller birds that live in the rock crevices. There are insects in the wet corners, near the channels and cisterns. There may be a few small mammals — rodents, perhaps, or something larger that passes through occasionally. But the town is quiet. No dogs barking, no chickens, no livestock. The people do not keep animals, or keep very few. The silence is noticeable. The dominant sounds are water, wind, and the occasional human voice.

The people of the town are... I don't need to describe them in detail. Their presence is felt in the maintenance of the town. The paths are swept. The channels are clear. The roofs are in repair. There is no litter, no debris, no sign of recent construction or recent destruction. Everything is in a state of careful preservation. This is not a town that is decaying, and it is not a town that is growing. It is a town that is holding.

The daily routine is simple. Water is drawn. Food is prepared. The channels are checked. A wall is repaired. A roof is patched. The same people do the same tasks, or if one person is unable, another takes their place, and the task is done the same way. There are no new buildings. There are no demolitions. The population is stable — births and deaths balance out over years. The town is not dying, and it is not being born. It is simply continuing.

The light is clear and cool. At this elevation, the sun is bright, but the air is thin and the temperature stays moderate. Shadows are sharp when the sun is out, but the fog softens everything for hours at a time. The quality of light changes the town completely — in fog, it is gray and intimate, the buildings fading into the background, the sounds muffled. In sun, it is precise and slightly austere, every stone visible, the whole layout of the town clear at a glance.

The most striking thing about the place is the equilibrium. The town has reached a state where it is perfectly adapted to its environment, and it has stayed there. The people do not talk about the future because the future is the same as the present. They do not talk about the past because the past is too long and too continuous to summarize. They talk about the water, the weather, the condition of the roofs. Their world is small and complete.

There is something slightly uncanny about this, though it takes a while to identify. It is not that the town is empty — it is not. It is not that the people are strange — they are not. It is that the whole place feels like a diorama, or a snapshot, or a memory of a town rather than a town itself. But this feeling fades as you stay longer. The town is real. The people are real. The dampness is real. The equilibrium is real. It is just that the equilibrium is so complete that it is hard to believe it is possible.
