## The Roar

There is a plain so large it makes its own weather. It sits high — a plateau of ancient rock, hot under a huge sky, dry as a kiln for most of the year. The horizon is a long way off in every direction. The light is hard and clear and the air is thin enough that a run leaves you breathless, and the sun is close and brutal. This is the Roar.

The ground is old. Not old like a forest is old — old like a mountain worn down to its bones. The rock is weathered into soft, rounded forms: low domes, shallow basins, undulating sheets of stone the color of rust and dried blood and pale dust. Soil is thin — a skin of dust and gravel in the low places, bare stone on the rises. The erosion is finished; the land has found its shape and holds it. Channels run across the plain, but they are dry nine months of the year — the old watercourses, carved when the climate was wetter, now used only when the storms come.

And scattered across this bareness — sparse, like boulders dropped by a giant — are the hollows.

Each hollow is one plant. Not a clump of plants; one organism, a single genetic individual, grown for centuries or millennia into a wooden hill. The core is a lignotuber, a swollen base of dense fibrous wood, riddled with chambers and tunnels, the size of a house, or a barn, or — in the oldest ones — a small hill. From this core, living stems rise: hollow, jointed, like a bamboo scaled up beyond reason, each stem as thick as a person's thigh or waist, rising thirty, fifty, eighty feet before arching over and drooping. Where the drooping tips touch the ground, they root and send up new stems, so a hollow grows outward in rings and lobes, a labyrinth of arches and tunnels — a plant that is also architecture. The dead stems do not rot; the wood is dense and resinous, and it accumulates, generation upon generation, into a tangled wooden hill. The living stems are crowned with long strap-like leaves, tough and fibrous, that rasp and whisper in the wind.

The hollows are far apart. You can stand on a rise and see one — a green crown on a wooden mound, a mile off. Then another, on the horizon. Then nothing, for hours of walking. Just rock and dust and the wind. The emptiness between them is as much a part of the place as the hollows themselves. The plain is not a forest. It is a sea of stone with islands of wood.

---

Water comes and goes. The wet season — if it comes — is a matter of storms, not rain. Heat builds over the plain for weeks, the air shimmers, the pressure drops, and then the storms break: convectional towers of cloud that rise in the afternoon and dump water in sheets. The bare rock sheds water instantly. It gathers in the shallow basins, rises, and runs in the old channels — brown and roaring, carrying gravel and sand. For a few days or weeks the plain is a network of rivers. Then it stops. The water sinks into the ground or evaporates under the sun, and the plain is dry again, the channels dust, the basins cracked mud.

But the hollows drink. The stems are pipes. The leaves are funnels. The lignotuber is a sponge. A single storm can leave a hollow holding enough water to last the dry season. The wood swells, the chambers seep, the pools at the base stay full. And at night, when the temperature drops — and it drops hard, high and thin-aired as the plain is — moisture condenses on every surface and drips, drips, drips, and the hollows collect it. The water inside a hollow is warm — the plant's own slow metabolism heats it — and on cold mornings it steams. A hollow in the dry season is a warm, damp, green cave in a landscape of dust and glare.

And life clusters there. The hollows are the only water for miles, and everything knows it. The air around a hollow is thick with insects — clouds of midges, dragonflies the size of hawks, beetles that clatter like dice, swarming termites after the rains. Lizards bask on the warm wood. Snakes — some of them large — coil in the shade of the stems. Birds nest in the hollow stems by the thousand; the stems are full of holes and chambers, and the birds drum and whistle and scream. Small mammals tunnel in the dead wood and come out at dusk. Frogs chorus in the pools, deafening. And the herds come: great slow grazing things — horned ungulates, or something between ungulate and lizard — that walk between hollows, eating the tough leaves and the annual grasses that sprout after rain, and the predators follow them, and the sky is full of scavengers.

---

The sound of the plain is the sound of the hollows. The wind blows across ten million hollow stems, and each one whistles, and the hollows are instruments the size of hills, and the plain roars. It is a low, constant roar, like a waterfall heard from far away, like the sea in a shell, and it changes with the wind and the weather and the season. Each hollow has its own chord — a low one, a high one, a throbbing one — and when the wind is right, the plain is a chord of roaring hills. The people who live here call the plain the Roar, and they are not wrong. Inside a hollow, the roar is everywhere and nowhere; you stop hearing it, and you hear it in your bones, and when you walk out onto the open plain, it is a physical presence at your back.

At night the insects take over. The drone of them is a wall of sound, and the frogs add, and the herds call — great honking, belling, grunting — and the predators answer. The plain is never quiet. Even in the dry season, even in the calm before a storm, there is the roar of the wind and the drone of insects and the distant calls of animals.

---

The people of the plain are few and scattered. They live in the hollows — in the chambers of the dead wood, in lean-tos built against the core, in stone-walled enclosures in the lee of the stems. Their tools are stone and bone and wood: axes of knapped stone to harvest the old stems, wedges of wood to split them, scrapers of obsidian or chert, needles of bone, cord of woven leaf-fiber, baskets and mats and rope. They burn the dead stems for fuel — the wood burns hot and slow — and they burn the old growth to clear the chambers and encourage new shoots. They hunt the herds and the lizards and the birds, and they gather the plant's products: the seeds, the young shoots, the fibers, the resin. They store water in the hollows' pools and in woven bags lined with resin.

They move. In the wet season, when the plain is briefly green, they spread out, hunting and gathering and burning. In the dry season they contract to the deepest hollows, the ones that always hold water, and they wait. Their paths are worn into the rock and the wood — faint marks, lines of travel between hollows, generations deep. On the bare rock they have carved: spirals, handprints, animal figures, the outlines of hollows. A few stone circles stand on the rises — low walls of stacked rock, of unknown age, perhaps shelters, perhaps markers, perhaps altars.

Their language is partly whistled. The hollow stems are pipes, and the people have learned to play them — to blow across a stem's mouth and get a note, to stop holes with their fingers and get others. They make horns and flutes and speaking-tubes from the stems, and they call each other across the distances of the plain in whistles that carry for miles. A hollow is a telephone exchange. The people can speak from one island of wood to another, and the hollows carry the sound.

---

And every few decades, the hollows flower.

It is synchronous, across the whole plain — as if the organism knows, though it has no brain; as if the chemical signals pass through the connected roots, or the timing is read from the length of the days, or the moisture, or something else. The hollows send up flower spikes from their centers: great columns of blossom, thirty, forty feet tall, that rise above the green crowns and open in clouds of scent and pollen. The plain becomes a forest of bloom. The air is thick with it — sweet, heavy, almost nauseating. The insects explode: clouds of pollinators, swarms of beetles, birds gorging on the bounty. The herds come to eat the flowers and the seed. The predators follow. The people feast and store and celebrate, and they know what comes next.

After the bloom, the flowering stems die. The hollows draw back into their cores, and the plain is harsh for a while — the old stems gone, the new ones not yet grown, the herds migrated or dead, the predators ranging. The people eat their stores and tighten their belts and wait. Then the new stems rise, and the green crowns return, and the herds come back, and the plain is itself again. The people count their years in blooms. A person might see two, maybe three, in a lifetime. The bloom is the calendar, the festival, the reckoning.

---

The rules of the plain are simple, and they are not forgiving:

**Water flows to the hollows.** Everything else follows. Where there is a hollow, there is life; where there is none, there is rock and dust and the wind. The hollows are the nodes of the world, and the distances between them are the measure of distance.

**The hollows are one organism.** The people know this — they have seen how a fire in one hollow sometimes smokes from another, how a wound in one place weeps sap in another. The plant is not many; it is one, spread across the plain, and it is nearly immortal. It can be killed by drought, by fire, by old age — but it has survived all three for longer than the people have been here, and it will survive them. The people do not own the hollows. They live in them, and the plant tolerates them, and perhaps it even uses them — their fires, their harvest, their paths, their whistles.

**The plain has its own weather, and its own time.** Storms rise over the high center and die there; rain falls in curtains you can watch from a hundred miles away; the wind circles the plateau and comes back changed. The people read the sky and the sound of the hollows and the behavior of the herds, and they know what is coming. They do not fight the plain. They move with it, or they do not survive it.

**The bloom is the clock.** Everything is measured against it — the stores, the journeys, the age of a person, the age of a hollow. When the bloom comes, the world is made new. When it ends, the world is made hard. The people do not know why the hollows bloom together. They have a story about it, and it is a good story, and it does not matter whether it is true.

---

Stand on a rise in the Roar at midday, and you will see: the plain of rust-colored stone, shimmering with heat; the hollows scattered like green fires; the dust-devils spinning across the basins; the herds moving in the middle distance, dark against the pale ground; the sky enormous and hard blue. And you will hear: the roar, the drone, the rasp of leaves, the distant calls. And you will feel the sun on your skin like a weight, and the thin air in your lungs, and the age of the land pressing up through the soles of your feet.

The hollows are old. The rock is older. The plain has been here a long time, and it will be here when you are gone, and it will be singing its roaring song to anyone who comes.
