**The Dry Chamber**

It is a small, low room of stone, a bubble in the earth that has forgotten water. The ceiling is a shallow dome, no more than four or five meters high at its crown, and it presses down like a held breath. The walls curve into the floor without a single sharp corner, as if the whole space were smoothed by a hand — or by water, long ago, when water still moved here. The rock is evaporite: banded gypsum and anhydrite, cream and pale grey, with faint ribbons of rose and amber that follow the curve of the walls like the growth rings of a tree. The surface is smooth, waxy, cool to the eye even in the warmth of the air, and it holds a soft, dull sheen, as if the stone were polished by a cloth.

The floor is a flat pan of compacted silt and mineral dust, pale as bone, cracked into low, irregular polygons that have been filled and refilled with finer dust until they are barely more than lines on the surface. Scattered across it are rounded pebbles and cobbles, polished by ancient water, in shades of grey and cream and a faint, rust-red. They sit in the silt like eggs in a nest, spaced well apart, each one a small, patient presence in the emptiness. There are no bones, no sticks, no fragments of leaf — nothing that was ever alive except the stone itself. The emptiness is part of the place: the pebbles are few, the silt is wide, and the walls rise from it with a kind of bare, quiet patience.

The chamber is dark, but not utterly. A narrow cleft runs along the crown of the dome, a crack perhaps a meter long and a few centimeters wide, partially choked with fallen rubble and the fine, dry dust of ages. Through it comes a faint, cold, blue-grey light — diffuse, steady, the color of a winter sky seen from deep underwater. It does not move. It falls on a small patch of the wall and floor directly below the cleft, a patch perhaps three meters across, and there it is just bright enough to see by. Beyond that patch, the chamber fades into a darkness that is not black but a deep, warm, velvety brown-grey, the darkness of closed eyes in a warm room. The light catches the edge of a pebble, the rim of a scallop in the wall, the tip of a dead stalactite, and makes them faintly luminous, as if they were remembering something.

In that small pool of light, and only there, is the life of the chamber. A sparse, pale grey-green moss clings to the wall in thin, thread-like tufts, and a grey film of cyanobacteria or cave algae spreads over the stone and the nearest pebbles. The moss is dry-looking, brittle, the color of dust and old leaves, but it is alive — the only alive thing in the chamber. It grows, if “grows” is the word, at a rate too slow to perceive. A single season, a single year, would leave no trace. There is nothing else: no fern, no flower, no insect, no spider, no bat, no worm, no snail. The chamber has no fauna at all. It is a world of stone and a little light and a little moss, and nothing else.

The air is warm — perhaps thirty-two or thirty-three degrees — and dry, though not parched. The rock holds a little moisture in its mineral structure, and the air carries it faintly, so that the warmth is not the flat, biting warmth of a desert noon but a soft, enclosing warmth, like the warmth of a body. The air is perfectly still. Nothing moves it. There is no draft from the cleft, no breath from any passage, no convection from temperature differences. The warmth is uniform, the stillness complete. If you stand in the chamber, you feel the air settle around you like a held breath. It smells of warm stone, a faint sulfurous edge, a metallic tang that catches at the back of the throat, and the dry, dusty scent of mineral surfaces that have not known water in eons.

The silence is the first thing you notice, and then it is the only thing. There is no drip of water, no rustle of air, no scuttle of insect, no distant rumble of the world outside. The silence is not empty; it is full, mineral, a substance in itself. It presses gently on the ears. The only sounds are the ones you make — your breath, your heartbeat, the faint crunch of your foot on the silt — and even those seem to be absorbed quickly, swallowed by the stone and the stillness. If you stay long enough, you hear your own blood, and then you hear nothing at all.

The chamber is old. The formations on the walls are dead — flowstone draperies, thin and ribbed, like curtains frozen mid-fold; stubby stalactites, some broken, their tips dark with old mineral stains; a single column where a stalactite and stalagmite met and grew together before the water stopped coming. They have not grown in eons. The water that made them is gone — evaporated, drawn into the rock, locked in mineral bonds. The chamber is a fossil of a wetter time, a relic of a cave that was once alive with dripping water and is now a dry, warm, still vault. The scallops in the walls, the polished pebbles on the floor, the very smoothness of the rock — all of it is a memory of water, written in stone and then abandoned.

And the chamber is in equilibrium. Nothing enters. Nothing leaves. The faint light from the cleft is the only energy that comes in, and it is small; the warmth of the earth is the only heat, and it is steady. The air is not exchanged with the surface — only the faintest diffusion of gases through the crack, so slow it might as well be nothing. The moss takes the light and the trace moisture and makes a little living tissue, and that is the whole of the chamber’s economy. There is no decay, no cycle, no wheel of growth and rot. The moss grows, and the stone endures, and the silence holds, and that is all. The chamber is a system at rest, a held moment, a place outside the rush of time. It has been this way for thousands of years, and it will be this way for thousands more, unless the cleft widens or the mountain shifts — and even then, the change will be so slow that the chamber will not notice.

There is no mark of a hand here. No chipped stone, no scratch, no arrangement of pebbles, no charcoal stain, no fragment of bone or tool. The chamber has never known a visitor who made anything. If the world outside has its tool-users — and it does, though they are primitive, with their chipped flakes and their bone awls and their knowledge of nothing beyond the next valley — they have not come here, or they have come and left nothing, which amounts to the same thing. The chamber is untouched, undeveloped, virgin. It is a place that has been spared even the simplest human mark.

And that is what it is: a small, low, warm, dry, dark room of stone, with a little moss in a pool of faint light and a silence that is almost solid. It is a place of waiting, of held breath, of patience. It is the inside of the earth, and it is complete. It does not want anything. It does not need anything. It simply is, and it will continue to be, in its quiet equilibrium, long after the names of the tool-users and their valleys are forgotten. It is a stone womb that has forgotten its child, a seed that will never germinate, a room that has been waiting for ten thousand years for someone to enter, and no one has, and the waiting is not sad — it is just the nature of the place, which is to wait, and to be still, and to hold its silence, and to be warm, and to be dark, and to be dry, and to be.
