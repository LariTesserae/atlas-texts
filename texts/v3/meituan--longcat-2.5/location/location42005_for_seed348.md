The basin is shallow and broad, a saucer pressed into the land, perhaps two kilometers across and only a few tens of meters deep. It sits below the surrounding terrain — a cold steppe, a flat gray plain, something low and exposed — and it collects everything that flows downhill. Water, mainly. Rain, snowmelt, groundwater. The water carries dissolved minerals from the surrounding rock, and over millennia it has concentrated here, evaporating slowly under a cool sky, leaving behind salt and alkali and other deposits. The floor of the basin is a cracked plain of white and gray and rust, a mineral crust that crunches underfoot, that breaks into plates and tilts at angles, that reveals damp, dark sediment beneath.

At the lowest point, there is a pool. Not a lake — a pool, a spreading, irregular sheet of brine that might be a hundred meters across, its edges feathered with salt crusts that grow and dissolve and regrow in the same patterns, endlessly. The water is heavy, dense with dissolved salts, so dense that a person could float on it like a cork, their body held at the surface, their ears below. It is the color of weak tea, or of old glass, or of something not quite natural — a pale, yellowish brown that seems to glow from within. It reflects the sky perfectly when it is still, which is often, because the basin is sheltered from wind by the low rim of land around it. When the surface is disturbed — by a pump intake, by an animal breaking through, by a drop of condensation falling from a pipe overhead — it shimmers with iridescent colors, oil-slick rainbows that spread in rings and fade, leaving the surface mirror-still again.

The pool is fed by hot springs. The water that bubbles up from below is warm, mineral-rich, and it feeds the brine with a constant supply of dissolved salts — sodium, potassium, magnesium, lithium, other things. The hot springs also provide energy. Steam rises from vents in the basin floor and is captured by pipes, channeled to the settlement's generators. The machinery runs on geothermal power, which is constant and reliable, which does not vary with the weather or the season. This is one reason the system has been stable for so long: the energy source is geological, not biological, not solar. It has been running for centuries and will run for centuries more.

The settlement is built on the gentle slope of the basin, above the brine pool but below the rim. It consists of perhaps a dozen structures, spread out with open space between them — a power station, a few processing buildings, storage tanks, a workshop, a couple of low shelters that might house people or might house only equipment. The buildings are connected by pipes, channels, cables, and paths, but the connections are loose, with gaps and detours, as if the settlement grew without a plan. Everything is weathered, stained, corroded, patched. The metal surfaces are white with salt, or orange with rust, or black with industrial grime. The concrete is spalled and crumbling at the edges, revealing the aggregate beneath. The buildings are old — decades old, perhaps centuries old — but they are maintained. They are functional. They hum.

The machinery runs constantly. Steam hisses through pipes. Pumps draw brine from the pool and send it through the processing buildings in a steady flow. Evaporators boil off water, leaving behind salt and mineral cakes that are raked, scraped, and conveyed to storage. Centrifuges separate different salts by density, spinning with a high-pitched whine. Conveyor belts move material from one stage to the next, clattering and rattling. Generators hum at a low frequency that you feel in your chest more than you hear. The sound is constant and loud — a layered drone of mechanical noise, steam, and moving water, punctuated by sharper sounds: the clank of a valve, the hiss of a pressure release, the rattle of a loose panel. The sound is so constant that after a while you stop hearing it as sound and start feeling it as pressure, as a vibration in the air, as the baseline against which silence would be noticeable.

The air is cool and damp. The temperature is perhaps 10 to 15 degrees Celsius — cool but not cold, the kind of cool that seeps into your clothes and stays there. The moisture comes from the brine pool, from the hot springs, from condensation on the pipes, from intermittent rain that falls in a fine, persistent drizzle and is absorbed by the salt crust or evaporates slowly. The air feels wet on the skin. Everything is slightly damp — the buildings, the ground, the machinery, the animals. Condensation beads on every surface and runs down in slow trails. Metal is always wet. Concrete is always dark with moisture. The salt crust is always slightly soft, slightly yielding underfoot, never fully dry.

There are no plants. Nothing green, nothing soft, nothing that grows toward the sun. But there are fungi. Pale, rubbery, mineral-stained fungi grow on the wet surfaces of the buildings, on the damp ground, on the pipes where condensation collects. They form crusts, shelves, irregular blobs — white, gray, yellowish, some of them with a faint bioluminescent glow in the dimmer corners. They are the base of the food chain, or one base. They break down the mineral deposits, the industrial waste, the dead bodies of animals. They are tough, slow-growing, and they have been here as long as the settlement has.

And the animals. The overwhelming animals.

They are everywhere. In the brine pool, small crustacean-like creatures swarm in such numbers that the water seems to boil. They are perhaps one to three centimeters long, with translucent, segmented bodies and crystalline shells that catch the light and refract it into small rainbows. They move in synchronized swarms, thousands or millions of them, creating visible currents and waves in the water, patterns that shift and flow like smoke. Their shells click together as they move, a constant dry rustling sound, like paper being crumpled, or like rain on a tin roof. When they swarm near the surface, the pool shimmers and clicks and seems to breathe. When they swarm deeper, the water darkens with their bodies.

On the damp ground around the pool, larger creatures move in columns and swarms. They are perhaps five to fifteen centimeters long, something like isopods or beetles, with segmented bodies, multiple legs, and hard shells that are white or gray or rust-colored, encrusted with salt crystals that give them a rough, stony texture. They click and scrape as they move, feeding on the brine creatures that wash up on the salt crust, on the fungi, on mineral deposits, on each other. They move in visible trails — fine, winding lines in the salt crust that crisscross the basin floor, a vast, shifting network of paths that is never the same twice but is always the same in its overall pattern. The ground seems to move with them. The sound of their movement is a constant dry rustling, a billion tiny legs on salt, a sound like static, like the ocean heard through a wall.

In the air, clouds of small flying creatures swarm around the processing buildings. They are perhaps one to two centimeters long, something like flies or midges, but stranger — their bodies are metallic or iridescent, their wings are transparent and beat too fast to see, their movements are too quick, too synchronized, too purposeful. They swarm around the exhaust pipes, drawn to the heat, the moisture, the organic compounds in the steam. They form visible columns and clouds, thick enough to obscure the buildings at times, thick enough to get in your eyes and ears and mouth if you stand too close. Their wings create a constant buzzing or humming sound, a high-pitched drone that cuts through the lower mechanical rumble, that sets your teeth on edge. When they swarm, the air itself seems to thicken, to become granular, to resist movement.

Larger creatures move among the smaller ones. Something like birds, or like wading birds, or like something else — they are perhaps twenty to fifty centimeters long, with long legs, long necks, and broad, flat bills or filter-feeding apparatus. They wade through the brine pool, their heads submerged, straining the small creatures from the water through comb-like structures in their bills. They walk through the swarms on the ground, snapping up mouthfuls of the larger creatures. They fly through the clouds of insects, their beaks open, scooping. They are mineral-stained, their feathers encrusted with salt, giving them a stony, statuesque appearance — white and gray and rust-colored, like the basin floor given wings. They have strange calls — mechanical-sounding clicks and whistles, or perhaps musical, haunting tones that carry across the basin and echo off the rim. They nest on the buildings, on the pipes, on the storage tanks, in the sheltered corners of the machinery. Their droppings add to the mineral crust, to the fungal growth, to the cycle.

The whole system is in equilibrium. The hot springs feed the brine pool. The brine pool feeds the small creatures. The small creatures feed the larger ones, and the fungi, and each other. The fungi break down the waste and the dead. The industrial process draws water from the pool, extracts minerals, and returns something — processed water, waste heat, organic compounds in the steam — that feeds the animals and the fungi. The animals' waste feeds the process, or feeds the fungi, or feeds the brine. Nothing is lost. Nothing accumulates. The system is closed and self-sustaining.

The settlement has been running for generations. The machinery has been maintained, repaired, replaced piece by piece, but the pattern is the same. The animal populations have been stable for as long as anyone can remember — no population explosions, no crashes, no new species arriving, no old species disappearing. The weather follows its stable pattern — cool, damp, intermittent rain, no seasons that matter, no storms that disrupt. The light is normal daylight, filtered through mist and industrial haze, often diffuse and shadowless under an overcast sky, occasionally sharp and clear when the clouds break. The light does not change the place. The place does not respond to the light.

The place is strange because of the abundance of life in a dead landscape, and because of the uncanny stability. Nothing changes. The machinery hums at the same frequency. The animals swarm in the same patterns. The water flows at the same rate. The salt crust grows and dissolves and regrows in the same shapes. A visitor might watch for hours and see no difference. The place is a living machine in steady state, a perpetual motion of industry and biology, a clockwork ecosystem that has been running for so long it has forgotten how to stop. The animals are not wild — they are part of the machinery, components in a process that includes them without their knowledge. The machinery is not alive — it is a geological process given form, a hot spring that happens to have pipes and pumps attached.

The rules of this world are the rules of equilibrium. The system is closed. Nothing enters or leaves, or what enters is perfectly balanced by what leaves. Disturbances are absorbed — the equilibrium is resilient. If the animal population increases, the predators increase, and the population is brought back down. If the brine pool level drops, the hot springs replenish it. If a machine breaks, it is repaired — by someone, by something, by the system itself. The system maintains itself. It has been maintaining itself for so long that maintenance is no longer a conscious act — it is simply what happens, the way the world works here. The place is not dying. The place is not growing. The place is not changing. The place is.

This is the basin. This is the brine pool and the salt crust and the hot springs and the settlement and the machinery and the animals. It is loud, damp, cool, strange, and utterly still. It has always been this way. It will always be this way. The only thing that changes is the light, and the light does not matter.
