**The Singing Bowl**

High enough that the air has thinned to a blue edge, the land folds into a single hollow of granite — a cirque left by a glacier that withdrew before anyone learned to write. It is small as such places go: two hundred paces across at its widest, ringed by walls that step up to a rim of broken stone. Herders who bring their alpaca through in the dry months call it the Listening Lake, or simply the Bowl. From the altiplano above it is nearly invisible — a dent in the puna, a darker green. Inside, it is a complete world, and a hollow one: mostly open water and open flat, with everything that stands in it standing alone.

**The shape of it.** The floor tilts gently toward a lake of black water, sixty paces across, with no visible outlet. Around it: gravel flats polished first by ice and since then by water, and peat — a bofedal, a high wetland of moss and cushion plants, black and wet and compressed over centuries. The hummocks of yareta rise from it dense as green brains, some old as the shelter. The walls weep. Seeps run down the granite in dark mineral streaks; in the wet season they join into white threads of waterfall, in the dry season they shrink to wet stains and the constant sound of dripping. One seep, low on the east wall, runs warm — never more than blood temperature, never freezing — and the moss around it is the brightest green in the bowl.

The only opening is a notch in the north wall where the moraine sits low. The lake's water leaves there, but underground: it sinks into the moraine and emerges below as a dozen springs that begin the stream. The bowl is a closed vessel. What water falls or flows in stays, and leaves only as vapor.

**Marks of people.** Granite, polished. Peat. Lichen the color of old copper. Totora reeds at the lake edge. The human marks are few and small: a half-ring of dry-stone wall, shoulder-high, collapsed in three places — a herder's shelter, its stones sunk into the peat to the height of a hand, so it seems to grow out of the ground rather than stand on it. A line of cairns leading to the water, one topped with a vicuña skull. A low abandoned corral. Faint channels cut in the peat to drain the bofedal — old, silted, the work of digging sticks. A few wind-bleached posts. A potsherd of plain brown clay, a copper pin gone green, a broken bronze knife-leaf carried perhaps as a charm and dropped. Everything made by hand. Everything that the weather has had its way with.

**Sound.** This is the loudest quiet place in the world. Wind funnels over the rim and holds a constant low note — a throat-sound, like a finger circling the lip of a jar. Water is everywhere audible: dripping from the walls, sheeted across the flats, lapping the lake edge, hissing in the reeds. Hail arrives without warning and raps the lake like a thousand knuckles. Insects drone. Bees work the cushion plants with a dense, contented hum. Vizcachas — rabbit-sized rodents living in the talus — whistle three rising notes all day long, like a man calling a dog. Lapwings cry *klee-klee-klee* and harass anything that moves. Andean geese honk from the water. A caracara on the wall gives a dry rattle.

And then the echo. Sound does not return from these walls on the instant. It returns a half-beat late, and changed — a note lower than the call that made it, as though the mountain repeats what you said in its own voice, to teach you how it sounds to others. Shout a word and the word comes back wrong. Whistle, and the bowl whistles back, deeper. The effect is that the air is always full of sounds that happened some minutes ago: the morning's hail arrives in the afternoon; yesterday's geese seem to lift off again at dusk. Herders speak quietly here — not from reverence, but from habit, from having learned that everything said will be repeated, and that the repetition is never quite yours.

**Weather and the living.** Four seasons in a day. Dawn: ice on the lake, your breath a cloud. Midmorning: the ice melts with a sound like cracking sugar, and the sun — thin and hard at this altitude, burning in the light, freezing in the shade — turns the peat steamy. Noon: wind. Afternoon: hail, or mist, or both. Evening: stillness, the lake gone black and mirror-flat under a sky so thick with stars it seems to hum. Climb two hundred paces up the wall and you are breathless; the bowl below you holds its weather like a cup holds water.

The mist is the bowl's private weather. Clouds spill over the rim like milk, fill the hollow to the walls, and then drain out through the notch again, sometimes within minutes. You can watch the lake disappear and return. Sound carries strangely in it — you hear the lapwing before you see it, and sometimes, the herders say, you hear yesterday's hail.

Fauna is abundant but small, scattered across a big emptiness: everything alive in the bowl is quick and bright and darted. Dragonflies — astonishing at this altitude — stitch the air above the reeds. Water boatmen dimple the shallows. In the warm seep, Andean water frogs gather, large and baggy-skinned, and sing in a chord — a low knocking, like knuckles on wood. The lake itself holds diatoms and the small invisible life that feeds the geese. Vizcachas explode from the talus and vanish. A fox passes through twice a year, and the lapwings remember it for weeks. On the rim, occasionally, vicuña. The herders come in the dry months with their alpaca, sleep in the old shelter, mend the wall a little, and leave. The bowl accepts this.

**What it asks.** Water that enters stays — leave nothing in the lake you would not drink later. Sound returns late and lower — if you would speak to the bowl, speak once and wait; it will answer, and the answer is not yours. Weather turns four times a day — carry nothing you cannot afford to have wet, frozen, and dry again inside the hour. And the small rule the herders keep without saying it: the warm seep is for the frogs; do not put your hands in it. No one remembers why. The moss there is the brightest green in the bowl, and it has never once frosted.
