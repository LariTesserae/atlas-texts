**Vlethaven**

You come to Vlethaven by water, because there is no other way to come. The Vleth runs brown and slow between banks of reed that stand twice a man's height, and for an hour the sky is only a strip overhead, and the only sounds are the squeal of the pole in the rowlock and the lap of water against the bow. Then the reeds thin, and the sound arrives — a thump you feel in your sternum before your ears, four seconds apart, regular as a heartbeat, and under it a continuous pouring, like a river being emptied down a drain. The town appears all at once: a low green mass of roofs and chimneys, and above it the great beam of *Perpetua*, rocking against the sky, slow as a wave.

The town sits at the level of the water. Its streets are canals — brick-lined, warm as bathwater, their surfaces skimmed with duckweed so that from the little bridges they look like lawns, until a boat's bow cuts them and they part and close. Foot travel is along the ridges: narrow brick walks at first-floor height, their footing of ash and clinker slowly rising year by year, little arched bridges every twenty paces, mooring rings wherever a door meets the water. The ground floors of most houses are given over to the flood. You enter a home by descending a short ladder from the threshold into a boat, or by climbing to the first floor, where the family lives. The drowned lower stories are storage, boat garages, dark cisterns; in some, eels come and go through the windows.

There is no bare surface in Vlethaven. Brick, iron, timber, tile — everything wears a living skin. Moss three fingers deep on the north faces; liverworts writing their green script on every shaded wall; ferns fountaining from gutters and window boxes no one planted. The roofs are turfed with soil blown and grown over a hundred years, and alders and crack-willows stand on them, pollarded every few years for timber, so that each house carries its own small copse. The canals are canopied. On the water, the light comes down green. The town is not a town with plants growing in it. It is a wood that happens to be built.

At the center stands the beam house, the largest building in the marsh: a brick nave forty feet high, its clerestory windows long broken, its roof sagging but uncollapsed, because a vast alder has grown up through the east end, its trunk thick as a barrel, its roots gripping the roof timbers, and it holds the roof up as much as the walls do. Inside is Perpetua. A steam beam engine of the old kind — a cast-iron spine, a beam thirty feet long rocking on its trunnion like the rib of a breathing animal, a flywheel twelve feet across turning so slowly you can watch its spokes. The thump you heard from the reed beds is her exhaust stroke. She has run for a hundred and ten years. She has never once stopped. She has also never once been replaced: every part of her has been repaired or remade in kind, so that Perpetua is a palimpsest of a hundred forgotten engineers — a machine with no original pieces and no end.

What she pumps is the town's own water. Everything in Vlethaven drains to the Cistern, a brick well thirty feet across at the town's heart, and Perpetua — with two smaller engines, the Twins — lifts it up to the culvert that runs out under the reed beds and spills into the river. The water table here stands at a man's hip, and the town is slowly sinking, because the peat it stands on is drying, oxidizing, subsiding, an inch, two inches a year. The pumps run day and night. There are shifts for people; the engines have no shifts. The sound is the clock. Babies are born into it; the old die in it; nobody hears it anymore. Visitors say they cannot sleep, and then they cannot wake. There is no church bell in Vlethaven; there has not been one for fifty years. Nobody can hear it.

The town eats the marsh and holds the marsh back at the same time. On the eastern edge lie the peat faces: cutters in long lines, cutting blocks, footing them out to dry on racks over the waste heat of the boilers; presses that extrude the dried peat into briquettes, which feed the boilers, which pump the water, which drains the bog, which exposes more peat. It is the oldest machine in Vlethaven and the slowest, and the town is its flywheel. Westward lie the reed beds: the reeds are cut in winter and pulped in the mill — great stone vats, a grind you feel through the soles of your feet — and pressed into a paper the color of weak tea, famous up and down the river for being thin and strong and for taking ink without blooming. Sedge paper. The mill smells of cut grass gone sweet and sour; the peat yards smell of smoke and old tannin; the beam house smells of hot oil and wet iron; and under everything lies the smell of the marsh itself — geosmin, algae, warm rot, the smell of rain on a place that is never dry.

Everything has settled into its long argument. The brick has eroded to pillow-round edges; the iron is fatigued but sound; the timber is alder, water-resistant, replaced plank by plank, so that no boat and no house is ever wholly new or wholly old. Buildings lean and do not fall. Canals silt and are dredged. The bridges have been patched so many times they are mostly patch. Nothing here is in a state of collapse, and nothing is in a state of repair; it has all reached its level, like the water.

The air is warm as breath, wet as a wrung cloth. The engines exhaust into the streets, and the steam hangs in the canopy, and every surface sweats: walls weep, rails weep, the underside of every bridge strings water in a continuous tinkering drip. On the rare cool mornings the whole town steams like a bathhouse. Condensation runs down the inside of windowpanes. Paper mildews in a week. Iron blooms orange and is painted and blooms again. Mornings are white with mist; noon is a white-green glare off the canals; under the alder canopy it is dim and emerald, and the light comes down in moving coins where the leaves turn.

Animals are scarce. The marsh is a monoculture of sedge and reed, and the noise is a wall. There are eels in the canals, thick as a wrist. There are dragonflies in summer. There is a heron — one heron, grey and patient, the same one, they say, though of course it isn't — that stands at the edge of the outfall and takes eels from the froth, and the town calls him the Mayor and will not have him disturbed. Once a year, perhaps, a bittern calls from the sedge, a low boom nobody can locate. The biomass of Vlethaven is overwhelmingly, ridiculously green.

The people live upstairs and travel by boat. Three hundred souls, more or less; no one counts exactly, because the water counts for them. They coppice the alder and willow for timber, mend boats, mend engines, mend engines, mend engines. They measure the year by the brass gauge let into the Cistern's brick, and they speak of the water the way other towns speak of weather: a finger up, a finger down; the Twins will take it, Perpetua will take it. Their saying is: *the swamp is patient, and so are we.* The church on the Hard — a sand ridge two feet above the flood, all that remains of the road that founded the town — is the only building with a ground floor still in use, and its churchyard is old, and the coffins are weighted with cobblestones.

The rules are few and old. The engines must not stop; if they stop, the town is ankle-deep in a day and waist-deep in a week, and the peat is back. The peat is finite. The bog is sinking. The town is slowly eating its own foundation and slowly being drunk by the swamp, and the two processes are in balance, and the balance is called Vlethaven. Change here is measured in the height of moss, in the growth of trees, in the slow climb of the gauge, in the generational repairs of Perpetua — an inch of wear, a new bearing, an inch, a new bearing. Nothing is sudden. The swamp is patient, and so is the town.

From the roof of the beam house, on a still afternoon, you can see all of it: the four canal streets, the mill's stone vats steaming gently, the peat yards, the alder copse on every roof, the Cistern's black water, the culvert pouring its long white rope into the Vleth, the wall of reed beyond, and above the reed, the sky. The beam rocks overhead with a sound like a sigh, and the steam rises through the broken clerestory and joins the mist, and the heron stands on the culvert below, and the whole town breathes, in and out, four seconds apart, and has done so for a hundred and ten years, and will do so — that is the faith — until the peat gives out or the engines do, and nobody can say which will be first.
