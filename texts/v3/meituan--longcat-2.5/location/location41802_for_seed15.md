# The Cistern

The first thing is the light. It falls through the oculus — a circular opening in the crown of the vault, perhaps two cubits across — in a single bright column that strikes the water's surface and scatters into a thousand shifting patterns on the floor below. Caustic networks of bright lines pulse and slide across the tiles, warping with the slight tremor of the water, reforming with every drip that falls from the ceiling. The light is intense, warm-white, the color of full sun in a dry climate. It illuminates the column of water beneath the oculus so that the water itself seems to glow — a pillar of brightness in the center of the chamber — while the edges of the space fall into a softer, greenish half-light where the scattered light reaches indirectly, filtered and reflected and diminished.

The second thing is the sound. It is loud — continuously, envelopingly loud. The enclosed space amplifies everything. Water enters through a channel at floor level at one end of the chamber, a steady inflow that creates a low rushing murmur, a white noise that fills the room and never stops. Water exits at the other end, maintaining a constant level, but the outflow is quieter — a gentle drawing-away that you feel more than hear, a slight suction that pulls the surface into slow ripples near the drain. Between the two, the water circulates, creating small currents visible in the movement of particles — tiny grains of clay, mineral flakes, the occasional air bubble rising from the floor in slow spirals that catch the light as they go. The surface of the water is almost still but not quite; it has a continuous slight tremor from the inflow, and it rises and falls by a finger's width with the variations in the flow.

Above the water, condensation forms on the ceiling and upper walls, beading on the porous brick and the clay mortar. The droplets grow and fall, each one striking the water with a sharp, clear note that rings and echoes in the vaulted space. The drips are irregular — sometimes a single drop, sometimes a quick succession, sometimes a pause of several seconds — creating a random percussion that overlays the continuous murmur. The sound of a drop in this space is disproportionately loud, a bright click that reverberates off the hard surfaces and lingers for a moment before the next one falls. Sometimes air moves through the oculus, creating a low hollow sound, a brief wind that disturbs the water's surface and changes the light patterns, sending the caustic lines sliding across the floor in new configurations. The overall effect is a dense, reverberant soundscape — water and air and echo — loud without being violent, a sustained murmur with sharp, clear accents.

The third thing is the warmth. The water is warm — distinctly warm, bath-warm, the temperature of skin. The warmth is nearly uniform throughout the chamber, with no cold layers or currents, though there are slight variations: warmer in the column of light, cooler in the shadowed corners, warmer near the inflow, cooler near the outflow. The air above the water is warm and very humid, thick with moisture, almost drinkable. Condensation beads on every surface above the waterline, and the air feels dense, heavy, warm — the air of a room that has been closed for a long time, but without the stale quality, because the water is constantly moving and the air is constantly exchanging through the oculus. The surfaces themselves are warm — the brick, the tile, the glaze — heated by the water and the sun. The smell is mineral, clay, wet brick — an earthy, warm, slightly metallic smell, like the smell of a potter's workshop after rain, or the smell of a well that has been opened after a long time. There is no organic smell, no decay, no life. The air tastes of minerals, faintly metallic, faintly sweet.

---

The chamber is small. Three paces across, perhaps five long, with a ceiling that arches overhead at the height of a raised arm. You could stand in the center and almost touch both walls. The space is entirely enclosed — no windows, no doors, no openings except the oculus. Everything else is wall, floor, ceiling. And everything is made.

There is no natural substrate anywhere. No stone, no earth, no exposed ground, no raw material. The floor is laid with ceramic tiles — flat, square, hand-pressed in wooden molds, each one slightly different in size and thickness. The clay was mixed with sand and perhaps ground brick, and it was fired in a simple kiln to a pale, warm buff. The tiles are laid edge to edge with thin seams of clay mortar, and they are still sharp-edged, unworn, their surfaces showing the granular texture of the clay and the occasional imprint of a finger or a tool — a thumbprint pressed into the corner of one tile, the drag of a smoothing tool across the surface of another. In the brightest light, they glow like pale honeycomb. The tiles are slightly uneven — some sit a fraction higher than others, creating small ridges and depressions that catch the light and cast tiny shadows, that create small pools of still water in the depressions where the circulation does not reach.

The walls rise from the floor in courses of hand-made brick. Each brick is rectangular but irregular — the clay was shaped in wooden molds, turned out, dried, and fired, and each one carries the slight variations of hand-work: the texture of the mold, the marks of the thumb or the smoothing tool, the uneven color where the kiln heat was stronger or weaker. The bricks are laid in clay mortar, and the mortar is still soft in places, still curing, still absorbing water. The dominant color is buff and ochre, but some bricks are glazed — a simple alkaline glaze with copper or iron, giving patches of turquoise and rust-red. The glaze is uneven, pooling thick in the center of some bricks and thinning to a wash at the edges, with a slightly raised rim where the glaze surface tension held it before firing. Bubbles and pinholes mark the surface where the kiln heat was uneven or the glaze was too thick. The unglazed brick is porous and slightly rough, and in the water it darkens and lightens in patches, showing the variations in the clay body — some bricks more orange, some more yellow, some with dark flecks of iron or manganese in the clay. The glazed brick is smoother, harder, and the water beads slightly on its surface, refusing to wet it completely, so that the glaze seems to glow from within, the color intensified by the thin film of air between the water and the glass.

The ceiling is a barrel vault of brick, laid in concentric courses that curve overhead. The bricks here are smaller, thinner, shaped to fit the curve — wedge-shaped, really, with the narrow edge toward the center of the vault. The mortar is the same clay, and the surface is slightly rough, showing the marks of the mason's tool — the edge of the trowel, the drag of the smoothing board, the occasional scrape where a brick was adjusted after it was laid. The oculus is a simple opening in the crown of the vault, framed by a ring of brick that rises slightly above the curve, a low collar that prevents debris from falling in and that shapes the column of light. The shaft above is lined with pale ceramic — the same buff tile as the floor, or perhaps a finer, whiter ceramic — and it rises to the surface, to daylight, to sun. The shaft is narrow enough that the light comes through as a defined column, not a broad wash, and it moves across the chamber as the sun moves across the sky — slowly, over the course of hours, the column sliding across the floor and up the opposite wall, the caustic patterns following it, the shadow of the oculus collar cutting a sharp arc through the light.

The water fills the chamber to within an arm's length of the ceiling. It is clear enough to see the floor and walls distinctly — the tiles, the brick, the glaze, the patterns of light — but it is not perfectly clear. It has a slight mineral haze, a faint warmth in its transparency, like looking through thin honey or through breath on glass. The haze is the dissolved minerals — calcium, perhaps, or silica, or iron — that leach from the clay and the mortar and the glaze, that saturate the water and give it body. The water tastes mineral, slightly metallic, slightly sweet. It is warm and slightly viscous, with a density that you can feel when you move your hand through it — a slight resistance, a slight thickness, as if the water were heavier than ordinary water.

Particles drift in the light column — tiny grains of clay, mineral flakes, occasional air bubbles that rise slowly from the floor or the walls. The bubbles form on the rough surfaces of the unglazed brick, where the water is slightly supersaturated with dissolved gases, and they grow and detach and rise in slow spirals, catching the light as they go, bright points ascending through the column. The clay grains drift and settle, accumulating in the slight depressions between tiles, creating thin films of pale sediment that are barely visible but that you can see in the angled light — a faint softening of the tile edges, a slight blurring of the grout lines. The sediment is finest in the center of the chamber, where the circulation is slowest, and coarser near the walls, where the currents are stronger.

---

The surfaces are in various states of freshness. The submerged walls and floor are still new, sharp-edged, unweathered. The brick and tile have not been smoothed by water or worn by time. The edges are crisp, the textures distinct — the granular tile, the rough brick, the smooth glaze with its bubbles and pinholes. The construction is recent. The flooding is recent. Everything is fresh.

But in places, mineral deposits are already forming. Thin films of calcium or lime, white or pale yellow, beginning to coat the surfaces — especially on the glazed brick, where the smooth surface gives the minerals a footing, and in the areas of strongest light, where the water is warmest and the evaporation is greatest. These deposits are the first sign of change, of the water beginning to alter the made environment. They form in delicate patterns — thin rings around bubbles that once clung to the surface, dendritic films spreading from points of nucleation, soft gradients of color where the mineral content varies. In the brightest light, these mineral films catch and scatter the light, adding a faint iridescence, a subtle shimmer, a slight softening of the colors beneath — the turquoise glaze becomes slightly milky, the rust-red becomes slightly orange, the buff tile becomes slightly cream. The deposits are thinnest at the edges and thickest at the centers of the patterns, and they are still translucent, still delicate, still new.

The mortar is still curing. In places, it is still soft, still absorbing water, still changing. The clay in the mortar is reacting with the water — swelling, softening, beginning to break down in the smallest seams. This is the beginning of erosion, but it is very early, very slight. The edges of the bricks are still sharp. The surfaces are still distinct. The space is young.

Vegetation is sparse. There is no plant life to speak of — no algae mats, no moss, no roots, no stems, no leaves. But in the brightest areas, where the column of light falls most directly on the floor and walls, there are faint films of something. A beginning algae, perhaps, or just a biofilm of mineral-tolerant microorganisms — the first scouts of life, testing the conditions, finding the warm water and the mineral haze and the bright light sufficient to begin. These are barely visible — a faint greenish or brownish tint on the brightest tiles, a slight discoloration that might be mineral or might be life, that resolves into nothing when you look directly at it and only appears at the edge of vision. They are the first colonizers, the beginning of an ecology in a space that has been underwater for only a short time. They are sparse, scattered, balanced with emptiness. Most of the surfaces are still bare, still mineral, still made.

There are no animals. No fish, no insects, no larvae, no worms, no crustaceans. The water is too new, too mineral, too empty. Nothing has found its way in yet, or nothing has survived. The channels that bring the water in and take it out are too narrow, too fast, too exposed — nothing could swim through them against the current, and nothing has been carried in on the feet of birds or in the fur of mammals, because there is no way for birds or mammals to reach this place. The oculus is too high, the shaft too smooth, the water too deep. The chamber is sealed against the world, and the world has not yet found a way in. The only movement is the water itself, the light, the shifting of particles, the drip of condensation, the slow rise of bubbles.

---

The chamber is mostly empty. The water fills most of the space, and the air is a thin layer at the top — a space of perhaps two hand-spans between the water's surface and the curve of the vault. There are few objects. The inflow channel at one end — a brick-lined channel at floor level, with a simple copper pipe or a ceramic conduit that emerges from the wall and pours water into the chamber in a steady, silent stream that only becomes sound when it strikes the surface. The outflow at the other end — a similar channel, or a drain in the floor, a circular opening ringed with brick, from which the water draws away with a slight suction. Perhaps a ceramic vessel, left behind, sitting on the floor in the half-light — a wide bowl or a storage jar, hand-thrown, with the marks of the potter's wheel still visible in the slight ridges on its surface, filled with water and sediment and the faint film of mineral deposit. Perhaps a tool — a trowel, a smoothing board, a wooden level — resting on a ledge or caught in a corner, the wood dark and swollen with water, the metal rusted to a deep orange. Perhaps a simple ladder or steps leading down from the oculus, their lower rungs submerged, their upper rungs dry, the wood silvered and cracked from the sun. But these are few, scattered, low in saturation. The space is balanced with emptiness. The made environment is the presence: the walls, floor, ceiling, the water, the light. The sparseness is part of the atmosphere — the sense of a space that is mostly water and air, enclosed in made materials, with little else.

---

The chamber is active. Water flows in and out, creating currents and sounds. Condensation forms and drips — a drop every few seconds, sometimes faster, sometimes slower, depending on the temperature and the humidity and the movement of air through the oculus. Minerals deposit on surfaces, the thin films growing almost imperceptibly, the patterns slowly expanding. The mortar cures and softens, the clay absorbing water, the seams beginning to yield. Light shifts as the sun moves across the oculus, changing the patterns on the floor and walls — the caustic networks of bright lines that pulse and shift with the water's surface, that slide across the tiles as the sun moves, that brighten and dim as clouds pass or as the angle of the sun changes, the column of light migrating slowly across the chamber from one end to the other over the course of the day, so that the brightest spot on the floor moves, and the shadows shift, and the quality of the light changes from warm-white in the morning to gold-white at midday to amber-white in the afternoon. The water level rises and falls slightly with the inflow — a finger's width, perhaps two, over the course of hours, as the source varies with the season or the weather or the use upstream. Temperature fluctuates with the sun and the inflow — warmer in the light column, cooler in the shadowed corners, warmer near the inflow, cooler near the outflow, the variations small but real, detectable by the skin, creating a slight thermal texture to the water that you can feel when you move through the chamber. The space is not static. It is in process, changing, settling into its new submerged state. Everything is fresh, new, in the early stages of its underwater existence.

---

The feeling of the place is one of enclosure, warmth, brightness, and sound. It is intimate — the space is small, the ceiling low, the walls close, the water surrounding you on all sides. It is hidden — below ground, below water, enclosed in made materials, accessible only through the oculus or through the channels, unknown to the world above. It is fresh — newly built, newly flooded, sharp-edged, unweathered, the mortar still curing, the mineral deposits just beginning, the biofilm just starting. It is all-made — nothing natural, everything shaped by hand, every surface constructed, no raw material exposed anywhere, the floor and walls and ceiling all of brick and clay and ceramic, the water itself altered by the materials it touches. It is loud — water and air and echo filling the small space, the drip-percussion, the rushing murmur, the reverberation off the hard surfaces. It is warm — the water, the air, the surfaces, the sun on the shaft. It is bright — the column of light, the reflected light, the glowing tiles, the caustic patterns, the mineral iridescence, the way the glazed brick seems to glow from within. It is empty of life but full of process — water, minerals, light, air, all in motion, the space in transition from dry to wet, from new to old, from made to altered.

You stand in the center of the chamber, in the water, in the light, in the sound. The water is warm around you, slightly viscous, mineral-heavy. The light falls on your shoulders and arms, bright enough to read by, bright enough to cast a sharp shadow on the tiles below. The sound envelops you — the rushing, the dripping, the echoing, the low hollow wind through the oculus. The walls are close. The ceiling is low. The space is small and entirely made and entirely present. You are inside something that was built by hand — shaped, dried, fired, laid, mortared, glazed — that was recently flooded, and that is now in the process of becoming something else. The water is altering it. The minerals are depositing. The light is shifting. The sounds are constant. The warmth is constant. The emptiness is balanced. The place is alive with process, even though there is no life. It is a made environment, submerged, in transition, becoming.
