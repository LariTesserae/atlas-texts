The world is all water. It has been all water for longer than there has been anything to measure the time with — the flood, if it was a flood, is a memory in the rock, and the rock is gone, ground down, buried, replaced. There is no land. There is the surface — a lighter patch of black, kilometers up, a rumor — and the bottom — the plain, the trench, the drowned mountains — and between them the whole of the world: the water column, the cold, the dark, the sound.

You hang in the water. The cold is immediate and total — two degrees, the temperature of the deep — and it slows everything: your thought, your movement, the life around you. You are neutrally buoyant. You do not stand or sit. You hang, and the water holds you, and the current — a deep, slow, massive river — presses against you and moves you, and you drift, and the towers drift past, and the snow falls, and the sound is everywhere.

The dark is total. The sun is old and dim and far, and its light does not reach here. The only light is the light of life: the faint blue-green of bioluminescence, the glow of the fauna, the faint luminescence of the mat itself, and far above — the lighter patch of black — the surface, the unreachable. Your eyes are nearly useless. You see with sound, with pressure, with the faint electrical sense of the creatures around you. The world is heard, not seen.

---

The seafloor is the plain — the abyssal plain — ancient, flat, the oldest stable state of the world. The mountains that were once above the water are ground down to their roots; the roots are buried under the mat; the mat is the floor. The plain extends to the horizon of the seafloor — a horizon of fine, pale sediment, the compressed remains of a billion billion dead — and on it, scattered, spaced far apart, the towers rise.

The towers are the forest. They are the vegetation of this world — plant-as-substrate, plant-as-architecture, all-living-mass — and they are the only relief. They rise from the mat — kilometers tall — and they are made of the mat's own tissue, grown, not built. They are not random. They are structured. They have buttresses, arches, vaults, galleries, chambers, tunnels, chimneys, lattices, domes, spires. They are the size of cathedrals, of skyscrapers, of mountains, and they are alive — every surface is tissue, every chamber is lined with filter-feeding polyps, every tunnel is a gallery of grazing creatures, every chimney vents the mat's slow breath. There is no bare rock anywhere. The mat has replaced the planet's geology with biology. The world is a made world — a grown world — and the making is biological, without tools, without devices, without anything but the mat's own growth. The world is a natural artifact, a living architecture, a planet that is a single organism and a single building at once.

The towers are translucent. The tissue is glass-like, a lattice of silica or chitin or something between, and it is faintly lit — from within, by the mat's own luminescence, by the fauna that lives in it, by the faint blue that filters down from the unreachable surface. In the dark, the towers appear as columns of faint light — pillars of smoke, ghosts, memories of trees — and you can see them for kilometers, fading into the black, one after another, a forest of ghosts on the plain. They are spaced far apart — the plain is open, the density is low, the emptiness is balanced against the presences — and between them there is only water, snow, and the drifters.

---

The canopy — the tops of the towers — is a false surface. The towers grow toward the light, but the light is too far, and they die back before they reach it. The tallest towers are on the highest ground — the drowned mountains, the seamounts — and the shortest are in the trenches, and the canopy is the same distance below the surface everywhere — a false surface, an inverted map of the seafloor. The forest's canopy mirrors the drowned landscape: the mountains make the tall towers, the trenches make the short towers, and the whole is a planet-scale inverted relief, a reflection of the ground in the ceiling of the green fringe — the thin skin of light-harvesting tissue at the towers' tips.

And the tips are in the twilight — the last few hundred meters where a little light penetrates — and there the tissue is green, photosynthetic, a thin skin of solar panel on a vast dark structure. Below that, the towers are dark — the bulk of the structure is in the dark, and it runs on other things: the marine snow, the chemical seeps, the slow rain of the dead from the sunlit world above. The towers are conduits between the light and the dark, between the surface and the abyss — they catch the snow, they channel the current, they house the fauna, they breathe. They grow toward the light they cannot reach, and they have grown toward it forever, and they will grow toward it forever, and they will never reach it. The longing is the point. The growth is the point. The song is the point.

---

The mat is the floor and the material. It covers everything — the plain, the trench walls, the drowned mountains — a living carpet, meters thick, tens of meters thick in places, and it is soft, yielding, like a sponge, like a lung, like a brain. Its surface is folded, grooved, a labyrinth of filter-feeding pores. It is pale, translucent, faintly blue-green — the color of deep water, of glass, of bone, of moonlight. The sediment beneath it is the mat's own compressed dead — a soil made of ancestors — and the mat grows on it, and the towers rise from it, and the whole world is the mat's body. The mat is a colony — a single species, perhaps a single individual, a superorganism — a filter-feeder and a chemoautotroph, farming symbiotic bacteria that process the minerals from the seeps and the organic matter from the snow. It builds its own skeleton — the glass-like lattice — and it grows into the towers, the arches, the vaults, the chimneys. It is the builder, the material, and the product.

The trench walls are terraced — the mat grows up the walls in steps, like a rice paddy, like a quarry, like the rings of a tree. Each bench is a generation, a level of growth — the mat grows up the wall in steps, and each step is a flat terrace with towers on it, and the risers are sheer walls of tissue. The whole trench wall is a staircase of gardens, a ziggurat of living tissue, rising from the trench floor to the surface. The original rock is gone — the walls are the mat's own construction — the mat has replaced the rock — the ancient rock was ground down and the mat built up in its place — the world is a palimpsest — the rock is the underwriting, the mat is the overwriting — and the mat's terraces are the text.

---

The water is clear. The visibility is enormous — kilometers — and the towers fade into the dark like ghosts. The marine snow falls — a constant, gentle rain of white particles — the detritus of the sunlit world: the dead plankton, the fecal pellets, the shed skin, the dust of the surface, falling for weeks, for months, to reach the bottom. It falls on the mat, on the towers, on you — a soft, endless patter, the sound of the world's bread. The snow is the base of the food web — the mat catches it, the fauna eats the mat, the great beasts eat the fauna, the great beasts die and fall and become snow again. The cycle is closed. The equilibrium is perfect.

The current is a river in the ocean — deep, slow, massive — and it presses against the towers, and the towers sway — slowly, massively, their tops moving meters, tens of meters, in the flow — and the swaying drives the song. The towers are instruments. They have hollow chambers, tubes, resonators — the current drives them like organs, like flutes, like the pipes of a planet-sized organ — and each tower has a voice: a low hum, a drone, a chord. The forest is a chord of drones. The song is constant, loud, felt in the bones, in the chest, in the swim bladder. The world is never silent.

---

The fauna is abundant. The towers teem with life — a city of creatures. Fish school in the towers' branches, their scales flashing faintly in the bioluminescence; cephalopods change color and texture, their skin a screen of light and pattern, communicating, hunting, hiding; crustaceans crawl and snap, their claws clicking like rain; gelatinous things pulse and drift in the galleries; worms burrow in the mat; things that glow; things that are transparent; things with teeth; things with many eyes; things that are new and unnamed. The mat itself is alive with smaller things — the grazers, the filter feeders, the detritivores, the things that farm the mat's surface, the things that live in its tunnels, the giant single-celled things in the sediment — the size of plates, of platters, of shields. The water column between the towers is full of drifters — the marine snow eaters, the predators, the things that pulse and glow and wait.

And the great beasts — the whale analogues — move through the forest, vast and slow, their bodies the size of towers, their calls felt across the basin. They are the only creatures that know the whole world; they sing the maps; their songs are the memory of the world. They sing the locations of the seamounts, the vents, the towers; they sing the histories of the currents; they sing the names of the other great beasts, the ones who are still alive, the ones who are singing back. Their songs travel for thousands of kilometers; they are the loudest things in the world; they are the world's voice, the world's memory, the world's conversation. When a great beast dies, it falls — a slow, silent fall, kilometers down — and it becomes an oasis: the mat grows over it, the fauna consumes it, the tower that rises from its bones is the tallest for kilometers, a monument, a garden, a new voice in the chord. The dead build the living. The great beasts are the soil.

---

The vents — the warm seeps — are the planet's breath. They are scattered across the plain, across the trench walls, across the drowned mountains — oases of heat in the cold, of minerals in the clear water, of chemical energy in the dark. The mat grows thickest there; the towers are densest; the fauna clusters; the chimneys rise — mineral chimneys, grown by the mat and by the minerals together — and the water shimmers with heat — a few degrees warmer — and the sound is different there: the low rumble of the planet's interior, the hiss of the mineral-rich water, the creak of the chimneys growing. The vents are the centers of life, the hearths of the world, and they are ancient and stable — they have been the same for epochs — the planet's breath is slow and steady.

---

The world is old. The sun is old and dim; the planet's tectonics have slowed; the water is cold; the erosion is finished. The mountains are ground down; the rock is buried; the mat has replaced the geology; the plain is flat; the world has settled into its final form. The equilibrium is frozen — a climax at the scale of a world. The mat's growth is balanced by its shedding, by the grazing of the fauna, by the slow compression of its base into sediment. The towers grow to a set height and stop. The vents are stable. The climate is stable — the ocean's circulation is stable, the temperature is stable, the marine snow is stable. The great beasts sing the same songs. The snow falls. The world sings. Nothing changes. The world has been this way for epochs, and it will be this way until the sun dies. It is the end of history — not dying, but finished — perfect, complete, still.

The rules of the world are simple: the mat builds; the fauna lives in the building; the snow falls; the current sings; the great beasts remember; the world is still. There is no tool, no device, no technology — only biology. The mat is the only builder; the fauna is the only tenant; the world is the only artifact. The distinction between nature and artifact collapses: the world is a grown machine, a living architecture, a planet that is a single organism and a single building at once.

---

The mood of the place — the feeling of hanging in the cold, in the dark, in the sound — is awe and melancholy, stillness and grandeur, beauty and loneliness. It is a world at the end of its history, singing to itself in the dark. The towers grow toward a light they cannot reach. The snow falls. The great beasts sing the maps of a world that does not change. The mat breathes. The current presses. The song goes on. It has gone on for epochs. It will go on until the sun dies. The world is finished, and it is perfect, and it is alone, and it sings.
