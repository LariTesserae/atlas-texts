The water is warm. That is the first thing — before the darkness, before the stone, before the life. Warm like blood, warm like a body, warm like something that is breathing from below. The heat comes from vents somewhere far down the mountain's slopes, mineral-rich exhalations that rise through the stone and into the water and make this place — at this depth, in this darkness — almost tropical. The water holds the heat like a memory. It has not cooled for a very long time.

And it is dark. Not the absolute dark of the abyssal plain — there is a faint, blue-green suggestion of light from far above, a twilight that never quite becomes day — but the dark of a room with the curtains drawn, the dark of a held breath. Shapes are visible in the middle distance: the mass of the mountain, the angles of the construction, the slow movement of fish. But detail is lost. The texture of the stone, the features of the fish, the far walls — all dissolve into the dimness. You would need a light to see clearly. There is no light here except what the animals make, and the animals make very little.

The mountain rises from the plain below. Its summit — the highest point for many kilometers in any direction — is entirely covered by the structure. Not built on top of the mountain. Not built into the mountain. Built *as* the mountain, replacing it, consuming it, transforming it into something else. From the upper slopes to the crest, every surface is worked stone. Block upon block of dark, fine-grained basalt, fitted together without mortar, without metal, without any visible binding agent. The blocks are small — perhaps thirty centimeters in their longest dimension, perhaps less — small enough to have been carried by a single person, small enough to have been set in place by hands. They are laid in courses, one upon another, with joints so tight that a knife blade cannot pass between them. The precision is extraordinary. It is also clearly the work of many hands over many generations — the slight irregularities of handwork, the small variations in block size and joint width, the occasional block that is slightly out of true, all speak of individual craftsmen working with individual tools.

The tools were simple. This is evident from the marks they left — the pick marks, the chisel marks, the grinding marks — which are visible wherever the mineral deposits have not yet covered the stone. The marks are small, closely spaced, and slightly irregular. They were made by something harder than the stone but not much harder — perhaps obsidian, perhaps a copper alloy, perhaps a kind of hardened bone. The builders did not have metal in any abundance. They did not have the wheel, or the pulley, or the crane. They had levers and sledges and ropes and the strength of many arms. And they had time. They had time measured in generations, in centuries, in the slow accumulation of one block upon another until the mountain was entirely consumed and the structure was complete.

The structure is dense. It is not a city — it is too contained, too singular, too all-of-a-piece for that. It is more like a single vast piece of architecture, a complex of chambers and passages that occupies the entire summit and upper slopes of the mountain. The chambers are tall and narrow, often rising through multiple levels, connected by vertical shafts and spiraling staircases that are themselves narrow enough that a person must turn sideways to climb them. The passages are low and close, sometimes requiring you to crawl, sometimes requiring you to remove your pack and push it ahead of you. The walls are enormously thick — in places, many meters of solid stone separate one chamber from the next. The overall impression is of something that was built to last, and to contain, and to separate. The chambers are not rooms in any domestic sense. They have no hearths, no beds, no storage jars, no signs of habitation. They are simply spaces — tall, narrow, stone-lined spaces — connected by passages and shafts into a dense, three-dimensional lattice that fills the mountain from the summit to perhaps a hundred meters down its flanks.

The scale is cathedral-like. The largest chamber — if it is a chamber — rises perhaps sixty meters from floor to ceiling, and its walls are lined with thousands of small niches, each just large enough to hold a human skull, or a jar, or a carefully shaped stone. The niches are arranged in regular rows and columns, and they extend from the floor to a height of perhaps twenty meters, above which the walls are smooth and unbroken. The ceiling is lost in darkness. The floor is covered in a thick layer of fine, pale sediment — the accumulated residue of millennia of mineral precipitation and biological activity. The niches are mostly empty now. A few still hold objects: a fragment of carved stone, a piece of shaped bone, a dark, glassy lump that might be obsidian. Most are empty. Whatever was placed here is gone — taken by the water, by time, by the animals that now inhabit the structure.

Inside the structure, there is no natural substrate. No raw rock, no soil, no unworked surface. Every wall, every floor, every ceiling is built — is fitted, shaped, intentional. The mountain has been entirely consumed and replaced. Even where the construction has collapsed or eroded, what lies beneath is more construction — older courses of blocks, earlier phases of building, deeper layers of the same dense, deliberate stonework. The builders did not build on the mountain. They built the mountain. And then they built it again, and again, each generation adding to what came before, until the original peak was entirely gone and only the structure remained — a mountain of fitted stone rising from the plain, indistinguishable from a natural peak except for the regularity of its angles and the precision of its joints.

The structure is ancient beyond reckoning. The warm water and the minerals it carries have been working on the stone for thousands of years, and the evidence is everywhere. The sharp edges of the original carving have been rounded and softened. The joints between blocks have been widened by dissolution and narrowed by deposition, sometimes in the same place. Pale, flowstone-like formations of calcium and silica have accumulated on every surface — on the walls, on the floors, on the ceilings, on the niches and their contents. In some chambers, the mineral deposits have grown into columns and curtains and draperies, like the interior of a limestone cave, except that they are growing on and through a built environment. The deposits are not uniform. They follow the paths of water flow — the channels and openings that direct the warm water through the structure — and they are thickest where the water moves slowest, where it has time to give up its dissolved minerals. In some places, the deposits have entirely filled a niche, or a passage, or a small chamber, transforming it from a space into a solid mass of stone. In other places, the dissolution of the stone has created new spaces — small cavities and channels that did not exist in the original construction.

The warm water circulates through the structure in slow, patient currents. Channels and openings — some original, some the result of collapse and erosion — direct the flow of water from the thermal vents below, up through the complex, and out into the dark. The water is thick with minerals and with the microscopic life that feeds on them. It is not quite clear. It has a faint, opalescent quality, as if it is slightly more than water — as if it is water that has been enriched, thickened, made more substantial by its passage through the stone and the heat. The current is slow but persistent. It moves through the structure like breath through lungs, like blood through veins. It is the pulse of the place.

There are no plants. None. The warm water and the minerals and the faint light from above would be enough to support algae, or seaweed, or the simpler photosynthetic organisms — but there are none. The water is clear of them. The stone is bare of them. Whatever process governs this place, whatever chemistry or biology or something else, it excludes the photosynthetic. The only life here is animal. The only growth is mineral and animal. The structure is a world without green.

The fauna is abundant. Every surface, every niche, every passage is inhabited. Fish of many kinds — pale, eyeless or nearly eyeless, their scales faintly bioluminescent in the blue-green spectrum — move in slow schools through the chambers. They are small, most of them, rarely more than a hand's length, and they move with the unhurried grace of animals that have never known a predator they could not see. Crustaceans — crabs, shrimp, things that are not quite either — pick at the mineral deposits and at each other, their shells thick with accretions that make them look like walking stones. Octopuses, small and large, occupy the darker niches, their bodies shifting color to match the stone, their eyes — large, intelligent, ancient — watching the fish and the crabs and anything else that moves. Eels thread through the passages like living roots, their bodies pale and translucent, their movements sinuous and silent. Tube worms and other sessile forms cluster around the vents and around any surface where the warm water flows, their plumes — red, white, orange — waving in the current like flowers in a slow wind. Small sharks patrol the outer passages, their movements precise and unhurried. Rays settle on the sediment-covered floors, their bodies half-buried in the fine, pale dust. And there are other things — things that are harder to categorize, things that live in the deepest, warmest, darkest parts of the structure, things that have evolved in this specific place and exist nowhere else. The structure is not merely inhabited by life — it is a reef, an ecosystem, a dense and layered community of organisms that has grown up in and around and through the construction. The boundary between the built and the living has been dissolving for thousands of years.

The sound is almost nothing. The deep water here is remarkably quiet — the silence of depth, of distance, of a place that is far from any source of noise. The structure itself creaks faintly as it settles and shifts — a sound like a wooden ship, except that it is stone, and the sound is deeper, slower, more patient. The thermal vents produce a subsonic rumble that is felt more than heard, a vibration in the stone that you sense in your bones before you hear it in your ears. The animals make occasional sounds — clicks, pops, scrapes, the faint rustle of a crustacean moving across stone — but these are small, isolated, quickly swallowed by the water. For long stretches, there is only the faint, almost imperceptible sound of water moving through stone — a whisper, a breath, a pulse. The silence is not empty. It is full of the slow, patient processes of the place — mineral growth, biological colonization, the imperceptible settling of ancient stone.

The change here is slow. Things happen, but they happen across centuries, not years. A wall shifts by a centimeter. A new mineral formation grows by a millimeter. A species of fish colonizes a new chamber. A niche fills with sediment. A passage widens. A block settles. The structure is settling into its final form — not collapsing, exactly, but completing. It is becoming something that is part architecture, part geology, part biology — a hybrid of the made and the grown, the built and the lived-in. The warm water will continue to flow. The minerals will continue to deposit. The life will continue to spread. And the stone will continue its slow, patient return to the shape it held before the builders came — though it will never quite get there. The memory of the construction is too deep in the stone. The structure will always be, in some sense, a building. Even when no trace of the individual blocks remains. Even when the mountain is entirely dissolved into the water and the life that fills it. The shape will remain. The intention will remain. The memory of the hands that placed each block, one upon another, in the darkness and the warmth and the silence, will remain in the stone.
