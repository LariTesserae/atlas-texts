# The Tarn of Unfinished Stone

The path ends where the moraine begins. You cross a ridge of loose, unsorted rock — blocks the size of fists beside blocks the size of cars, all angular, all sharp-edged, all fresh from the ice — and then the cirque opens below you like a palm cupped to hold something.

The lake is the first thing you see. Its color is wrong. Not blue, not green, but a saturated milky turquoise, as if someone has dissolved a piece of sky into the water and then stirred in chalk. This is glacial flour — rock ground so fine by the glacier's basal sliding that it remains suspended for months, individual particles too small to settle, scattering light in every direction. From above, the lake appears solid. Like poured ceramic. Like a sheet of condensed atmosphere. You could almost walk on it.

You cannot. You pick your way down the talus, and the second thing arrives before the first is finished: sound. It hits you ten meters below the moraine crest — a low, constant roar, layered with sharper sounds: cracks like gunshots, grinding bass tones, continuous hissing. The glacier. It fills the bowl of the cirque the way water fills a basin — completely, overflowing its banks. Sound has weight here. You feel it in your sternum, in your teeth. It is not a noise you hear so much as a pressure you endure.

---

The glacier terminates at the lake's far end, a wall of ice perhaps forty meters high, streaked with black — bands of rock debris it has carried down from the peaks and now expels at its terminus like sediment in a wound. The ice is blue in its depths, white where it is fractured, grey where the debris concentrates. It is not a static thing. Even when you stand motionless, watching, you can see it fail. Sections crack and slump. Every few minutes something gives way: a serac — a tower of ice the size of a building — calves from the terminus and falls into the lake with a sound too large to be called a splash. The sound is a detonation followed immediately by a wave, and the wave crosses the lake in seconds and slaps the shore where you stand, sending sheets of milky water hissing up the talus. The new iceberg — because ice floats — bobs in the impact zone, grinding against its neighbors with a sound like millstones, like jawbones, like two mountains deciding whether to become one.

The lake is small. You could throw a stone across its narrow dimension. It sits in a bowl the glacier carved over millennia, but the lake itself is new — decades old, perhaps less. A child compared to the ice. It exists because the glacier retreated past a threshold: the moraine it had pushed ahead of itself like a snowplow formed a dam across the valley's lowest point, and meltwater began to pool behind it. The lake is a consequence of retreat. It is the glacier's absence made visible, a blue wound where ice used to be.

And it may not last. The moraine dam is still settling. Sections slump periodically — you can see fresh scars on its inner face where saturated debris has let go and slid into the lake, sending its own waves. The lake level has been rising, slowly. Or it might one day breach entirely, draining in a single catastrophic afternoon, leaving behind a flat grey plain of wet sediment and the stranded bones of icebergs. The lake is provisional. Everything here is provisional. This is the first rule of the place.

---

The cirque walls rise steeply on three sides. The rock is raw — freshly exposed by the ice's retreat, not yet weathered, not yet rounded. It is all sharp edges and planar faces, still showing the scratch-marks — *striations* — where the glacier's basal debris scraped across it like a engraver's chisel. The color is grey, grey, grey, but a grey that shifts: blue-grey in shadow, almost black where wet, a warm gunmetal where the sun strikes directly. No soil. No lichen except in the most sheltered pockets, where a few square centimeters of orange crustose lichen have begun their slow colonization — the only color besides the lake's own.

The rock is still adjusting to being uncovered. Frost-shattering works constantly: water seeps into cracks, freezes, expands, and the rock breaks. You hear the results — intermittent sharp cracks from the walls, and occasionally a larger rockfall that sends a cloud of grey dust down the slope and into the lake, where the impact makes its own small thunder and a column of white spray rises and falls.

The shoreline is chaos. Talus blocks extend into the water, their submerged portions visible for a few inches before the glacial flour blocks all light. The water's edge is not a line but a zone — sometimes a beach of fine grey sediment, sometimes a wall of rock, sometimes a steep drop where a large block has rolled in and the lake bed falls away immediately into opacity. There is no gentle gradient, no sandy shore. The lake has not been here long enough to sort its margins. It has not learned the shapes that older lakes know.

At the lake's edge, if you kneel and look closely, you can see the underwater world only in the first few inches: the pale faces of submerged boulders, a drift of fine sediment, the occasional bubble rising from somewhere below — deep, resonant, breaking the surface with a sound like a large door closing somewhere far away. These are gases — carbon dioxide, perhaps, or methane from decomposing organic matter trapped beneath the moraine — that seep up through the lakebed and release when the pressure overcomes the surface tension. Each bubble is a small exhalation from the earth's interior, a reminder that the lake is not just water but a membrane between the atmosphere and something deeper, something still working.

---

Vegetation is almost absent. In the seeps where meltwater trickles down the cirque walls, there are patches of moss — bright green, improbably vivid against the grey, the color of something that has decided to live despite every discouragement. A few alpine saxifrages have colonized the most stable rock ledges, their small white flowers improbable and brave, opening and closing with the passage of clouds. Near the outlet stream — a loud, braided channel of milky water that carries the lake's overflow down to the valley below in a continuous high-pitched hiss — there is a thin margin of sedge and rush, establishing itself in the wet sediment with the tentative energy of pioneers who are not sure the land will hold. These are exceptions. The overwhelming impression is mineral: rock, ice, water, sky. The biological is a rumor here.

Fauna is similarly scarce. Chironomid midges dance in swarms over the water's surface on calm mornings, the only visible insects, their bodies catching the light like flecks of metallic dust. An alpine accentor — a small, sparrow-like bird with a rusty flank — picks methodically along the shoreline, appearing to find something to eat in the raw gravel, though you cannot imagine what. Once, a fox crosses the moraine above, heading somewhere else, its red coat absurd against the grey. The lake has no fish. It is too new, too isolated, its water too choked with sediment. If fish ever come, they will arrive as eggs stuck to the feet of birds, or they will be carried here in the plastic containers of humans. For now, the lake is a water world without swimmers. Its ecology is a blank page.

---

The artifacts are minimal but present. Near the outlet stream, half-buried in gravel, stands a stone structure — a rough bivouac shelter, perhaps two meters by three, built from flat slabs without mortar. It is old. The stones are not fresh; they have begun to weather, to develop the faintest patina of lichen. The roof is gone — collapsed or removed. Nineteenth-century surveyors, perhaps, or early alpinists seeking the summit of the peak above. It is too crude for permanent habitation: a windbreak, a place to wait out a storm, to melt snow, to leave a note in a tin box that has long since rusted to nothing. Inside, there is gravel and a few fragments of rusted metal: a tin cup, the remains of a fire pit ringed with blackened stones. The firewood would have been carried up from the valley — there is nothing to burn at this altitude. The people who used this place accepted a level of hardship that approaches the ascetic.

There are cairns — piles of stones marking the route across the moraine, marking the lake's edge. Some are ancient, weathered, stable, their stones bonded by lichen into a single organism. Others are fresh, the stones still sharp-edged, placed by someone who passed through last season, or last week. The cairns are a conversation across time: each traveler adding a stone, leaving a mark that says *I was here, the lake was here, this is the way.*

And there are iron pitons driven into the cirque wall near the outlet — rusted, bent, their hangers broken — remnants of some climbing route that has since been abandoned, perhaps because the rock proved too loose, too fresh, too prone to shedding its skin onto anyone below. The metal has stained the surrounding stone orange, a slow rust-bleed that will take centuries to fade.

---

Let us speak of the sound, because the sound is the place. It is deafening not in volume alone but in *density* — there is no silence to retreat into, no gap between sounds, no moment when the world is quiet. The soundscape is continuous and multi-layered, and it is worth inventorying:

The **glacier's voice** is the baseline: a continuous, shifting roar, somewhere between a waterfall and a freight train, with distinct events within it — the sharp *crack* of calving ice (a rifle shot, a cannon, a bone breaking), the deeper *groan* of the ice mass shifting (felt more than heard, a subsonic rumble that makes your inner ear uncertain which way is up), the *hiss* of meltwater streams on the ice surface (like static, like rain on a tin roof, like something frying).

The **lake's voice**: waves from calving events slapping the shore in a rhythm of violence and return. Icebergs grinding against each other — stone millstones, jawbones, tectonic plates. Occasional deep bubbles breaking the surface — resonant plops that suggest something large turning over in the depths. The lapping of water against the underside of floating ice rafts, a sound like fingers drumming on a hollow table.

The **cirque's voice**: rockfall from the walls — intermittent sharp cracks and the larger sustained roar of a significant slide. Wind funnelling down from the peaks, a constant low moan that rises and falls with the shifting of air masses above. The outlet stream, a continuous high-pitched hiss, like a radio tuned between stations, like the sound of a thousand small things being worn away simultaneously.

And sometimes, beneath all of this, a deeper sound: the moraine dam settling, shifting, the lake's foundations adjusting to the weight of the water they hold. It is felt in the ground more than heard — a subsonic tremor that makes your teeth ache and your vision blur at the edges. The mountain is not still. The mountain is *working.*

---

The light at this altitude — above 3,000 meters, say, though the exact figure hardly matters — has a quality of exaggerated clarity. The air is thin and dry. Shadows are almost black, pooling in the lee of boulders like spilled ink. The sky is a deep, saturated blue that seems to press down on the cirque like a physical weight. But the glacier and the lake and the pale rock reflect so much light that the overall impression is one of brilliance, of overexposure. The lake's surface mirrors the sky and the ice wall simultaneously, doubling the brightness. You find yourself squinting. The light has a physical presence — it warms the rock surfaces even as the air remains cool, creating a dissonance between what you feel on your skin (chill, always chill) and what you feel on your face (heat, a constant gentle burning). The temperature hovers just above freezing in sun, drops sharply in shadow. Your shadow is a cold zone you step into and out of like a room with the heating turned off.

---

The weirdness of the place accumulates slowly, then all at once. It is the color of the water — impossible, artificial, like something from a chemical set, a color that has no business existing at this altitude, in this grey bowl of stone. It is the sound — too loud for such a small body of water, such a contained space, such a pocket of landscape that you can cross in ten minutes on foot. It is the rawness of the rock — it looks wounded, newly born, like bone exposed after the skin has been removed. It is the absence of life — the silence of the biological in a place that is so loud geologically, so densely populated by processes that have no consciousness but have enormous energy. It is the knowledge that this place is temporary — that the lake is a mayfly, a blink, a provisional pooling that could end tomorrow or in a century, but will end.

And there is something else, something harder to name. The glacier sounds almost organic. In certain registers, in certain moods of the ice, it sounds like breathing — slow, rhythmic, enormous. It sounds like something sleeping. It sounds like something dreaming. When a large calving event occurs, the sound is not mechanical but *vocal* — a roar, a bellow, a protest. You begin to feel that the glacier is not an object but a being, and the lake is its thought, and the cirque is its body, and you are standing inside a mind that is thinking very slowly about something you cannot comprehend.

This is the second rule of the place: the boundary between the geological and the alive is not where you were taught it was.

---

If you stay until evening — and you should, though the cold will test you — the light changes in stages. The glacier goes from white to gold to rose to blue-grey, each color lasting only minutes. The lake, which has been mirroring the sky all day, begins to mirror the darkening ice, and its color deepens toward something like teal, then toward indigo, then toward a darkness that is not quite black but the absence of turquoise. The sounds continue — the glacier does not sleep — but the visual drama subsides, and the auditory takes over completely. In darkness, the place becomes almost purely acoustic. The glacier is a voice without a body. The rockfalls are hands clapping in the dark. The lake is a drum being struck by falling ice. You lie in your shelter — the old stone hut, perhaps, if the wind is not too fierce — and listen to the mountain being born, or dying, or both. The distinction has lost meaning.

In the morning, if you are lucky, the lake is calm. The overnight meltwater has slowed. The glacier has been quiet for an hour. A skin of ice has formed in the shallows near the shore — thin, transparent, a film of glass over the grey sediment. The surface of the lake is perfect: a mirror of the cirque walls, the ice, the sky, the first light touching the summit ridge above. For a few minutes, the place achieves a kind of perfection — the provisional made eternal, the temporary made still. The turquoise water holds the mountains like a held breath.

Then the glacier cracks. A calving event — larger than most, a slab of ice the size of a cathedral separating from the terminus and falling. The mirror shatters. Waves radiate outward in concentric rings, each one smaller than the last, each one slapping the shore in a diminishing rhythm. The icebergs grind. The lake resumes its temporary existence. The mountain resumes its becoming.

You stand at the shore and watch. You have been here for a day, or perhaps you have been here for years — time moves differently in a place that is still deciding what it is. The water laps at your boots. The glacier roars. The rock waits to fall. The moss grows, invisibly, in its seep. The lake holds its breath again, and again, and again, each time a little longer, each time a little less certain, each time more beautiful for the certainty that it will end.
