# The Roaring Field

There is no soil on the plateau. There is no green. There is stone, water, and the sound.

The place is a high shelf of pale basalt, lifted above every line — above the tree line, above the grass line, above the reach of anything that grows. It runs for tens of kilometers in every direction, rim to rim packed with pillars: polygonal columns, many six-sided, weathered to bone-white where mineral crusts bloom on them, darker where they weep. From the rim you can look out over the whole field and it reads as a frozen foam, a cracked white skin stretched to every horizon. From inside, it is a maze. The pillars stand mostly head-height to three or four times a person; a few rise like towers. Their crests are rounded and polished, wet and slick underfoot. There is no other ground. You walk the tops or you squeeze the crevices, and the crevices are where the water lives.

The pillars grew, not cracked — grown, like stalagmites the size of buildings, from water saturated with lime. The whole plateau is a cave that turned itself inside-out and kept growing. Where columns lean together, the mineral water fuses them; down low, the base of the field is slowly becoming one continuous stone mass. Feathery crusts and candelabras of white salt — stone-flowers — spread across every lee surface.

**Water.** The plateau sits in the cloud belt, and the clouds condense on the cooler stone. The entire field weeps. Droplets gather on the crusts, thread down the flutes, collect in crevices, pool in hollows the color of milk and weak sky. The water is hard and mineral-heavy; it tastes of chalk and metal, and it deposits as fast as it dissolves — anything left in a pool comes back crusted white, a stick or a bone slowly becoming part of the architecture. The excess leaves at the rim, where hundreds of small falls pour off the edge into the cloud below — the Weeping Rim, audible from inside the field as a constant, distant thunder.

**Light.** The sun at this altitude, filtered through thin air and wet white stone and drifting mist, is close to unbearable. The light is everywhere at once — direct glare and diffuse glow, halos around the sun, no shadow anywhere except inside the crevices, and not much there. You squint until your eyes ache. The white is total. It is cool, though — a raw, damp, steady cold; the wind sees to that. Your clothes never dry. Condensation beads on your skin faster than you can wipe it. The smell is wet rock and nothing else — no earth, no leaf-smell at all.

**Sound.** The first thing, and the always thing. Wind moving through the packed pillars roars — a huge, many-throated, constant roar that is the field's voice. Columns of different heights resonate, and some are hollow: the field hums a low chord beneath the roar, an organ-drone that never resolves. Add the drips — thousands of drips on stone, a bright metallic patter — and the rim falls muttering beyond. It is deafening. You shout and hear a shred of it. The sound lives in your chest and teeth; put a palm flat on a pillar and the stone buzzes against it. You navigate by it: the hollow columns drone as you pass, and the pitch of the roar shifts over the towers versus the low ground, so the ear builds a map the eye cannot. There are strange pockets where the geometry cancels everything — small, sudden rooms of mufflement where you can hear your own heartbeat — but they are rare, and the roar closes behind you like water.

**What lives here.** Almost nothing. The only animals are the soarers — big, patient, vulture-sized things that ride the updrafts off the rim and rarely land, black against the white. You can go a whole day and see nothing living but one of them, hanging in the wind at the edge of the world. Their cries vanish into the roar. In the wettest crevices, black and rust films of something simpler than plants stain the stone — mineral-eaters, not life as the lowlands know it. No insects to speak of. No birdsong; there is no room for it.

**The rule of the place.** Equilibrium. Condensation equals spillover; dissolution equals deposition. The pillars grow at exactly the rate they erode — the tall lose their tops, the short accrete, and the field keeps itself. Nothing accumulates. Nothing is lost. There are no storms and no still days; the wind is a constant, the cloud deck a constant, the drip a constant. The roar today is the roar of a thousand years ago. The water weeping now is the same water, give or take the cloud. The place has no history because it has no change; the light swings through its arc, the drip keeps its time, and that is all the change there is.

No one built anything here. The nearest people — herders in the valleys far below — call it the Roaring Field, or the Weeping Stone, and they don't come. Sometimes the cloud tops break at the rim and you can see their country: green, impossibly green, belonging to another world entirely.
