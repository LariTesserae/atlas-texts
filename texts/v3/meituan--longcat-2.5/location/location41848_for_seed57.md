# The Hollow

The chamber has no name that the living know. It sits inside a stone the size of a small house, and the stone rests on the floor of a cold ocean, and the ocean is old, and the chamber is older.

You reach it through a crack. The stone's exterior is black basalt, glassy and sharp, still faintly warm to the touch where the rock has not yet finished cooling. The crack is narrow — a vertical wound in the stone, barely wide enough to admit a body, winding down and then up and then down again before it opens, without warning, into the chamber. The transition is abrupt. The passage water is cold, ordinary, moving with the slow pulse of the outer sea. The chamber water is still. It has been still for longer than the stone has existed. It is cool — the temperature of deep earth, not deep water — and slightly thick, slightly viscous, and it resists movement in a way that feels almost deliberate, almost like the water is considering whether to let you pass.

The chamber is small. Intimate. You can see the far wall, or you believe you can — it is dark, and the darkness has texture, and the far wall recedes as you approach. The ceiling is low enough to touch. The floor is uneven, sloping, covered in a thick mat of growth. The walls are the same black basalt as the exterior, but here the rock is sharp, freshly fractured, still shedding fine mineral dust that hangs in the water like smoke, like the rock is slowly dissolving into the sea that surrounds it. The rock is young. It cracked open recently — a few thousand years ago, perhaps, or a few hundred thousand — and the edges are still knife-sharp, still raw. But the water inside is old. It was here before the rock. It was trapped here when the stone formed around it, or it seeped in through pores too small to see, or it has always been here and the rock grew around it like a shell around a pearl. The water is not the same water that fills the outer ocean. It is denser, more mineral, and it does not mix. At the passage mouth, the boundary is sharp — a slight resistance, a change in pressure, a line where the water becomes something else, something older, something that has been waiting.

The chamber is packed with growth. Every surface is covered. The walls are encrusted with a pale, branching organism that is not quite coral and not quite fungus and not quite anything that has a name. It grows in delicate, tree-like forms — white and cream and faintly yellow — and it is fed by minerals seeping from the rock, by the chemical breath of the stone. It hangs in curtains from the ceiling, long strands that do not drift, that hang motionless in the still water like they are frozen, like they are holding their breath. It grows in mats on the floor, thick and soft and resilient, and in the crevices it forms dense, tangled masses that fill every gap, every hollow, every space between stone and stone. There is almost no open water. To move through the chamber is to push through hanging growth, to feel it brush against you, to part it and have it slowly close behind you. The growth is not like surface plants. It is slow. It has been growing for a long time. It is patient. It is the chamber's primary inhabitant, its dominant life, and it has filled every niche, every corner, every surface with itself.

And it is not entirely without light. In places, the growth bioluminesces — not brightly, but with a faint, cold glow, blue-green and pale, that pulses slowly, like breathing, like a heartbeat heard from very far away. The pulses are not synchronized. They spread through the growth in waves, or in patterns that almost resemble meaning, almost resemble language, but never quite resolve into anything you can read. The light is enough to see by, if you let your eyes adjust — enough to see the shapes of things, the contours of the growth, the edges of the stone. Not enough to see clearly. Not enough to be sure of what you are seeing. The darkness between the pulses is deep and textured, and it seems to press against the light, to limit it, to keep it contained in small, cold pools.

There are structures here. Not many. A low wall of stacked stones, partially collapsed, running along one side of the chamber. The stones are not the same rock as the walls — they are different, smoother, darker, brought from elsewhere or formed differently. The wall is crude, unworked, but deliberate. The stones are stacked without mortar, without tools, fitted together by hands that knew what they were doing. Someone built it. Something built it. There is a platform of flat stones, raised a short distance above the floor, covered now in a thick layer of growth, so that it is almost a natural part of the chamber, almost indistinguishable from the floor. There are marks on the walls — scratches, grooves, patterns of arranged pebbles, a spiral of small stones set into a niche. There are a few artifacts — a stone that has been shaped, perhaps, or a bone that has been placed, or a shell that has been arranged. Nothing made with tools. Nothing metal. Nothing that suggests technology. But something made with intention. Something made by hands, or by a body, or by a mind that was here and wanted to leave a mark.

The structures are older than the rock. This is impossible, but it is true. The rock is young — freshly fractured, still shedding dust, still cooling. The structures are worn, smoothed, covered in growth that has had centuries or millennia to establish itself. The rock formed around them. The stone grew around the wall and the platform and the arranged pebbles, and the water was here before both, and the growth came after, and the chamber has been this way for a long time, and it will be this way for a long time more.

The chamber is quiet. The water absorbs sound. The growth muffles it further. You hear your own breathing, if you are breathing. You hear the faint creak of the rock, settling, adjusting, speaking in a language of stress and release. You hear the occasional small sound of the growth — a crack, a release, a slow shift, the sound of something growing so slowly that the sound itself is stretched out over hours. There is a low hum, below hearing, that you feel more than hear — the sound of the stone, or the water, or the chamber itself, a vibration that is not quite sound and not quite warmth and not quite anything you have a name for. It is mostly silent. It is the silence of deep places, of places that have been undisturbed for a long time, of places that are in no hurry.

And the chamber is wrong.

The geometry does not quite work. The chamber is larger inside than the stone that contains it. From outside, the stone is the size of a small house. Inside, the chamber extends further than that — the far wall is further away than it should be, the ceiling is higher in some places than the exterior allows, the floor slopes down into depths that don't match the stone's dimensions. You can see the far wall, or you think you can, but when you swim toward it, it recedes. Not quickly — not obviously — but it is further than it should be, and it is further still when you look back. The corners don't quite meet at right angles. The passage you came through is not quite where you left it. The chamber is not a simple room. It is something that doesn't quite fit together, or that fits together in a way that doesn't match the world outside, or that is still in the process of becoming something else.

Time moves differently here. Not dramatically — not hours passing in minutes — but slowly. A long time inside might be a short time outside. Or a short time inside might be a long time outside. It is hard to tell. The water is so still that there is no way to measure. The growth grows so slowly that you cannot see it change. The rock sheds its dust so slowly that the dust hangs in the water like a permanent haze. Everything here is patient. Everything here is in no hurry. You might stay for what feels like an hour and emerge to find that a day has passed. Or you might stay for what feels like a day and emerge to find that an hour has passed. Or you might stay for what feels like a long time and emerge to find that no time has passed at all, and the water outside is the same temperature, and the light is the same angle, and the world has been waiting for you without moving.

The water is strange. It is ancient, and it has properties that water should not have. It is slightly warm in places — not hot, but warm, near the walls where minerals seep, where the rock is still breathing its heat into the sea. It is slightly cool in other places, near the ceiling, near the passage mouth. The layers do not mix. You can feel the boundary between warm and cool as a slight change in density, a line you can cross, a membrane you can push through. The water near the ceiling is different from the water near the floor — thicker, older, more mineral. The water near the growth is different from the water in the open spaces — sweeter, more viscous, almost like it is being digested by the growth. The chamber is stratified, layered, and the layers are stable in a way that suggests they have been undisturbed for a very long time, in a way that suggests they are not layers of water at all but something else, something that only resembles water.

And the chamber responds to you. Not consciously. Not with intention. But it responds. The growth grows toward you, slowly, over hours or days, in ways that are too directed to be chance — a strand that was hanging straight now curves toward you, a curtain that was closed now opens to reveal a space you could rest in. The bioluminescence pulses in patterns that follow your movement — brighter when you are near, dimmer when you are far, pulsing faster when you move quickly, slower when you are still. The water level changes slightly when you enter — rises, or falls, or shifts in ways that don't correspond to your displacement, as if the chamber is adjusting its volume to accommodate you, or to accommodate something else. The geometry shifts when you are not looking — the far wall is further away after you turn around, the passage is narrower when you try to leave, the ceiling is lower when you look up. The chamber is not static. It is slow, but it is not static. It is alive, in some way that is not quite life, in some way that is older than life, in some way that the world outside has forgotten.

There are few animals here. A small, pale creature that lives in the growth — something between a shrimp and a worm, translucent, that feeds on the growth and is fed on by nothing, that moves through the curtains of growth like a fish through kelp, that freezes when you approach and then darts away into the pale branches. A few tiny fish, or fish-like things, that appear occasionally at the edge of the bioluminescence and disappear into the darkness, that seem to be made of the same pale material as the growth, that might be part of the growth, that might be separate from it. Perhaps something larger, rarely seen, that moves in the darkness beyond the light — a shape, a shadow, a disturbance in the water, a pressure change, a sense of presence that is not quite presence and not quite absence. But mostly there is only the growth, and the water, and the stone, and the structures, and the slow, patient strangeness of the place.

The chamber is hidden. It is embedded. It is a pocket of something else, inside a stone, inside an ocean, inside a world. It is not a place you find by accident, and it is not a place you leave easily. The passage is narrow, and the water is thick, and the geometry is uncertain, and the chamber does not want to let go. Or perhaps it does, and you simply don't want to leave. Or perhaps you leave, and you find that you have been gone for longer than you thought, or shorter, or that you have brought something back with you — a strand of the growth, a pocket of the water, a piece of the chamber's wrong geometry that stays with you, that makes the world outside feel slightly less real, slightly less solid, slightly less certain. You might find that you can still feel the hum, below hearing, in your bones, in your teeth, in the fluid of your inner ear. You might find that the growth has left something in you — a spore, a seed, a fragment of its slow, patient life, that will grow in you in ways you cannot predict, that will make you slightly more like the chamber, slightly less like the world. You might find that you dream of the chamber, that you feel the pull of it, that you want to return, that you will always want to return, that the chamber has marked you as its own, that you are part of its slow, patient, ancient life now, whether you are there or not.

The chamber waits. It has always waited. It will always wait. It is patient. It is in no hurry. It is the hollow inside the stone inside the sea, and it is older than the stone, and it is older than the sea, and it will be here when the sea is gone, when the stone is dust, when the world outside has forgotten that there was ever anything but the chamber, and the water, and the growth, and the slow, patient, ancient dark.
