# The Heart

You come down from the dry world on a stair that ten thousand bare feet have worn into a shallow channel. The stone is warm — at first only slightly, then more, then hot enough that you feel it through your soles. The air thickens. The silence of the surface — the wind, the birds, the distance — falls away behind you, and a sound grows ahead of you: first a drizzle, then a rush, then a roar that you feel in your chest before you hear it with your ears. The walls weep. Water runs down them in sheets and threads, and the stair is a stream. You round a corner, and the dark opens up, and you are standing on a gallery above the Great Hall, and the warmth and the noise and the smell take your breath.

The Great Hall is a stone belly. Fifty paces long, twenty wide, vaulting up into a dark that your lamps cannot find the top of. Two rows of thick square pillars hold the vault, and every pillar is carved with waves and handprints and the marks of counting — tallies of water, of fish, of years, of people, layered over each other until the stone is a manuscript no one alive can fully read. The floor of the hall is not a floor. It is water — a shallow, warm, slow-moving sea, knee-deep at its deepest, ankle-deep at its edges, the color of pale jade from the minerals it carries. You can see the bottom through it: worn stone, the remains of older channels, rimstone terraces built up over centuries into pale frozen waterfalls a hand-span high. Raised walkways of dry stone cross the hall in a loose grid, a foot above the water, and the people walk them barefoot, balancing with the ease of those who have never known a dry floor. The water is full of fish. Pale, blind, pinkish-white, they move through it like slow thoughts, some as long as your forearm, some small as your thumb. They are the wealth of the place.

At the far end of the hall the water pours into a low arch and is gone. At the near end it comes from the Well-Mouth — a shaft in the floor, ten paces across, where the spring rises from the deep earth with a sound like a continuous avalanche. The water there is hotter, and steam rises from it, and the stone around it is crusted with minerals in shades of rust and cream and blood-red. The pulse of the spring is the heartbeat of the Heart. It swells and falls — not with the sun, not with the moon, not with any cycle the surface world would recognize, but with a rhythm of its own, roughly a day, roughly a breath, never quite the same twice. The water level in the hall rises and falls with it by a hand's width. The people set their lives by it. They call the place the Heart because of it, and because it is warm, and because it is where they live, and because it is alive.

Everything here is made. The vault is dressed stone, block on block, fitted without mortar. The pillars are built, not left standing. The channels are cut, the pools are lined, the walkways are laid. Even the rough parts are deliberately rough — the tool marks are visible, the chisel strokes, the drag of the bronze adze. The stone is a soft limestone, easy to work, and it has been worked for so long that the working is the stone. Generations of hands. The style changes as you move through the complex — earlier chambers finer, later chambers rougher, or the reverse, or a mixture, depending on who was carving and when and in what mood. The whole place is a palimpsest of labor. And yet it is not a ruin. It is in balance. The water has worn the channels to their final shape — they no longer cut down, they flow evenly. The pillars stand. The travertine builds up and is harvested and builds up again. The place is mature. It has achieved itself.

The water is the life of it. It comes from the deep earth, warm and mineral-heavy, slightly milky, slightly sweet to drink. It carries calcium and iron and sulfur and things no one has names for. It deposits them as it flows — travertine terraces, rimstone dams, flowstone curtains in the smaller chambers, all in bands of cream and rust and pale green. The people harvest the travertine — it is soft when fresh, hardens in the air, and they carve it into dishes and lamps and figures and building blocks. The water is slightly radioactive, maybe, or maybe just slightly strange — no one knows. It is good for the joints, for the skin, for the lungs. People come from the surface to soak in it, to drink it, to take it away in sealed clay jars. The Bath is the chamber where they do this — a deep warm pool off the Great Hall, steam rising, the stone around it smooth and warm and worn to a polish by centuries of soaking bodies. The Bath is where the people socialize, where they heal, where they argue, where they fall in love, where they die. The water in the Bath is the same water as in the hall, but it has been still long enough to warm to the temperature of blood. You sink into it and you cannot tell where you end and it begins.

The fish are the center of the diet and the economy. There are several species, all pale, all blind, all descended from surface fish that found their way down through the spring and the channels and adapted. The largest — the "old women" — are as long as your arm, slow, ancient, scarred by fights and parasites and the hooks of generations. They are not eaten. They are revered, or at least respected, and they are known individually by the old women who tend them. The medium fish are the staple — farmed in the Fish Hall, a series of tanks and channels off the Great Hall where the water flows through and the fish crowd and feed and grow. They are harvested with nets and traps and spears, and they are eaten fresh, smoked, dried, ground into flour, rendered for oil. The oil is for the lamps, and for the surface trade, and for the anointing of the sick and the newborn. The small fish are everywhere, in every pool, eating whatever falls in — crickets, spores, guano, the scraps of the harvest. The fish are the great recyclers. Nothing is wasted in the Heart. The fish see to that.

Above the fish, the bats. A colony of perhaps ten thousand, in the highest vaults, where it is warmest and driest. They roost on the ceiling in clusters that hang like fruit, and they fly out at dusk through the shafts — a river of bats, a sound like soft rain, a smell like a barn. They return at dawn, and their guano falls on the ledges below, and the people collect it — it is valuable, for the fungus farms, for the surface trade, for the making of saltpeter, for the blessing of the dead. The bats eat the insects — the crickets, the spiders, the beetles, the moths that find their way down — and without them the Heart would be overrun. The bats are the top of the food chain here, along with the fish and the people. They are not killed. They are kin. Their flight patterns are read for omens. Their sounds are part of the music of the place. And sometimes — not often, but sometimes — they fly in formation, tracing patterns on the air that match the patterns carved on the ceiling, as if they are reading the stone, or writing on the air, or dancing with the dead.

The crickets are everywhere. In the walls, the ceiling, the warm damp corners, the Fungus Warrens, the Dwellings. Their chirping is a constant layer of the soundscape — a high, dry, rhythmic sound, like the ticking of a thousand small clocks. Some are farmed for food — roasted, ground into flour, eaten whole, pressed into cakes. Some are kept as pets — singing crickets in small carved cages, prized for their song. The best singers are worth a lot. There is a whole culture around cricket-singing — competitions, breeding, training, the composition of songs to which the crickets are taught to chirp in rhythm. The people say a house without crickets is a house without a soul. The crickets are the heartbeat of the domestic world, as the spring is the heartbeat of the Great Hall.

The fungi are the great crop. Wood is precious — it comes from the surface, brought down in exchange for fish and fungi and songs and travertine carvings. The racks in the Fungus Warrens are old, repaired, repaired again, the wood black with age and moisture and the stain of spores. On them grow the fungi — pale, fleshy, broad-capped, a bit like oyster mushrooms, a bit like ears, a bit like nothing on the surface. They are fed by bat guano and warm water and the constant humidity. The air in the Warrens is thick with spores — you breathe through a cloth, and the people who work there have a cough, a reputation, a pride. The fungi are a major food source and a major export. They are dried, powdered, fermented, made into a kind of bread, a kind of cheese, a kind of beer. The beer is famous on the surface — dark, earthy, slightly hallucinogenic, slightly nourishing, completely addictive. The surface traders will trade almost anything for it.

The plants are sparse, as plants are sparse in the dark. But they are present. At the Root Gate — a vertical shaft open to the surface — tree roots hang down like a curtain, thick ropes of root, some as thick as a person's arm, reaching for the water. They do not reach it — they stop short, or they rot, or they are harvested for fiber and medicine. The shaft is the brightest place in the Heart, a column of daylight, pale gold, that moves across the floor as the sun moves. The floor below is a garden — mosses, ferns, small flowering plants, pale or white or faintly blue, insects, spiders, the occasional bird that falls down and is caught and eaten. The air here is cooler, fresher, and it smells of green growing things, a smell so rare that people weep when they smell it. The root curtain is a boundary. You can go up it, if you are brave and strong, to the surface. Most people have never been to the surface. Some have. The ones who have are changed — they speak differently, they look at the light differently, they are both revered and pitied. The surface is a story to most people in the Heart, a myth, a threat, a promise. The Root Gate is where the story is told.

The people are small, stocky, pale. Large eyes, dark-adapted, that reflect the lamplight like a cat's. Good hearing — they can navigate by echolocation, clicking their tongues against the roof of their mouths, and they can hear the mood of the water, the health of the fish, the approach of a bat in the dark. They go barefoot. They wear wool and leather, simple, undyed or dyed with fungi — rust-red, pale green, a deep blue-black. They smell of the place — wet stone, fish, lamp oil, woodsmoke, the earthy sweetness of the fungus farms. They are healthy, long-lived, strong. The warm water and the mineral-rich diet see to that. They have a rich oral culture — songs, stories, histories, genealogies, all memorized, because writing is done on perishable materials or not at all. They are famous for their singing — the place demands it. They have songs for every chamber, every task, every hour of the pulse. They have songs for the fish, for the bats, for the crickets, for the spring, for the dead. The surface traders say you can hear the Heart singing from the top of the stair.

The sound of the place is loud. The baseline is the water — the roar of the Well-Mouth, the rush of the channels, the drip of the ceiling, the slap of the pools, the gurgle of the drains. Over that, the bats — clicks, chirps, the soft rain of wings. Over that, the crickets — a constant high chirring. Over that, the people — voices, footsteps on stone, tools on stone, songs, arguments, laughter, the cries of children, the moans of the sick in the Bath, the chanting of the mourners in the passages. The net effect is a roar, a constant enveloping roar, so loud that you have to shout to be heard in the Great Hall, and so constant that after a while you stop hearing it and it becomes a kind of silence, a presence rather than a sound. And the place has acoustic properties — the vaults carry sound, the chambers shape it, the pillars block it. You can hear a conversation from across the Great Hall, sometimes. You can hear the bats in the highest vaults from the floor. You can hear the pulse of the spring in every chamber, as a vibration in the stone, a subsonic throb that you feel in your teeth. And the Quiet Room is the exception — the one place where the sound stops.

The Quiet Room is a small chamber off a passage near the Market. The door is a low arch. Inside, the acoustics are dead. Sound does not carry. If you speak, your voice stays in your mouth — you hear it only as a vibration in your skull, a strange intimate buzzing. The water sound is gone. The bat sound is gone. The cricket sound is gone. The air is still. The light is dim — a single lamp, a single flame, a single point of warmth in a small stone cell. The walls are carved with a tight, dense pattern — maybe the pattern is what kills the sound, maybe it is the shape of the room, maybe it is something else. People go there for secrets, for mourning, for prayer, for the naming of the dead. You do not stay long — it is unsettling. The silence is not peaceful. It is heavy. It presses on you. Some people hear things there — their own heartbeat, amplified, or a distant roar, like the water from very far away, or a voice. No one agrees on what they hear. The custom is to leave an offering of water — a small clay cup of it, poured into a niche in the wall — and to leave without speaking. The water is gone when you come back. No one knows where it goes.

The Market is a chamber off the Great Hall, raised floor, dry-ish. Stalls of stone and wood. Fish, fungi, crickets (live and roasted), lamp oil, wool, leather, stone tools, travertine carvings, songs, news. The market is loud even by the standards of the Heart — people shouting over the water, the crickets in their cages singing, the bats overhead, the clatter of dishes and tools. The market happens every day, but the big market is when the surface traders come down — every few days, or every pulse, or whenever the surface people can make the journey. The surface traders are tall, pale in a different way (from the sun, not the dark), loud, strange. They bring wood, metal, salt, grain, news. They trade for fish, fungi, lamp oil, carvings, songs, beer. They are both necessary and resented — they are loud, they do not understand the water, they smell wrong, they take too much and give too little, but they bring what the Heart needs. The market is where the two worlds meet, and the tension between them is the tension of the place — the need for the surface and the knowledge that the surface is not home.

The Dwellings are carved recesses in the walls of the Great Hall and the passages — like a dovecote, hundreds of niches, each just big enough for a person to sleep in, with a curtain of wool or leather. The niches are warm, dry-ish (above the water line), private. People sleep in them, store their things in them, die in them. The niches are inherited, traded, fought over. The best ones are near the warmth of the Well-Mouth, or near the Root Gate, or near the Market. The worst ones are near the drains, or in the cold passages, or in the Fungus Warrens. The walls of the dwellings are covered with personal things — carved fish, cricket cages, lamps, offerings, the marks of generations. A person's niche is their biography. You can read a life in the objects hung on the walls, the worn smooth stone of the sleeping ledge, the soot-stained ceiling above the lamp. The dead are buried in the walls — a niche is a tomb as well as a bed, and the bones of the ancestors are the foundation of the place.

And then there is the Old Chamber. It is off a side passage, down a short stair, behind a low arch. It is smaller than the Great Hall, perhaps ten paces across, domed. The carving is different — older, smoother, more flowing. Not the wave-and-handprint style of the rest. The patterns are more like water, like flow, like something organic, something grown rather than cut. There is a carving of a creature that does not match any known animal — a fish with too many fins, a bat with too many wings, something else, something that might be a map or a diagram or a god. The floor is dry — a rarity. There is a basin in the center, empty. No one knows what it was for. No one knows who carved it. The style does not match any other chamber in the Heart. It might be the first chamber, carved by a different people, before the Heart was the Heart. Or it might be the last, carved by something that is still here. People leave offerings there — small carved fish, drops of lamp oil, a handful of cricket flour, a song whispered into the stone. The chamber is used for rites of passage, for mourning, for asking forgiveness, for the naming of children. It is the most sacred place in the Heart. And no one knows why.

Below the Well-Mouth, in the deepest pool, where the water is hottest and the light does not reach, there are jellyfish. Pale, translucent, small as a cupped hand, pulsing slowly. No one knows how they got there. They might have always been there, from before the place was built, from before the people came, from the deep water that was here before anything. They pulse with the same rhythm as the spring. They are considered sacred — or at least, you do not disturb them. People leave offerings at the edge of the pool. The jellyfish are a mystery. The old women who tend the old women say the jellyfish are the dreams of the spring. The priests — if there are priests, if the Heart has priests — say the jellyfish are the souls of the first builders. The people who work in the deep channels say the jellyfish are just jellyfish, and the mystery is that anyone makes a mystery of them. Everyone is right. Everyone is wrong. The jellyfish pulse. The spring pulses. The Heart pulses.

The pulse is the clock and the calendar and the god of the place. It is roughly a day, but it drifts — sometimes faster, sometimes slower, sometimes pausing, sometimes surging. The people have a count for it — a system of marks on the pillars, a song with a verse for each phase, a dance with a step for each swell. The pulse is never the same twice, and yet it is always itself. The people say the pulse is the breath of the mountain, or the heartbeat of the world, or the rhythm of the water remembering the sea. They say the pulse changed once, long ago, and the change was a warning, and the warning was heeded, and the Heart was saved. They say the pulse will change again, and the change will be a judgment, and the Heart will be judged, and the people will be found worthy or wanting. They say the pulse is the only truth, and everything else is story. They say the pulse is the story, and everything else is truth. The pulse swells. The water rises. The fish stir. The bats click. The crickets chirr. The people sing. The pulse falls. The water falls. The Heart breathes.

Once a year, the fish Gathering. The fish swim up the channels to the Well-Mouth and crowd there, thousands of them, a living mass of pale flesh, churning the water, blocking the spring. The old women are at the center, motionless, their ancient bodies supported by the press of the crowd. The water rises. The pulse quickens. The people sing the Gathering songs, the oldest songs, the songs that are older than the Heart. They feast. They tell stories. They take it as a sign of the health of the Heart — if the fish gather, the Heart is well; if the fish do not gather, the Heart is sick, and the people must find the sickness and heal it. No one knows why the fish gather. The fish do not say. The fish churn. The water rises. The people sing. The old women wait. And then, after a day, or two, or three, the fish disperse, and the water falls, and the pulse slows, and the Heart returns to itself, and the people return to their work, and the year turns.

The place is in constant, slow motion. The water deposits minerals, and the travertine grows, and the people harvest it, and it grows again. The fish are born and grow and are eaten and are born again. The bats are born and fly and die and fall and are eaten by the fish and the crickets and the fungi. The fungi grow and are harvested and grow again. The crickets hatch and sing and die and are eaten and hatch again. The people are born and grow and work and sing and die and are buried in the walls and become the foundation. The stone is worn by water and feet and tools, and carved again, and worn again. The channels silt up and are cleared. The pools grow stagnant and are flushed. The lamps burn and are refilled. The songs are sung and forgotten and remembered and changed and sung again. Nothing is still. Nothing is finished. The Heart is alive, and it is in motion, and it has been in motion for longer than anyone knows, and it will be in motion after the last person is buried in the walls and the last song is forgotten and the last fish is eaten and the last cricket falls silent and the last bat flies out through the Root Gate and does not return. The pulse will continue. The water will rise and fall. The stone will weep. The dark will hold. The Heart will beat.
