**The Weep**

It sits high in the mountains, above the last real treeline, in a zone where the clouds come to rest. The air is thin and wet at once — you can feel the altitude in your breath and the moisture in your skin. The hollow is not large. From rim to rim it measures perhaps a hundred meters, and from the floor the walls rise steeply enough that you cannot see the surrounding landscape. You see only the bowl, and the vegetation that fills it, and the small circle of grey-white sky above, and nothing else. The sky is always overcast. The sun has not been directly visible here in living memory, and perhaps not for much longer.

The hollow was made by collapse. Long ago — long enough that no trace of the event remains in the shape of the land, only in the fact of the hollow itself — a chamber in the limestone gave way. The result is a bowl with walls of broken stone and compacted earth, steep but not vertical, furred with roots and vegetation. The limestone is soluble, and the water has been working on it for as long as there has been water, so the walls are carved with channels and pockets and thin, vertical grooves, and every surface is streaked with mineral deposits — white, grey, orange, black — and every groove holds a seep, and every pocket holds a pool, and every surface is wet. The floor is not flat. It is a mosaic of hummocks and hollows, of saturated peat and standing water, of moss so deep it has the consistency of wet bread. The peat is old — thousands of years — and it is deep in places, three meters or more, and it holds the history of the hollow in its layers: the pollen of plants that no longer grow here, the bones of animals that no longer live here, the charcoal of fires that burned before there were people to light them. There is no dry ground. There is no bare ground. Every surface is either water or life or both.

Water is the governing presence. It seeps from the walls in thin, continuous sheets, emerging from the stone at every level, so that the rock is always wet and always streaked and always furred with moss. It drips from the canopy in a steady, irregular percussion — not rain, but the accumulated moisture of the leaves, released one drop at a time. It pools in every depression, no matter how small, so that the floor is a grid of pools, some no larger than a cup, some large enough to wade into, all connected by a web of saturated soil and thin channels. The water is cool, fresh, slightly acidic, mineral-rich from the limestone. It tastes of stone and time. The air is so full of moisture that it is visible — a faint mist hangs at shoulder height, thickening and thinning with the imperceptible movements of the air. Your clothing becomes damp within minutes. Your hair. Your skin. Everything you carry. The water is everywhere, and it is always moving, and it is always the same.

The vegetation is excessive. Every inch of soil, every crack in the rock, every fallen branch, every standing trunk is occupied. Mosses in dozens of species — deep green, pale green, yellow-green, almost black — cover every surface in thick, wet mats that squelch underfoot and spring back unchanged. Ferns unfurl in every crevice, some small as fingernails, some large as a person. Liverworts form dark, leathery carpets. Hornworts rise in thin, green-black spikes. Epiphytes — orchids, bromeliads, ferns, mosses — grow on every trunk and branch, and other plants grow on the epiphytes, and mosses grow on those plants, so that the trees are not trees anymore but pillars of accumulated life, columns of green and brown and grey that rise into the canopy and disappear into the dimness. The canopy itself is so dense that it is less a roof than a solid thing, a ceiling of interlocked branches and leaves and vines and roots, through which no direct light has passed in centuries. The light that reaches the floor is the color of old honey held up to a candle — dim, amber-green, directionless. It is never brighter than this. It is never darker. It is always exactly this.

The trees are ancient. Not tall — the darkness and the moisture have kept them stunted, twisted, slow — but old. Their trunks are thick with age, gnarled, fluted, buttressed. They have been growing here for thousands of years, and they show it in their shape, in the way their roots grip the wet stone, in the way their branches have grown and died and been absorbed and grown again. Some of them are not individual trees anymore but colonies — a single organism that has spread across dozens of meters, putting up new trunks as old ones die, so that what looks like a grove is actually one thing. The largest of these colonies occupies the eastern wall of the hollow, its roots emerging from the stone in a dozen places, its trunks rising in a cluster that might be a forest but is not. It has been here since before the hollow was a hollow. It was here when the mountain fell in. It will be here when the mountain falls again.

The fauna is overwhelming. The hollow is alive with animals in every niche, at every scale. Insects in impossible numbers — beetles, flies, moths, ants, springtails, mites, lice, aphids, scale insects, thrips, earwigs, crickets, katydids, leafhoppers, planthoppers, true bugs of a dozen families — carpet every surface, fill every pool, cloud the air in the still places. Spiders everywhere, in every web shape, in every size from pinhead to hand-span, some sitting motionless for hours, some moving with a speed that makes them invisible. Worms and slugs and snails in the soil and on the vegetation, some bright as jewels, some dull as the mud they crawl through. Amphibians — frogs, toads, salamanders, caecilians — in the pools and on the wet rocks and in the moss, some small as a fingernail, some large as a forearm, some bright green, some black, some patterned with colors that have no name. Small mammals — rodents, shrews, marsupials or their equivalent — in the root tangles and the peat, some arboreal, some fossorial, some that live their entire lives in the canopy and never touch the ground. Birds in the canopy and at the edges of the pools — small, mostly brown and grey, with calls that are soft and brief and almost never repeated. Reptiles — lizards, snakes, amphisbaenians — on the trunks and in the leaf litter, some bright as the frogs, some camouflaged to the point of invisibility. And fungi, which are not plants but which are everywhere, in every form — mushrooms, brackets, cups, corals, jellies, crusts, molds, mildews — decomposing everything that dies before it can fall, so that the hollow is as much a kingdom of fungi as of plants or animals.

And yet it is quiet. Not silent — there are sounds — but quiet. The density of the vegetation absorbs sound. The water muffles it. The soft ground and the thick moss and the layers of leaves and the saturated air all work together to dampen every noise. The insects do not buzz; they move in silence, or in sounds so small they are more like texture than noise — a faint ticking, a barely audible rustling, a whisper of wings that is more felt than heard. The spiders do not make sounds. The amphibians do not chorus; they call once, softly, and then are silent, as if they have learned that loudness is unnecessary or dangerous. The mammals move through the undergrowth without breaking twigs; their feet are soft, their bodies are light, their passage is a small displacement of air and nothing more. The birds call, but quietly, and rarely, and always the same call — a single note, a brief phrase, as if they are not communicating but simply acknowledging. The only constant sound is the water: the drip, drip, drip from the canopy, the seep from the walls, the small movements of the pools. It is a steady, irregular, quiet percussion, like a clock that has forgotten what time it keeps, or like the breathing of something very large and very slow.

The temperature is cool and constant. Not cold — the hollow is protected from wind, and the water and the density of life hold the temperature steady — but cool, the kind of cool that seeps into your bones if you stay too long, the kind of cool that makes you want to move, to generate heat, to do something. But there is nothing to do here. There is no path. There is no destination. There is only the hollow, and the life in it, and the quiet, and the dim amber-green light.

The place is old. The water has carved its channels and now they are fixed — the seeps emerge from the same points, the drips fall from the same leaves, the pools occupy the same depressions. The peat is deep and stable. The soil chemistry is constant. The nutrient cycle is closed and balanced. Nothing is eroding that was not already eroded. Nothing is growing that was not already growing. The ecosystem is in equilibrium — a climax community that has reached its final form and now simply persists. The populations of every species are stable. The births balance the deaths. The growth balances the decay. There are no seasons here, or if there are, they do not affect the hollow. There is no succession. There is no disturbance. There is only this: the same life, in the same balance, indefinitely.

This is the uncanny thing. This is what makes the hollow more than just a beautiful, wet, dark, quiet place. It is the stability. It is the sense that nothing here has changed in a very long time and that nothing here will change. The water flows, but the flow does not alter the channels. The plants grow, but the growth does not spread or diminish. The animals move, but the movements do not shift the balance. The light is always the same. The temperature is always the same. The sounds are always the same. It is not that the place is dead — it is very much alive — but that it is alive in a way that has reached a kind of perfection, a kind of stasis, that feels almost deliberate. As if the hollow has become exactly what it is and has decided to be that forever.

There are signs of use, but no structures. A stone that has been turned over and turned back, worn smooth on one side by the pressure of a beak or a paw. A branch that has been stripped of its bark in a way that suggests teeth or claw, the exposed wood darkened with age. A small pile of cracked shells beside a pool, arranged with what might be intention or might be accident, the fragments worn and ancient. A fiber cord, or something like a cord, twisted from plant material, hanging from a branch and frayed and retied many times. A sharpened stick, too regular to be natural, pressed into the mud at the edge of a pool, its point worn to a polished nub. These are not buildings. They are not tools in the sense of technology. They are the marks of simple use, of simple fabrication, of minds that are not human but that are not entirely without purpose.

A small, dark bird — no larger than a fist — works at the edge of the largest pool, cracking the seeds of a particular sedge with a stone it has worn into a shape that fits its beak. The stone is ancient, passed down through generations, and the ground around the bird's working spot is littered with shell fragments and worn stones, a midden that has been accumulating for centuries. The bird works with a methodical precision that is almost mechanical, and it does not look up when you approach, and it does not stop, and it does not change its rhythm, and you realize that it has been doing this for as long as you have been watching and will continue to do this for as long as you stay, and that its rhythm is the same as the rhythm of the drips from the canopy, and that the hollow is not just in equilibrium — it is in concert.

But even these signs are old. The cord is frayed and has been retied many times. The stick is worn smooth. The pile of shells has been added to and taken from so many times that it has become a feature of the landscape, as stable as the pools. The tool use here is not new. It is ancient. It has been happening for as long as the hollow has existed, and it has not changed. The tools are always the same tools. The uses are always the same uses. The minds that make them are always the same minds, generation after generation, doing what their parents did, as the hollow does what it has always done.

To be in the hollow is to be in a place that is complete. It does not need anything. It does not want anything. It is not waiting for anything. It is simply here, in this small, dark, wet, cool, quiet, balanced, perfect way, and it will continue to be here, in this same way, for as long as there is a here. The water will seep. The moss will grow. The insects will move. The light will be dim. The temperature will be cool. The sounds will be quiet. And nothing will change.

This is the hollow. This is the weep. This is the place where water and life and time have reached an agreement, and the agreement is: nothing more. Nothing less. Only this. Only now. Only forever.
