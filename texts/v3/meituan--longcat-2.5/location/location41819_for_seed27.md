# The Thall

From the escarpment's broken lip, the plateau extends to a horizon that seems too near—not because the land ends, but because the haze thickens with distance, swallowing the far reaches in a grey-green dimness that never quite resolves into sky. You have climbed for days to reach this elevation, and your lungs remember every switchback, every ledge, every breath of thinning air. Below you, the lowlands are a memory of warmth and moisture. Above you, the escarpment's crest cuts a ragged line against a sky the color of old pewter. And before you, the plateau.

The Thall.

It covers everything. Not like grass covers soil, or moss covers stone—those metaphors are too gentle, too partial. The Thall *is* the ground. It is a continuous, undulating crust of intergrown filaments and crystalline platelets, ranging in color from pale sage to deep olive to a bruised purple-black in the hollows where something—*not water*, never water—accumulates in thin, iridescent films. It is several centimeters thick on the rises, meters deep in the depressions, accumulated over time spans that make the escarpment's geological strata look like yesterday's sediment. The surface is not smooth. It is textured with ridges, whorls, and patterns that resemble the grain of wood, or the convolutions of a brain, or the flow of a liquid that froze mid-motion—but at a scale of kilometers. Standing at the edge, you can trace the pattern of the Thall's growth across the entire visible plateau, following the dendritic channels where nutrients once flowed, the concentric rings around ancient growth centers, the long, sinuous ridges where competing colonies met and merged.

There is no water here. *None.* The air is so dry that exposed skin cracks within hours, that wood shrivels and splits, that the very idea of rain sounds like a myth told by soft, lowland creatures. The Thall does not need it. Its metabolism—if that word even applies—runs on something else entirely. The filaments draw dissolved minerals from the ancient rock below, precipitating new minerals at the surface, building the crust layer by layer, ion by ion. It is a slow, solid-state metabolism, more geological than biological, and it operates on timescales that make a mayfly's lifespan seem like a quantum flicker. But it is alive. It grows. It repairs itself. It responds to damage—not quickly, not dramatically, but *persistently*, over days and weeks, with the patient inevitability of crystallization. And though this is debated, though it is spoken of only in certain company, it may be aware.

---

The city of the Sinter sits in a broad, shallow depression where the Thall is thickest and the growth is oldest. It is not built. It is *grown*—or it grew itself, or it was grown by something that no longer remembers what it was doing, or it is still growing and the distinction between construction and biology has long since ceased to mean anything. The structures rise from the Thall like extruded forms, curving and branching in ways that no architect would design but that are perfectly adapted to the plateau's conditions. Walls thicken where the wind is strongest. Passages narrow where the mat needs to contract. Chambers open where the growth wanted to open. The buildings are the same material as the Thall—intergrown filaments and crystalline platelets—but denser, harder, more structured, with a faint translucency that catches the dim light and holds it, glowing from within like something half-alive.

Which, of course, it is.

The Sinter extends below the surface as much as above it. The Thall is deep here—tens of meters in places—and the city has grown downward as well as upward, creating tunnels, chambers, and conduits within the living crust. The deepest levels are the oldest, and the oldest are the strangest. The architecture changes as you descend: the upper levels are open, airy, full of chambers and passages that feel almost organic in their irregularity; the middle levels are denser, more structured, with repeating patterns that suggest planning or purpose; the deepest levels are *alien*—not in the sense of being foreign, but in the sense of being from a different world, a different mind, a different physics. The walls there are too smooth, too regular, too *intentional*. The angles are wrong. The proportions are unsettling. And the Thall itself behaves differently in those depths—growing in directions it shouldn't, forming structures that serve no discernible function, humming at frequencies that you feel in your teeth rather than hear.

The inhabitants—the Growers, they call themselves, though whether they grow the Thall or the Thall grows them is a question they find amusing—move through the Sinter with the ease of creatures who have never known any other home. They are small, adapted to the dim light and the cool, thin air. Their eyes are large, their skin is dry and slightly iridescent, and they communicate as much through vibration as through sound—tapping the walls, humming in the passages, sending signals through the Thall itself that travel faster than air and arrive with a clarity that speech cannot match. They are not numerous, not by lowland standards, but they are present throughout the Sinter, and their activity is constant. They tend the growth. They shape it. They prune it. They introduce mineral solutions at specific points, adjusting the local chemistry with the precision of a gardener grafting a branch. And they listen—always they listen—to the Thall, to its creaking and groaning and humming, reading in those sounds the state of the growth, the health of the city, the mood of something that may or may not have moods.

---

The sounds.

You do not notice them at first, because they are too constant, too all-encompassing. They become the background, the baseline, the silence against which other sounds are measured. But once you attend to them, you realize that the plateau is *loud*. Relentlessly, oppressively, magnificently loud.

The Thall itself produces sound—a constant, low-frequency creaking and groaning as it grows and shifts, the sound of mineral platelets sliding past each other, of filaments expanding and contracting with the slow temperature cycles of the plateau's dim day. It is the sound of a building settling, magnified a thousandfold, stretched across a landscape, slowed to a geological pace. You feel it through your feet before you hear it with your ears: a subsonic rumble, a vibration in the bones of the world, rising and falling like breathing.

Over this, the wind. At this elevation, the wind never stops. It howls across the flat expanse of the plateau, a constant, unrelenting blast that carries dust—fine, mineral dust, the residue of billions of years of erosion—and hurls it against the Thall, against the Sinter, against anything that stands upright. The dust scours. It polishes. It accumulates in drifts against every obstacle, and the Thall absorbs it, incorporates it, builds with it. The wind is the plateau's metabolism made audible: the transfer of material from one place to another, the slow, incessant redistribution of the world.

And the fauna.

The Thall teems with life—more life than you would expect in a place with no water, no rain, no rivers, no lakes. The creatures here are small, mostly, and hard-shelled, and they move through the Thall's surface layers with the ease of fish through water. Some graze on the Thall's fresh growth, their mouthparts adapted to scrape and filter the mineral-rich filaments. Some burrow into the crust, creating tunnels and chambers that the Thall slowly fills in behind them, a perpetual cycle of excavation and regrowth. Some prey on the grazers, and some prey on the predators, and some prey on each other in a churning, tangled food web that the Growers have been mapping for generations and have only begun to understand.

They are loud. They click and chirp and rasp and buzz, and the larger ones—the burrowers, the predators—produce sounds that are deeper, more resonant, more *felt*: bellows, groans, territorial rumbles that carry for kilometers across the flat plateau. The air is never silent. Even in the deepest chambers of the Sinter, you can hear them—the distant, muffled percussion of the surface, the constant, churning industry of the Thall's fauna, filtered through meters of living crust and emerging as a kind of music, a rhythm, a pulse.

And there are flyers. At this elevation, with the constant wind, flight is easy—or at least, it is possible. The things that soar above the Thall are not birds, not insects, not anything with a name in any lowland taxonomy. They are flat, wide things, with membranes that catch the thin air and hold it, riding the wind for hours without a single wingbeat. They are silent, most of them, but some produce sounds—thin, high cries that carry for kilometers, or low, thrumming calls that you feel in your chest before you hear them. They wheel and spiral above the plateau, and the Growers watch them with an attention that borders on reverence, because the flyers are the Thall's messengers, its scouts, its eyes on the wider world—or so the Growers believe. The relationship between the Thall and its fauna is not simply ecological. It is something more. The Growers have theories. They always have theories.

---

The light is dim. The star—a small, old, red-tinged sun—provides less illumination than you would expect from its apparent size, and the haze thickens it further. There are no sharp shadows. The light falls diffusely, evenly, giving everything a flat, twilight quality that makes depth perception unreliable and distances hard to judge. It is always the hour before dusk, or the hour after dawn, or some hour that has no name because it has no counterpart in any lowland sky. The colors of the Thall are muted by this light—the greens are greyed, the purples are deepened, the whites are dulled to a soft, luminous grey. The Sinter glows faintly in this light, its translucent walls catching and holding what little illumination there is, releasing it slowly, like a ember that never quite cools.

The temperature is cool. Not cold—never cold, not at the Sinter's elevation, not with the Thall's insulating bulk beneath you—but a persistent chill that seeps into everything, that makes the air feel thin and sharp, that reminds you with every breath that you are far above the world that made you. The Thall provides some warmth, generated by its slow mineral metabolism, and the deepest chambers of the Sinter are almost comfortable, but the open plateau is a different matter. The wind strips heat from exposed skin. The thin atmosphere holds little warmth. The dim star provides little. You are always slightly cold, always slightly breathless, always slightly aware that this place is not made for you.

And there is something else. Something the Growers don't talk about, or talk about only in the deepest chambers, or talk about only in the humming, vibrational language that passes through the Thall itself and does not travel through air.

The physics here is *wrong*.

Not dramatically. Not obviously. Not in ways that you would notice unless you were looking, unless you were measuring, unless you had been taught to attend to the subtle wrongness of the place. Light bends slightly more than it should. Time passes slightly unevenly—not enough to notice in a day, but enough to measure over a season, enough to make the Growers' calendars a matter of constant adjustment. Certain measurements give results that don't match expectations: the local gravitational constant is not quite what it should be, the speed of light in the Thall's crystalline matrix is not quite what it should be, the decay rates of certain isotopes are not quite what they should be. The Growers have known this for generations. They have built their technology around it, exploiting these anomalies, creating devices and structures that shouldn't work but do.

The Thall's growth involves processes that current physics cannot fully describe. Quantum coherence in structures that should be too warm, too noisy, too *large* for such effects. Entanglement between filaments separated by meters, by kilometers, by the entire width of the plateau. Information transfer through the crystalline matrix at speeds that violate—or seem to violate—the ordinary limits of causality. The Growers use these effects. They have built computational substrates within the Thall itself, networks of entangled filaments that process information in ways that no lowland computer can match. They have built sensors that detect gravitational fluctuations, that map the plateau's subtle topology, that read the Thall's growth patterns with a precision that borders on prophecy. They have built weapons, too, though they do not speak of those—devices that exploit the local physics to produce effects that have no name in any lowland language: the ability to make a region of the Thall *forget* its structure, to cause it to revert to an earlier state, to ungrow itself, to dissolve back into undifferentiated mineral potential.

The Growers are not the Thall's masters. They are its students, its tenders, its interpreters. They do not command it. They *ask* it, and sometimes it answers, and sometimes it does not, and the difference between those outcomes is not always clear.

---

The plateau is old. So old that the original topography—mountains, valleys, rivers, if there ever were rivers—has been almost entirely erased. What remains is a vast, gently undulating plain, rising and falling in swells that are kilometers wide and only meters high, like the surface of a sea that froze mid-swell. The escarpment that bounds it is the last remnant of the original elevation, a cliff that drops away to the lowlands with a violence that suggests the plateau was once much higher, much more dramatic, much more *mountainous*. Now it is nearly flat. The Thall has contributed to this flattening, filling in the hollows, wearing down the rises, slowly leveling the surface with the patience of something that has all the time in the world. The erosion is ancient, and it is nearing its end. The plateau is approaching a final, stable state—a perfectly flat, perfectly Thall-covered surface, in equilibrium with itself, requiring no further change, no further growth, no further erosion.

How long this will take—millions of years? billions?—no one knows. The Growers have tried to calculate it, using their quantum substrates and their gravitational sensors and their deep knowledge of the Thall's growth rates, and they have arrived at numbers that are too large to be meaningful. But the direction is clear. The plateau is settling. The Thall is settling. The Sinter is settling. Everything is slowly, inexorably, approaching its final form.

And yet.

The Thall grows. New filaments push through the old crust, mineral platelets precipitate in patterns that have never been seen before, the surface texture changes in ways that the Growers record and study and marvel at. The fauna evolve—slowly, yes, on timescales of generations, but measurably, observably, in the span of a single Grower's lifetime. New species appear. Old species vanish. The food web reconfigures itself in response to changes in the Thall's chemistry, in the wind patterns, in the availability of the iridescent films that accumulate in the hollows. The weather cycles—not rain, never rain, but dust storms that last for weeks, temperature inversions that trap the cold air in the depressions and make the Thall's surface crackle with frost, electrical discharges that dance across the plateau in silent, branching patterns, and something that might be precipitation but is not water: a fine, mineral dust that settles from the haze in slow, grey curtains, coating the Thall, feeding it, building it, one particle at a time.

The Sinter changes. New chambers grow—or are grown—at the periphery, extending the city into regions of the Thall that have never been shaped before. Old chambers are absorbed back into the crust, their structures dissolved, their materials recycled into new growth. The Growers maintain a constant, deliberate program of renewal, of reshaping, of re-growing, and the city's architecture is never the same from one generation to the next. The deepest levels, the oldest levels, the *alien* levels—those are left alone, preserved, studied, feared. No one grows there. No one shapes there. The Thall does what it wants in the deep places, and the Growers have learned not to interfere.

---

Standing at the edge of the Sinter, looking out across the Thall toward the hazed horizon, you feel the weight of the place. Not its physical weight—the plateau is not heavy, not oppressive—but its *temporal* weight, the accumulated pressure of billions of years of slow, patient, persistent growth. The Thall has been here longer than any lowland mountain, any lowland river, any lowland forest. It has seen the star age and dim. It has seen the atmosphere thicken and thin and thicken again. It has seen the escarpment erode from a mountain range to a cliff to the broken, ragged lip that you stand on now. And it has grown, and grown, and grown, covering everything, incorporating everything, building itself from the bones of the world.

It is beautiful. It is alien. It is loud and dry and cold and dim and strange and alive and ancient and active and approaching its end and still, still, still growing.

The Growers say that the Thall dreams. They say that its growth is not random, not merely chemical, not simply the mechanical precipitation of minerals from solution. They say that the Thall is building something—not the Sinter, not the city, not anything so small and local—but something larger, something that extends across the entire plateau, something that has been growing for billions of years and will continue to grow for billions more, and that the Sinter is merely a byproduct, a side effect, a place where the Thall's dream happened to become dense enough for small, short-lived creatures to inhabit.

They say that the Thall is building a mind.

Or that it is a mind already, and the plateau is its body, and the Sinter is a thought it is thinking, and the Growers are neurons firing in a cortex too large to comprehend, too slow to perceive, too ancient to imagine.

They say many things. They hum and tap and vibrate through the walls of the Sinter, and the Thall hums back, and the wind howls, and the fauna click and chirp and bellow, and the star burns dim and old and patient in the pewter sky, and the dust settles, and the Thall grows, and the plateau settles toward its final, flat, perfect form, and the Growers tend their city and listen to the walls and wait for something they cannot name.

You stand at the edge. You breathe the thin, dry, cold air. You feel the subsonic rumble of the Thall's growth through your feet. You hear the wind. You hear the fauna. You hear the Growers' humming, vibrational language, passing through the walls and the crust and the bones of the world. You see the dim, diffuse light on the grey-green surface of the Thall, extending to the hazed horizon, unbroken, total, alive.

And you understand, with a clarity that arrives not as thought but as feeling, as vibration, as something that passes through the soles of your feet and up through your spine and into the base of your skull, that this place is not for you. It was never for you. It was here before you, and it will be here after you, and your presence on its surface is a brief, small, temporary thing—a mote of dust, a flicker of warmth, a single note in a chord that has been sounding for longer than your species has existed.

The Thall grows. The wind howls. The plateau settles. The star burns.

And you are here, now, in the dim light and the loud air and the cool, thin atmosphere, standing at the edge of something that is not a landscape but a life, not a place but a process, not a world but a mind dreaming itself into being, one mineral platelet at a time, in a silence that is not silence but the sound of everything, growing.
