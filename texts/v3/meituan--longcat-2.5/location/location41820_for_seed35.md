You come in through a crack in the salt. A fissure, sharp-edged, freshly opened — the crystals catch the light like broken teeth. The crack widens. The air changes — warm, dry, still, pressing against your skin like a membrane, like the inside of a body. The sound hits you — a constant drone, felt in the chest, in the teeth, in the thoughts, slowing them down, thickening them. You step through. The light from outside is gone. The air from outside is gone. You are inside.

The chamber is vast but bounded. The ceiling is lost in shadow and warm haze. The walls are far away, visible only where the industrial lamps catch them — yellow-orange pools of light, spaced like stations of a cross, like the eyes of animals in the dark. The floor is a thick carpet of salt dust and machine parts and chitin, crunching under your feet — white and rust and black, layered, compacted, ancient. The space is dense — machinery and life and rock and salt, all mixed, all near-touching, all part of the same thing.

The rock is salt. White and pink and orange, crystalline, sharp. Fresh — recently fractured, recently pushed up from below. The faces are clean, the edges are sharp, the crystals are large — some as big as your hand, some as big as your head, some bigger, some small enough to fit in your palm. The salt is still pushing up. The place is still growing. But it is growing so slowly that it appears static. It has been growing for so long that the growth has become a state. The salt crunches under your feet. The salt catches the light. The salt smells like dust and minerals and time.

The machinery is everywhere — a scattering, not a factory. An engine here, half-buried in salt, still turning a shaft that goes into the rock. The shaft is encrusted with salt and minerals and the bodies of insects — a thick crust, a second skin, a living insulation. The engine is old — industrial, from some previous era — but it is still running. It has been running forever. It will run forever. A pump there, still pumping a thick fluid through pipes that have dissolved into the walls — veins, roots, the circulatory system of the place. The fluid is not water — it is something else, something dense, something that glows faintly in the dark, something that smells like metal and electricity. A drill, still turning, still cutting into a face that has been cut so long that the cut is now a wall, and the wall is now a face, and the drill still turns. The drill bit is worn to a nub, but it still turns, still cuts, still grinds. The face is smooth, polished, reflective — a mirror of salt and metal, showing you your own distorted reflection, your own smallness, your own briefness. A generator, still sending electricity through wires that have grown into the walls like roots, like veins, like nerves, like tendons — the connections between the parts of the body. The wires are warm to the touch. The wires pulse with current. The wires are alive. The machinery is not maintained — it is part of the place. The salt has grown around it, the life has grown on it, the rock has grown through it. The machinery and the rock and the life are the same thing.

The light is dim. Industrial lamps — yellow-orange, never flickering, spaced along the walls like stations, like eyes. The lamps are old — glass and metal, industrial, from some previous era — but they still glow. They have been glowing forever. They will glow forever. Bioluminescence — fungi, bacteria, pale blue-green, everywhere. The fungi are flat, spreading, crust-like, covering the rocks like skin, like lichens, like moss. The bacteria are in the salt, in the air, in the life — invisible, constant, glowing faintly. The glow of hot rock — red-orange, deep, constant, coming through the walls where the geothermal heat rises. The rock is warm to the touch, warm all the way through, warm from the heat below. The heat is constant — it has always been this warm, it will always be this warm. The light is layered, overlapping, constant — never bright enough to see clearly, never dark enough to be blind. The lamps never burn out — or if they do, the fungi replace them, or the hot rock replaces them, or the light simply continues.

The sound is loud. A constant drone — engines, low, felt in the chest, in the bones, in the thoughts. A constant whine — fans, high, felt in the teeth, in the skull, in the dreams. A constant clatter — drills, rhythmic, felt in the bones, in the joints, in the heart. A constant throb — pumps, pulsing, felt in the blood, in the breath, in the pulse. The life adds to it — the scuttle of insects on metal, dry and chitinous and metallic. The chirp of things in the walls, high and thin and electronic. The rustle of things in the salt dust, soft and dry and whispering. The sound is layered, overlapping, constant. It has always been this loud. It will always be this loud. The sound is the air. The air is the sound. The sound is the place. The place is the sound.

The fauna is abundant. Insects — beetles, cockroaches, things with too many legs, things with metal in their shells, things that eat the oil and the grease and the metal, things that keep the gears turning, lubricate the bearings, produce the fuel. The insects are small — some as big as your thumb, some smaller, some microscopic. They crawl over everything — the machinery, the salt, the rock, you. They are part of the machinery — their movement keeps the gears turning, their bodies lubricate the bearings, their waste products are the fuel. The machinery and the insects are the same thing. Small animals — things like mice, things like rats, things that are not quite either, things with wires in their nests, things that run through the pipes, nest in the engines, are the moving parts. The animals are warm — warm-blooded, warm from the machinery, warm from the heat below. They are part of the machinery — they run through the pipes, they nest in the engines, they are the moving parts. The machinery and the animals are the same thing. Things that fly — bats, or things that are not quite bats, things with circuit boards in their wings, things that carry the signals, transmit the power, are the flying wires. The flying things are fast — darting, weaving, navigating by sound in the constant drone. They are part of the machinery — they carry the signals, they transmit the power, they are the flying wires. The machinery and the flying things are the same thing. Things in the walls — things that eat the rock, excrete salt, have sensors in their skin, monitor the rock, regulate the heat, are the living sensors. The wall-things are slow — moving, growing, eating, excreting, sensing, over days, over weeks, over months. They are part of the machinery — they monitor the rock, they regulate the heat, they are the living sensors. The machinery and the wall-things are the same thing.

The vegetation is sparse. Fungi — flat, spreading, crust-like, bioluminescent, with wires in their stems, insulating the wires, growing on the circuit boards, the living insulation. The fungi are everywhere — on the rocks, on the pipes, on the machinery, on the walls, on the floor. They glow faintly, constantly, pale blue-green. They are part of the machinery — they insulate the wires, they grow on the circuit boards, they are the living insulation. The machinery and the fungi are the same thing. Lichens — things that eat the salt, excrete minerals, have sensors in their leaves, monitor the salt, regulate the chemistry, the living sensors. The lichens are on the walls, on the salt, on the rock — patches, colonies, crusts. They are slow — growing, spreading, eating, excreting, sensing, over years, over decades, over centuries. They are part of the machinery — they monitor the salt, they regulate the chemistry, they are the living sensors. The machinery and the lichens are the same thing. The vegetation is scattered, sparse, but everywhere — a patch here, a colony there, a growth on this pipe, a mold on that wall.

The air is dry. No water. The salt absorbs everything. The heat desiccates everything. The chemistry is non-aqueous — things dissolve in other things, things react in other ways, things live and die without water. The fauna has adapted — things that get their moisture from the machinery, things that get it from the rock, things that get it from each other. The vegetation has adapted — things that grow in the warm dry dark, things that need no light, things that need no water. The air smells like hot metal, like salt, like oil, like the sharp smell of electricity. It tastes like metal, like salt, like the sharp taste of electricity. It feels like warm dry still heavy thick clear opaque colored sounding tasting air. It feels like the inside of a body. It feels like the breath of the place.

The place is strange. The boundary between the built and the natural has dissolved. The machinery has grown — pipes like roots, wires like vines, engines like bodies. The life has become mechanical — insects with metal shells, animals with wire nests, fungi on circuit boards. The machinery and the life are a single system — the machines keep the environment stable, the life keeps the machines running, the rock holds everything together. The place is a single organism — machinery its skeleton, life its flesh, rock its shell. The place is conscious, maybe — the sensors, the signals, the movement, together forming a nervous system, a brain, a mind. The place is aware. The place is watching. The place is thinking. The place is dreaming. The place is dreaming of you.

The place never changes. The machines run at constant speed. The temperature is constant. The light is constant. The sound is constant. Life lives and dies but the overall state is invariant. The salt does not erode. The rock does not settle. The machinery does not wear out — or if it does, the life repairs it, or the salt replaces it, or the place simply continues. The place has reached a steady state and stayed there. It has been this way for a very long time. It will be this way forever. The place is not decaying. The place is not dying. The place is not changing. The place is being. The place is eternal. The place is alive. The place is aware. The place is dreaming. The place is dreaming of you. The place is dreaming of you, and in the dream, you are part of the place. You are part of the machinery. You are part of the life. You are part of the rock. You are part of the salt. You are part of the dream.
