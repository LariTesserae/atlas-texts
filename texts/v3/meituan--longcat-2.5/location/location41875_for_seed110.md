# The Womb of Unbecoming

A fissure in the high mountain's belly, narrow as a forearm, opens into a single chamber no wider than a dwelling room. The stone here is *young* — not young by any human measure, but young the way a scab is young, raw and unweathered, its fracture faces still showing the clean geometry of the break that made this space. No rounding. No patina of age. The walls are planar and sharp-edged, and where the rock sheared, the exposed interior gleams with a wet, dark lustre, as though the mountain has only just noticed it has an inside.

The chamber breathes warmth. It rises from below — a deep, geothermal exhale that fills the space with air thick enough to taste. The temperature sits at the edge of discomfort, the heat of a fevered forehead, constant as a held note. Against this warmth, the stone walls weep. Every surface carries a film of moisture, condensation forming and reforming in an endless, silent exchange. Droplets swell at the tips of nascent mineral spikes — not stalactites yet, only the *idea* of stalactites, tiny calcified beads no larger than pinheads — and when they grow heavy enough to fall, they strike the floor with a sound so soft it barely qualifies as sound at all. A breath. A finger touching cloth.

The water gathers in shallow pools across the uneven floor, each one perfectly still, each one reflecting nothing in the absolute dark. The pools are warm to the touch — blood-warm — and their surfaces hold a faint iridescence, a oil-slick shimmer of dissolved minerals, sulfur compounds, things with no common name. The water is clear but thick-looking, viscous with what it carries. It does not smell of rot. It smells of nothing, or almost nothing — a faint metallic edge, like licking a coin.

---

The darkness is total. Not the darkness of a moonless night, which still carries ambient scatter, sky-glow, the memory of light — this is the darkness of a sealed thing, the darkness that fills the space behind a wall. It has weight. It presses against the eyes. Without a light source, a visitor would be aware of the chamber only through touch and the faintly mineral taste of the air, the warmth, the soft percussion of droplets.

And yet the chamber is *dense* — packed with presences. The walls are not bare. They are layered with accretion. Thin skins of deposit, built molecule by molecule from the mineral-laden water, drape the stone in folds and ripples that echo the shape of fabric — curtains, perhaps, or the suggestion of something hung and long dissolved. In places the deposits have formed low, undulating ridges, like the ripples on a beach seen in cross-section, frozen mid-pattern. Elsewhere they cluster in botryoidal masses, grape-like globes of calcite or aragonite no bigger than a thumbnail, packed so tightly they form a continuous, bumpy crust. Every square handspan of wall holds dozens of these forms, layered atop one another, generations of deposit pressed into a space barely thicker than a coin. The chamber is small, but its surfaces contain multitudes.

A pale film traces the waterline of each pool — bacterial, perhaps, or something older and stranger, a chemosynthetic mat that draws its energy from the warm, mineral-rich water rather than any sun. It is the colour of old ivory, of a nail moon, and it is the closest thing to vegetation this place holds. Here and there it thickens into small, leathery sheets that curl at their edges, and these are the only organic forms in the chamber: sparse, slow, and silent.

---

No wind enters. No current moves the air. The chamber's atmosphere is perfectly still, and this stillness is part of its strangeness — it feels *maintained*, as though the equilibrium is not accidental but somehow *held*. Water enters through seepage in the walls and exits through a drain somewhere below the pool surfaces, and the rate of entering and the rate of leaving are matched with impossible precision. The pools never rise. They never fall. The humidity never shifts. The temperature never wavers. Droplets form and fall and are replaced, but the total water in the chamber is, and has been, and will be, *the same*. The mineral deposits grow, but so slowly that a thousand years would add less than a hair's breadth. The place is not static — it is *arrested*, caught in a moment of becoming that extends indefinitely, a birth that never completes itself.

Perhaps this is why it feels wrong. The chamber is not ancient and settled, the way caves usually are. It is *new*, and it has been new for as long as anyone can remember. The raw stone will not accept the idea of age. The formations are embryonic and show no intention of maturing. The whole space feels like a held breath, a suspended animation, a place that has forgotten how to change.

---

Someone found it once. The evidence is scattered and faint — a single low wall of stacked stones, unmortared, built across a natural alcove in the chamber's far corner. The wall is crude, each stone placed by hand, fitted only roughly, and it stands no higher than a knee. Behind it, the alcove holds a flat stone that might have served as a sleeping place, and beside it, a small, shallow depression worn into the floor — a grinding hollow, perhaps, or the base of a fire long extinguished. A handful of artifacts rest in the depression: a fist-sized hammerstone, its edges chipped and polished; a fragment of something ceramic, its surface rough and unglazed, the colour of the chamber's own stone; a few splinters of worked bone, their purpose unclear.

The construction is old — not geologically old, but old in the way of human things. The wall has partially collapsed. The artifacts sit where they were left, undisturbed. There is no ash. No charcoal. Whoever built this shelter did not burn fires here, or burned them so sparingly that no trace remains. Perhaps they used the chamber's own warmth. Perhaps they came for the water, for the minerals it carried — pigment, maybe, or something medicinal, something that could be dissolved and taken away. The bone splinters might be tools for scraping the bacterial mats from the pool edges, harvesting the pale, rubbery sheets for some purpose now forgotten.

No bones. No remains. They left, or they died elsewhere. The chamber took back nothing, because the chamber takes nothing. It only holds.

---

A single living creature makes its home here — or perhaps it is not one creature but a colony, a species so reduced in number that it barely registers as present. Small, pale things, nearly transparent, with long, delicate legs and no eyes to speak of. They move along the water's edge with the slow, deliberate gait of animals that have never known urgency. They are not crickets. They are not spiders. They are something between — segmented, many-legged, barely larger than a grain of rice, and they feed on the bacterial films with mouthparts too fine to see. Their numbers are few. An observer might spend an hour in the chamber and see only three or four, each one isolated, each one moving along its own path with the patience of something that has nowhere else to be. They make no sound. They cast no shadow. They are the chamber's only concession to the idea of animal life, and they are so quiet, so sparse, so perfectly adapted to stillness that they might almost be mineral themselves — tiny, accidental formations that learned to walk.

---

The chamber has one further quality, difficult to name and impossible to verify: it feels *watched*. Not by anything present, not by the pale crawlers or the bacterial mats or the stone itself, but by the chamber as a whole, as though the equilibrium that holds it so perfectly in place is not passive but *attentive*. The warmth presses. The darkness waits. The water forms and falls and is reformed, and the cycle has a quality of observation, of patience, of something that is waiting for the chamber to complete the act of its own becoming — waiting, perhaps, for the first true stalactite to descend from the ceiling, for the first pool to rise, for the raw stone to finally accept the insult of time and begin, at last, to age.

It will not happen. The chamber is what it is: a room-sized moment, suspended in the mountain's warm, wet dark, full of everything and changing not at all. A place that was born and never finished being born. A secret the mountain keeps even from itself.
