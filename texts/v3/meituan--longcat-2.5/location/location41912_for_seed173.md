**The Sunken Works of Morvane**

Two hundred meters down, the light gives out. The water here is a blue-gray twilight, thick and cold and pressing, and the only ground is the Deck — a single massive platform of iron and concrete, a hamlet-sized slab resting on the seafloor. The Deck is the world. There is no natural substrate: the original seabed is buried under meters of poured foundation, and every surface above it is built — riveted plate, cast iron, brick, ceramic, glass. The Deck is ringed by a low brick-and-iron Parapet, chest-high. Beyond it, the water falls away into darkness. The Works are a bounded place: you can walk the perimeter in an hour.

On the Deck stand two dozen structures in rough rows, like a factory town compressed into a single room. The largest is the Foundry Hall, a vaulted space the size of a cathedral nave — sixty meters long, twenty wide, its iron-truss roof lost in the dim water above. Inside, the great crucible sits on its pedestal, a bowl of black iron ten meters across, filled with cold slag and the bones of machines. The Hall is the heart of the Works, the nave of this industrial church; the Chimney, rising forty meters from the roof, is its spire, a stack of brick and iron whose top is lost in the dim water above and whose interior is a highway for eels and ratfish. Around the Hall stand the smaller works: the Forge with its dies and presses; the Engine House, where the great flywheel still turns; the Boiler Room, its three boilers cold and colonized by tube worms; the Pipe Gallery, a long hall of valves and gauges; the Canteen, the Offices, the Pump House, the Condenser, the winch house, the gauge house, the powder magazine, the tool store, the pattern shop, the mould lofts.

All of it is ancient. The Works have been down here for three centuries, perhaps four. The iron is worn smooth as river stone; the brick has lost its edges, its corners rounded, its mortar softened to a gray paste. The structures have settled into their final form — a stable, ancient ruin, no longer collapsing, no longer changing much, worn to its essential shape. The erosion has done its work: everything is soft, smoothed, settled.

The water is cool — four degrees, perhaps five near the vents. It is thick, heavy, pressing. The light is dim: a faint blue-gray from far above, enough to see shapes but not colors, enough to know the water is there. Against this, the Works carry their own lights: a few industrial lamps still burning, powered by the vents, casting a sickly yellow-green glow; the bioluminescent smears of algae and fauna, blue and green and cold white; the occasional pulse of a jellyfish drifting through the Hall like a lantern. The Hall is a vast dark space, and the light moves through it like breath — a pulse here, a glow there, the slow turn of a school of fish catching the lamplight and throwing it back in silver.

The sound is loud. The Works are never silent. The Engine House still turns — the tide drives the great flywheel, and the pistons move, slow and immense, with a rhythm like breathing. The pumps throb. Water rushes through the Pipe Gallery with a continuous roar. Metal groans as it expands and contracts with the temperature shifts. Valves hiss. The Chimney draws a constant draft, a low moaning note. Somewhere, a pressure door bangs, irregularly, a slow percussion. The whole complex is an instrument, and the sea plays it. The sound fills the Hall like water fills a tank — it is everywhere, it presses on the ears, it vibrates through the iron underfoot. The rhythm of the flywheel is the heartbeat of the place; everything else — the pumps, the water, the groaning metal, the banging door — is the pulse around it.

The Works are alive. The Deck is a reef.

Fish school in the Foundry Hall — hundreds of them, silver and gray, turning as one in the dim light. Ratfish hover in the doorways, their tails long and thin. Eels coil in the pipes — conger eels, moray eels, some pale and blind from generations in the dark. Octopuses den in the mould lofts and the offices, their arms hanging from the windows. Crabs and lobsters walk the Deck in hundreds, picking at the algae. Shrimp cloud in the warm water near the vents. A giant isopod, the size of a dog, sleeps in the powder magazine.

Jellyfish drift through the complex in their seasons — moon jellies, comb jellies, the occasional deep-sea medusa, pulsing with cold light. Tube worms cluster on the warm pipes near the vents, their plumes red and white. Sponges coat the iron in thick, colorful mats — yellow, orange, red, violet.

The vegetation is sparse. Algae coat most surfaces in a thin green-brown film. Kelp grows in a few places — on the Parapet, in the courtyard, where the current brings nutrients — long fronds swaying in the flow, ten meters tall, a small sparse forest. A few sea anemones, a few sea stars, a scattering of sea cucumbers on the Deck. Not much. The Works are too built, too hard, too active for a rich flora.

The Works are a machine that never stopped. The tide drives the flywheel; the flywheel drives the pumps; the pumps move water through the condensers; the condensers cool the boilers (cold now, but the system still cycles). The geothermal vents beneath the Deck warm the southern end and power a few of the lights. The Works are a closed, self-sustaining system — not alive, but not dead either, a steady state.

The fauna has made the Works its own. The fish school in the Hall because the roof shelters them from currents. The eels use the Chimney as a highway. The octopuses use the offices as dens. The crabs use the algae on the Deck. The Works are a habitat as much as a ruin.

The erosion is ancient and complete. The Works will not collapse; they have settled into their final form. They will slowly be buried in their own debris, slowly be overgrown, but the iron will last centuries more. The Works are an ancient machine at the bottom of the sea, still turning, still loud, still home.
