# The Weeping Plateau

A high equatorial tableland, four thousand meters up and the size of a small country, set in a ring of older peaks that are slowly coming apart. The ring is both wall and watershed. Snow survives there only in patches, and even those weep constantly, feeding threads of meltwater that descend through gullies to the basin below. Nothing has ever been built here — no road, no hearth, no felled trunk, no tool. The place has no need of builders. It operates like a machine that has been running since before anyone thought to call it one.

**The terrain is old.** Erosion finished its rough work long ago and has settled into quiet upkeep. Granite domes rise whale-backed from the vegetation, their skins flaking in thin sheets, warm to the touch even in shade. Streams have cut down to base level and now wander sluggishly across wide marsh flats. Where the water carries iron, it has left shelves of laterite — brick-red, hard as pottery — stepping down to the pools in terraces made by chemistry rather than hands. The soil is deep, weathered, and in balance: what the rain takes, the roots and mats give back. Nothing here looks newly broken.

**Water is everywhere near the surface, and over a third of the plateau it *is* the surface.** Warm rain falls most afternoons — storms that build by noon and break in soft, sustained hisses rather than drumming, because the rain lands through moss and mist. Afterward every surface sheets and drips for an hour. At night the rock cools below the dew point and the entire plateau sweats: rock faces weep, moss plumps like sponges, and the pools rise by a finger's width. Beetles tilt their bodies at dawn and channel condensation into their mouths along grooved elytra.

At the center sits **the Cauldron**, a jade-white mineral lake with no outlet, hot enough to scald, its surface dimpled by rain and by bubbles rising from below. It leaves only as vapor, and the vapor returns as rain, and the loop has no end. On its northwestern shore, **the Scalding Stairs** — sinter terraces in cream, ochre, and rust — overflow lip to lip, each pool painted by thermophile mats in gradients of sulfur-yellow, iron-orange, and ink-black, every mat edged with an oily sheen. Geysers keep their own schedules. The largest, the Cough, goes every few hours, a column that rolls its boom across the flats. Nearby, a field of vents called **the Organ** whistles actual chords when pressure shifts, low pipes moaning in thirds.

**The Boiler Marsh** covers the eastern low ground: warm mud at a perpetual low simmer, gas bubbles the size of fists rising and bursting with soft pops, sedges and reeds stitched together into floating meadows that drift and re-anchor themselves. The ground there is warm as blood.

The sun is fierce — thin air, equatorial latitude, UV that reddens exposed skin in minutes — but the ground is hotter and the water hottest of all. Steam zones are stifling; sweat dries fast everywhere else. Snow persists only on the rim peaks, and even there it is dissolving. The heat lives in the stone, the water, and the blood.

**Vegetation stacks in near-touching layers.** Tree ferns and lobelia towers form a broken canopy; every branch carries gardens of epiphytes until the wood beneath vanishes entirely; moss hangs in curtains meters long. Bromeliad barrels hold warm water — canopy pools with their own microfauna, whole food chains suspended ten meters up. At the vents, mats carpet hectares in gradients of temperature. The marsh meadows bloom with hot-water lilies. Green on green on green; the saturation is total. The forest shades the marsh, the marsh feeds the mist, and the mist feeds the moss, and none of it was arranged.

**Fauna is abundant, and much of it behaves like machinery.** Click-beetles with copper elytra swarm in columns that move like looms, their bodies clicking in rhythms that carry for hundreds of meters. Midge clouds over the marsh at dusk form the low continuous hum that is the plateau's resting sound. Frogs chorus after every storm, breeding in the warm pools. Steam-doves bathe in drifting mist, and raptors hunt them at dusk, when the birds rise with the steam.

The largest animals are the **boilerbacks** — huge, slow herbivores, dense with muscle, warm-blooded to the point of radiating heat you can feel at paces-length away. Mats and small ferns grow on their warm backs like living saddles, and birds ride them to stir up insects. They graze in columns, crop the fern brakes in orderly swathes, and at dusk they park in rows on the warm granite domes, aligned and silent, steaming gently — machinery at rest. Their dung is warm when it lands. A seasonal migration follows the warm rains between basins, herds moving on schedules older than memory.

**This is where the industry lives.** Not in anything made, but in everything done. The terraces accrete centimeters per season, a scaled production of mineral. The mats manufacture biomass by the hectare. The boilerbacks are engines — combustion, motion, and fertilizer in one body. The quartz ridges of the rim click and spark at dawn as the rock warms, piezoelectric snaps like tiny static discharges; fur and feathers hold a charge in the dry upper air, and birds preening at sunrise crackle faintly. On still nights, St. Elmo's fire dances along the vent lips, and a few gas seeps burn with small blue flames that have never been lit and never go out. The vents keep time. The flocks turn in synchrony. The whole plateau breathes on a schedule: dawn clicking, noon steam cap, afternoon warm rain, dusk exhalation, when every vent lifts its voice and the beasts park and the birds rise.

It is a refinery with no refiners, a factory staffed by weather and blood.

**The sound of the place is mostly quiet.** Wet air and distance hush everything. What remains are close, small things: the drip, the bubble, the click, the wing-whirr, the frog's soft plop, the sustained hiss of rain through moss. The hum underneath it all. The Cough, far off. The Organ, before it shifts.

**The light is normal high-altitude light — bright, unfiltered, silver-gold —** but the steam softens it at the margins. After rain, everything glazes; the sinter crusts glare white; the Cauldron keeps a permanent rainbow haze at its shoreline. The rim snow catches the dawn. At night: cold stars, the blue pinpricks of the gas seeps, the faint violet stitching of St. Elmo.

**The rules are few and strictly kept.** Water never leaves; what falls must rise again. Heat is currency, and every layer harvests the gradient — sun to mat, mat to beast, beast to microbe — and nothing is wasted; carcasses cool fast and are dismantled faster. Growth and decay hold their balance; the land is old and settled in its own state. Nothing burns wood — the only fires are the seeps — and life cooks with the earth. And the order, that eerie mechanical order, is unplanned: no engineer, only appetite and timing, only the schedule that appetite keeps.

A visitor who stays long enough stops looking for the operators. The plateau is the machine. The machine is alive.
