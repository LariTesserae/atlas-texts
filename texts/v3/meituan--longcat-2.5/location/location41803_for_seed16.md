The cavity has no name that translates cleanly. The people who live along its lower wall call it, in their own language, something like *the warm dark that holds* — though the word they use for "holds" also means "remembers," and the word for "warm" is the same one they use for "alive."

It is a bubble of stone, deep below any surface that ever saw light. How deep, no one knows. The oldest stories say the first people came down through a winding fissure that took a generation to descend, and that the fissure collapsed behind them. Whether this is history or metaphor is not a question the people of the cavity spend much time on. They are here. The cavity is here. That is sufficient.

The space is vast — perhaps three kilometers from wall to wall at its widest, though distance is difficult to judge in the dimness and the heat-shimmer. The ceiling is lost in darkness; the people know it is there only because sounds sometimes come from above — the tick of falling mineral grains, the slow grinding of rock adjusting to temperature, once in a great while a fall of stone that crashes into the growths below like thunder. The floor slopes gently toward a central depression where the rock is hot enough to glow a dull, sullen red. The walls curve up and away, smooth and rounded, shaped by long centuries of exposure to the hot, mineral-laden air. The stone is dark — basalt, or something like it — and it sweats minerals: faint crusts of salt and sulfur that bloom on the surface and flake away, that accumulate in drifts below the growths, that the people harvest and use.

There is no water. There has never been water. The word for it exists in the old songs — a legendary substance, cold and liquid, that falls from the sky — but no living person has seen it, and most do not believe it is real. The air is bone-dry. It tastes of hot stone and sulfur and something metallic, like the air after a lightning strike. It moves in slow, heavy currents — convection cells that rise from the hot floor, spread across the ceiling, and sink down the cooler walls. These currents are constant but variable in strength, and they carry with them the fine mineral dust that coats every surface in the cavity. The dust is the basis of the growths. It is also, itself, a presence: it films the skin, it grits between the teeth, it accumulates in the folds of clothing, it is swept from the streets each day in small, ritual piles that are carried to the edge and tipped into the darkness.

The heat is the first thing you feel, and the last thing you stop feeling. It comes from the rock, from the air, from the deep fractures in the floor where gas seeps up and occasionally ignites in pale, flickering flames. The temperature at the floor, near the central depression, is hot enough to cook food on the stone — the people lay flat cakes of ground growth-flour on the hot rock and let them bake in seconds. Higher up, along the walls where the town is built, it is merely — merely — the temperature of a fever that never breaks. The people live here without complaint. Their bodies have adapted over generations: short, compact, with skin that sweats efficiently and lungs that can tolerate the sulfurous air for hours at a time. Sweat evaporates almost instantly in the dry air, leaving a faint crust of salt on the skin that the people brush away absently, constantly, the way a person in a colder climate might brush away snow.

The growths are not plants, exactly, though the people use the same word for them. They are mineral-organisms — chemosynthetic, thermophilic, drawing their energy from the heat differentials between the deep rock and the cavity air, and their substance from the mineral vapors that seep through the floor. They grow as branching, coral-like structures, some no taller than a finger, others rising like trees to heights of twelve or fifteen meters. Their surfaces are ridged, pitted, sometimes smooth as glazed ceramic. They are ochre, rust, bone-white, charcoal-black, and in places — where the mineral content of the vapors shifts — a faint, oily iridescence. They are not soft. They are stony, woody, sometimes brittle. When the wind blows through them, they click and scrape against each other, a constant dry percussion, like rain on a stone roof. Touch one and it is warm — always warm, sometimes hot — and dry, and faintly gritty, like sun-baked clay.

There are mats of smaller growths — crusts, tufts, shelf-like formations — that cover broad areas of the floor and the lower walls. These are the most lush: dense, varied, layered, in places forming deep drifts of fallen mineral flakes and spore-dust. The larger structures grow among them like trees in a forest of moss. The people cultivate them, in their way — scraping the crusts for medicine, harvesting the flakes for material, transplanting small growths to new locations where the vapors are rich. They do not farm them so much as tend them, the way a shepherd tends a flock that moves too slowly to see. The growths compete, slowly, for access to the vapor-venting cracks; they die, slowly, when a vent shifts or a new growth overshadows them; they are replaced, slowly, by new growths that settle from the spore-dust on the wind. The people read these changes the way a farmer reads weather: a new vent opening in the floor will, in a few years, be surrounded by a halo of fresh growth; a collapse in the rock will leave a bare scar that may take decades to recolonize. The cavity is not static. It is in motion, but the motion is geological, and the people have learned to see it.

Some of the growths are edible — or at least, they can be processed into food. The people harvest the crusts and the soft inner layers of certain species, grind them into a flour, and bake them on hot stone into flat, dense cakes that taste of minerals and heat. Moisture comes from the growths too: some of the fleshy species contain water bound in mineral compounds, and the people extract it by slow heating, collecting the vapor on cool stone. It is not pleasant-tasting water — it is metallic, slightly sour — but it sustains. There are no animals to hunt, no fish to catch, no insects to gather. The growths are the only source of food and drink, and the people have learned, over centuries, to live on them entirely. The old songs speak of meat, of honey, of fruit — but these are as legendary as water, and the people who sing the songs do not know what the words mean.

The town is built along the lower third of the western wall, where the rock is more stable and the heat slightly less brutal. It is not large — perhaps three thousand people, in a dense cluster of stone and ceramic buildings that climb the wall in terraces. The buildings are low, thick-walled, with small openings oriented to catch the updrafts of slightly cooler air that rise from the lower slopes. They are built of stone blocks fitted without mortar, of ceramic bricks fired in kilns that use the cavity's own heat, of a kind of concrete made from the mineral flakes and a binding agent extracted from the growths. They are old. Some of the lowest buildings have been continuously occupied for centuries, repaired and rebuilt so many times that no original stone remains, yet the same walls, the same doors, the same narrow streets. The town has the feeling of something grown rather than built — a slow accretion of human presence on the rock.

The streets are narrow, winding, following the contours of the wall. They are lit by glow-organisms kept in ceramic vessels — cultivated strains that produce a steady, yellowish light — and by the faint phosphorescence of the growths that colonize the lower walls. The overall illumination is very low: the darkness above and between the buildings is nearly absolute, and the light extends only a few meters. The people navigate as much by memory and by the feel of the air currents as by sight. The main streets are wide enough for two people to pass; the side streets are narrow enough that your shoulders brush the walls. There are no squares, no open spaces — the town is too dense, too vertical, too pressed against the rock. The only open space is the edge, where the town gives way to the empty cavity, and the people go there to look out at the growths and the darkness. There are gathering places — wide spots in the streets where the wall recedes, where people sit and talk and trade — and there are the kilns, which are always warm and always crowded, and there are the edge-platforms, where the old go to die, lying on the warm stone and looking out at the growths until they stop breathing, and their bodies are carried down to the floor and left for the growths to claim.

The cavity is loud. The rock itself groans and ticks as it expands and contracts with the slow thermal cycles of the deep — a cycle of perhaps twelve to sixteen hours, driven by geological processes no one understands. When the heat peaks, the convection currents strengthen, the air becomes almost unbreathable in the lower areas, and the growths sing louder — a constant, dry, rattling chorus, like a thousand bone chimes in a wind. When the heat ebbs, the town breathes easier, people move about more freely, and the sound drops to a lower, steadier drone. There are also periodic gas releases — every few days, pressure builds in deep fractures and is suddenly vented, filling parts of the cavity with thick, acrid clouds that take hours to clear. The people have learned to read the signs: the change in the wind's note, the behavior of the growths, the smell. They shelter when these come, pressing cloth masks soaked in mineral solutions over their faces, waiting for the air to clear. The gas releases are the closest thing the cavity has to weather — predictable in their unpredictability, dangerous in their routine, respected and watched for.

And there are other sounds. In certain parts of the cavity, the wind through the growths produces tones — low, resonant, almost musical. These are natural, the people say: just the shapes of the growths and the force of the wind. But they are strange, and beautiful, and a little unsettling. Some of the growths seem to vibrate in sympathy with these tones, adding their own harmonics. The people have songs that imitate these sounds — long, droning chants that are felt in the chest more than heard with the ears. They sing them at funerals, and at the start of the hot season, and when a child is born. The songs are not happy or sad; they are simply *of* the place, in a way that nothing else is.

The boundary between the living and the mineral is not clear here. The growths are slow — so slow that a human lifetime sees only modest change — yet they are unmistakably alive, or at least active. They respond to disturbance. A broken branch will, over weeks, begin to seal and regrow. A surface that is scratched will slowly, over months, develop a new texture, a new growth pattern. They seem, in some subtle way, aware — not intelligent, but reactive in ways that go beyond simple chemistry. There are places in the cavity where the growths form patterns — spirals, lattices, clusters that seem almost deliberate. The people have myths about these. Some say they are the remnants of an older, greater growth — a mind that once filled the cavity and has since gone dormant, leaving only these patterns as traces. Others say they are natural formations, and the appearance of design is just the human need to see meaning. Either way, the people do not disturb these places. They are sacred, or at least respected. The patterns are old — older than the town, older than the oldest songs — and they are in equilibrium with the growths around them, neither growing nor fading, simply persisting.

The people themselves are not numerous, and they are not old as a species — but they are old as a community. They have been here for hundreds of generations, long enough to adapt, long enough to forget (almost) where they came from. Their technology is simple: stone tools, ceramic, some worked metal — copper, mostly, traded from a vein somewhere deep below, or meteoric iron found in the rock. They have no wheels (the terrain is too uneven), no written language (or a very simple one — marks scratched on stone, used for counting and for sacred inscriptions). Their knowledge is oral, practical, deep: they know every vent, every growth, every thermal cycle. They know which crusts cure fever and which cause it. They know how to read the wind, how to find the coolest air, how to build a wall that will stand for a century in the heat. This knowledge is their wealth, and their prison.

They are aware that the cavity is the only habitable space for a vast distance in every direction. Their myths speak of the surface — a place of impossible cold and blinding light, where water falls from the sky and everything is drenched in it. They do not believe they could survive there. Some say they were once there, and fled below. Others say they were always here, and the surface is a fantasy. The oldest stories are ambiguous. There is a word in their language for "outside" that is also the word for "before," and another for "above" that is also the word for "after." What this means, no one can say.

The cavity holds them. The warm dark remembers them. The growths grow and shed and click in the wind, and the rock groans in its slow cycles, and the gas vents and clears, and the people live and die and are carried down to the floor and left for the growths to claim. The place is not cruel and not kind. It is simply itself: hot, dry, dark, loud, alive with slow processes that do not need water or animals or light. The people are part of it, as the growths are part of it, as the dust and the gas and the stone are part of it. They do not ask it to be anything else. They have nowhere else to go.
