# Caelbrach, the Weeping Plateau

The plateau rises from the surrounding lowlands like a stone altar left out in the rain for ten thousand years. It is not dramatic — no cliffs, no peaks — just a broad, slow elevation, a wide back of earth that lifts the sky a little closer and holds water the way a held palm holds water: imperfectly, constantly losing it, constantly gathering more.

## The Stone

The rock here is volcanic, ancient beyond any living memory of it, and it has been eating water for so long that it has become half-porous, a grey-brown sponge riddled with channels. Over the plateau's broad face, the stone has weathered into columnar formations — hexagonal pillars, mostly, some as thick as a house, some as thin as a leg, tilting at angles that suggest they were never quite finished forming. They stand in clusters and in long colonnades, and between them the water moves.

It moves everywhere. That is the first thing you understand about Caelbrach. The plateau is not wet like a riverbank or a marsh; it is wet like a body — water coming up from below through warm springs that never cool, water condensing from the warm heavy air onto the cooler stone, water running down from the sky almost daily in warm, brief, soaking rains that the stone drinks and then slowly releases through its million pores. The columns weep. The ground does not quite pool but never quite dries. The air is a warm damp pressing against your skin like a cloth.

## The Sound

You hear Caelbrach before you see it, and then you never stop hearing it. The water is the sound — not a gentle sound, not the pastoral murmur of a brook. The channels that carry water through the stone have been widened and shaped over centuries, and many of them are narrow enough that the water moves fast, and it roars through these constrictions with a continuous, percussive, deafening insistence. Add to this the industrial works — the pumping stations, the forges, the textile mills with their water-driven looms, the dye-works whose vats are fed by dedicated channels — and the plateau becomes a place of ceaseless mechanical and hydraulic noise. The buildings are stone, and stone carries sound, and the whole plateau is essentially a single resonant body. You feel it in your sternum before you hear it in your ears. Conversations happen close, mouths near ears, and even then you catch only fragments. The people of Caelbrach speak in a clipped, efficient dialect, and they have a gesture — two fingers pressed to the chest — that means *I hear you* or *I understand*, a small acknowledgment against the roar.

## The City

Caelbrach sits in the plateau's center, where the springs are most active and the columns are thickest. It is not a large city — perhaps thirty thousand souls — but it is dense in a way that belies its modest population, because it is built vertically into the rock. The oldest structures are carved directly into the columns: rooms and passages hollowed out of the stone, their walls still damp, their floors worn into shallow bowls by centuries of dripping. Newer construction layers outward and upward from these cores — stone blocks quarried from the plateau's edges, timber brought up from the lowlands at considerable expense, iron and brass and copper for the industrial works that have made Caelbrach wealthy.

The industry is water-driven, always. The plateau's water does not fall dramatically — there are no great waterfalls here — but it moves with enormous, patient force through its channels, and the Caelbrach engineers have spent generations learning to harness that force. Wooden wheels the size of houses turn in the widest channels, their paddles shaped to exacting specifications, and they drive shafts and gears and bellows and looms. In the forges, the hammers fall with a rhythm that merges with the water's roar until you cannot tell one from the other. The textile mills produce a heavy, dense wool cloth dyed in the mineral-rich spring water — colours that cannot be replicated elsewhere, deep rust-reds and ochres and a blue-black that seems to absorb light. The dye-works are famous. The whole plateau smells of wet wool and iron and hot mineral water.

The city is organized around its water. The main channels are the main streets — stone-paved walkways running alongside open waterways, the water sometimes arched over with bridges, sometimes left bare so that you walk beside it and feel the spray. The residential districts climb the columns, connected by staircases carved into the rock and by iron ladders and by wooden platforms that creak and sway. The industrial districts are down, always down, close to the water's force, and they are hot — the forges add their heat to the warm air, and the steam from the dye-vats rises in columns that mingle with the mist from the springs until the lower city is perpetually wrapped in a warm, scented, mineral fog.

## The Ecology

There is little green here. The plateau's thin soil, constantly saturated, supports mostly mosses — thick, spongy carpets of them in the greens and yellows and deep browns, growing over every surface that is not actively walked on or built over. Lichens crust the columns in patterns that resemble maps or scripts, and in the wettest crevices, where water drips steadily and the warm air is trapped, ferns grow in profusion — small, delicate, ancient-looking things, their fronds perpetually unfurling. There are no trees on the plateau itself. The people import timber, and they guard it jealously. A wooden beam in Caelbrach is a sign of wealth, and the great wooden wheels of the mills are maintained with a devotion that borders on religious — they are repaired, replaced piece by piece, an eternal construction that is never the same and never entirely new.

Fauna is scarce. The roar and the wet and the heat have driven most creatures away, or perhaps they never came. There are birds — small, dark, quick things that nest in the columns' crevices and seem to have no song, only sharp calls that cut through the noise. There are insects in the warm mist, pale things with long legs that drift through the fog like thistledown. In the deepest channels, where the water is warmest, there are fish — blind, translucent, the colour of the stone — that have never seen light and that the Caelbrach people consider a delicacy, though catching them requires descending into the dark wet passages beneath the city, and not many bother. There are rats, of course, and the cats that hunt them, and the dogs that bark at the cats, and the sounds of these small dramas are swallowed immediately by the greater sound of water and machine.

## The Strange

The water here is not ordinary. It carries minerals in suspension — iron, copper, sulfur, and something else, something the Caelbrach people call *calx* without being able to define it further. The calx stains everything it touches: the stone is streaked with rust and verdigris, the wooden beams are darkened and hardened by it, the skin of the people who work in the dye-works is tinted faintly, permanently, in shades of brown and grey. This is not considered unhealthy. It is simply what the water does.

But there is more. The channels, in certain places, have been shaped — by natural erosion, by human hands, by the slow patient work of both together — into forms that create acoustic effects. Stand in the right place, at the right time of day, and the water's roar resolves into something almost like a voice, or like music, a deep harmonic drone that rises and falls with the water's pressure. The Caelbrach people know these places. They are marked, some of them, with small brass plates or with carved signs, and they have names: the Throat, the Bell, the Choir. No one agrees on what causes them. The engineers have theories about resonance chambers and flow rates. The older residents have other theories, less technical, involving the plateau's spirit or the memory of the water or the voices of those who built the first channels and were swallowed by the stone.

There is also the matter of the upward springs. In several locations on the plateau, water emerges from the stone and flows not down but up — rises through narrow fissures against gravity, a few centimeters, a few inches, before succumbing and falling back. The Caelbrach engineers have tried to harness these, and they have failed. The water will not be piped upward; it will only do its strange, brief ascent on its own terms. Some of the upward springs are surrounded by small, crude structures — not buildings, exactly, but arrangements of stones, placed by no one knows whom, at no time that can be determined. They are always damp. The moss grows thickest around them.

## The Rhythm

Time moves differently on the plateau. Not slowly in the sense of stillness — there is too much sound, too much motion, too much industry for stillness — but slowly in the sense that the changes are imperceptible. The water level rises and falls on a cycle that spans decades, and the people who live through the high years and the low years barely notice the transition, only remarking, sometimes, that the Throat sounds different this season, or that a channel that was dry in their grandmother's time has begun to flow again. The columns grow — not upward, but inward, the mineral deposits slowly filling their hollows, and this process is measurable only across generations. A column that was a room in one century is a solid pillar in the next. The people build around this. They know that their city is temporary in its details, that the stone is slowly reclaiming what was carved from it, and they do not fight this. They build for their own lifetimes and their children's, and they accept that their grandchildren will live in a different city on the same plateau, hearing the same water through different channels.

The days are warm and wet and loud. The nights are warm and wet and loud, but differently — the industrial works slow after dark, and the water's voice becomes more distinct, the harmonics more audible, the plateau more itself. People sleep with the windows open, and the sound enters their dreams, and they wake with the taste of minerals on their lips.

## The Approach

You come to Caelbrach from the lowlands, up a road that switchbacks across the plateau's southern face. The road is paved with stone from the plateau itself, and it is wet — always wet — and the wagons that climb it have iron-shod wheels that spark against the stone in the rain. As you rise, the air grows warmer, not cooler, and the mist thickens, and the sound begins — first as a vibration in the wooden frame of your vehicle, then as a rumble you feel in your chest, then as a roar that overtakes everything. You round a final bend, and the plateau opens before you: the columns, the channels, the city climbing the rock, the steam rising from the lower districts, the iron bridges arching over water that moves too fast and too constantly to ever be still, and the whole vast, patient, roaring, weeping, stained, ancient, living, industrial, strange, warm, wet, loud, slow thing that Caelbrach is, and that it has been, and that it will continue to be, for as long as the water rises and the stone remembers how to weep.
