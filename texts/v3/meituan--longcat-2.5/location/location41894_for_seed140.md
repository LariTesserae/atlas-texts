**The Weeping Hall**

On the summit of a mountain that has forgotten how to be a mountain, there is a crack that breathes. The peak itself is a peneplain — the last, patient work of erosion that has had all the time there is. Gone are cliffs, gone are couloirs; the high old stone has been worn to a near-flatness that rolls under wind and weather like a frozen sea. It is cold up there, and thin, and loud with wind. The crack exhales warmth. It smells of wet lime, mushroom earth, a trace of sulfur. You descend into it sideways, and the cold lets go of you like a hand unclasping.

Then: darkness, absolute enough that your eyes become ornaments. Then, a moment later: sound. The Hall is loud.

It is a single room — a grove-or-room, cathedral hollow — perhaps two hundred paces across, shaped like the inside of a bell. All its edges were softened by water long before anything living had a name. There is no sharp fracture anywhere; even the cracks have rounded lips. Flowstone hangs and rises in great sheets, ribbed and scalloped, worn to the texture of old cloth. Stalactites survive only as blunt nubs along the high ribs. The Hall is past its age of making such things. It has settled into its final form, and the final form is smooth, and warm, and wet.

The walls weep. Condensation gathers on the cooler stone and runs down in shining threads, and the air is so near saturation that your skin never dries — the whole chamber is a bath, a womb, a held breath. Drips fall everywhere. They are the Hall's clock and its orchestra: a random music of small struck cups, rising and falling in slow weathers of its own, and every so often a great drop lands in a pool with a note like a gong that rolls around the walls for seconds before it gives up. A seam in the far wall sheds a steady thread of water into the marsh below. The marsh is the room's low center — a warm, shallow lagoon, its bottom black sediment, its surface dimpled by the constant rain of drips and by bubbles that rise lazily from below. The bubbles are warm gas from the deep rock. It feeds pale bacterial films that skirt the pools, and the films feed everything else. No sun has ever entered here. The food web runs on chemistry and on heat.

And it is lush. There is no green — nothing here has ever been green — but there is growth upon growth, soft and layered and endless. Mold lawns inches deep spring underfoot over the floor stones, cream and ochre and rust. Shelf-fungi rise in tiered balconies on the walls, the color of old ivory and old teeth. Cave-cotton hangs in long curtains from the high ribs, filamentous, swaying in air too faint to call a breeze. In the middle of the marsh rises a stand of hollow reeds — a fungus grown tall as ten meters, pale as candle wax, a small forest of bare pale stalks with closed caps, their tops lost in the dark above. Every few hours a cap opens with a soft audible *whuff*, like a bellows, and releases a puff of spores that drifts through the room and settles on everything. That is the Hall's weather.

Its fauna is abundant, in pockets. Clouds of pale midges hang over the lagoon in slow columns. Blind grey moths move through them. Spiders hang on long threads from the ribs and pluck them like harps when a moth struggles. Glass snails, translucent, write shining slow trails across the walls. In the pools, clouds of blind white fish, finger-length, turn as one body. On the water, marsh-striders write their small rings. And on a flowstone ledge above the marsh lies the lamb — the Hall's great slow animal, a flesh-colored salamander the length of a person, eyeless, warm-skinned as bread. It spends whole days motionless, then slides into the water with barely a ripple to graze on snails. It is very old. There may be only one; they are too still to count. High above, a colony of small pale bats roosts on the ribs, and their chitter runs underneath everything like a second, quicker river. They leave by the crack in their own seasons and return across generations to the same stone. Insects drone. Crickets click. Fish plash. The drips conduct it all. The sound is thick as the air.

Scattered through the room — widely, widely scattered, for this is a hollow place and most of it is empty dark — stand a few presences that were not made by water.

The Bell hangs above the marsh from a worn stone rib: a ring of dark metal the size of a cartwheel, worn smooth as a river pebble, with no seam and no mark of any tool. Its lip weeps a steady sheet of condensation. Once every few days — or weeks; the Hall keeps its own count — it sounds: no strike, only a low note rising from nowhere, felt in the sternum and the teeth more than heard. When it sounds, the water on its lip gathers into one trembling bead that does not fall, the bats cry out all at once, the reeds release their spores, and the faint blue points on the ceiling brighten, just slightly, like a held breath.

The Stair is on the far side of the room, cut from a single mound of flowstone, spiraling up into the dark and ending at nothing — a wall, a ceiling, a dark that gives nothing back. Count the steps: there are more going up than coming down, though the stair never branches and never turns.

The Door stands alone on the empty floor: a rectangular slab of the same seamless dark metal, no frame, no wall, nothing behind it but floor and dark. Its face carries a worn relief of curves and circles too softened to read, and it is warm — warm on the face that faces nothing at all. Sound dies at it. Clap your hands anywhere else in the Hall and the room returns your voice; clap before the Door and the sound comes back as a thud, as if the dark had swallowed the echo.

High on the walls, in the unreachable dark, clusters of faint cold blue points mark the ceiling's presence — colonies of something that lives only on the warmest seams, or lamps, or eyes. They are no more light than a held breath is wind. They do not illuminate the Hall; they only give the darkness a roof you can almost see.

Between these presences lies the Hollow: open floor, worn smooth, empty for a hundred paces, crossed by nothing but drips and the occasional moth. The density of the place is the density of a grove, not a forest — a few presences standing in a great quiet room, each alone with itself.

The rules of the Hall are the rules of a finished thing. Change happens at the speed of stone: the marsh rises and falls over years; the stalactite nubs grow a hand's breadth in a lifetime; the Bell keeps its slow count; the bats keep their seasons; the lamb keeps its decades. Nothing here dies quickly. Nothing here is young. The chamber is not dying — it is completed, the oldest stable state, a world at the end of its changes, still warm, still wet, still singing to itself in the dark. The builders, whoever they were, left a door with nothing behind it, a stair to nowhere, and a bell that keeps a time no one is left to keep. Perhaps the Hall itself was their machine, and this is it still running: a warm wet room, weeping, counting, listening. Perhaps it only sounds because the room was worn — or built — to that pitch, and the note is the chamber hearing itself.

Stand in it a while. You will learn to know it by ear: the drip-chorus, the insect drone, the moth-wing's paper rustle, the lamb sliding into the water. You will learn that the dark is not empty but full — full of slow warm patient life, full of water finishing its long work, full of a single room the size of a grove, humming, every few days, to itself.
