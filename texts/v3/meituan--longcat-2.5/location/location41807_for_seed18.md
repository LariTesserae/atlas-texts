# The Lung

At the summit — not near the summit, but *at* it, the absolute crown — the mountain has been hollowed out and fitted with a machine the size of a cathedral. From below, you would see only the peak: a dark plug of riveted iron and green-patinated bronze rising from the rock like a rivet-head, stained with vertical rust-trails and tufted with moss that has no business surviving at this altitude. But inside — inside is a single vast chamber, a vertical vault, and it is this chamber that is the world.

The floor is a grate of cast iron, and through it you can see down. Down a long way. Down into warmth and red-lit dark where something turns, something enormous and slow, something that has been turning since before anyone alive can say. The grate is rimed with lime and verdigris. Water beads on it constantly — not rain, but condensation, the exhalation of the machine itself, which runs warm, which runs *very* warm, warm as a animal's body, warm as a fever. The air is thick with it. It drips from the vaulted ceiling in a hundred places, not randomly but in lines, in courses, following the seams between the bronze ribs where the solder has grown porous. The drips strike collection trays — great shallow bowls of beaten copper, green as flags — and the trays overflow into channels cut into the floor, and the channels lead to grates, and the grates lead down, down, into the warmth.

The walls are not walls so much as the inside of an engine. Pipes run in them. Valves the size of cartwheels protrude at intervals, their handles worn smooth. Gauges with cracked glass faces still hold their needles at positions that might mean something. Everything is made — there is no natural substrate here, no stone that was not placed, no surface that is not iron or bronze or the ceramic of old insulators — and yet the moss has found it. The moss has found everything. It grows in the drip-lines, in the joints between plates, in the felt packing around pipe-flanges, and it is lush, it is *jungle*-lush, a vertical garden of ferns and small creeping things that root in rust and condensation and the thin film of mineral deposit that the machine sweats. In the warmest corners, near the great bronze ribs where the wall-pipes carry whatever hot thing they carry, there are ferns as tall as a person, prehistoric in their vigor, unfurling fronds that never see direct sun but do not seem to mind.

And the animals. The animals have found it too. Birds — small grey birds with no name, or too many names — nest in the rafters by the hundred, their droppings whitening the upper reaches of the pipes, their murmuring and cheeping a constant undertone to the greater sound. Insects: beetles that have never seen a flower, moths the color of iron-rust that roost in the valve-housings, a species of pale cricket that lives its entire life in the warm drip-zone and has forgone wings. Rats, fat and slow, that drink from the collection trays and eat the ferns and are eaten in turn by something — you rarely see it, but you find the remains, the neat piles of fur and bone on the widest horizontal pipe-flanges, and you suspect an owl, or something older than an owl. The chamber is a vertical ecosystem, warm, wet, lit by the red glow from below and the grey light that filters through the glass panes in the dome — normal light, ordinary light, the light of a high-altitude overcast day, neither golden nor dim, just *there*, illuminating the green and the bronze and the falling drops.

The sound. The sound is the reason you cannot stay long. It is not loud in the way an explosion is loud; it is loud in the way an ocean is loud, or a foundry, or a heartbeat heard from inside the body. The machine — the thing below, the thing that turns — produces a drone at the threshold of hearing, a pressure more than a tone, felt in the sternum and the teeth. Over it, around it, the chamber itself adds its layer: the drip of water (a hundred drips, a thousand, each one distinct but together a texture, a hiss), the tick and ping of cooling metal, the subsonic groan of the great bronze ribs as they take the strain, the birds, the insects, the rats, the whole biological murmur. Together it is a wall of sound, constant, unchanging, the acoustic equivalent of a climate. You stop hearing it after a while, the way you stop hearing your own breathing, and then you notice that you are shouting when you speak, and that your thoughts have taken on the rhythm of the drone, and that this is perhaps not accidental.

And it is slow. It is so slow. The machine turns at a rate that might be one revolution per minute, or one per hour — it is difficult to tell, and no one has ever been able to say for certain because the last person who tried to measure it left before finishing, and the person before that left before starting, and the person before that is still here, in a manner of speaking. Change in the Lung is glacial. A fern unfolds a frond over the course of a month. A drip wears a groove in the grate over a decade. The moss advances by the width of a hand each year. The machine itself — if it is running, if it is doing anything — is doing it on a timescale that makes geology look hasty. The needles on the cracked gauges have not moved in living memory. The valves have not been turned. The process, whatever it is, is either perfectly stable or perfectly stalled, and after a certain point those two states become indistinguishable.

The weathering is complete. Everything here is in balance. The rust has stopped advancing; the patina has closed over the bronze like a scab that has become skin. The iron is not decaying — it has reached the state where decay *is* its surface, where the oxidation is a finished thing, a color, a texture, not a process. The building and the mountain have reached an accommodation. The mountain holds the building. The building holds the mountain apart. Neither is winning. Neither is losing. The drips fall. The birds nest. The ferns unfurl. The great thing below turns, or does not turn, or turns so slowly that the distinction has lost its meaning.

No one comes here. No one maintains it. No one knows who built it, or why, or what it is for. There are no records, or the records are on the floor below the grate, or the records were eaten by the rats, or the records were written in a language that has not been spoken for ten thousand years, or the records are the machine itself and it is still writing them, slowly, in a script of heat and pressure and the chemistry of condensation, a document that will not be finished for another hundred million years, a single sentence in a book the size of a mountain.

You stand on the grate. The warmth rises through it. The drips fall. The drone holds you like a hand. The ferns breathe. The birds speak their small grey language in the rafters, and the rats move in the walls, and the pale crickets sing their wingless song, and the moss does what moss does, which is to say: it stays, it spreads, it holds, it is patient, it has nowhere else to be and no other way to be, and it is, in this warm wet deafening hollow at the top of the world, exactly and entirely at home.
