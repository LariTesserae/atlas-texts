# The Matted

## I. The Plateau

It is flat. That is the first thing, and it takes a long time to understand how flat. Not the flat of a prairie or a dry lakebed—those have relief, undulation, the memory of hills. This is the flat of something that has been worn down past memory. Once, hundreds of millions of years ago, there were mountains here. They have been reduced to their own sediment, to the suggestion of a slope so gentle that a year of walking might gain you a hand's breadth of elevation. The sky, at this altitude, is a lid. Cool air presses down. Clouds move fast and low, and when they touch the ground, which is often, the world becomes a room.

The plateau extends to the horizon in every direction—regional, valley-sized, a territory you could spend a lifetime crossing. And everywhere, under everything, there is the Matted.

---

## II. The Substrate

The Matted is the plant mass, and it is total. Not a forest, not a meadow—those imply things growing *on* something. The Matted *is* the something. It is a living skin between three and nine meters deep, resting on the ancient planed bedrock, and it is composed of so many generations of growth and compression that it has become a kind of vegetable geology. The uppermost layer is a dense, springy turf of mosses—*Sphagnum*, *Polytrichum*, and forms that don't have names—interwoven with cushion plants, liverworts, and lichens that spread in lobes the size of plates. Below that, the living tissue transitions through progressively older, denser strata: fibrous peat that still retains the shape of stems and leaves, then a half-rotted matrix where individual structures dissolve into a uniform brown-black felt, then deeper layers where the plant matter has been compressed into something like lignite, still technically organic, still technically alive at the molecular level, but ancient beyond reckoning.

You do not walk on the Matted. You walk on a particular expression of it, a surface layer that heaves and settles with the weather, that is firm in cold and springy-wet in rain. In places it has grown over obstacles and incorporated them—boulders, gullies, the remnants of older surfaces—so that the ground has a strange, lumpy topography that is not geological but organic, the shape of swallowed things.

And it is not one organism. It is millions, competing and cooperating in a matrix so dense and so old that the distinctions have blurred. A single square meter might contain dozens of species, and the roots and rhizoids of all of them have grown together into a continuous fabric. The Matted is a community that has become a substrate. The substrate has become a body.

## III. Water

Rain falls often—not heavily, but persistently, a cool misting that can last for days. The Matted drinks it. The upper moss layer saturates quickly, becoming a sponge that holds water against gravity, and the excess moves downward through the fibrous strata in a slow percolation that can take weeks. The result is that the plateau is never dry, never flooded, but perpetually at the threshold of saturation. You press your boot down and water films up around the sole. You dig a hand's depth and the hole fills.

In the slight hollows—and there are many, because the Matted grows unevenly over its swallowed topography—water collects into pools and shallow lakes. These are not blue. They are the color of strong tea, stained dark by tannins leached from the plant matter, and they are perfectly still. The surface of a pool is a mirror, and on windless days you can look down into dark water and see the sky doubled. The pools have no visible outlet. Their water leaves by seeping into the Matted, by evaporation, by the slow vascular lift of the plant mass itself—because the Matted, in some sense, is drinking them.

There are channels, too. Not natural ones. They are cut through the Matted in straight lines and gentle curves, maintained by the settlement, and they carry water from the high-central region of the plateau toward its edges. The channels are anywhere from a pace wide to twenty paces across, and their banks are reinforced with something that is not quite wood and not quite stone—more on that later. The water in them moves with purpose, and its sound is the constant background of the inhabited parts of the plateau: not loud, but everywhere, a murmuring grid.

## IV. The Settlement: Peatcut

The town is called Peatcut, though the name is older than the place and no longer quite accurate. It sits in a broad shallow basin near the center of the plateau, where the Matted is deepest and the water collects most abundantly, and it is not built *on* the Matted so much as *within* it—excavated, compressed, cultivated, and negotiated with. The settlement is spread over an area the size of a modest city, but its density is sparse: clusters of structures separated by wide gaps of open Matted, connected by the channel grid and by paths that are pressed flat and maintained by constant use. From a distance, Peatcut looks like a pattern of dark stains on the plateau surface, or like something the Matted is in the process of digesting.

The buildings are of two kinds, and the distinction between them is not always visible.

There are the *raised structures*: platforms on piles driven deep into the Matted, with walls and roofs of timber, corrugated metal, and slabs of compressed peat cut from the substrate. These are the older parts of the settlement, and they have the look of industrial practicality—box-like, multi-story, connected by walkways and conveyor frames. They are the factories, the processing plants, the engine halls. They smell of smoke and hot oil and the particular sweet-rot smell of cut peat. They are loud.

And there are the *grown structures*: shapes that have been cultivated from the Matted itself, or that have grown up around and through the older construction, or that emerged from some collaboration between human intention and vegetal habit. These are harder to describe. They are walls that are also stems—thick vertical columns of a woody, slow-growing bog plant that has been trained and grafted over decades into load-bearing shapes. They are roofs that are living turf, meters thick, supported on frames of the same semi-plant material. They are chambers that are hollows in the Matted, reinforced and shaped, their walls still living, still growing, still respiring. The grown structures are quieter, cooler, and they have a faint green light in their interiors because the walls transluce—not much, but enough to navigate by on the darkest days.

Between these two kinds of building, and within them, there is the machinery.

---

## V. The Machinery

Peatcut is industrial, and the industry is the Matted. The plant mass is harvested—cut in slabs, chipped, pressed, dried, rendered—and converted into fuel, fiber, chemical feedstock, and building material. The engines that power this processing are a mixture of the familiar and the other: diesel generators, steam turbines, conveyor systems, hydraulic presses, all of standard industrial design, but integrated into the settlement in ways that are not standard at all. An engine is not simply placed in a building; it is *set into* the Matted, mounted on a foundation of living substrate that has been cultivated around its base, and over time the substrate grows up around the mounting, incorporating it, so that after a decade the engine is held in a socket of living wood and peat as much as in bolts and brackets. This is not a failure of maintenance. It is deliberate. The Matted stabilizes the machines, dampens their vibration, regulates their temperature. The machines, in turn, provide heat and CO₂ that the Matted uses. The relationship is symbiotic, and it has been running so long that no one remembers which came first.

The factories are loud. The processing of peat is a violent business—cutting, grinding, pressing, the scream of metal on stone when a blade hits a piece of ancient wood that has been compressed to the density of coal. The engine halls produce a constant low-frequency rumble that you feel in your chest before you hear it. And over all of this, the wind: the plateau is flat and high and the wind has nowhere to go but across it, and it carries the sound of the settlement for miles, so that from any distance Peatcut announces itself as a kind of mechanical weather, a low roaring on the horizon.

## VI. The Breakdown

But there is something else, and it is not easy to point to because it is not a thing but a set of tendencies, a loosening.

The Matted is ancient beyond the lifespan of any individual organism. It has been growing, dying, and compressing for so long that its own strata have become a kind of record—not a fossil record, because the organic matter has never fully mineralized, but something more like a memory. The Matted remembers. Not in any way that can be read directly, but in its structure, in the distribution of its layers, in the way certain pockets of deeper, older material behave differently from the surrounding substrate. And the settlement, in its industrial processing of the Matted, has been cutting into this memory.

Sometimes, the cuts reveal things that should not be there.

A block of ancient compressed peat, split open in the factory, contains a piece of metal—a gear, a bracket, a length of pipe—that is not old enough to have been buried by natural processes. It is from the settlement itself. From a part of the settlement that was built a century ago, two kilometers away. It has been *relocated*, transported through the Matted by some process no one understands, and delivered to the cutting floor as if the substrate had been digesting and rearranging its contents and had now, by chance or intention, returned one.

Other times: a section of channel will be found to have changed its course overnight, not by erosion but by *growth*—the Matted has built a new bank, a new lip of vegetal material, redirecting the water flow by a degree or two. The change is subtle, but it is not random. It follows the pattern of an older channel, one that was abandoned fifty years ago, one that is not on any current map. The Matted is restoring its own infrastructure.

Or: a worker in the deep-cutting district, where the Matted is peeled back to expose the oldest strata, will report that the exposed surface is warm. Not warm from the day's sun—warm from within, at a depth where the temperature should be constant and cool. And that the warmth has a rhythm, a pulse, slow and massive, like the thermal signature of something vast breathing.

And once, twice in the recorded history of the settlement, a grown structure—a living wall, a turf roof—has been found to contain a room that no one built. It is there when the wall is opened for repair: a hollow space, meter across, perfectly spherical, its interior surface smooth and dark and *warm*, and empty except for a smell that is not the smell of the Matted but something sharper, something like ozone, like the air after lightning. The room is sealed again. The wall heals over it. No one speaks of it.

The reality of Peatcut is not stable. The boundary between the grown and the built, between the ancient and the recent, between the living substrate and the machines embedded in it, is not a boundary but a zone of exchange. Things move across it. The Matted takes the settlement's works into itself, and occasionally returns them, changed. The settlement takes the Matted into itself—cuts it, burns it, builds with it—and the Matted tolerates this, or uses it, or is slowly converting it into something else. No one knows which. No one who has asked has received an answer that satisfied them.

## VII. The Fauna

There are almost no animals. This is the strangest thing about the Matted, and the least discussed, because it is so constant that it has become invisible.

There are insects, of course—small ones, midges and gnats that rise in clouds from the pools on warm days, and beetles in the deeper peat, and something like a cricket that lives in the moss and makes a sound like a dripping tap. There are birds, rarely, passing through on migration, silhouettes against the low sky. There are small rodents in the settlement's margins, something like a vole that has adapted to the Matted and lives in the upper turf layer, surfacing only at dusk.

But there are no deer, no rabbits, no foxes, no wolves, no bears—none of the large animals that should live on a plateau this size, even a cold one. There are no fish in the pools, though the water is deep enough and cool enough to support them. There are no amphibians. The Matted is a plant kingdom, and it has no room for animals, or it has consumed them, or it has never permitted them.

Some of the older workers say that the animals are still there, but *inside*—incorporated into the substrate, their bones and teeth and genetic material distributed through the peat, not as fossils but as active components of the Matted's metabolism. That the Matted is not a plant community but a *digestive system*, and that everything that has ever lived on the plateau is still being processed. That this is why the cuts sometimes reveal impossible things. That the Matted is not just remembering the settlement's machines—it is remembering everything, and occasionally it *reconstructs* what it remembers, imperfectly, in the only material it has: itself.

No one knows if this is true. The settlement's scientists have sampled the deep strata and found DNA, yes—ancient DNA, fragmented, from dozens of species. But whether that DNA is functional, whether it is being *used*, whether the Matted is carrying out some vast slow project of resurrection or recycling or simply keeping its options open—no one knows.

The absence of animals is the silence underneath the noise. The Matted is loud with wind and water and machinery, but it is silent in the way a forest is never silent: there is no birdsong, no rustle of large things moving through undergrowth, no howl, no bark. The only voices are human and vegetal and mechanical.

## VIII. The Weather and the Rhythm

The plateau has weather, and it has cycles, and they are active and multiple and overlapping.

There is the daily cycle: cool damp mornings, mist rising from the pools, the Matted drinking the night's condensation. Midday, if the clouds break, a pale sun that does not warm so much as illuminate, turning the moss a luminous green-gold and making the pools into mirrors. Afternoon, the wind rises, always from the west, and the sound of the settlement's engines competes with the sound of the air moving across the flat land. Evening, the mist returns, and the temperature drops, and the Matted settles and creaks—a sound like a ship's timbers, like something large adjusting its weight.

There is the seasonal cycle: a brief, intense summer when the cushion plants flower—not showy, but dense, a carpet of white and yellow and pale purple that transforms the plateau's color for a few weeks—and a long, grey, wet winter when the snow comes and the Matted freezes at the surface but stays warm below, and the settlement huddles in its own smoke and the channels run black with cold water. And there is a longer cycle, measured in decades, that the inhabitants have only recently begun to notice: the Matted's surface level rises and falls. It is growing, but not evenly. In some years the turf is thick and high, and the settlement's lowest platforms are damp, and the channels run full. In other years the Matted subsides, and old foundations are exposed, and the channels need to be re-cut. The cycle is not predictable. It does not correlate with rainfall or temperature. The settlement's engineers have stopped trying to explain it and have started to treat it as a kind of slow tide, a breathing of the substrate, and they maintain their works accordingly—flexibly, redundantly, with an acceptance of being periodically overtaken.

And there is the industrial rhythm: the shifts at the factories, the convoys of pressed peat moving on the channel barges, the generators cycling up and down with demand, the periodic maintenance of the grown structures, which is less like repair and more like *pruning*, like gardening, like a negotiation. The settlement is never still. Even at night, even in the deepest cold, there are lights on the plateau, and sounds, and the sense of something working.

## IX. The Light

The light is normal, which is to say: it is the light of a cool, high, wet place, and it is changeable. There are days of flat grey overcast when the world has no shadows and the Matted stretches away in all directions as a single undifferentiated surface, and the horizon is so close and so even that it feels like the edge of a table. There are days of broken cloud when the sun comes through in columns and the plateau is divided into moving patches of gold and grey, and the pools flash. There are days of fog when visibility drops to a hundred paces and the settlement disappears and you navigate by the sound of the channels and by the feel of the Matted underfoot, which is different over the paths, different over the open substrate, different over the deep cuts.

At night, if the sky is clear, the stars are very close. The plateau is high enough that the air is thin, and there is no light pollution except the settlement's own, which is orange and localized. The Milky Way is a structure you can study. But this is rare. Most nights are clouded, and the plateau is dark, and the only light is the settlement's, and the only sound is the wind and the water and the engines, and the sense of the Matted stretching away in all directions, living, breathing, remembering, waiting.

## X. The Rules

There are rules here, though they are not written down.

You do not dig below the third stratum. The third stratum is the deepest layer of recognizable plant material; below it is the compressed lignite-like layer, the ancient memory, and the settlement's geologists say it is unstable, that it releases gas, that it is not safe. But the real reason is older and unspoken: below the third stratum, things are different. The Matted is different. The cuts that go too deep do not heal properly. The machinery that is lowered too far does not function reliably. The workers who spend too long in the deep cuts report dreams, or déjà vu, or the sensation of being watched by something that has no eyes. The third stratum is the boundary. The settlement respects it.

You do not damage the grown structures without permission. This is partly practical—they are load-bearing, they are part of the settlement's infrastructure—but partly something else. The grown structures are not just buildings. They are *arrangements*, negotiations between human intention and Matted habit, and damaging one is not like damaging a wall; it is like damaging a relationship. The grown structures respond to harm. They grow differently afterward. They develop hollows, or thicken in wrong places, or their interiors become warm in a way that is not comfortable. The settlement has learned to ask, and to wait for an answer, which comes in the form of growth patterns over days, and is interpreted by the growers, who are a distinct caste within the settlement and who speak a language of angles and rates that no one else fully understands.

You do not take animals onto the plateau. This rule is absolute and enforced. The settlement's founders brought livestock in the early years—sheep, a few cattle, chickens—and they disappeared. Not gradually, not through predation or disease, but *completely*, overnight, without traces. The Matted took them. The settlement does not test this rule anymore. The inhabitants are vegetarian by necessity, and their protein comes from the Matted itself—from the mosses, from the lichens, from a starchy tuber that grows in the deeper turf and that the growers have cultivated into something almost like a crop. The food is bland and damp and it keeps you alive.

And you do not leave the plateau. This is the oldest rule, and the one that is most often broken, and the one that is never discussed. Because the plateau is flat, and the edges are far away, and there is no wall. But the settlement's maps show the plateau as an island—not surrounded by water, but by something else, a boundary marked in the oldest documents as *the Limit*, and beyond it, nothing that anyone has returned to describe. The few who have walked to the edge and kept walking have not come back. Their absence is not mourned loudly. It is treated as a kind of weather, a loss to the Matted, like the livestock, like everything else that the substrate takes.

The settlement endures. The Matted endures. The machinery hums and the water moves and the wind blows across the flat ancient worn-down world, and the plant mass breathes, and the boundary between the living and the built remains permeable, and the past surfaces occasionally in the cuts, and no one knows what the Matted is making, or remembering, or becoming, but they live within it, and they work, and they listen, and they wait.

---

*The Matted is. The settlement is in it. The distinction is not always clear, and perhaps it never was.*
