# The Terrace

It rises from the deep water like a stepped altar — not grown, but made. A pillar of laminated aggregate, poured and cured in strata of pale grey and bone-white, lifts the whole structure a hundred meters off the abyssal floor. The pillar flares at its crown into a broad, square basin, tier on tier of ceramic decking, each step a meter high and two deep, descending inward like the rings of an inverted ziggurat. The edges are sharp. Nothing here has been rounded by weather. The corners of every terrace are crisp right angles; the ceramic is unmarked by stain or scale or the roughness of age. It looks, impossibly, as though it was finished yesterday — though it was not yesterday, and no one finished it.

The basin holds water. Warm water. The lower two-thirds of the terraces lie submerged beneath a lagoon that fills the structure's interior, held in by its own walls — a cistern the size of a hamlet, perched at the top of its pillar high above the dark sea floor. The water is warm as blood. Something in the deep still turns, still burns: geothermal taps driven into the seabed, or the structure's own buried engines, or a slow chemical warmth in the aggregate itself. Whatever the source, the lagoon never cools. Its surface is calm, barely dimpled, a disc of silver-green mirror set into the crown of the structure. Around the rim, the upper terraces stand dry and bare, their ceramic surfaces empty, pale, waiting. Beyond them there is only the open sea — warm, still, empty to every horizon. This is the only made thing in the water. This is the only land.

But the drowned tiers are not empty. They are packed. Every terrace below the waterline is built upon, shoulder to shoulder, wall to wall — a dense, saturated construction of rooms, channels, vats, engine-houses, stairwells, corridors, and shafts. There is no bare ground anywhere. The substrate itself is made: the floors are metal decking, the walls are ceramic block, the ceilings are poured aggregate ribbed with iron. The rooms are small, uniform, packed together like cells in a honeycomb. The channels between them are narrow, straight, precise — streets in a city designed without curves. The vats are round, huge, built into the terraces like wells, their rims rising above the waterline in places, their interiors dark with depth. Pipes run everywhere, along walls, across ceilings, through floors, connecting everything to everything else. Valves, wheels, gauges, hatches, wheels-within-wheels — the machinery of a place that once did something, though what it did is no longer legible. The engines themselves are deep in the lowest tiers, cold and still, their flywheels motionless, their belts hanging slack. They are built of iron and brass and something like porcelain, and they are the size of houses.

Everything is overgrown. Kelp rises from the submerged streets in dense, lush forests — tall, broad-fronded, swaying in the imperceptible currents. It grows thicker than a person's arm in places, and taller than a person, and it fills the lower tiers so completely that the streets have become green tunnels, the rooms have become green chambers. Algae mats the walls and floors in thick, velvet layers of green and bronze and rust-red. Water-weeds fill the vats to their rims, spilling over in trailing curtains. In the shallower tiers, near the waterline, the growth is so dense that it forms a continuous canopy, a green skin across the water, broken only where a chimney or a shaft or the rim of a vat pierces through. The kelp grows through windows, out of doorways, coils around pipes and railings and valve-wheels. It spills from the terraces in hanging gardens. It is the only living thing here — and it is everywhere.

There are no animals. No fish move in the warm water. No birds perch on the bare upper terraces. No insects drone in the heavy air. The lagoon is a garden without a single grazer, a forest without a single browser. The kelp sways, and nothing sways it. The water is still, and nothing breaks its surface. The silence is total, patient, and deep. The only sounds are the soft, slow creak of metal settling in the warmth, the occasional drip of water from a higher tier to a lower one, and the faint, fabric-like rustle of kelp fronds rubbing together in currents too slow to feel. The machinery does not hum. The water does not lap. The place is quiet in a way that feels deliberate, as if it were built to be quiet, or as if it has only just stopped making noise and the silence has not yet finished settling.

The light is normal — daylight, filtered through a warm, humid sky. Above the rim, the sky is pale, hazy, bright, the color of a shell. Below the waterline, the light turns green-gold, diffused, falling in soft columns through the kelp canopy. The water is thick with suspended plant matter, so visibility is short — a few meters in the lower tiers, a little more in the upper, open lagoon. Shapes loom out of the green: a wall, a vat, a forest of kelp, a stairwell descending into darkness. The upper terraces are bright, bleached, stark — the pale ceramic almost white in the sun, the shadows sharp and dark and perfectly still. The air above the rim is warm and heavy, smelling of brine and wet metal and the sweet, thick scent of vegetation — green, aquatic, faintly sulfurous from the warm depths. The water itself is warm when you put a hand in it. Bath-warm. Blood-warm. It is the most alive thing about the place.

The structure's geometry is subtly wrong. The terraces are square, but the basin they form is not — the angles shift slightly from tier to tier, the walls lean in ways that suggest the whole thing was not built to a plan but grown, or settled, or dreamed. Corridors end in blank walls. Stairwells descend into flooded rooms and do not continue. Vats are built into the floors of other vats, one inside another, like nested bowls. Doorways open onto other doorways. Shafts rise through ceilings and do not appear in the floors above. The scale is small — the whole complex would fit in a single large field — but it feels vast, because it is so dense, so packed, so saturated with construction that there is no empty space, no sky within, no horizon below the waterline. It is a room the size of a hamlet, and it is full.

Time moves slowly here. The kelp grows, but slowly — inches in a season. The water warms and cools by degrees across the year. The metal settles, the ceramic sweats, the algae thickens. The structure is patient. It was built to last, or it is still being built, or it has only just emerged — the sharpness of its edges, the freshness of its surfaces, suggest it has only just arrived, fully formed, from somewhere else. Perhaps it rose from the seabed on its pillar of aggregate, breaking the surface for the first time, already built, already flooded, already growing its kelp. Perhaps it has always been here. There is no way to tell. There is no one to ask. The sea around it is empty, and has perhaps always been empty, and the structure is the only thing that breaks the flat line of the horizon — a square, pale, stepped shape in an endless warm ocean.

The rule of the place is stillness. The rule is warmth. The rule is growth — slow, green, patient. The rule is that everything here is made, and the only thing that grows is the kelp, and the kelp grows through the made things, and in time it will cover them all. The made world and the grown world are not separate here; they are the same world, and the kelp does not distinguish between a ceramic wall and a metal pipe and a stone that was never laid. It grows on everything. It grows through everything. It is the only animal in a world without animals — the only thing that moves, and it moves so slowly. The structure does not resist it. The structure, perhaps, invited it. The warm water, the stillness, the absence of anything that would eat or trample or disturb — these are the conditions the kelp was made for, if it was made, or the conditions it found, if it came on its own. Either way, it is at home here. It is the only one at home. The engines sleep. The vats fill with green. The terraces settle, almost imperceptibly, into the warm water. And the silence holds.
