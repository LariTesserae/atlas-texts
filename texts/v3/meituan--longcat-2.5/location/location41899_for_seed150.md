The basin sits in a high fold of the mountain, a cirque carved young and sharp by ice that left only decades ago. The walls are still raw — grey stone flaking in sheets, fractures running clean and unsoftened, no soil yet in the cracks. The floor, though, is another matter. The floor has become something else entirely.

Water is everywhere and nowhere. It does not pool in lakes; it saturates. The ground is a living sponge, a mat of moss and sedge and cushion plant so dense that to step on it would be to step on a single organism the size of a village. The water moves through this mat slowly, capillaried upward, wept from leaf surfaces, condensing on the underside of the dense canopy and falling again in a constant fine rain that has no sky behind it. The air is a liquid. You do not breathe it so much as absorb it.

The plants have grown into architecture. Not by any design, but by the sheer pressure of living in this place — the cold that keeps them low and tight, the wet that lets them grow without pause, the dim light that makes every stem reach and tangle. The dominant form is the cushion: mounds of a dense, slow moss-analogue, each one a metre or two across, packed so tightly together that the gaps between them have become corridors — narrow, winding passages that turn at angles, that open into chambers, that rise and fall with the contours of the mounds. The cushions themselves are the buildings. Some have grown tall, two or three metres, their sides steep and vertical, their tops flattened into platforms. Others have merged into walls, long ridges of living matter with a density that feels masonry-like, though it is all still growing, still soft, still wet to the touch.

There are layers. The ground mat is the foundation — a continuous skin of moss and root and decomposing matter, inches thick, under which the water moves. Above it, the cushion structures rise. Between them, in the corridors, the air is still and cold and smells of chlorophyll and wet stone and something faintly sweet, like overripe fruit. Above the cushions, a sparse canopy of dwarf woody stems — some kind of willow or birch analogue, stunted to shrub height — forms a loose roof, their branches so tangled with hanging moss that the light is filtered twice, once by the cloud and once by the vegetation, leaving the corridors in a perpetual greenish twilight.

The scale is that of a hamlet. You could walk the whole of it in twenty minutes, if you followed the corridors. But the density makes it feel larger. The passages turn back on themselves. Chambers open into other chambers. Some of the cushion structures have grown into arches — not deliberately, but because two mounds met and fused, or because a stem bent under its own weight and rooted where it touched the ground, forming a natural bridge. The effect is of a settlement that has grown rather than been built, but grown with the density and complexity of a city. There are what look like streets — wider corridors where the mat has been compressed by passage, though no large animal has walked here in living memory. There are what look like walls, and towers, and courtyards where the cushions have grown around an open space, leaving a room of air in the middle of the green.

The cold is absolute and patient. It is the reason everything is small, the reason the growth is so slow — a cushion mound might grow a centimetre in a year, might be centuries old. The cold keeps the water liquid only because of the density of the plant matter, which insulates, which holds heat like a body. The place is warm, relatively, at its core — the interior of a cushion mound, where the moss is oldest and densest, is a degree or two above the air. Small creatures know this. There are insects — midges, a few beetles, something like a springtail that lives in the corridors in vast numbers but is so small and so still that you only see them when the light catches their movements. There are birds, sometimes — a small grey species that nests in the cushion tops, that feeds on the insects, that is the only large animal you are likely to see. There is a mammal, perhaps — something small and furred that leaves tracks in the mat near the water, that may be a rodent or a marsupial analogue, that is rarely seen and never heard. The food web is thin, a few strands stretched across the green.

The sounds are water and plant. The drip of condensation from the canopy. The slow creak of woody stems under their own weight. The faint rustle of moss as it grows, which is not really a sound but a vibration you feel more than hear. The wind is muffled by the walls of the cirque and the density of the vegetation; it arrives as a sigh, not a gust. Sometimes a bird calls — a single note, repeated, that echoes in the corridors and seems to come from everywhere. The place is mostly quiet, but the quiet is alive.

Weather moves through. The cloud that fills the cirque is not constant; it thickens and thins, it rises to expose the sharp rim of the basin, it lowers until the far walls are lost. When it thins, the light increases — not to brightness, but to a grey luminosity that makes the green more vivid, that reveals the wet surfaces, the sheen of water on every leaf. When it thickens, the place closes in, the corridors darken, the air becomes almost drinkable. Rain falls frequently — not heavily, but steadily, a fine mist that adds to the saturation, that keeps the water moving through the mat in a constant cycle. There are no seasons as you might recognize them; there is only the rhythm of the cloud, the slow pulse of the plant growth, the occasional storm that brings wind and a temporary clearing.

The sharpness of the terrain — the young rock, the fresh fractures — contrasts with the softness of the plant matter. The cirque walls are bare, uncolonized, too cold and too steep and too new for the moss to have reached them. They stand above the green like the ruins of something older and harder, a reminder that this place is temporary, that the ice will return or the climate will shift, that the city of cushions is a brief experiment in a long geological story. But for now, it holds. The mat spreads slowly, centimetre by centimetre, into the cracks where soil has begun to form. The cushions grow. The corridors shift as old mounds collapse and new ones rise. The water continues its slow circulation, rising, condensing, falling, rising again.

It is a place that feels made, though nothing made it. The density, the layering, the corridors and chambers and arches — all of it is the result of plants doing what plants do: reaching for light, holding water, growing and dying and growing again. The result is a settlement, a small city of living matter, bounded by sharp stone, saturated with water, held in the cold and the dim light. It has no name. It has no people. It is only itself, and it is enough.
