# Ossyn, on the Vhar Plateau

The Vhar Plateau is young — not young for a city, young for a mountain. The rock beneath Ossyn is perhaps three million years old, which means it has not yet been touched by the long patience of erosion. The cliffs are sharp enough to cut. The valleys are steep and angular, as if the land was broken rather than carved. There is no soil to speak of, only raw stone — grey and iron-red and sometimes a pale, almost luminous white that seems to hold the dim light rather than reflect it. The plateau rises from the surrounding lowlands like a clenched fist, and it is still rising. Surveyors have measured it: two centimeters per year, steady as a heartbeat. The city grows upward with the land, and the people who live here have learned to think in geological time.

Ossyn is built on this rising stone. It is a city of perhaps forty thousand souls, spread across an area that could hold four times that number. The density is low — districts of scattered buildings separated by wide empty expanses of bare rock, industrial zones where a few massive structures stand alone in the open like islands, residential areas where houses are strung along the roads like beads with long gaps between them. The city feels less like a dense urban center and more like a network of outposts connected by roads and rail lines, a civilization spread thin across a vast high-altitude wilderness. From the edge of the city, you can see the plateau extending westward for kilometers — a rolling, broken landscape of raw rock and scattered vegetation, punctuated by the distant smelter towers of other settlements, their smoke joining the perpetual cloud layer that sits above everything.

The industry is the reason for the city's existence. The Vhar Plateau's young rock is rich in minerals — iron, copper, tin, and stranger things that don't appear in any standard assay. The mines are the deepest structures in Ossyn, going down into the plateau's roots. The mine headframes are massive iron lattice structures that tower above the landscape, their wheels turning slowly, lifting cages of ore and miners from the depths. The smelters and refineries are the largest buildings in the city — brick and iron structures with tall chimneys that belch smoke into the cloud layer, their furnaces glowing orange and white and red at all hours. The factories produce materials — alloys, chemicals, processed minerals — that are shipped down from the plateau on a funicular railway that takes six hours to descend the escarpment to the lowlands. The industrial processes are slow and heavy. The smelters run for decades without stopping. The refineries process material in batches that take years. The city's economy is built on patience.

The climate is cool and damp. The plateau sits at the cloud line for most of the year, and the clouds press against the western escarpment, bringing intermittent rain and constant condensation. Everything is damp. The rock glistens. The iron of the industrial structures weeps rust-colored water that runs down the sides of buildings and pools in the streets. Mosses colonize every surface — the walls of buildings, the rails of the funicular, the sides of the mine headframes, the ledges of the cliffs. In sheltered pockets where water collects, the vegetation is lush — dense mats of moss in shades of green and gold and copper, ferns with fronds as long as a person's arm, small shrubs with dark waxy leaves, and in the warmest pockets near the smelter exhausts, stunted trees that grow perhaps a meter in a century, their trunks twisted and their bark thick and corky. The air is thin and cold, and it smells of wet stone, smoke, and something else — a sharp, metallic tang that might be the minerals in the rock or might be something stranger. The dampness gets into everything — into clothing, into bedding, into the mechanisms of machines, into the stone of the buildings themselves.

The light is dim. The cloud cover blocks the sun for most of the day, and when the clouds thin, the sun is low on the horizon, casting long shadows across the plateau. The city exists in a perpetual twilight, lit by the orange glow of the smelters, the harsh white of industrial arc-lights, and the warm yellow of domestic lamps. The light has a quality of depth — it doesn't seem to come from a single direction but from everywhere at once, as if the air itself is faintly luminous. This is one of the first signs that something is not quite right with the light on the Vhar Plateau. Shadows don't always fall where they should. The sun, when it is visible, sometimes seems to be in two places at once — a pale disc low in the west and another, fainter disc higher and to the east, both casting shadows in different directions. The city's lighting engineers have learned to compensate, but they cannot fully correct for the wrongness. They install their lamps in patterns that account for the double shadows, and they accept that some corners of the city will always be darker than they should be.

The fauna is abundant. The plateau is home to large birds — eagles, vultures, and something like a hawk but larger, with a wingspan of three meters and a call that sounds like a human scream. They circle the thermals above the smelters, where the air is warmest, riding the columns of hot exhaust with their wings locked and their eyes fixed on the ground. Insects are everywhere — clouds of small flies in the warm pockets near the industrial structures, beetles that crawl over the moss with shells that gleam like polished metal, moths that are attracted to the arc-lights and batter themselves against the glass in spiraling patterns. There are mammals too — sure-footed goats with wide hooves and thick coats that climb the cliffs with insulting ease, something like a snow leopard but with too many joints in its legs and a tail that moves independently of its body, small rodents that live in the walls of the buildings and emerge at night to scavenging among the refuse. And then there are the strange ones — the creatures that seem to be still becoming themselves, that haven't quite decided what they are. A bird that might be a mammal, with fur instead of feathers and a gait that is more loping than flying. An insect with too many wings, six or seven or eight, that moves in directions that don't correspond to the arrangement of its wings. A small, furry thing that casts a shadow of something much larger — a shadow with antlers, or wings, or too many legs. These creatures are not dangerous, exactly, but they are unsettling. They are the fauna of a place where the rules of biology are still being negotiated, where the land has not yet committed to what lives on it.

The sounds of Ossyn are loud and constant. The industrial machinery — the crushers, the smelters, the compressors, the rail yards — produces a low, continuous rumble that you feel in your chest more than hear with your ears. It is a sound that lives in the body, a vibration that becomes part of you after a few days and that you miss, strangely, when you leave the city. The wind at this elevation is strong and constant, howling through the gaps between buildings, whistling through the mine headframes, pressing against the walls with a force that makes the older structures groan. The rock itself makes sounds — cracking, settling, groaning as the plateau continues its slow rise. Deep in the mines, the rock speaks more clearly — a low, continuous mutter, as if the stone is dreaming. The animals add their voices — the screams of the large birds, the chirping of insects, the calls of the strange creatures, which are often sounds that don't correspond to the size or shape of the creature making them. The city is never quiet. Even in the deepest night, when the smelters are running at reduced capacity and the wind drops, there is a low hum — the sound of the land itself, the sound of the plateau rising, the sound of reality being negotiated.

And the reality breakdown — this is the deepest strangeness of Ossyn. The Vhar Plateau is young, and it has not yet fully committed to being real. The rock is still deciding what kind of rock it is. The laws of physics are still being negotiated. In certain districts of the city, gravity is slightly wrong — not enough to make you float, but enough to make you feel lighter or heavier than you should, enough to make a dropped tool fall at the wrong speed, enough to make your footsteps sound wrong on the stone. Distances are unreliable — the same street might be longer or shorter depending on the day, or the direction you're walking, or whether you're carrying something heavy, or whether you're alone or in a group. The surveyors have mapped these variations and produced charts that show which streets are reliable on which days, and the people of Ossyn consult these charts the way people in other cities consult weather forecasts. Buildings have interiors that don't quite match their exteriors — rooms that are larger or smaller than they should be, corridors that turn in directions that don't exist, staircases that have more or fewer steps than they did last year. The mine shafts intersect in ways that don't make geometric sense — a shaft that goes down and also goes sideways, a tunnel that connects two levels that should be separated by a hundred meters of solid rock, a chamber that is somehow larger than the mountain that contains it. The city's maps are always slightly wrong, and the wrongness shifts slowly over the years. The surveyors have learned to account for it, but they cannot fully correct for it.

The people of Ossyn have adapted. They live with the weirdness as a fact of life, the way people in other cities live with traffic or noise or weather. They know which districts have unreliable gravity and adjust their walking pace accordingly. They know which streets are longer on Tuesdays and shorter on Fridays. They know which buildings have rooms that don't match their exteriors and they use those rooms for storage or for meetings that require a certain quality of strangeness. They know which mine shafts are safe to travel and which ones should be avoided on certain days. The reality breakdown is not a disaster — it is simply the condition of life on the Vhar Plateau. It is the price of living on land that is still becoming real. The people have a dark humor about it. They tell stories about the man who walked into a building on Monday and came out on Thursday, about the woman who lived in a room that was larger on the inside than the outside and used the extra space to store her furniture, about the child who was born in Ossyn and grew up to be slightly out of phase with the rest of the world, always arriving a moment before or after everyone else. These stories are told with pride, as proof of the city's strangeness and the people's resilience.

And the industry — the mines, the smelters, the refineries — is not just extracting minerals. It is extracting certainty. The raw rock of the Vhar Plateau is rich in possibility — it has not yet decided what it is, and so it can be persuaded to be many things. The smelters melt this possibility into actuality. The refineries process uncertainty into stability. The factories produce materials that are more real than they should be — alloys that are harder, lighter, more durable than the laws of physics should allow, chemicals that react in ways that shouldn't be possible, minerals that glow faintly in the dark. The city's products are prized in the lowlands precisely because they are made from the raw stuff of becoming, refined into the definite. Ossyn is a machine for making the land real, and the people who live here are part of that machine. The miners descend into the uncertain rock and bring up ore that is still deciding what it is. The smelter workers feed it into furnaces and apply heat and pressure and time, and the ore becomes metal — definite, stable, real. The refinery workers process the metal into alloys, and the alloys are stronger and stranger than any natural material. The factory workers shape the alloys into tools and parts and components, and these products are shipped down the funicular railway to the lowlands, where they are used to build machines and structures that are more real than the land they stand on. The city is an engine of actuality, and it runs on the raw material of the plateau's uncertainty.

The pace of change is slow. The plateau rises two centimeters per year. The city grows over decades. The reality breakdown shifts over years — a street that becomes slightly longer, a building that becomes slightly more interior, a gravity that becomes slightly lighter. A person can live their whole life in the same district and notice only gradual changes, the way a person watching a glacier notices that it has moved. The vegetation grows slowly — mosses that take decades to colonize a wall, lichens that grow a millimeter per year, trees that take a century to reach knee-height. The animals live long lives — the large birds can live for a hundred years, the strange creatures seem almost immortal, their forms shifting so slowly that no one can say when a change has occurred. The industrial processes are slow — the smelters run for decades, the refineries process material in batches that take years, the mines go deeper over centuries. The city is patient. The land is patient. Everything here happens in geological time, and the people have learned to measure their lives not in years but in the slow changes they can observe — the growth of a moss colony, the shift of a street, the deepening of a mine shaft, the rising of the plateau.

The broader region is open and multi-featured. The Vhar Plateau extends for hundreds of kilometers, and Ossyn is just one settlement on it. There are other towns — mining outposts, refinery stations, agricultural settlements in the warm pockets near the smelters, a port town on the eastern escarpment where the funicular railway meets the lowland roads. There are roads connecting them, rough tracks that follow the contours of the plateau and are sometimes rerouted when the reality breakdown makes a section unreliable. There are waystations and relay towers and signal fires. The landscape is vast and varied — high peaks with permanent snow, deep valleys with rivers that have not yet carved their final courses, wide plateaus of bare rock, escarpments that drop a thousand meters in a single cliff, scree slopes that shift and settle with a sound like breathing, glacial lakes that are milky with rock flour and that sometimes glow faintly at night. The elevation is extreme throughout — the plateau averages three thousand meters above sea level, and the peaks rise another thousand meters above that. The air is thin everywhere, and the wind is strong, and the clouds are always present. The sky is vast and close, and the horizon is distant. The scale is open — you can see for kilometers in every direction, and the land extends past the horizon, feature after feature, peak after peak, valley after valley, all of it rising slowly, all of it still becoming.
