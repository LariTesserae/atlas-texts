**The Hollow**

The collapse opened the ground forty years ago, and the town has been growing inside the wound ever since.

From the surrounding grassland you would not know it was here. The rim is a line of cracked earth and exposed stone, easy to walk past. Then you look down and the world drops away — a bowl three hundred meters across, a hundred and fifty deep, with walls that terrace and fracture in irregular benches. The collapse was recent enough that the rock is still raw. No soil, no softening, no real weathering. Every surface is sharp, angular, freshly broken. You can read the geology like a ledger: bands of pale limestone, darker shale, a seam of rust-colored stone that rings the entire circumference. The mineral dust hangs in the air when the wind blows, and the smell — wet stone, faint iron, something older — rises from the opening like breath.

The town is built on these walls, from rim to floor, and there is no empty space. Every ledge has a structure. Every flat surface is built over. Buildings press against each other, share walls, stack vertically in layers that follow the natural terraces. From across the Hollow you see a continuous fabric of stone and timber, interrupted only by the stairways and passages that cut through it, and by the occasional gap where a rockfall has taken a structure with it. The density is total. There is no gap between presences. The town is the wall and the wall is the town.

The construction is stone and wood and simple iron, all hand-worked. The walls have been quarried — you can see the squared recesses where blocks were extracted, the tool marks still crisp — and the stone used to build upward and outward from the natural ledges. Timber comes from above: rafters, joists, scaffolding, hauled down by rope and pulley. Iron is used sparingly — hinges, brackets, tool heads, the fittings that must hold. Most of the work is done with chisel and wedge and saw. The sound of it is sharp but brief: a ring, a scrape, a call from a scaffold. Then the stone takes the sound and the Hollow goes quiet again.

The work is ongoing. Scaffolding clings to sections of wall. Piles of fresh-cut stone sit on ledges, waiting. Half-finished structures stand open to the air, their roofs absent, their window openings empty squares. The town is expanding into the raw space as fast as the space allows. New ledges are quarried, new terraces built up from the debris, new structures wedged into crevices that were empty a season ago. There is a sense of a place that is still being born — not a ruin, not a restoration, but a construction that has not yet finished its first act.

Water is everywhere, though it does not flow in rivers. It seeps from the walls — the lower sections are always damp, the stone dark with it, the surface slick. It condenses on cool stone, beads, drips. Rain falls through the opening and runs down the terraces in sheets, finding the channels that have been cut to direct it, pooling on the floor in a network of shallow basins and mud. Everything is slightly wet. Wood is perpetually damp. Iron rusts quickly. Moss grows on walls that never see direct sun, a thin green film that thickens in the crevices where seepage is constant. The sound of dripping is the constant, quiet percussion of the place — irregular, patient, absorbed so completely by the stone that it seems to come from nowhere and everywhere at once.

The channels are the town's veins. Cut into the stone of the terraces, they direct the seepage and the rainwater into cisterns and settling pools on the floor. They are maintained constantly — cleared of debris, repaired where the rock has shifted, extended as the town grows. Every household on every terrace is responsible for the section that passes its ledge. This is one of the unwritten laws of the Hollow: you maintain your channel, or the water finds its own way through your floor. The channels are shallow, precise, and everywhere. They click and murmur as they carry the water down, and their sound is as much a part of the town as the voices of its people.

The light comes from above. The opening is wide enough that normal daylight reaches the floor for several hours each day — a moving patch of brightness that climbs the walls as the sun travels, illuminating first the eastern terraces, then the western, leaving the floor in a dim, reflected glow. The rim is brightest. The mid-walls live in partial shadow. The floor is always cool and dim, lit by the open sky overhead but never directly. This rhythm governs everything. People arrange their days around it — when their ledge gets sun, when the light reaches the lower terraces, when the floor brightens enough to work. A terrace that gets two hours of direct sun is prized. A terrace that gets none is for storage, or for those who cannot afford better.

The air is cool. The mass of surrounding rock holds the temperature down, and the dampness makes it feel cooler still. Breath does not mist, but it could. The lower walls are cold to the touch. In the warmest months the Hollow is a refuge from the heat of the grassland; in the cold months it is a trap for cold air, and the floor stays frigid while the rim freezes and thaws in the open wind. The temperature shifts as you descend — a degree or two for every ten meters — and the people who live on the lower terraces dress for it, and complain about it, and will not move upward because the rent is cheaper and the water is closer.

Life is sparse but present. Mosses colonize the damp walls, especially in the light zone — the thin green film that thickens in the crevices where seepage is constant. Lichens spot the upper walls where it is drier, pale and crusty, the first dry-land colonists. Ferns grow in the wettest fractures, small and tough, their fronds dark with moisture. Insects are the most visible fauna: spiders that hunt the ledges, cave crickets that chirp in the dark corners, beetles that scuttle through the damp debris on the floor. Bats roost in the higher fractures and overhangs, emerging at dusk in quick, silent flights. Birds — swifts, mostly — nest on the rim and sometimes drop into the bowl to hunt the insects that rise on warm air. A few hardy weeds have taken root on the floor where sediment has accumulated in pockets, and someone has planted a small garden there, in raised beds, using the soil that is slowly, slowly forming. The garden is a point of pride. It is the only place in the Hollow where things grow in the ground.

The floor is the working heart of the Hollow. It is where the water collects, where the quarries are, where the waste goes, where the cultivation happens in the thin soil pockets. It is dim and damp and busy. The channels converge here, feeding cisterns and settling pools. The quarries are cut into the lowest walls — fresh stone, easy to extract, hauled up by rope and pulley to the terraces above. The waste is managed carefully; the Hollow is a closed system, and what goes down stays down until it is carried up. The floor is uneven: boulders from the collapse, smaller debris, areas of mud and standing water. In heavy rain the floor floods shallowly, and the water finds its way through the drainage channels or pools in the lowest basins. The sound of running water is louder here, and the air is thick with moisture, and the smell is stronger — wet stone, organic decay, the faint sharpness of mineral.

The Hollow is not a quiet place, but it is a muted one. The stone absorbs sound. Voices carry a short distance and stop — you hear your neighbor clearly, but not the next terrace over. The ring of a chisel is sharp but brief. The drip of water is the most persistent sound, a quiet, irregular rhythm that fills the silence between other noises. Wind through the opening creates a low hum or a whistle, depending on its direction and strength. Occasionally the rock itself makes a sound — a sharp crack, a low groan, the rattle of small stones shifting. These are the sounds of a place that is still settling. Rockfalls happen, not often, but often enough that everyone knows the sound and knows to look up. Fresh cracks appear in the walls. Dust falls. The Hollow is still becoming.

There are rules that are not written down. Do not build on a fresh crack. Maintain your channel. Look up when you hear the rock groan. Do not quarry the ledge beneath someone else's foundation. Do not block a passage; the passages are the streets, and a blocked passage is a blocked life. These are the rules of a place that is not yet finished, where the ground is not yet trustworthy, where the town and the geology are still negotiating their boundaries. The people who live here know this. They build knowing that the rock may shift, that a wall may crack, that a ledge may fall. They build anyway. They build because the Hollow is theirs, because it is shelter and stone and water, because it is the place they have made inside the raw geology.

Moving through the Hollow is a vertical experience. The streets are stairs — stone steps cut into the terraces, worn smooth in the centers by traffic. Bridges span the gaps between structures, some simple planks, some stone arches. Passages cut through buildings where the terraces are too steep to climb directly. Rope ladders and pulleys handle goods that cannot be carried on the stairways. The densest sections are near the mid-walls, where the most people live and where the construction is oldest and most layered. Here the passages are narrow, the ceilings low, the walls damp and moss-furred. Light comes from openings cut through the roofs above, or from the occasional window — a small square of brightness in the stone. The air is cool and still and smells of wet stone and woodsmoke and the faint, mineral tang of the rock.

The rim is different. Here the structures are newer, more spread out, with access to the surface and the open sky. Gardens grow on the rim — real gardens, in soil, with sun. The stairways begin here, descending into the Hollow. Goods come down from the surface: timber, food, tools, news. Waste goes up. The rim is the boundary between the world above and the world below, and it is busy with the traffic between them. From the rim, looking down, you see the whole of the Hollow — the terraced walls, the packed construction, the channels glinting with water, the moving patch of sunlight on the floor. It looks like something grown, something organic, something that has always been there. It has not. It is forty years old. The rock is still settling. The town is still growing. The water is still finding its paths. The soil is still forming. The moss is still spreading. Everything is in process, nothing is settled. The Hollow is not a finished place, and neither are the people who live in it. They are all — the rock, the water, the town, the lives — still becoming.
