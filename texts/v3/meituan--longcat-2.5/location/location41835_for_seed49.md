# Morvane, the Reef-City

You come to it from the rim, down a long slope of worn stone steps, and the smell reaches you first: warm wet mineral, shell and salt and something alive, like a tide pool at noon. Then the sound — a constant roar, not unlike standing beneath a waterfall, though you cannot yet see the water. Then the sight: the basin opens, and Morvane fills it, tower and wall and canal, to every horizon. It is white and cream and the color of old bone, streaked here and there with rust or amber or black where the mineral water has stained it. There is no green anywhere. Nothing grows here that you would call a plant. The city is all mineral, all animal, all water.

The basin is a continental depression, below the level of the sea that rings the world, held back by the city's own outer walls and by the reef that *is* the city. The water is everywhere — it is the floor of the place. Streets are canals, some narrow as corridors, some broad as lakes; buildings rise from the water on arches, on stilts, on great pedestals of accreted stone. Where the ground sits above the water it is only barely — a step up, a platform, a bridge. The water is warm, fed by deep vents and by the sheer heat of the city's life; it is thick with minerals, sometimes clear, sometimes milky with calcium, sometimes clouded with the plankton-like drift that feeds half the city.

Rain comes almost every day, warm and sudden, and the city is built for it. Every roof is a collector, every wall a gutter; water runs in threads down the facades, beads and drips from every cornice, every bridge, every ledge. The stones weep. Stand in any alley and you will hear the drip and trickle, the small splash, the run — water is never silent here. The city makes its own weather: heat and moisture rise from the basin, meet the cooler air off the sea, and the rain falls, and the mists form, and the winds come funneling between the towers. It has weathers you can name — the warm rain, the white fog, the "silver hour" when the haze turns the sun to a coin — and weathers that belong only to Morvane, like the "weeping wind" that drives horizontal spray for hours.

There are no plants. No grass, no trees, no moss in the way you would expect. What grows instead is simpler: films, crusts, mats. In the damp shade of the lower districts, where the sun never reaches, there are fungal sheets — pale, rubbery things that crawl up the walls and digest the stone's loose minerals — and slime molds that pulse along the canal edges in yellow and orange veins. Microbial crusts paint the lower walls in bands of black, gray, and rust where the water lingers. That is the vegetation of Morvane: fungus and slime and film, and the great animal-reef the city is made of.

The stone is grown, not cut. It is accreted — layer on layer of calcium carbonate, of shell, of mineral precipitated from the warm water, laid down by the builders. The builders are the size of a dog, many-legged, plated, patient; they crawl over the city in their millions, tending the stone, drinking the mineral slurry, extruding the reef. They are animals, not machines, but they are the city's masons. The people of Morvane garden them. They seed structures with slurry and with the chemical cues that tell the builders where to build; they cull the blooms; they guide the growth the way a gardener guides a hedge. A tower in Morvane is not raised; it is grown, over decades, and it grows still. A bridge is not built; it is coaxed. The city is a garden of animals and stone, and it is never finished.

Because the builders are alive, the city is alive in a loose, distributed way. It heals. Break a wall and the builders come, within hours, to patch it — you can hear them, a dry rustling, a clicking, like rain on a tin roof, as they work. The city grows toward water and toward food; it thickens where the canals run rich; it thins where the water is poor. It responds, slowly, to damage and to need, the way a reef does. Whether it is aware, the people of Morvane disagree about; but they agree that it is old, that it has moods, and that you do not damage it lightly.

It is old. Morvane has been growing for longer than anyone can say — the layers of the oldest towers are counted in thousands of years, fine as pages, each a season's growth. And it is worn. The water has rounded every edge; the wind has smoothed every face; the stone is polished by feet and by constant spray. The city looks settled, in balance, as if it has reached its final form — which it probably has, in the way that a reef has. It will keep growing, but it will keep being Morvane.

The sound is the thing you cannot get used to. It is deafening — a constant roar, the sum of water and wind and life. The canals rush; the weeps patter; the rain drums on ten million roofs; the wind howls between the towers; the builders rustle and click; the fish jump; the birds — there are birds, or things like birds, shell-winged and loud — scream from every ledge; the insects drone in clouds; the people shout over it all; the markets roar; the pumps thump; the whole city hums, a low note you feel in your chest and your teeth. There is no quiet place in Morvane. Even at night, when the light fails and the canals glow faintly with the luminescence of the drift, the roar goes on. You learn to sleep in it the way you learn to sleep on a ship.

The light is ordinary — sun and sky, day and night — but the city makes it strange. The white stone reflects it; the water reflects it; the haze diffuses it; the towers cast long bands of shade across the canals. The city is bright, often glaring; the sun on the white stone at noon is blinding, and people carry veils and shade-cloths. At night the canals glow — the drift in the water is luminous, and some of the builders' handiwork is faintly lit too — so the city after dark is a black mass shot through with green-gold threads. But mostly it is daylight, warm daylight, the sun standing over the basin, the rain falling in bright sheets, the mist glowing.

The fauna is abundant — the city is an animal world. The canals are full of fish, of eel-like things, of shelled grazers, of filter-feeders that wave in the current; the water is thick with the drift that feeds them. The towers are full of flyers — the shell-winged birds, the gliders, the insects in their clouds; ledges are stacked with nests, and the droppings of a thousand generations have painted the stone white. Scavengers are everywhere — crab-like things in the lower canals, rat-like things in the walls, something that is always eating the city's leavings. There are predators, too, in the deep canals and the dark lower districts, and there are the people's creatures — domesticated builders, messenger-gliders, haulers. The people themselves, of course, are part of the abundance. The city is crowded with life, packed with it, every niche filled.

And it is packed with people. Morvane is dense — there is no empty space. Every platform holds a market or a workshop or a house; every bridge is a street; every canal is a road; the towers rise in layers, floor upon floor, and the gaps between them are spanned with bridges and awnings and ropewalks. The city is layered vertically, horizontally, in depth — the lower districts at the waterline, the middle city above, the high towers and the platforms and the roofs, and below it all the undercity, the drowned levels, the old city beneath the water, where the builders still work and the light does not reach. You can travel for days in Morvane and not cross the same canal twice. It is a continent of a city; it has its own weather, its own distances, its own maps. A district is a country. The horizon is towers.

The markets are the city's pulse. They swarm and migrate — a bazaar that never closes, that moves through the canals on raft-stalls and climbs the towers on rope-ladders. You can buy fish still twitching, shell by the basket, fungal bread, drift-paste, mineral slurry in skins, shade-cloths, veils, builder-cues, polished stone, second-hand wings, maps of quarters that will be wrong by the season. The people shout their prices into the roar; the stalls crowd shoulder to shoulder; the canals are jammed with boats; the bridges are solid with bodies. It is anthill-energy, bazaar-energy, a churn of buying and selling and eating and building and moving that never stops.

And it is never still. It is turbulent — it churns. Districts grow; districts die. A bloom of builders will raise a wild quarter in a season, and the people will move in, and the market will follow, and then the water will shift, and the quarter will thin and sag and be reclaimed. Buildings collapse — not often, but often enough; the city heals, but it also forgets. The markets swarm and migrate; the population churns; the canals are dug and silted and dug again; the rain comes and the floods come and the water rises and falls. The city is in a state of perpetual becoming, like a storm that never breaks. And yet — it is old, and worn, and in balance. The turbulence *is* the equilibrium. Morvane has been churning like this for longer than memory, and it will churn like this after.

The technology of the place is beyond any machine-shop. The city is grown, not made; the builders are livestock and labor both; the water is moved by engineered currents and by living pumps — great filter-feeders, bred and trained, that drive the canals; the stone can be softened with a chemical wash and re-set, so a wall can be "poured" like wax and left to harden; the towers are shaped to sing in the wind, and the people read the city's mood in the notes; the reef itself is a slow computer of sorts, the builders' chemical signals passing through it, so the city "knows" — in a dim, slow way — where it is hurt and where it is whole. It is a wet technology, a biological technology, an engineering that current physics does not quite describe, because it is engineering that is also husbandry, also gardening, also music.

And the rules of the world, such as they are: The water must move. If the canals stagnate, the city sickens — the drift dies, the builders sicken, the smell turns, and the people must open the sluices and let the sea in, or the rain, or the deep vents. The builders must be fed — the mineral slurry, the cues, the tending; a neglected quarter will thin and crumble. The city must be damaged, a little, regularly — it heals toward wholeness, and a quarter that is never touched will overgrow, will thicken into a maze, will become unlivable. So the people prune it, flood it, break it, and it grows back, and the balance holds. It is a managed wildness. It is a domesticated reef. It is a city that is also an organism, and the people are its symbiotes, its gardeners — some say its organs.

You stand on a bridge in Morvane, in the warm rain, in the roar, in the glare of the white stone and the glint of the water, and you understand that you are inside something alive and old and ceaselessly making itself. The drip of the weeps, the rustle of the builders, the scream of the shell-wings, the drum of the rain, the hum in the stone. The city breathes. The city grows. The city is never finished, and it has never been otherwise.
