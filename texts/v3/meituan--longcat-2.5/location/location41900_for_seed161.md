# The Hum

The rim is not a dramatic thing. After a day's walk across the high plain — thin air, cold wind, the ground worn down to a sheet of dark polished stone and gravel — you come to a gentle fold in the earth and cross it without knowing, and the world changes.

Below you: a bowl, perhaps a mile across, its floor a few hundred feet down, its walls sloping in smooth, slow curves. The bowl is full of fog. Not clouds above a landscape — the fog *is* the landscape, a still, slow-churning sea that fills the bowl to within a stone's throw of the rim, so that the walls seem to rise out of a grey-white ocean. The air that flows over the rim is cold and thin; the air in the bowl is warm, thick, wet, heavy with the smell of wet stone and green growing things and, faintly, sulfur.

The people of the bowl call their home "the Hum," after the sound of it.

Everything here is old beyond measuring, and worn to its final shape. The bowl is the remnant of an ancient volcano — or something like one — grinding down since before there were eyes to see it. There are no sharp edges anywhere. The walls are smooth, flowing curves, like a pot thrown on a wheel; the boulders that litter the slopes are pillow-shaped, soft-looking, though they are stone; the rock itself is dark and full of tiny holes, and it rings when you strike it — a dull, deep bell-note you feel in your teeth. Underfoot, everything is springy with moss. No one in the bowl has ever seen a jagged mountain or a cliff-edge. Their word for "sharp" means *freshly broken*: a chipped blade, a snapped branch, a bone just fractured.

There is no water in the bowl. No streams, no pools, no springs. The rock is too porous and the floor too warm; anything that falls is drunk by the stone or steamed away within hours. And yet the place is drenched. All its water is in the air.

The fog is the water. On most days it pours over the rim — a slow, silent cascade of cloud spilling down the walls and joining the sea below. Everything in the bowl is built to drink from it. The mosses have no roots so much as meshes; they sponge moisture straight from the air. The trees — stunted, broad-canopied, wind-shaped — have leaves that channel condensation along their veins to their cores. The people have learned the same trick at their own scale: they hang nets.

Fog-nets are the great infrastructure of the place. Coarse mesh of twisted moss-fiber, strung between poles on the rim terraces and in the tree-canopies, sometimes covering whole acres of slope, a filigree of droplets in the grey light. The drops bead, coalesce, run down into stone troughs, and from there into cisterns — chambers cut into the soft rock, lined, covered with stone lids, cool and dark. A good net on a good fog-night fills a cistern. The water tastes of stone and smoke and something faintly metallic. It is the only water anyone has ever known.

The bowl is upholstered. That is the word. Every surface carries a layer of green a handspan deep or deeper. Cushion mosses in a dozen varieties — grey-green, yellow-green, the deep emerald of wet shade — form continuous mats over the stone, so that the ground is a springy raft and the shapes beneath it are only suggestions. Ferns grow everywhere the moss thins: tall, arching fronds that uncurl constantly in the warm wet air, so that the floor of the bowl is a prairie of ferns up to the waist, and on the steeper walls they hang in curtains, and when the wind moves them it is like watching slow green water.

The trees are the bones of the place. Low, broad, twisted by the wind that funnels over the rim, every branch is itself a garden — moss, ferns, lichens, and small pale flowers that drink through their leaves and never touch soil. Nothing here has thorns. Nothing has poison that matters. There is no winter and no dry season; the growing never stops. A fallen branch does not rot so much as get absorbed, swallowed by the moss within a season, and the tree's ghost lives on as a ridge in the green.

Insects are the base of everything. The air is thick with them — midges in their thousands, moths with wings like hands hanging in the ferns, beetles the size of thumbs in metallic greens and browns crunching through the litter of dead fronds. The birds eat the insects, and there are birds everywhere: small, mostly dark-plumaged so they vanish in the fog, and almost all of them calling — whistles, clicks, gurgles, and one particular harsh alarm note that rings through the whole bowl when something large moves below. They nest in every eave and cliff-pocket, and the sound of them is the clock by which the people tell the hours.

At dusk the bats come out in ribbons from caves high on the walls, and the birds explode upward in a panic-and-feed, and for a quarter-hour the air is thick with wings. Then the bats stream away over the rim, the birds settle, and the insect hum returns.

The herds are woolbacks: small, rotund, four-legged, broad-footed, covered in a dense grey-brown fleece. They graze the moss lawns, their padded feet spreading their weight so they do not sink. They are gentle, incurious, and they wander the terraces of the settlement as freely as the lanes. Their wool is the fiber; their dried dung is the fuel; their milk is drunk fresh. Their bells — copper, green-patinated — are part of the soundscape.

And the sound. The bowl is loud. The first thing you notice is the bed-note: the wind pouring over the rim, a low constant drone that you feel in your sternum more than hear. Over that, the insect hum — a high, steady tone that rises and falls with the hour. Over that, the birds. And then the punctuations: the hiss of steam vents, the irregular deep *whump* of a fumarole opening somewhere in the dark, the drip of condensation from a thousand nets, the grind of stone mills, the bellows of the forge, voices, the bleat of woolbacks, the clatter of a loose pot, the flapping of wings as a flock crosses a lane.

The shape of the bowl holds sound. Everything is audible. The people speak loudly, sing at their work, and sleep through what would elsewhere be noise — it is the silence that wakes them. Once in a long while the wind drops, the rim goes quiet, and the bowl falls silent for the first time in living memory. This is "the quiet," and it is considered ill-omen, and the people go about their work with their ears pricked, waiting for the Hum to return.

The sky is the fog. There is no sun, no stars, no horizon — the fog diffuses everything to a pearl-grey light with no direction, so that there are no shadows, or only the faintest darker patches beneath the trees. Colors are muted: greens, greys, browns, the black of wet stone, the pale cream of unfurling fern. The light barely changes from hour to hour. The people tell time by the birds and by the rhythm of the vents, not by the sun.

At night the darkness is nearly total — the fog keeps what little light there is — and the bowl becomes a scatter of lamps: stone dishes burning rendered fat, hearths behind open doors, and, in the deepest shade of the moss forest, the faint green glimmer of glow-moss, a lichen that phosphoresces when the air is warm and wet. The glow is faint; you navigate by it only if you know the paths.

The village terraces the gentler eastern slope, climbing from the fern-prairie floor to within a few hundred feet of the rim. It is dense. Houses of dark volcanic stone, fitted without mortar or with a crude ash-mortar — and because nothing here is sharp, the walls are rounded, the corners nonexistent, the doorways arched or square but worn soft at the edges. They climb in tiers, sharing walls, some carved directly into the soft rock of the slope. The roofs are living: a layer of soil and moss, so that from the rim above, the village is a green cascade, indistinguishable from the forest, broken only by the smoke of the vents and the poles of the fog-nets.

Everything is connected by stairs. The steps are worn concave by generations of feet, and in the busy lanes they are polished like river-stone. There are no carts — the lanes are too steep, too stepped — everything is carried on backs, in baskets, up and down the stairways, all day. The great fog-nets hang on the rim terraces above the village, vast meshes strung between poles like the webs of an enormous spider, dripping steadily into the troughs. Below them, the cisterns: chambers cut in the rock, cool, dark, covered with stone lids. The village's wealth is measured in cisterns.

The forge-quarter lies near the fumarole field on the southern slope, where the ground is hot enough to warm the forges without fuel. Copper is the only metal — soft, green-patinated, worked cold or cast in clay molds. Knives, needles, the woolbacks' bells, ornaments. Everything else is stone, bone, wood, fiber. Obsidian flakes for the fine blades. Stone axes, hoes, grinding slabs. Loom-frames of bone and wood. Pots of the local clay, fired in dung-fires, glazed with ash.

The people keep hive-baskets in the eaves — fat, docile bees that work the pale epiphyte flowers — and the honey is the one luxury: a sharp, pale mead, drunk at the festivals, used to seal agreements, offered to the dead.

The day begins not with light but with the birds. Work: tending the nets, clearing the troughs of moss, hauling water up the stairs, cutting fern-fronds for the herds, grinding dried fern and moss into flour on the stone slabs, weaving at the looms, repairing the roofs, checking the nets for tears. The village is never still. At the same time, nothing is urgent. There is no word for "hurry" in the language. A task that takes the afternoon takes the afternoon; a house that takes a generation takes a generation.

The dead are returned to the warm earth of the fumarole slopes — wrapped in moss-fiber, laid in shallow graves where the ground-heat and the mineral breath dry them to leather and bone. The graveyard is a garden, warm even in the damp, and the old sit there in the evenings, among the stones and the warm hissing vents, and talk to the dead.

The place is old and slow. The fog thickens over weeks and thins over weeks; the vents shift over years — a new hiss opens somewhere on the slope, an old one seals itself with mineral crust. The moss grows a finger-length in a year. A house is raised across a generation, and rebuilt the same way when it sinks. The cisterns are cleaned on a generational cycle, and the cleaning is a festival. The people's calendar is marked by the flowering of one particular epiphyte — a pale star-flower that blooms for three days once every two years — and by the bat migration, and by the deep pulsing of the greatest vent, the Lung, which has a rhythm of its own, a slow breath of years.

Three rules, the children learn:

Water is air. Nothing is sharp. Sound is communal — what you do, everyone hears; and what everyone does, you hear.

At night, from the rim, the bowl is a field of faint lights in a sea of fog — the lamps, the hearths, the glow-moss — and the fog itself seems to hold the light and give it back, so that the whole bowl glows faintly, a living thing breathing in the dark, with the low drone of the wind over the rim and the thousand small sounds of the wet warm dark, and the nets dripping, and the birds turning in their sleep.
