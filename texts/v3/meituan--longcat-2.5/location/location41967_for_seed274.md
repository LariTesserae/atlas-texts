**The Weeping Hall**

You come to it by a throat. The entrance is a low crack in a hillside, or a sinkhole half-hidden in a fold of the land — it doesn't matter, because the approach is always a descent. The passage is a tube of smooth stone, worn to the softness of a river stone, and the air rises to meet you before you see anything: warm, wet, tasting of chalk and metal. Your breath sounds loud. The walls weep a continuous fine film; water is not here a stream or a drop but a condition, a state of things. The stone is the color of old bone, of cream, of rust where iron has bled into the calcite. Everything is rounded. There are no sharp edges anywhere; the place has been worked by water for longer than people have existed, and it has reached a state where water no longer cuts, only polishes.

The hall sits low — below the water table, below the land above, in a warm basin of the earth. You descend to it; the air grows warmer as you go down. The throat opens, and you are inside.

It is a single chamber, the size of a large room or a small cathedral — thirty paces across, perhaps, with a ceiling that is low at the edges and lifts at the center to a narrow rift high above. The hall is the whole of the place; there is nothing else. The rift is the only source of light, and it gives a soft, diffuse, warm illumination, the color of late afternoon, scattered by the mist that hangs in the chamber at a fixed height — steam from the warm pool, held in equilibrium. The light does not make shadows so much as a general dim glow, brighter in a pale column under the rift and fading to deep brown shadow at the walls. The mist never rises and never falls. It hangs.

The chamber is packed. There is no empty space. Every surface is occupied by stone that has grown in slow cascades — flowstone draperies, stalactites, stalagmites fused into columns, rimstone pools stacked one above another like a crowded city of shallow bowls. The formations are cream and ochre and rust and grey, banded with the mineral history of the water. They are fully grown. Nothing here is young. The floor is a jumble of flowstone mounds and low walls; passage threads between them, worn smooth by water and by feet. You must walk carefully; the stone is wet and slick, and there is no dry place to put a hand.

At the center is the pool.

It is still, warm, and perfectly level, and it is the brightest thing in the chamber because it reflects everything — the formations, the mist, the pale column of the rift. The chamber is doubled in its surface; it seems to extend downward into an identical chamber below, and for a moment you cannot tell which is the real. The pool's bottom is visible through water that is heavy with dissolved stone — a pale sand, a drift of cave pearls, smooth stone. The water is so still that the reflection is exact.

And into the pool falls the drip.

It is the only motion in the hall, and it is perfectly regular. A drop forms at a point on the ceiling, swells, falls, and strikes the water with a sound like a finger touching a drum — soft, close, intimate. Then stillness. Then, after an interval you can count, another. The interval never varies. The drip is the chamber's heartbeat, and it is a heartbeat so slow and so constant that it feels like stillness. The pool's surface rings faintly with each drop and then returns to its mirror. A few tiny translucent fish, pale as water itself, rise to the ripples and then sink back. There is one larger fish, old and slow, that hangs in the center of the pool and barely moves. It was here before the hearth was built. It is the oldest thing in the hall. In a crevice at the pool's edge, a pale crab, the size of a coin, withdraws into the dark. Near the rift, a bat sometimes hangs in the shadow, and a pale cricket sits on a ledge and does not sing.

The air is warm — bath-warm, stone-warm, water-warm. There is no cold corner; the whole chamber is one temperature, and has been for as long as anyone knows. The warmth comes from the earth, not the light. Breathing is like breathing warm vapor; your clothes dampen; metal fogs at once. The smell is wet stone, hot rock, a faint edge of sulfur, and nothing organic — no soil, no leaf, no rot. The air tastes of minerals, clean and slightly metallic. It is the air of a place that has been washed for eons.

Water is everywhere, but it is not falling, not flowing, not rushing. It is present as a film on every surface, as a slow sheet that moves down the walls without sound, as condensation that beads on the ceiling and does not drop (except the one drip), as mist in the air, as the pool, as the saturated atmosphere itself. The chamber is at the edge of liquid. Everything is wet. Everything has been wet for so long that dryness is unimaginable.

There are no plants. No moss, no fern, no algae — only, near the light shaft, a faint stain on the stone that might be a film of something simpler than life, a mineral whisper of biology, barely alive and barely visible. The world here is mineral. The stone is the only organism, in the loose sense that it grows, though it grows so slowly now that it has stopped.

For the chamber is finished.

This is the rule of the place, and it is the thing you feel before you understand it: the chamber is in equilibrium. Water seeps in through the porous stone at exactly the rate it seeps out. Heat enters from below and leaves through the rift at exactly the balance. The water is saturated with dissolved mineral — it holds exactly as much as it can, so it deposits nothing and dissolves nothing. The formations neither grow nor shrink. The pool neither rises nor falls; it drains through a hidden channel at exactly the drip's rate. The mist neither thickens nor thins. Nothing is added. Nothing is lost. Time passes, but the place does not change. It is a completed world, a held breath, a sentence that has reached its period and does not need another word.

And because nothing changes, everything is preserved.

On a dry ledge near the pool — dry only in the sense that it is above the seep line — there is a ring of blackened stones, a hearth, small and old. The soot is still on the ceiling above it, a faint dark stain. Beside it, a grinding stone, its surface worn to a shallow bowl. A few flaked tools of dark stone, their edges still sharp, left where they were dropped. A clay basin, patched and re-patched with clay of a slightly different color, as if someone repaired it more than once. A bronze bowl, green with verdigris, holding a little of the pool's water. A stone bench, worn smooth by sitting. A cairn of flat stones, stacked and settled. Carved steps lead down to the pool's edge, worn to gentle curves by feet that have not come for a long time. On a flowstone wall, a faint ochre hand stencil — a hand, pressed and blown, the pigment now a pale ghost of itself. A scatter of small bones near the hearth, too small to say what they were. And on a ledge, a single hand-axe, perfect, its edge still keen, set down as if its owner meant to return in a moment.

The makers are gone. The chamber does not notice. The hearth's soot does not fade; the bronze does not corrode further; the clay does not soften; the stencil does not flake. The wetness that would destroy them is held in equilibrium, and so they remain, suspended in the finished place. The fish hangs in the pool. The drip falls. The mist hangs. The stone weeps its fine film. Everything is exactly as it was, and exactly as it will be.

The sounds of the hall are few and close: the drip, the faint lap of the pool against its rimstone lip, the soft sheet-sound of water moving on stone, a low warm hum that might be the earth or might be your own blood. The chamber absorbs sound; voices are swallowed; a word spoken aloud dies at arm's length. It is a good place for silence. It is a good place to sit on the worn bench and watch the drip and the fish and the doubled chamber in the pool, and to understand that the place is not waiting for anything. It is not waiting. It is complete.

You leave by the same throat. The drip continues. The fish hangs. The mist hangs. The hall keeps its perfect balance, as it did before you and as it will after, and the water holds its long breath, and the stone is warm, and the dark is soft, and the hall is finished.
