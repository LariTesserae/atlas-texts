# The Roaring

From anywhere on the plain, the horizon is a near-perfect ring. You stand only a few arm-lengths above the sea, and the land has been worn down for so long that almost nothing interrupts the view except the haze. The light comes from everywhere and from nowhere — a thick ceiling of cloud the colour of skimmed milk, with the sun a slightly paler smudge that takes hours to cross it. No shadows here. Everything is silvered: the mud, the water, the wet faces of rock, your own outstretched hand.

The plain is a river's end and the land's end. A hundred shallow channels cross it, dividing and rejoining around bars of grey gravel — too many to name, too faint to map. A day's rain moves them; a season rewrites them. Twice a day the sea breathes across the whole region. The tide is only a hand's breadth high, but the land is so level that the waterline walks miles inland with it. Channels that ran one way at dawn run the other way by dusk. A gravel bar you walked at noon is an island by evening. The people read the water the way other peoples read the sky.

Rain is not really the right word for what falls here. It is a suspended drizzle, a thickening of the air, a hiss on miles of water that never quite stops. Mist comes and goes in slow banks. Moisture condenses on everything — on hides, on stone, on eyelashes. Even the rock weeps: the one high place on the plain, a low whaleback of banded ancient stone, is wet from a dozen seeps, its flanks streaked white and ochre with minerals, glazed with slick film. Breath steams. The cold is damp and steady and patient; frost is rare enough to be an event.

* * *

Water is the place's first element. Sound is its second, and the sound is, by any ordinary measure, deafening.

First there is the rain's hiss on the flat water, and the wind — wind with no obstacle for a day's walk, a steady unbroken push you feel in the chest. Then the water itself: the gurgle and grumble of channels shifting their courses, the distant low rumble of the tide turning. Then the mud. In places it *booms* — a deep drum note when a slab of it settles, or when your weight lifts off it. The plain is always settling, always adjusting its infinite small angles to the weight of water. At the lowest spots, gas seeps up through the shallows in slow chains of bubbles, a sound like a giant's pot left simmering. On rare nights when lightning touches one, a pale flame stands on the flat for as long as the gas lasts, and the people leave it alone.

And then there are the birds.

They come in flocks that rise like smoke off the bars and darken even the pale sky-smudge. Small grey shorebirds nest on the gravel in densities that turn whole islands the colour of their own feathers; their combined voice is a wall. In nesting season the noise is physical — you feel it in your teeth, in your ribs. Add the bigger white waders, the dark divers packed shoulder-to-shoulder in the channels, the migrations arriving and departing in revolving storms of wings, and the plain earns its name. The people call it a word that means, roughly, *the Roaring* — though in the quiet months they sometimes call it *the Shallows*, and sometimes *the End of Land*.

Conversation at any distance is impossible. The people speak with their hands, in a sign-language older than any of their spoken words, and by the age of thirty most of them are half-deaf in one ear. They say the Roaring gives you a choice: learn to watch, or go silent.

* * *

Nothing rooted grows here. The silt is too young, too often drowned, too salt, too shifted. But the mud is alive.

It is smeared in mats — sheets of ochre, rust, black, silver-green, and on the still days, purple — microbial leathers that coat the flats and the channel edges, thickening and thinning with the seasons, glistening like wet skin when the tide exposes them. Some of them phosphoresce faintly when disturbed; a canoe at the darkest hour leaves a pale trail that lingers a while, nowhere near enough to read by, but enough to know the water itself is awake.

The mats feed everything. Thumb-sized snails graze them in uncountable millions. Crustaceans comb them. In the warm-ish weeks the channels turn silver with fish — runs that pulse through the braids like a second tide, and everything gorges: the birds, the people, the slide-eels that lurk in the deeper cuts. The biggest living things on the plain are the siltbacks — slab-sided, barnacle-crusted filter-feeders the size of canoes, with broad sieve mouths, that move through the shallows grazing the mats like herds of something dreamed of the sea. Their wandering leaves winding furrows that fill with water and become new channels. Their calls are too low to hear; you feel them in the sternum, a slow *whom-whom-whom* rolling across the flats. Birds stand on their backs and pick at their crusts, and the siltbacks do not mind.

Stilts stalk the waterline — heron-things the height of a person, motionless for an hour, then a spear of a beak. They take fish, and the occasional unguarded chick, and nothing larger than that, and they are the plain's only patience.

* * *

There are few people, and they are scattered. A few hundred, perhaps, across the whole region — camps of a dozen hide domes on gravel islands, one larger camp on the whaleback hill. The domes are framed with driftwood, the only wood anyone ever sees: it comes down the rivers from forests a world away, and a straight piece of it is worth more than a good harpoon. Over the frames they stretch mat-leather — the tough microbial sheets, harvested, dried, and worked soft with rendered fat — and fat is precious too. Their fires burn dried turf cut from the mat-flats, smoky and sweetish; their lamps burn siltback oil. They have bone needles, stone scrapers, cord twisted from mat-fiber, nets of sinew. Nothing metal lasts a season here; the damp eats it.

Their marks on the land are small and patient. Stone-and-bone weirs across the channels, half-buried now, rebuilt each generation in slightly different places. Cairns marking the safe fords — though the fords move. Middens: shell and bone heaps that grow over centuries into low mounds, the old eating-places, which the living avoid. A half-silted causeway runs from one bar toward a gravel island that no longer exists at low water. And on top of the whaleback hill stand slabs of stone set on end in a circle — clearly made, clearly old, clearly not by any living memory's people. The people leave them alone. They use them as a landmark, and as a story.

They hunt the birds in fog and at the darkest hours, working in silence in their sign-tongue, driving flocks into sinew nets. They take eggs by the basket. Twice a year, when the siltbacks wander into the shallow cuts, the bold ones go after them in hide boats with driftwood-shafted harpoons — a week's work for a year's meat and oil.

* * *

The whaleback hill is the last of a mountain range. The rock is folded and banded in ribbons of grey and black, absurdly old, and it has been ground down to a smooth wet knuckle rising perhaps thirty feet above the flats. The mountains that fed these rivers are gone — not fallen, *gone* — ground into the silt that now feeds the rivers. Nothing on this plain will ever be higher than this. The land has reached its final shape; the only direction left for it is into the sea, and the people say the sea is walking inland, one day-walk per thousand years. Their oldest story is that the circle of stones on the hill marks where the last dry ground will be, and that someone is meant to be standing there when the water arrives.

So far, no one has volunteered. The people stay where the birds are, and the birds stay where the mats are, and the mats stay where the water is, and the water is everywhere, and twice a day it breathes. In the nesting months — when the flocks come in like weather, and the mud booms underfoot, and the seeps simmer their pale chains, and the tide walks miles inland across the flats — the plain's true name rises out of the noise without anyone speaking it, and it is exactly right: *the Roaring*.
