# The Throat

A hollow mineral column, roughly three hundred meters across, rising and falling beyond any visible limit. The interior surface is the only ground. Everything clings to the wall.

---

## The Rock

The column formed recently — in a geological instant, perhaps within the span of a few centuries. The rock is young, and it shows: the interior surface is sharp, unweathered, fresh-fractured. Basaltic and silicate layers run in vertical striations, and in places the wall glitters with crystalline inclusions that haven't yet dulled under oxidation. No water has ever touched this place. There are no dissolution features, no smooth curves, no sedimentary bedding — only the raw geometry of rapid mineral formation. The rock is still settling. Every few hours, somewhere in the depths, a sharp crack echoes — a new fracture propagating through a load-bearing layer, a shelf of stone the size of a room shearing free and falling into the dark below. The wall is alive with small shifts. Dust falls constantly, fine mineral powder that coats every surface and fills the lower air in a permanent pale haze.

The rock is warm to the touch in some zones — not hot, but feverish, the residual heat of formation — and cool in others where air currents pull through fissures. The temperature everywhere is cool, stable, slightly below what a human would find comfortable. The air is dry and carries a faint mineral tang, a smell like hot stone and ozone.

---

## The City

The settlement — the inhabitants call it simply the Throat, or sometimes the Climb — is built directly from the wall. Not against it: *from* it. The same basaltic stone, quarried and re-cut, forms the terraces, platforms, walls, and roofs. The construction is dense, layered, stratified vertically. Hundreds of levels cling to the interior circumference, each jutting from the rock face at slightly different depths, creating a continuous band of human habitation that rings the shaft at every elevation. Bridges and cableways span the open center — thin, swaying connections between opposite walls, some carrying pedestrians, others hauling quarried stone or processed ore in small cage-lifts.

The architecture is industrial, functional, dense to the point of compression. Structures are built flush against one another, sharing walls, stacked and interlocked. There is no planning grid — the city grew in layers, each generation building on top of, below, or into the last. Narrow vertical streets run along the wall face; horizontal passages tunnel through the accumulated construction; open platforms project over the void, ringed with railings made of riveted iron and quarried slab. The dominant materials are the column's own stone, dark iron, fired brick, and a pale ceramic composite produced in the kiln-districts. Glass is rare and cloudy — made from the column's silica, it never achieves perfect transparency.

The lowest levels are the oldest, deepest in the wall, partially collapsed in places, abandoned and reoccupied in cycles. The newest construction is always pushing outward from the wall — cantilevered platforms extending over the central void, supported by iron brackets drilled into fresh rock. These outer platforms are the most precarious and the most desirable: they catch the faintest traces of light from above, and the view across the shaft — the opposite wall receding into haze, a thousand tiny windows glowing, the cableways strung like spider silk — is worth the risk.

---

## Light

Dim. The opening at the top is kilometers up, a circle of pale gray-white visible only from the highest platforms. The light that filters down is diffuse, directionless, a perpetual overcast twilight that barely separates wall from void. The lower levels receive nothing. There, the city makes its own light: furnaces, kilns, smelters, chemical lamps burning mineral oil, and racks of bioluminescent colonies cultivated in shallow troughs — a cultivated organism, neither plant nor fungus, that grows in thin sheets on damp stone and emits a steady blue-green glow. It is tended carefully, harvested and replanted, one of the few living things the city cultivates deliberately.

The overall impression is of a vast cylindrical cavern, its walls studded with points and pools of light — warm orange from furnaces, cold blue-green from the glow-colonies, harsh white from arc-lamps in the workshops. Between them, immense volumes of dimness. The central void is the darkest space, a column of shadow ringed by the lit walls.

---

## Fauna

Abundant, and all of it silent.

The column's ecology is built on mineral chemistry, not photosynthesis. The base of the food chain is lithotrophic — organisms that extract energy from the rock itself, from sulfide oxidation, from mineral gradients in the warm zones. These are simple: films, crusts, gelatinous mats. Above them, a bestiary of climbers, burrowers, and gliders has evolved — or perhaps was always here, in the deep rock, and simply expanded into the new space when the column formed.

The most common are the scrapers — six-legged, flat-bodied creatures the size of a hand, with mineral-hardened mouthparts that grind slowly through the rock surface, consuming the lithotrophic films. They move in dense colonies, covering sections of wall in a living carpet that advances and retreats. They make no sound. Their passage is marked only by the fine grit they excrete, which drifts down in slow streams.

Above them: the needles — slender, multi-jointed climbers that move across the vertical surfaces with unsettling speed, hunting scrapers. They have no eyes, navigating by vibration and chemical sensing. Larger still are the drapers — heavy, slow things that cling to the undersides of platforms and bridges, feeding on scrapers and needles alike, their broad bodies pressed flat against the stone like living lichen. And in the open void, the gliders — membrane-winged, long-tailed, riding the slow convection currents that rise and fall through the shaft, hunting the smaller things that lose their grip and fall.

There are others: burrowers in the rock itself, their tunnels a hidden network that occasionally breaks through into a cellar or a mine gallery; things that live in the glow-colony troughs and are harvested along with them; parasites and scavengers in the city's walls. The fauna is dense — in some districts, the walls seem to move. The inhabitants tolerate most of it. Some species are deliberately encouraged — the scrapers are protected in certain zones because their grazing keeps the walls clean of the more destructive mineral crusts; the glow-colonies' symbionts are guarded. Others are hunted for food or materials. The needles, whose dried bodies produce a fine ceramic powder, are trapped in bulk.

None of it is loud. The silence of the Throat is one of its defining qualities — a vast, dry, vertical silence broken only by the city.

---

## The City's Noise

Mostly quiet. The construction itself is a sound-dampening medium — thick stone, dense layering, irregular surfaces that scatter and absorb. Sound does not carry far. The kiln-districts produce a constant low rumble, felt in the feet more than heard. The quarries — active sections of wall where stone is being cut — produce a rhythmic, metallic ringing that carries a surprising distance, sharp and clear. The cableways sing faintly in the air currents, a high thin note. Footsteps, voices, the clatter of tools: these are intimate sounds, audible only within a few meters.

The great exceptions are the falls. When a section of wall shears off — and this happens, on average, several times a day somewhere in the column — the sound is enormous: a crack like a cannon, then a rising rumble as the mass descends, bouncing off protrusions, disintegrating, the sound building and building until it reaches a crescendo of crashing and grinding that echoes up and down the shaft for minutes. The dust that follows hangs in the air for hours. Falls are a constant background anxiety. The city's construction incorporates this: platforms are roofed with thick stone slabs, buildings are built with their weakest walls facing the rock (to absorb falling debris) and their strongest facing the void.

---

## Dynamics

The Throat is in a state of constant, turbulent becoming. It is a young city in a young geological formation, and both are changing faster than any individual can track.

The quarries never stop. New stone is always being cut from the wall — for construction, for export, for the ceramic and chemical industries that process the column's minerals. Each quarry removes a section of the wall and replaces it with a new construction phase: terraces, workshops, dwellings. The city is literally eating the column and rebuilding itself from the remains. This creates instability — the removal of material changes stress patterns in the rock, triggering new fractures, new falls. The city's engineers (such as they are) monitor the wall constantly, marking zones of dangerous stress, evacuating districts when the cracks propagate too fast.

The population churns. People arrive — from above, lowered in cargo cages, or from below, climbing the service ladders that run the full height of the inhabited zone. People leave, or die, or simply move — relocating from a collapsed district to a new platform, from a dark lower level to a brighter upper one. The city's layout is never the same from year to year. A district that was residential becomes industrial; a quarry becomes a market; a bridge is cut and rerouted. The vertical stratification means that social and economic status is mapped directly onto elevation: the highest levels, closest to the distant light, are the most expensive and the most contested.

The economy runs on extraction and processing. The column's minerals — silica, basaltic compounds, sulfide ores, the crystalline inclusions — are quarried, refined, and exported upward or downward. The glow-colonies are cultivated and harvested. Some of the fauna is processed: needle-ceramic, scraper-grit used in construction, certain chemical compounds distilled from draper glands. There is also manufacturing — the ceramic composite, ironwork, tools, the cages and cables and brackets that the city constantly needs to maintain and expand itself. The kiln-districts never cool. The forges never stop. Even in the quiet hours — though there are no true quiet hours, only shifts in intensity — the industrial machinery runs, powered by the warm zones' geothermal gradients, by chemical batteries, by the labor of people and animals.

The turbulence is not chaotic in the sense of random — it is chaotic in the sense of a living system, an anthill, a bazaar: thousands of individual agents making thousands of individual decisions that collectively produce a city that is never stable, never finished, never the same. A new platform is built in a week. A bridge is strung across the void in a day. A district is abandoned overnight when the cracks spread. The Throat is a place that is always building itself and partially destroying itself, and the inhabitants have learned to live in that rhythm — to sleep through the distant crashes, to pack quickly, to build fast and not grow too attached to any particular wall.

## The Rules

Gravity is the first rule, and it is absolute. Everything that is not held, falls. The city is built around this fact — every platform is roofed, every railing is load-bearing, every loose object is a potential projectile for kilometers below. The central void is a kill-zone; nothing is thrown into it deliberately, and the gliders are the only things that move through it with impunity.

The second rule is that the column is still forming. It is not a stable structure. The rock will continue to settle, fracture, and shift for centuries. The city exists in a temporary equilibrium, a holding action against geological time.

The third rule is verticality. Distance is measured in height, not in horizontal span. A journey of fifty meters horizontally across the void may take an hour via the nearest bridge; a journey of five hundred meters vertically may take minutes on a service lift. The city's psychology is vertical — people think in terms of up and down, of rising and falling, of the light above and the dark below.

The fourth rule is dryness. There is no water, anywhere, in any form. All moisture is extracted from mineral processes, from the bodies of organisms, from the slow condensation that occurs in the deepest cool zones. It is recycled, hoarded, valued. The city's chemistry is non-aqueous — industrial processes use mineral acids, molten salts, and organic solvents derived from the fauna. The inhabitants themselves are adapted, or have adapted their technology, to function with minimal water.

The fifth rule, unspoken but understood: the column may not be natural. The regularity of its diameter, the unusual mineral composition, the presence of organisms that seem too well-adapted to this specific environment — some inhabitants believe the column is a constructed thing, an artifact of something that came before. If so, its builders are long gone, and the column is still running whatever program it was built to run. The quarries occasionally break into hollow spaces that are too regular, too shaped, to be natural — chambers with smooth walls and geometric angles, filled with the same mineral dust as everywhere else. These spaces are used for storage. No one investigates them deeply. There is always new rock to cut, always new construction to build. The Throat is concerned with the present, with the next platform, the next bridge, the next fall. The deep history of the column — natural or otherwise — is a matter for the small, quiet community of scholars who live in the oldest, deepest levels, and who are generally ignored.

## Atmosphere

Cool. Dry. Dim. The air moves in slow vertical currents — rising in the warm zones, sinking in the cool, a vast convection cell that circulates the same air over and over. It is not stale — the column is open at both ends, and fresh air diffuses in from above and below — but it carries the accumulated mineral dust, the faint chemical tang of industry, the organic undertone of dense habitation. The dust is everywhere: on every surface, in every seam, ground into the stone by constant passage. It falls in slow drifts from above, and the city is in a perpetual state of being lightly coated in pale gray powder.

The silence is the most striking quality. For all its density, its industry, its population, the Throat is quiet. Sound dies quickly in the irregular stone geometry, absorbed by thick walls and dense construction. The dominant sensory experience is visual — the layered walls, the points of light, the vast vertical space — and tactile: the cool air, the grit underfoot, the vibration of machinery through the stone, the slight sway of a bridge in the air current.

It is a hidden place, in the deepest sense: not merely concealed, but contained, folded into the interior of a single feature. The rest of the world — whatever lies above or below — is distant, abstract, a source of supplies and departures. The Throat is self-contained, self-referential, a world entire. Its inhabitants do not look outward. They look up, toward the distant light, or down, toward the deepening dark, or across, at the opposite wall with its thousand glowing windows and its dense, layered, precarious, noisy, silent, living construction.
