**The Underworks**

The adit slopes down at a steady grade, blasted through dark basalt that still carries the curved tool-marks of the drill jumbo and the sharp, unrounded edges of fresh breakage. Nothing here has been exposed long. Cables run along the wall on ceramic insulators; a pipe weeps cold water that has cut a gully in the floor only centimeters deep, because it has only had forty years. The air smells of wet stone, ozone, and something green. The sound begins as a vibration you feel in your teeth before you hear it, and by the time the tunnel opens into the main gallery it has become a roar.

The gallery is a valley. It runs two kilometers, three hundred meters across, and its far end is lost in darkness. The ceiling is mostly out of sight; where the lamps reach it, it is ice-polished rock, and in places it is still ice itself — the glacier's underbelly, glistening, threaded with meltwater veins. Daylight comes down in pale columns through blowholes, shafts where the ice has cracked open or melted back, and those columns fall on a meadow.

The floor is moss. Not moss on stone — moss as stone. Knee-deep, hummocked, springy, pale green going to gold where the lamps touch it. You never touch rock underfoot in the gallery; you walk on a living mat that holds water like a sponge, so that every step wells cold water around your boot. The moss has buried the old workings. You can read the humps of it over buried plinths, over a length of narrow-gauge rail, over a valve wheel that still sticks up like a monument, its rim thick with haircap moss. The stream that crosses the gallery is the only bare ground: black, glacial-flour gray, shallow, sliding over stone it has not yet managed to round. It has only had forty years.

The sound is the weather here, and it has layers. Lowest is the drone of the axial ventilation fans in their concrete housings — two stories tall, blades wide as a man is long — a pressure you feel in the sternum before you hear it. Over that, the penstocks: two-meter pipes that drop the glacier's basal meltwater from a lake above, and where the water goes over the lip and falls six meters into the plunge pool, there is a mid-frequency roar that fills the stone like water fills a jar. Over that, the hiss of spray, the whine of generators, the hum of the transformer kiosk, the occasional crack and boom of the ice ceiling settling. The rock reflects everything. Near the plunge pool the level is past a hundred decibels; speech is impossible at two meters; you learn to read hands, and you learn to watch mouths. Everyone wears earplugs, and still the younger workers have the hearing of older men. After a week you stop noticing the roar. After a month you dream in it. After a year, when the pumps rest for their maintenance cycle and the gallery drops to a strange, ringing quiet, you cannot sleep.

The cold is constant: one to three degrees. Breath fogs. Frost feathers the intake ducts. Every cold surface is clammy with condensation, and the moss drips. The only warm places are the exhaust vents and the generator hall, where the air is six, maybe eight degrees — and there the moss grows thickest and greenest, a lusher, darker carpet than anywhere else, as if the machines are feeding it. The water is half a degree above freezing. In winter the ceiling near the blowholes skins over with new ice, and in summer it thins again, and the meltwater budget of the whole place swells and shrinks with the seasons like a slow heartbeat.

The light is amber and sparse. Strings of bare bulbs and sodium vapor lamps follow the pipe runs on sagging cables, and between one lit node and the next there is two hundred meters of complete dark. The moss glows under the lamps; the wet stone glints; the rest is black. The blowholes give the only real daylight — pale, cold columns that move across the meadow as the sun crosses, and when it rains on the surface, the rain comes down those columns and drums on the corrugated iron of the pump hall roof, a sound you can hear even under the roar, sharp and bright. In the far galleries, where no lamps run, you go by headlamp, and the dark closes behind you like water.

The works themselves are scattered. Pump Hall first: a concrete plinth, turbine housings green with paint and age, brass gauges, valve wheels, a gantry crane that hasn't moved in years. Then, across a kilometer of moss, the transformer kiosk and switchgear, humming behind a wire fence. Then the workshop, the bunkhouse with its notice board of faded safety sheets and a single motivational poster from a decade nobody remembers, the narrow-gauge flatcar sitting on its rusted track, drums of oil in a lean-to, a drill jumbo abandoned mid-blast with its booms still raised. Painted lines on the floor mark the walkways; survey marks and hand-painted numbers — *47, 48, 49* — mark the rock. Everything is far apart. The emptiness between structures is the dominant fact of the place; you walk ten minutes through dark and moss to check a gauge, and the dark and moss are the same before and after.

The ecology is simple and thin. The moss is the base of everything — haircap, cushion moss, peat moss, liverworts in the wettest corners, lichen plates on the walls like old maps, pale mushrooms fruiting in the damp behind the workshop where the exhaust heat reaches. Springtails jump in the moss when you disturb it. Moths gather at the lamps. There is a colony of a dozen bats in a high side gallery, and their droppings are the richest thing in the place. Fox tracks appear sometimes in the moss near a blowhole — a fox came down once, or its mother did, and lived on moths and what else, nobody knows. The sump holds a few fish, pale and quiet, in water that carries the vibration of the machines through the rock. Mostly there is absence. The noise is why: nothing that needs to hear can live here. The machines have excluded everything but the moss, and the moss does not listen.

The place runs on meltwater and electricity, and it runs slowly. The pumps cycle on schedules measured in weeks. The sump level rises over months and is drawn down over months. The melt season swells the penstocks to a brown torrent; winter reduces them to a green thread. The ice ceiling creeps, groans, and occasionally drops a piece — rockfall is the one sharp hazard in a place of patient processes, and the rules are simple: test the moss with a pole, because it hides holes; wear your ears; watch the ceiling near the blowholes; and remember that the blowholes bring the surface weather down — rain, wind, snow, and once, memorably, a raven that circled for three days before finding its way out.

Nothing rots quickly in the cold. The abandoned jumbo will be there in a century, rusted but intact, and the moss will be up to its seat. The glacier above is retreating, imperceptibly, and the blowholes widen over decades, and the cave — young, freshly emerged, its surfaces still sharp — is only just beginning its work of becoming old. The machines hold the dark at bay; the moss holds the machines in place; the glacier holds them both, and is in no hurry about any of it.
