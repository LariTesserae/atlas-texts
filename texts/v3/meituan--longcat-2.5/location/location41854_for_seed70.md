**The Hollow Sea**

At the top of the world there is a sea. It has no business being there. A warm, milky, wind-rippled ocean sits on a plateau so high that the clouds lie below its rim like a second, slower sea — a white plain seen from above, featureless except where a distant peak punches through. But there it is: the Hollow Sea, the Pale Basin, the Shallows, stretching to a horizon that curves away in every direction, dozens of miles of water and stone.

The sea is young. Geologically young — a few thousand years since the plateau's limestone heart dissolved from beneath and the basin filled, and it shows in every line of the place. There are no soft shores. No beaches. No delta mud, no river silt, no rounded cobbles. The water meets the rock with a knife-edge: vertical walls of pale gray stone rising straight out of the sea, their surfaces still sharp with the angular facets of recent fracture, still marked by the clean, unweathered planes where the rock broke and fell. The towers — hundreds of them, perhaps thousands — stand close enough to touch. Near-touching. Crowded like a forest of stone, their crests sharp, their flanks steep, many of them hollow, perforated by caves and windows where the water has already eaten through.

The water is warm. Not the warmth of a tropical shore, exactly, but a deeper warmth — the warmth of a living thing, of blood. It rises from below, from vents and fissures in the basin floor, from the slow exhalation of hot rock beneath the limestone. On still mornings the surface steams, and the steam hangs in layers, and the light comes through it diffused and golden, and the whole sea looks like something out of a fever. The color shifts with the depth: milky turquoise near the towers, where the water is shallow and the dissolved minerals catch the light; deep blue-gray in the sinkholes and channels, where the bottom falls away into cold darkness. The water is heavy — laden with dissolved limestone, with salt, with trace minerals. It is buoyant. You could lie on your back in it and not sink, and it would taste of metal and chalk and something faintly sulfurous.

The vegetation is dense. It crowds every surface. Stilt-rooted trees — mangrove analogues, but not mangroves — grip the bare rock with roots like brown fingers, and their roots descend into the water and keep descending, and where they touch the surface they send up new shoots, so that each tower becomes a grove of itself, a clone of itself, layered in generations of root and trunk. Ferns spill from every ledge. Mosses hang in curtains a yard long. Vines drape from the crowns of the towers down into the water, and where they touch the surface they root again. The effect is of a forest that drowned and refused to die — that learned to breathe through its roots, to drink the warm mineral water, to grow twice as fast as it should.

And on the water itself: floating meadows. Mats of vegetation — some small as a table, some large as a field — drift and collide and fuse, forming a patchwork quilt that covers whole stretches of the sea. They are dense, near-touching, layered: a carpet of small round leaves on top, and below them a tangled raft of roots and stems and trapped air, and below that the warm dark water. In places the mats are so thick and continuous that you could almost walk on them — almost, but not quite; they would sag, and the water would well up between the leaves, and the mat would swallow you slowly, warmly, root by root.

The sea is mostly quiet. Not silent — there is the lapping of small waves against stone, the drip of water from root to root, the rustle of leaves in the warm wind, the distant call of a bird — but quiet in the way of a warm place, where sound seems to sink into the humidity and die. The fauna is rare. A few great slow things move in the deep channels: rays, perhaps, or something like rays, wide as a door, pulsing through the warm water with the patience of continents. Great turtles with shells crusted in white mineral. And birds — a few species, mostly white, that nest on the highest tower crowns and spend hours motionless, watching the water. Insects in the vegetation, but even they seem quiet.

The strangeness is in the details. The sea has no visible inlet and no visible outlet. The water comes up from below and goes down through the karst, and the level changes only over centuries — rising a hand's breadth in a lifetime, falling the same. The towers are growing, too, in their way: travertine and tufa depositing around their bases where the mineral-laden water spills and spreads, building slow collars of new rock, so that the towers are simultaneously eroding and growing, dissolving and accreting, young and ancient at once. The floating mats migrate — slowly, over years, circling the basin in a drift so gradual you would need to mark a single mat with a stake to prove it moves at all. And the clouds below the rim never rise. They are a floor, not a ceiling. The sea floats above them.
