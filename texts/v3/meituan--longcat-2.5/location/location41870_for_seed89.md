The rim is a lip of pale limestone, wind-scoured and crumbly, sitting on a plateau so high that the air is thin and every breath is a negotiation. Above, the sky is a cold, depthless blue, fierce with sun. The plateau itself is a broken expanse of frost-shattered rock and sparse cushion plants — *yareta* maybe, or something like it, dense mossy lumps that have been growing for centuries at the pace of fingernails. The wind is constant, a dry, cold wind that smells of nothing but distance. But the rim is the edge of something else.

The sinkhole opens like a throat.

It is not vast — perhaps eighty meters across at the top, narrowing as it descends, a near-perfect shaft of collapsed limestone. The walls are close. You can see the opposite side. The scale is that of a cathedral nave, or a large room, or a grove of ancient trees. It is a single-feature place. The whole world here is this one pit.

The walls are near-vertical, fluted and weathered, a pale grey stone streaked with mineral stains — iron-ochre, manganese-black, the pale green of copper salts. They have the settled look of something that has been what it is for a very long time. The solution pockets are deep and rounded, the ledges are worn smooth, the dripstone formations are old and dry and crumbling. This is an established place, in equilibrium with itself. The erosion here is not a process of becoming; it is a process of being.

You descend. The light changes first. The sky above becomes a circle, then an oval, then a bright coin. The walls close in. The wind drops away. And then, at perhaps thirty meters down, the air changes. It warms. Not dramatically — not a bath — but enough that you notice it. Enough that you loosen your collar. The rock under your palm is warm, like skin. There is a smell: ammonia, wet stone, something organic and rich, something that has been accumulating for a very long time.

The walls are alive.

Bats. Millions of them. They coat every ledge, every crevice, every solution pocket and fluted groove. The wall is a seething, rustling carpet of them, a living surface that is never still. They are small, mostly — insectivorous bats, perhaps several species, their fur ranging from dark brown to pale grey to a strange, almost rufous tone. They are packed in, near-touching, layered, a density that is hard to comprehend. You can see individual bodies, individual wings, but they blur into a texture, a fabric, a living wall. The sound is constant — a low, chittering roar, the rustle of a million wing-membranes, the occasional sharp call or squeal. It is loud. It fills the sinkhole, presses against your chest, makes your own thoughts feel distant. You have to raise your voice to speak to yourself.

The bats are not the only life. The air is thick with insects — flies, moths, beetles, all drawn to the warmth and the guano. They cloud in the still air, they settle on the walls, they crawl over the bats themselves. Spiders hang in webs between the rock projections, their webs thick with insect debris. On the floor, in the guano, there is a whole ecosystem of detritivores — beetles, mites, springtails, nematodes, a teeming invertebrate world. The guano itself is deep, perhaps meters in places, accumulated over centuries. It is warm, damp, and it steams slightly in the cooler air near the top. It has the smell of an old stable, an old church cellar, a place where organic matter has been composting for a very long time.

The floor is uneven — a landscape of guano hummocks, rockfalls, and pools. Some pools are cold, fed by drip water from the walls. Others are warm — hot, even — fed by geothermal seeps that rise from fractures in the bedrock below. The warm pools are crusted with mineral deposits, pale yellow and orange and white, the colors of sulfur and salt and calcium. Steam rises from them in thin wisps, and the humidity is near saturation. Water condenses on the walls and drips. The sound of dripping is constant — a patter, a plink, a rush where the water finds a channel and runs down the fluted stone. The water is intermittent, not a stream but a moisture that appears and disappears, that beads and runs and pools and evaporates and condenses again. The whole sinkhole is a water cycle in miniature, a closed loop of evaporation and condensation and drip.

The floor has its own topography. There are ledges and overhangs, alcoves and clefts. In the deepest parts, there are passages that lead further down, into the dark — narrow, low, warm, smelling of sulfur and bat. These are the darkest parts of the sinkhole, the parts that never see even the diffuse light from above. And there, perhaps, there is bioluminescence — a faint green glow from fungi or insects, a cold, quiet light that has nothing to do with the sun. Or perhaps not. Perhaps the darkness is mostly just darkness, a true and absolute dark that your eyes never fully penetrate.

The light, such as it is, comes from above. At midday, when the sun is directly overhead, a shaft of light penetrates the throat and moves slowly across the floor — a bright, cold, blue-white blade that illuminates a patch of guano, a pool, a stretch of wall. It is a different light from the warm dimness of the rest of the sinkhole. It is the light of the high plateau, the light of the thin, cold air, the light of the world above. It moves. It changes. It is the only clock the sinkhole has. For the rest of the day, the floor is in deep shadow, lit only by the diffuse sky-light that filters down the shaft — a dim, blue-grey illumination, like the light at the bottom of a well. The walls are never fully lit. The recesses are never lit. The sinkhole is a realm of dimness, of shadow, of the edge of darkness.

The fauna is overwhelming. The bats are the dominant presence — the sound, the smell, the movement, the sheer biomass. But there are also birds — swiftlets or swallows, nesting on the walls in the upper reaches, flying in and out through the throat with a swift, silent flight. There are snakes — a species of cave-dwelling snake, pale and thin, that hunts the bats along the walls, that lies in wait on ledges and strikes at passing wings. There are amphibians in the warm pools — small, pale frogs, almost translucent, their eyes reduced to dark specks. There are fish in the deeper pools — small, white, blind, their fins delicate as lace. There are crickets in the crevices, their call a high, thin trill that cuts through the bat-roar. There are centipedes, pale and fast, hunting in the guano. There are mites on the bats themselves, and lice, and fleas, and a whole parasitic fauna that lives on the bodies of the larger animals. The place is saturated with life. Every surface is alive. The air is alive. The water is alive. The guano is alive. The rock itself seems alive with the slow, patient growth of mineral crusts and the slower, more patient growth of bacteria.

The sound is loud. The bat colony is a constant roar — not a cacophony but a texture, a layered, shifting sound that has a rhythm and a pulse. The insects add a high, thin hum. The dripping is a patter, a plink, a rush. The wind in the throat adds a low moan or a hum, depending on the weather above. The sinkhole has its own acoustics — sounds echo and blend, the walls reflect and absorb, the space resonates at certain frequencies. There is a low hum that is always present, a drone that is the sum of all the other sounds. It is the voice of the sinkhole. It is the sound of millions of animals breathing, moving, calling, living. It is the sound of water dripping, of steam rising, of guano settling. It is the sound of a place that is full.

The place is old. The sinkhole formed over millions of years — a cave chamber that collapsed, or a solution pipe that widened and deepened until the roof gave way. The walls are weathered, fluted, stained. The guano has accumulated for centuries, perhaps millennia. The bat colony has been here for thousands of years — maybe longer. The geothermal seeps have been flowing for millennia. The whole place is a slow, patient accumulation of time. Everything here happens slowly. The drip of water. The accumulation of guano. The growth and decline of the bat colony. The erosion of the walls. The mineral crusts growing on the warm pools. The place is in equilibrium. It is what it is. It has been what it is for a very long time, and it will continue to be what it is for a very long time more.

And there is something else. Something slightly off.

The bats here are not quite like bats elsewhere. They are more social, more densely packed, more tolerant of disturbance. They have a strange rhythm — the colony does not leave at dusk in a single stream, but in pulses, in waves, as if the sinkhole itself is breathing them out and drawing them back. Their echolocation calls are unusual — lower in frequency, more complex, almost melodic. They seem to use the sinkhole's acoustics, to sing into the space and listen to the echo, as if the sinkhole is a partner in their perception.

Or perhaps it is the sinkhole itself that is unusual. The warmth is not just geothermal — it is concentrated, focused, as if the earth's heat is rising through a particular channel and pooling here. The water is not just water — it is mineral-rich, sulfurous, warm, and it seems to support life in unusual densities. The guano is not just guano — it is a substrate for a unique ecosystem, a layered, stratified community of organisms that has evolved in isolation. The sinkhole is a refuge, a reservoir, a warm pocket in a cold, dry, high-altitude desert. It is an ark.

Or perhaps it is the light that is unusual. The way the sunbeam moves across the floor, slow and deliberate, like a searchlight. The way the bioluminescence in the recesses pulses, faintly, in rhythm with the bat calls. The way the diffuse light from above seems to come from everywhere and nowhere, a sourceless, directionless illumination. The sinkhole is a place where the normal rules of light and dark are suspended, where the sun is a visitor and the darkness is the native condition.

The rules of the world are simple here. The world has early tools — stone flakes, bone awls, woven fiber. But this place is untouched. No made structures, no artifacts, no fire-scars, no old bones piled in heaps. The only made things are natural — the bat colonies, the insect nests, the mineral crusts, the slow architecture of dripstone. The place is wild, self-willed, indifferent to any hand. It is a place that has never been owned, never been shaped, never been used. It is a place that simply is.

The time here is slow. The seasons above — the cold, the wind, the occasional snow — barely penetrate. The sinkhole has its own climate, its own rhythm. The daily cycle of the sunbeam, moving across the floor like a slow hand. The daily cycle of the bats, resting in the day, streaming out in the evening, returning in the night. The seasonal cycle of the colony — mating, birthing, weaning, the annual pulse of new life. The longer cycles of guano accumulation, of mineral growth, of slow erosion. The place is patient. It does not hurry. It has been here for a very long time, and it will be here for a very long time more.

You stand on the floor, in the warm dimness, surrounded by the roar of the colony, the drip of water, the smell of ammonia and life. The walls rise around you, alive with bats. The sky is a bright coin above. The sunbeam moves slowly across the floor, illuminating a patch of guano, a pool, a stretch of wall, then passing on. The steam rises from the warm pools. The crickets trill. The bats rustle and chitter and call. The sinkhole breathes.

It is a small world, a bounded world, a single-feature world. It is a room at the top of the world, a warm dark room, a room full of wings and voices and the slow, patient processes of water and stone and life. It is a place that has settled into itself, that is in balance with itself, that is what it is. And it is slightly, quietly, strangely alive.
