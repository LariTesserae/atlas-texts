# The Hollow

Deep in the high range, where the mountains are old and the rock remembers being liquid, there is a chamber. Not a cave — a hollow. A bubble pressed up from below and left behind when the world cooled, as if the mountain's heart once pushed against its ribs and left a shape in the mold.

It is the size of a cathedral. Perhaps two hundred feet from floor to the apex of its dome, and a little more across. The ceiling is dark, glassy rock — obsidian, or something near it — smooth and faintly concave, as though it was once the inside of a bubble and still remembers the curve. The walls bow outward and descend into a floor that is not flat but gently undulant, rising and falling in low swells. The floor is pale: a crust of mineral salts, sulfates, and sinter, layered and cracked into irregular plates. In places the crust is thin enough to show the darker stone beneath. In others it has mounded into low ridges and shallow basins, like a salt flat seen through deep water.

There is no water. There has been none here for longer than anything can say. The air is dry beyond the dryness of any desert — a dryness that pulls moisture from stone, from flesh, from the very idea of moisture. The heat comes from below: from the rock itself, from hairline fissures in the floor, from the deep places where the mountain's blood still runs warm. The temperature hovers near seventy, eighty degrees — not the killing heat of a vent field, but a steady, enveloping warmth that presses against you like a hand. In the places where it is hottest, the air shimmers faintly, though there is no light to see it by.

There is no light. The chamber is dark — utterly, profoundly dark. Or nearly so. In a few places, where the rock is thin or the heat intense, there is a faint, deep red glow. Not enough to see by. Just enough to know that the stone is warm. That the world below is still burning.

---

The chamber is not empty. It is full — packed, saturated, occupied.

The life here is all animal. No plants. Nothing green, nothing that reaches. The life here lives on the minerals and on each other, and it has been here so long that it has forgotten there was ever another way.

On the walls and ceiling, covering nearly every surface in a living skin, are the **carpet-lichen** — though they are not lichen. Flat, pale, limbless things that cling to the rock in layers, one over another, so that the stone beneath is only a memory. They are the color of the rock itself, or paler: a translucent, waxy white, faintly pink where the heat is greatest. They are sessile, or nearly so. They press their undersides against the mineral surface and they feed — slowly, steadily, imperceptibly — on the rock itself. They are lithovores. They take in minerals through their skin, process them with the heat, and they grow. Very slowly, they grow. They are the base of everything here, the primary turn of stone into flesh.

The walls are soft to the touch — not hard stone but a yielding, slightly warm surface, like pressing your palm against a living thing. Which you are. The carpet-lichen are warm because the rock is warm. They are still, or nearly so. Sometimes one detaches — slowly, over hours — and crawls to a new spot, leaving a pale scar on the rock that fills in within days. Sometimes they divide, or bud, adding another layer to the carpet. The oldest parts of the walls are many layers deep — a palimpsest of pale flesh, a record of centuries.

Hanging from the ceiling in clusters, on long thin stalks or threads, are the **danglers**. Their bodies are small, soft, dark — suspended in the hot air like fruit on invisible vines. They are filter-feeders of a kind, though there is nothing to filter but mineral dust, shed skin, and the smallest of the swarm-things. They sway in the convection currents — the hot air rising from the floor, the slightly cooler air sinking from the ceiling — though there is no wind, only the slow breathing of the chamber. They are the quietest things here, quieter even than the carpet-lichen. They do not move except to sway. They do not feed except to open themselves to what drifts past.

And in the air itself — in clouds that drift and cluster and disperse, that gather in the dome's apex and descend in slow columns, that pool in the low places of the floor — are the **swarm-mites**. Tiny creatures, each one barely visible, that move in coordinated groups. Thousands of them. Millions. They move like smoke, like dust in a beam — except there is no beam, only darkness. They feed on the carpet-lichen, on the danglers, on each other. They are the middle of the food web, the ones who take flesh and make more flesh. They move constantly, restlessly, in patterns that seem almost purposeful — spiraling, clustering, dispersing — but they make no sound. Their wings, if they have wings, are silent. Their bodies are soft. They are the reason the chamber is packed — their numbers are beyond counting, beyond the ability to count. They are the air, in some sense. The air is alive.

And among them, waiting, are the **lurkers**. The predators. Some of them are large — the size of dogs, or worse — and some are small, but all of them share the same posture: absolute stillness. They cling to the walls, the ceiling, the floor. They are the color of the rock, or darker — deep gray, brown, black. They have no eyes, or their eyes are vestigial, pale dots under the skin. They sense heat, or vibration, or the faint electrical whisper of a living body. They wait for hours. For days. For weeks. When a swarm comes too close, when a carpet-lichen detaches and falls, when another predator shifts — they strike. Quickly. Silently. They take their prey and they settle back into stillness, and they wait again.

There are smaller predators too — things that move through the carpet, grazing on it; things that burrow in the mineral crust, leaving faint trails like writing in a language no one reads; things that live in the fissures and take whatever falls in. The chamber is full of them. The walls are alive. The ceiling is alive. The air is alive. The floor is alive. There is no space between them — they are packed, saturated, fully occupied. Every surface, every volume, every niche has its creature, its layer, its tenant.

---

And yet it is quiet. Mostly quiet.

This is the strangest thing. With so many creatures, you would expect a din — calls, rustlings, the ceaseless small sounds of movement. But there is almost none. The carpet-lichen are silent. The danglers are silent. The swarm-mites move like smoke, without sound. The lurkers are silent — they have evolved to be silent, to wait in absolute stillness, to strike without warning. Sound, here, is death. Sound is how the lurkers find their prey — by listening, by feeling the vibrations in the rock, in the air, in the living carpet. So everything is silent. The silence is a survival strategy. The silence is the law of this place.

The only sounds are occasional and faint: a click, like a pebble settling; a scrape, like something moving across stone; a distant rumble from deep below, the mountain shifting in its sleep. The settling of the rock. The breathing of the heat. Once in a long while — so rare that you might not hear it in a day, or a week — the sound of a lurker striking: a quick, soft impact, a brief rustle of swarm-mites scattering, then nothing. Then silence again, deeper than before.

---

There are structures here. Small, scattered, old.

A low wall, perhaps three feet high, built from the same pale mineral as the floor — but shaped. Deliberately shaped. Straight lines. Right angles. It runs for ten feet along the base of one wall, then stops, as if the builder grew tired or called away. A platform, two feet high and four feet square, near the center of the chamber — its surface worn smooth, its edges rounded by centuries of slow mineral deposition. A niche in the wall, rectangular, perhaps a shelf or a bed or a place for something to rest. A few other marks: a groove cut into the floor, a row of small holes drilled in a line, a partial arch that leads nowhere.

They are not large. Nothing here is large except the chamber itself. But they are clearly made. They were not grown. They were not deposited. They were built — by hands, by tools, by intention. And they are old. Older than the carpet-lichen, older than the swarm-mites, older than the lurkers. The creatures have grown over them, around them, through them. The carpet-lichen cover some of them, softening their edges. The mineral crust has buried others, so that only a corner or an edge shows above the surface. But they are still there, if you know where to look — faint marks of purpose in a place that is otherwise pure accident.

Who built them? There is no sign. No tools. No remains. No marks, no writing, no bones. Just the structures — a few walls, a platform, a niche, an arch. Perhaps they were shelters. Perhaps they were markers. Perhaps they were something else entirely — something whose purpose has been forgotten, or something whose purpose was never meant for minds like yours. They are old enough that the question has become irrelevant. The mountain does not remember. The creatures do not care. Only the structures remain, half-buried, half-grown-over, waiting for the slow mineral rise to cover them completely.

---

The chamber is not static. It breathes.

The heat pulses — not constantly, but in cycles. Over hours, over days, the temperature rises slightly, then falls. A difference of only a few degrees, but everything responds. When the heat rises, the carpet-lichen grow faster — their pale bodies flush pinker, their edges creeping across the rock. The swarm-mites move faster, their clouds thickening, their patterns quickening. The danglers sway more. The lurkers grow hungry, shifting in their stillness, ready to strike. When the heat falls, everything slows. The carpet-lichen pale and still. The swarm-mites thin and drift. The lurkers settle into deeper wait. The chamber breathes in, and breathes out. The creatures breathe with it.

And the chamber is slowly closing. The mineral crust on the floor is growing — year by year, century by century — building up, filling in the low places, burying the structures, pressing upward. The carpet-lichen are spreading, covering new surfaces, layer upon layer. The dome is a little lower than it was, a thousand years ago. The walls are a little closer. The space is shrinking. Everything that lives here is living on borrowed time, in a place that is slowly, patiently, inevitably disappearing. In ten thousand years, perhaps, the chamber will be half its size. In a hundred thousand, perhaps, it will be gone — filled in, sealed, just another solid mass of warm rock in the mountain's heart.

But for now, it is here. It is alive. It is full.

---

The rules of this place:

**No water.** Nothing that needs water can live here. Everything has adapted to the dry — to pulling moisture from stone, from air, from the bodies of others.

**No light.** Nothing that needs light can live here. Everything has adapted to the dark — to heat and vibration and the faint electrical whisper of living flesh.

**The heat pulses.** Everything responds to the pulse. The pulse is the clock of this place, the rhythm that governs growth and hunger and stillness.

**The silence is absolute.** Sound is death. Sound is the hunt. Everything has learned to be silent — to move without noise, to strike without warning, to wait without stirring.

**The creatures do not leave.** They cannot. They have evolved for this chamber and this chamber only. Outside, they would die — too cold, too bright, too wet, too empty. The mountain is their world. The hollow is their universe.

**The chamber is closing.** The minerals grow. The space shrinks. Everything here is living in a place that is slowly, patiently, being buried.

---

You stand in the dark — if you stand here — and you feel the heat pressing against you, and the dryness pulling at your skin, and the silence pressing against your ears like depth. You feel the faint vibration of the chamber breathing. You feel the soft, warm surface of the carpet-lichen under your hand. You feel the swarm-mites brushing your face like dust, like ash, like the smallest snow. You feel the eyes of the lurkers on you — not eyes, but something like eyes, something that senses the heat of your body, the electricity of your nerves, the vibration of your heartbeat.

And you understand, standing here, that this place is not empty. It is not dead. It is not a wasteland. It is full — full of life, full of warmth, full of patience, full of silence. It is a world complete unto itself, sealed in warm stone, breathing in the dark. It does not need you. It does not know you are here. It will not remember you when you are gone. The carpet-lichen will cover your bones. The swarm-mites will take your flesh. The mineral crust will bury what remains. And the chamber will go on breathing, slowly, patiently, until it closes.
