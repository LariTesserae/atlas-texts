The floor is made.

This is the first thing to understand. There is no natural ground here. No stone, no sediment, no soil. The floor is constructed — hand-laid slabs of a material that is not quite stone, not quite ceramic, not quite bone. A hydraulic cement, perhaps, or a primitive concrete, or something older than either. The slabs are a meter across, maybe more, laid in a pattern that is still visible despite the erosion of ages. The joints between them are wide, worn smooth, filled with algae and mineral deposits and the fine silt of ages.

The slabs are pale — gray-white, or cream, or a faint blue-green. They are covered in a thick mat of algae, green-brown, that softens every edge. Mosses grow in the joints, dark and small. The algae mat is alive with the slow movement of water, the pulse of currents, the drip of mineral deposits.

The floor extends in every direction. It is flat, low, near-flattened by the erosion of ages. It is not perfectly flat — there are slight rises and falls, the ghost of a gradient, the memory of a slope. But it is essentially flat. A made plain, extending to the limit of vision.

On the floor, structures. Low walls, waist-high, worn down to rounded ridges. The walls are made of the same material as the floor — the same pale cement, the same hand-laid slabs. They are covered in algae, in moss, in the long strands of kelp that reach up from the floor. The walls form patterns — rectangles, circles, channels. The patterns are the ghost of a plan, the memory of a purpose. But the purpose is gone. The walls are just walls now, worn smooth, covered in life.

Channels are carved or molded into the floor. They are shallow, a hand's width deep, a hand's width wide. They run in straight lines, or gentle curves, connecting the structures. They are filled with silt and algae and the slow movement of water. Perhaps they carried water once. Perhaps they carried something else. Now they carry only the memory of flow.

Terraces are broad, shallow steps, worn smooth, covered in vegetation. They rise in gentle tiers, each tier a little higher than the last. The terraces are covered in a dense mat of algae and moss, and in the long strands of kelp that reach up from the floor. The terraces are the ghost of an agriculture, or an aquaculture, or something else. But the purpose is gone. The terraces are just terraces now, worn smooth, covered in life.

Reservoirs are shallow basins, perhaps for holding water, perhaps for something else. They are filled with silt and algae and the slow movement of water. The reservoirs are covered in a dense mat of vegetation, and in the long strands of kelp that reach up from the floor. The reservoirs are the ghost of a system, a network, a purpose. But the purpose is gone. The reservoirs are just reservoirs now, worn smooth, covered in life.

The structures are ancient. They have been here so long that they have become almost natural — but not quite. The corners are rounded. The surfaces are worn smooth. The structures have settled into their final forms, near-flattened, worn down to their essential shapes. Some have collapsed into low mounds that the water has planed flat. The mounds are covered in vegetation, in algae, in moss, in the long strands of kelp.

The construction is primitive. Hand-laid stones. Simple concrete. Rough ceramics. The marks of tools are still visible on some surfaces — the scrape of a trowel, the imprint of a hand, the scratch of a pattern. The patterns are worn, but they are there. A repeating motif. A symbol. A writing. The patterns are the ghost of a language, the memory of a meaning. But the meaning is gone. The patterns are just patterns now, worn smooth, covered in life.

The materials are pale. Gray-white, or cream, or a faint blue-green. They are covered in algae and mineral deposits, so it is hard to tell what the original color was. The mineral deposits are white, or yellow, or orange, or red. They form in the cracks, in the joints, on the surfaces. They drip, slowly, forming stalactites, stalagmites, columns. The mineral deposits are the ghost of a chemistry, the memory of a process. But the process is gone. The mineral deposits are just mineral deposits now, worn smooth, covered in life.

The vegetation is lush. This is the surprise. In the cold and the dark, life has found a way — not the loud, animal way, but the quiet, plant way. Kelp forests rise from the made floor, their strands long and pale, reaching up toward a surface they will never reach. The kelp is adapted to the dark — it doesn't need much light, or it feeds on the minerals that seep from the constructed substrate, or it has found some other way. The kelp forests are dense, layered, a vertical landscape of green-brown-red. The kelp strands are thick as a wrist, long as a body, reaching up through the water column. They sway in the slow currents, a gentle, patient movement.

Algae mats cover every surface — the floor, the walls, the kelp itself. The mats are thick, green-brown, soft. They are alive with the slow movement of water, the pulse of currents, the drip of mineral deposits. The mats are the skin of the made world, the living layer that covers the dead construction.

Mosses grow in the cracks, in the joints, in the sheltered places. The mosses are small, dark, dense. They are the pioneers, the first colonists, the slow builders of soil. The mosses are covered in a fine layer of silt, of mineral deposits, of the slow accumulation of ages.

The vegetation is patient. It grows slowly, in the cold, in the dark, but it grows without ceasing. The kelp adds a fraction of a millimeter each day. The algae mat thickens by a fraction of a millimeter each year. The mosses spread by a fraction of a millimeter each century. The growth is imperceptible, but it is constant. The vegetation is the slow conqueror, the patient builder, the quiet transformer.

There are no animals. No fish move through the kelp forests. No crustaceans scuttle across the made floor. No worms burrow into the ancient concrete. No jellyfish pulse through the water column. The silence is complete. The only sounds are the sounds of water — the slow movement of currents through the kelp, the creak of ancient structures settling, the drip of mineral deposits forming and falling. The water is quiet. The world is quiet.

The water is cold. Near-freezing. The cold is a presence, a weight, a constant. The cold slows everything — the growth of the vegetation, the erosion of the structures, the movement of the currents, the chemistry of the water. The cold is the great slows-everything-down, the great patience-maker. The cold water is dense, heavy, slow. It moves in massive, patient currents that take centuries to complete a cycle.

The water is dark. There is no light from above. The surface is so far above that the concept of "above" becomes theoretical. There is only the dim, directionless gray of deep water, and the darker gray of deeper water, and then the black of the deepest water. The darkness is a presence, a weight, a constant. The darkness slows everything — the growth of the vegetation, the erosion of the structures, the movement of the currents. The darkness is the great slows-everything-down, the great patience-maker.

The scale is vast. The kelp forests extend to the limit of vision — if there were a horizon, if there were light to see it. The made floor extends in every direction, flat and low, covered in its dense blanket of vegetation. The water column above is a continent of darkness. The currents move through this space like weather — slow, massive, patient. They carry the cold from one end of the world to the other. They carry the minerals that feed the vegetation. They carry the memory of the made world, ground fine as silt.

The density is high. The kelp forests are thick — many presences per volume. The built structures are densely packed, layered, a palimpsest of old constructions buried under newer ones, all worn into a single dense mass. The water itself is dense with minerals, with microscopic life, with the slow exhalations of the made world. The layers are everywhere — layers of construction, layers of vegetation, layers of water, layers of time.

The change is slow. The kelp grows imperceptibly. The structures erode imperceptibly. The water moves in slow, massive currents. The mineral deposits form and fall, form and fall, in a cycle that takes centuries. The vegetation spreads, slowly, patiently, covering more of the made world each century. The made world erodes, slowly, patiently, wearing down to its essential shapes. The change is imperceptible, but it is constant. The world is slow, patient, ancient.
