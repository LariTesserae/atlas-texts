# The Weeping Terrace

## I. The Plateau

It sits high — not on the edge of anything, but in the middle of a continent that has been rising for epochs. The plateau is the size of a small sea, flat enough that you can watch weather form above it like thoughts forming behind eyes. It has its own weather. Storms are born in the afternoon heat and die against the far rim by dusk, and the rim itself is a cliff-edge that drops into a different world entirely, one you cannot see from here because the condensation haze swallows it.

The air is warm and thick with moisture. Not rain — it rarely rains here in the way other places know rain. Instead, the atmosphere *condenses*. Every surface sweats. Every overhang drips. The rock is saturated to its bones, and it has been saturated for so long that saturation is its natural state, the way dryness is the natural state of stone elsewhere. You press your palm to a boulder and water beads up around your fingers as if the rock is perspiring through pores too small to see.

## II. The Ground

There is no soil. There is no soil here and there has not been soil for longer than the city has existed, which is longer than anyone can say.

What there is: mineral crust. The plateau surface is a pale, bone-colored sinter, layered in terraces and shallow basins like a vast travertine formation — which, in a sense, it is. Mineral-rich water has been welling up from below and evaporating at the surface for epochs, and the residue has built up into ledges, lips, shallow bowls, and channels. The ground is a palette of muted color: chalk white, sulfur yellow, rust orange where iron has oxidized, a pale celadon green where copper salts have leached. These are not plants. These are stains. These are the rock's own chemistry made visible.

The surface is not flat — it is terraced, gently, in steps so broad and shallow that you can walk across them without noticing the grade, but they are there. Water moves across these terraces in sheets, in films, in slow braided channels no wider than a finger. It finds the lowest path and takes it, and it has been taking the same paths long enough that the paths are now the dominant feature of the ground. The plateau is a delta of mineral deposit, built upward and outward by the patient accretion of dissolved solids.

In places the sinter has built up into formations that look almost architectural — hoodoos, overhangs, natural bridges where a harder layer of deposit has resisted erosion while the softer layer beneath has been undercut. The water weeping from these overhangs has built curtains of deposit beneath them, so that many of the formations look like they are draped in frozen fabric, or in wax that has run and cooled and run again.

## III. The Absence

There are no trees. There is no grass. There is no moss, no lichen in the way you would recognize it — no green film on the rocks, no softness. The palette is strictly mineral: the whites and yellows and rusts and celadons of deposited salts and oxides.

What grows — if *grows* is the word — are films. Thin, barely-visible coatings of chemosynthetic organisms on the wettest surfaces, the places where water flows continuously. They are not photosynthetic; the light is normal, unremarkable, but these organisms do not use it. They use the chemical energy of the mineral water itself, the reduced sulfur and iron and other compounds that well up from the deep. The films are colors you have no names for — not quite black, not quite silver, a dark iridescent sheen that shifts when you move. They are simpler than algae. They are barely alive by most definitions. But they are everywhere there is flowing water, and they are the base of the food web.

## IV. The Fauna

And the food web is *overwhelming*.

The plateau is dense with life — not the slow, sparse life of a mineral desert, but a teeming, layered, stacked abundance that presses in from every direction. The films support grazers, and the grazers support predators, and the predators support scavengers, and every level is populated by organisms that have evolved to live in this warm, wet, mineral-saturated world.

The most visible are the flyers.

They fill the air in numbers that make the sky a texture rather than a void. They are not insects, exactly — they are something parallel, something that arrived at a similar solution from a different direction. Their wings are membrane stretched over a frame of hollow struts, and they range in size from the span of your thumb to the span of your arms. They are filter-feeders, mostly, straining the chemosynthetic films and the smaller floating organisms from the air and the water surfaces. They move in loose aggregations that shift and flow like smoke, like schools of fish in three dimensions, and their wingbeats create a constant low hum that is the baseline sound of the plateau.

But they are only the beginning. There are waders in the shallow channels — jointed, multi-legged things that pick through the mineral deposits with delicate, probing mouthparts. There are burrowers in the softer sinter, leaving the ground pocked with holes and seamed with their castings. There are predators that hunt the waders, and scavengers that clean up after the predators, and parasites on everything, and organisms that live only on the parasites.

The largest animals are the slow ones — great grazing platforms that move across the terraces on dozens of stubby legs, their bodies low and broad, their mouths a continuous fringe of filtering bristles that scrape the films from the rock. They are the size of houses, and they move so slowly that you can walk faster than they do, and they live so long that they might be older than the city. They are not intelligent. They are not tame. They are simply *there*, in numbers that ensure you will never be out of sight of one, and the city has been built around their routes and their habits with the same patience that the rock has been built around the water.

## V. The City

The city is called Sinterhold, or it has been called that for as long as anyone has been writing things down, which is a very long time. It is built on the plateau, *into* the plateau, *of* the plateau.

The oldest parts of the city are carved directly into the sinter terraces — rooms and passages and cisterns excavated from the soft mineral deposit, their walls still weeping moisture, their floors gently damp. The stone — if it is stone — is easy to cut when first exposed, and it hardens as it dries, so the oldest structures were dug and then left to cure, and they have cured for centuries, and they are now harder than they were when they were laid down as sediment. The walls are banded with the same colors as the natural terraces: white and yellow and rust and pale green, and the city and the land are the same color, the same material, the same process. From a distance, the oldest districts are almost invisible against the plateau.

The newer parts of the city are built *up* from the terraces, using blocks cut from the sinter and bonded with mineral cements that are essentially the same material in a different form. The construction is dense, layered, stacked — buildings that are six or eight or ten stories, each story smaller than the one below, so that they step back like the terraces themselves. The roofs are collecting surfaces, gently sloped, channeling condensation into cisterns. Every building in Sinterhold is a water-harvesting device. The city drinks the sky.

And then there is the industry.

Sinterhold is not a pretty city, or it is pretty in the way that a functioning machine is pretty. The mineral water that weeps from every surface is not just water — it is a solution of dissolved salts, metals, sulfur compounds, and the chemical byproducts of the chemosynthetic films. This is valuable. This is the reason the city exists.

The industrial quarter — which is not a quarter but a district that wraps around the older core like a belt — is a landscape of evaporation pans, crystallization vats, filtration presses, and distillation columns. The pans are shallow and vast, heated from below by furnaces that burn a dense, resinous fuel extracted from the deep strata, and the water evaporates slowly in the warm air, leaving behind layers of salts that are scraped and sorted and processed. The crystallization vats are where the more valuable compounds are precipitated out in controlled conditions, and the filtration presses squeeze the films and organic matter from the water for processing into feedstock and chemical precursors. The distillation columns rise like towers, their surfaces streaked with mineral bloom, their bases surrounded by pools of condensate that are themselves harvested.

The sound of the industrial district is constant: the low rumble of the furnaces, the clatter of the scrapers, the hiss of steam, the rhythmic thud of the presses, the shout of workers, the creak of the wind-driven ventilators that turn slowly on their towers. It is loud, and it has been loud for so long that the city's residents hear it the way you hear your own heartbeat — as a presence so constant it becomes a kind of silence.

The electricity — for there is electricity — comes from the temperature gradient between the warm surface and the cooler deep wells, and from the slow, steady wind that crosses the plateau, and from the chemical potential of the mineral waters themselves, harnessed in cells that are essentially artificial versions of the chemosynthetic films. The grid is old and layered and patched, and it hums at a frequency that is felt in the teeth more than heard.

## VI. The Sound

The plateau is loud.

There is the hum of the flyers, a constant low drone that rises and falls with the hour — louder in the warm afternoons when they are most active, softer in the cool of night when they settle on every available surface in layers so dense that the buildings are coated with them, a living blanket that moves and breathes. There is the drip and trickle of water, everywhere, from every surface, a percussion that is never the same twice but never stops. There is the industrial sound from the belt of factories. There is the deeper sound of the great grazers, a subsonic rumble that you feel in your chest when they are near, and the clicking and scraping of their mouthparts, and the occasional deep, resonant call that they make for reasons no one has determined — a sound like a bell the size of a building, rung once, then silence for an hour.

At night, the flyers settle and the industrial district never sleeps and the water never stops and the great grazers continue their slow passage across the terraces, and the sound becomes a lower, deeper thing — the hum of the city's machines, the drip of water, the occasional bell-call of a grazer, the rustle of a billion wings shifting in sleep.

## VII. The Time

Nothing here is fast.

The city has been growing for longer than its records go back, and it grows still, but slowly — a new terrace added here, a new vat installed there, a new channel cut to redirect a seep. The erosion of the plateau is established, in balance: the water dissolves and deposits at roughly the same rate, and the formations that exist today are the same formations that existed a thousand years ago, give or take the slow accretion of a hand's width of new sinter. The city's oldest structures have been weathering for so long that their surfaces have developed the same patina as the natural rock — the same streaks of mineral bloom, the same rounding of edges, the same soft, porous texture. You cannot tell the oldest construction from the natural formation.

The fauna lives on the same timescale. The great grazers are decades old, centuries old, and they will not be hurried. The flyers live fast and die young, but their swarms are continuous, a standing wave of generation after generation, and the swarm is older than the city. The chemosynthetic films grow and are grazed and grow again in a cycle that has no seasons, only the slow pulse of the deep water rising and falling over epochs.

The people of Sinterhold — and there are people, though they are not the dominant presence the fauna are — have adapted to this timescale. They build slowly. They plan in generations. They do not demolish; they accreté. They add to what exists, and they wait for the new construction to cure and harden and take on the patina of age, and they do not distinguish between the natural and the built, because the distinction has never been meaningful here.

## VIII. The Light

The light is normal. The sun rises and sets. The sky is sometimes clear and sometimes filled with the storms that are born and die above the plateau. The condensation haze softens everything, gives the light a quality of diffusion, of being everywhere and nowhere, so that shadows are soft-edged and the colors of the mineral terraces are muted and gentle.

At midday, when the sun is high and the haze thins, the plateau gleams — the wet surfaces reflecting, the flyers' wings flashing, the industrial towers winking with condensation. It is almost beautiful. At dusk, the colors deepen — the rusts and yellows and celadons intensifying, the white sinter going pink and then grey, the haze turning gold and then violet and then dark. The flyers settle. The grazers continue their slow movement, dark shapes against the fading terraces. The industrial district lights its furnaces and the glow is visible from anywhere in the city, a warm pulse in the growing dark.

At night, the sky is full of stars — the plateau is high enough and dry enough above the condensation layer that the stars are sharp and numerous — and the flyers are quiet, and the water drips, and the grazers rumble, and the city hums, and the plateau exists in the dark as it exists in the light: wet, warm, dense with life, patient beyond measure, and utterly, completely itself.
