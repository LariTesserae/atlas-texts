The Wall runs east–west along the northern edge of the plateau, two hundred meters of dark volcanic rock, stepped and terraced by its own long sweating. It is not wet the way a rain-wet rock is wet; it is wet the way skin is wet. Water moves through its pores and fractures and reaches the surface by capillary action, by condensation, by the simple fact that the stone is warm and the air is warmer and the moisture has nowhere else to go. It beads on the face, gathers in the horizontal steps, runs in thin lines down the vertical ones. The lines are old. They have been running for centuries, and they have carved shallow channels, and the channels have deposited more mineral, and the Wall has built itself up, slowly, a few centimeters a year. The Wall is growing. The town knows this.

The town is Velo. It lies along the base of the Wall, spread thin — a scatter of low, dark buildings across a kilometer of white crust, each one separated from the next by open ground, by pipe runs, by rectangular tanks. No two buildings touch. The gaps are deliberate; the ground is warm and sometimes the crust gives way, and a house that stands alone can be abandoned without taking its neighbors. The buildings are cast basalt or sinter block, thick-walled, round-cornered by long weathering. Their roofs are flat and white with salt. Their walls are dark and streaked where the condensation runs off them. They look like they grew out of the plateau, which in a sense they did — the same material, the same process, just faster and with intention. The air smells of wet stone and hot metal and a faint, sweet sharpness, like a brewery, from the microbial pools.

At the west end, where the Wall is highest and the water comes down warmest, the plant stands: a cluster of tanks, heat exchangers, distillation columns, and a switchyard. The columns are the tallest things in Velo — thin, skeletal, wrapped in pipe, stained white at the joints where vapor escapes and deposits its mineral load. The switchyard hums, but the hum doesn't travel; you hear it only inside the fence. The plant takes brine from the wells, flashes off the steam, concentrates the liquor, and sends lithium and boron and silica down the mountain on the cableway. The power goes out on the high-tension lines. Velo is a machine for turning the Wall's sweat into money and light.

South of the town the plateau floor drops a little and the water collects — or is collected. The evaporation pools are a grid of shallow rectangles, hundreds of them, divided by low dikes of compacted salt. They are the color of the minerals they hold: turquoise, rust, sulfur yellow, white, a deep blue-green where the copper concentrates. From a distance they look like a map of a country that doesn't exist — provinces of color, borders of white. They are perfectly still. No wind reaches them, or the wind is so gentle that the surface never breaks. They mirror the sky and the Wall and the birds. The stillness is one of the things you notice first. The pools do not ripple. The mist does not stir them. They are liquid but they behave like solid.

The pools are alive. Brine shrimp, dense clouds of them, pale pink and white, filter-feeding on the microbial mats that coat the bottom. The mats are not plants — they are bacteria and archaea, chemosynthetic, feeding on the hot mineral water that seeps up through the pool floors. They are the base of the food web. Everything else eats them, or eats what eats them. The shrimp are eaten by the birds.

The birds are the reason you come to Velo, if you come. They are tall, long-legged waders, pale pink and white, with broad, spatulate bills. They stand in the shallows in their hundreds, motionless, heads down, filtering. They have no eyes. Their faces are smooth, unmarked, the bill emerging from a flat plane of pale feathers. They find the shrimp by sensing the chemical gradient of the water, the warmth of the mats, the faint electrical field of the microbial metabolism. They are not deaf — they hear something — but they are silent. They open their beaks and no sound comes, or a sound below hearing, a pressure you feel in your sternum.

And they move in unison. Not always — sometimes they stand for an hour without a wingbeat. But when they move, they move together. A thousand birds lift their heads at the same moment. A thousand birds take a step. A thousand birds rise from the pool in a single pale cloud, wheel once, and settle back in the same places. The coordination is perfect and unexplained. The people of Velo say the birds are one animal distributed across a thousand bodies. The people of Velo say a lot of things, in their low voices, in their signs.

At the pool margins, water boatmen row through the still water, and a pale moth flies in the warm mist, silent, slow. Small dark lizards bask on the warm rocks, quick and purposeful. The world has no green. Nothing photosynthesizes. The color is all mineral and microbial: white, rust, sulfur, turquoise, blue-green, and the pale pink of the birds and the shrimp.

The mist eats sound. It is not that Velo is quiet — there are pumps, there are trucks, there are voices — it is that the sound does not travel. Beyond a few hundred meters, the world is silent. You can watch a truck reverse across the square and hear nothing, see the driver's mouth moving and hear nothing. You can stand at the edge of the pools and watch ten thousand birds and hear nothing but your own breath and the soft creak of the salt crust under your feet. The silence is not empty. It is full — full of the sounds that are close to you, amplified by the lack of competition. Your own heartbeat. The tick of cooling metal. The faint, almost imagined whisper of water moving in the pipes. The silence is a medium you move through, like water, like air.

The people of Velo are quiet by culture and by necessity. They sign more than they speak — a local sign language, old, with a vocabulary rich in words for water, for heat, for mineral, for the states of the mist. Children learn it before they learn to read. Outsiders find it eerie. The people of Velo find shouting painful, not just physically but morally. A loud voice is a voice that doesn't understand where it is. The town has a reputation in the lowlands as a place of silence, a retreat, a penance. Some come to recover from noise. Some come because they have nothing left to say.

The Wall grows. The town retreats. The oldest quarter is closest to the Wall, and it is half-abandoned, the buildings being slowly entombed in white sinter, the streets become shallow channels, the doorways are half-blocked by mineral accretions that look like wax, like fat, like candle drippings. The people chip it back from the foundations, or they don't, and the Wall takes another room. The new town is further out, on the pool flats, where the ground is warmer and the crust is thinner and the buildings have to be built on pilings. The town moves south at the speed of geology, which is faster than you'd think when the geology is wet. In a human lifetime, a street can be lost. In a generation, a district.

The air is warm and wet, always. There is no cold season. There is a season when the mist is thinner and the light is whiter and the pools shrink, and a season when the mist is thick and the Wall disappears and the town is a scatter of dark shapes in a white room. But the temperature barely varies. The ground is hot. The water is hot. The walls are warm to the touch. The people work in the early morning and the late afternoon, and the midday is for rest, when the light is too bright and the heat is too much. They live in the upper rooms in the thin-mist season and the lower rooms in the thick-mist season, or they live underground, in cuts into the rock behind the Wall, where the temperature is constant and the mist is thinner. Velo is layered — the street level for the pipes and the birds and the machines, the below level for the living, the above level for the tanks and the condensers and the pigment driers.

The sun is bright and the shadows are soft and the rainbows are constant — small ones near the Wall, large ones over the pools when the wind shifts. The pigment is a secondary trade and a point of pride. The pools produce colors that the lowland cities cannot make — a turquoise that is almost luminous, a rust that is deep and warm, a blue-green that looks like a mineral and a sky at once. The town ships it in sealed drums, and it is used for paint, for dye, for the glazing of ceramics. "Velo blue" is known in the cities of the coast. The town's own buildings are mostly left dark, the color of the rock, but the tank farms and the pipe runs are painted in the pool colors, so that from a distance the industrial quarter is a grid of turquoise and rust and white, a second pool system, a man-made evaporation flat.

The rule of Velo is that the water gives and the water takes. The Wall gives water and mineral and heat and light, and the Wall takes the town, slowly, one room at a time. The town takes the water and turns it into power and pigment and profit, and the town gives back silence and retreat. The birds take the shrimp and give back beauty and strangeness. The people take the mineral and give back the town, the town that is always leaving, always being rebuilt a little further from the Wall, always a little quieter, always a little more pale. If you stand at the edge of the oldest quarter at dawn, when the mist is thick and the light is coming through it in long white shafts, you can see the Wall advancing — not moving, but growing, the white sinter creeping over the doorsteps, the windowsills, the thresholds of the abandoned houses — and the birds standing in the shallows in their hundreds, eyeless, silent, motionless, filtering the warm mineral water, and the whole world bright and wet and quiet and hot, and you understand that the place is not ending. It is just becoming more itself.
