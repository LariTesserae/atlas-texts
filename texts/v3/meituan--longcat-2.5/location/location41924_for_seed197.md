# The Hollow of the World-Root

The space is inside something. That is the first fact and the last, the condition of everything that follows — you are within a body, and the body is alive, and the body is so vast that its interior is a landscape.

The walls are warm. They breathe, a slow expansion and contraction you feel in your inner ear before your eyes, a tide of tissue that rises and falls over minutes, over hours. The material is woody but not wood — more like the inside of a stem magnified past all reason, fibrous and veined, with channels running through it that you can hear when you press your ear to the surface: a deep, slow pulse, the movement of fluid through conduits wider than rivers. The color is the color of living things — green-brown, gold-brown, the brown of earth and the green of leaves and the gold of sap — and it shifts in patches, in zones, as if the organism's chemistry varies from place to place, as if different districts of this interior have different metabolisms, different moods.

The chamber is vast. Cathedral-vast, grove-vast, the vastness of a single room that contains a hamlet. The ceiling — if it is a ceiling — arches up into shadow and then into a kind of luminous haze where translucent membranes thin enough to let light through create a diffuse, directionless glow. The light is normal light — the light of an overcast afternoon, or a bright morning filtered through layers of living tissue — and it comes from everywhere and nowhere. There are openings above, you realize, places where the organism has grown thin or torn or deliberately opened, and through these the outside world sends its illumination down in broad, soft columns that move slowly as the sun moves, that shift in color as clouds pass, that fade and brighten with the hours. The light is not dim. It is not the light of a cave. It is the light of a space that is open to the sky in ways you cannot see directly, that is roofed with something more like skin than stone.

The floor is not flat. It rises and falls in slow undulations, worn smooth by uncountable passages, polished to a soft sheen that reflects the light from above in muted, wavering patches. In places it dips into pools — still, dark water that has condensed on the walls and collected in the low places, that has been collecting for years, for decades, for centuries. The pools are not stagnant. They are fed by the constant beading of moisture on every surface, by the slow drip and trickle and seep of water through the organism's tissue, and they drain through channels in the floor, through root-pores and membrane-pores, into the deeper systems of the body. The water is warm. The air is warm. The temperature is the temperature of a body, of a living thing, and it does not vary much — it is always warm, always the warmth of growth and metabolism and slow, vast life.

The air is damp. It is thick with moisture, with the smell of green growth and wet earth and something sweeter — the smell of the organism itself, its sap or its breath or the chemical signature of its cells. Water beads on every surface. It drips. The sound of dripping is everywhere, a constant percussion, each drop amplified by the acoustics of the hollow space into something that rings and echoes and overlaps with every other drop until the sound becomes a kind of continuous shimmer, a curtain of noise that you can hear in your bones.

And the organism is loud. The pulse you feel in the walls is audible — a deep, subsonic throb that you hear in your chest and your teeth, a rhythm so slow that you can't quite tell if it's a heartbeat or the sound of growth, of cells dividing and expanding, of the slow crack of fiber lengthening. It is not rhythmic in any way you can count. It shifts, it varies, it has patterns that are too long for you to perceive as patterns — a quickening, a slowing, a moment of arrhythmic flutter, a return to the deep pulse. Over this, layered on top, is the sound of the fauna.

The fauna is abundant. The chamber is alive with movement. Things fly — not birds, exactly, though some have wings, but things that are part insect and part seed and part something else, things with translucent bodies and too many joints, things that hum and click and whir as they move through the air in clouds and streams and solitary drifting paths. Some are the size of your hand. Some are the size of your thumb. Some are smaller, so small that they move through the air like dust, like pollen, like the particulate matter of the organism's breath. They fill the central void, they thread through the columns of light, they settle on the walls and the galleries and the platforms in densities that turn the surfaces into a seething, living carpet. Things crawl on the walls — slow, methodical, grazing on the living surface, leaving trails of moisture or polish or faint luminescence. Things move through the pools — sinuous, pale, eyeless or many-eyed, disturbing the surface tension in expanding rings that intersect and interfere with each other in patterns of extraordinary complexity. Things live in the passages that open off the main chamber — dark holes in the walls that lead to other spaces, other chambers, the layered interior of the organism — and these things watch from the openings, or reach out, or call to each other in voices that range from clicks to whistles to something almost like song.

The sound is deafening. It is not one sound but the sum of all sounds, the organism and its inhabitants and the water and the air moving through the passages and the creak and settle of living wood and the rustle of growth and the calls and responses and the drip and the pulse and the hum — all of it filling the space, reflecting off the curved walls, amplifying in the hollow center, so that you cannot hear your own thoughts, cannot hear your own footsteps, can only feel the sound as a physical pressure, a presence, a weight. It is the sound of a world that is fully occupied, fully alive, fully in motion, and it does not stop. There is no silence here. There is no quiet. There is only the sound, and the variations in the sound, and the places where the sound is slightly less or slightly more.

And yet there are places of relative quiet — niches and ledges and chambers where the acoustics shift and the sound drops to a murmur, and in these places you can hear other things: the organism's pulse more clearly, or the sound of your own blood, or something else — a kind of listening presence, as if the organism itself is aware of you, is attending to you, is turning its vast, slow attention in your direction. You feel it as a pressure behind your eyes, as a change in the quality of the air, as a sense of being regarded by something that does not have eyes, that does not have a face, that has only the chamber and the walls and the space and the knowledge of your presence within it.

The structures are grown. That is the only word. The organism has produced them — or they have been produced within it, guided by the fauna, or by the organism's own intention, if it has intention. There are galleries that run along the walls at different heights, connected by ramps and spiral channels and vertical shafts that you can climb or descend. The galleries are wide enough for several creatures to pass, or narrow enough that you must turn sideways, and they are smooth with use, worn into the living tissue by generations of feet, of bodies, of the constant traffic of the settlement. There are platforms that jut out into the central void — observation points, or resting places, or places where the fauna gathers in densities that turn the surface into a seething, living carpet. Some platforms are connected by bridges of living fiber, by spanning roots, by arches of tissue that have been encouraged to grow in particular directions and have been worn smooth by use. Some are isolated, accessible only by flight or by climbing, and these are occupied by creatures that are different from the creatures on the lower galleries — solitary things, or paired things, or things that are so still they might be part of the organism itself until they move.

There are chambers that branch off the main space — smaller, more intimate, their walls more densely veined, their light more golden or more blue or more green depending on the thickness and character of the membrane above them. Some chambers are round, some are elongated, some are irregular in ways that suggest they have been shaped by use rather than by growth — hollowed out, worn down, expanded by the creatures that live in them. Some are filled with water to varying depths — pools within pools, lakes within the lake. Some are dry, or drier, and their walls are covered with growths that are not the organism's tissue — fungal mats, lichen colonies, moss beds, things that live on the organism but are not the organism, that form their own ecosystems, their own micro-landscapes. Some chambers are occupied by single creatures, or pairs, or families, or colonies — the organism's interior is divided into territories, into niches, into habitats, and the fauna has organized itself into a community, a society, a city.

The architecture is layered. It has depth and history. The oldest parts — the deepest chambers, the lowest galleries — are worn smooth, polished by uncountable passages, and the organism's tissue there is darker, denser, more scarred. You can see the history of the place in the walls: the layers of growth, the repairs, the places where damage was healed and the tissue is thicker, more knotted, more textured. Newer growth is lighter in color, softer, still shaped by whatever force or intelligence guides it. And everywhere there is evidence of the fauna's role — chew marks, polish, the removal of material, the encouragement of growth in certain directions. The settlement — for it is a settlement, a town, a city of living tissue — is a collaboration between the organism and its inhabitants, or it is the organism's own body organized into habitable form, or both. There are districts. There are specialized areas — the pool districts, the gallery districts, the high platforms, the deep chambers. There is infrastructure: the channels that carry water, the passages that carry air, the shafts that connect the levels, the membranes that control light. There is organization. There is planning, or the biological equivalent of planning — the slow, emergent order of a system that has been evolving together for longer than you can imagine.

The erosion is complete. Everything is in balance. The organism is not young; it is ancient beyond measuring, and it has been in this state — this hollow, this chambered, this inhabited state — for so long that the forces that created it and the forces that wear it down have reached equilibrium. The water that condenses and drips and pools is the same water that has been cycling through this system for millennia, and the pools are not growing or shrinking, and the wear on the galleries is not increasing, and the organism's growth exactly replaces what is lost. The place is finished. It is in its own state. It is what it is, and it has been what it is for longer than the world outside has had its current shape. You can feel this. You can feel the age of the place, the depth of time, the sense of something that has been here so long that it has become part of the structure of the world, that the world has grown around it, that the world and the organism are no longer separate things.

And yet it is active. The ecosystem is in motion. There are cycles — daily cycles of light and temperature as the organism's upper reaches respond to the sun outside, seasonal cycles of growth and dormancy, longer cycles that you can only perceive as patterns in the fauna's behavior or the water's chemistry or the organism's pulse. There are predators and prey, and their populations rise and fall in patterns that the settlement's inhabitants understand and respond to. There are spore releases — sudden, visible clouds of particulate matter that fill the air and settle on every surface and are gathered by the fauna in densities that turn the galleries into crowded, busy thoroughfares. There are pollination events — the organism's reproduction, or part of it, a process that involves the fauna in ways that are not entirely clear, that involve the transfer of material from one chamber to another, from one level to another, by creatures that seem to know exactly where to go and what to do. There are emergences — the winged forms rising from the pools in great numbers, climbing the columns of light, dispersing into the upper reaches of the chamber and beyond, into the passages that lead to other spaces, other chambers, the vast interior of the organism. There are returns — the flyers coming back to their roosts in the high galleries, settling in densities that turn the platforms into living, rustling, chirping surfaces. All of it timed, all of it coordinated, all of it part of a system that is not static but is stable, not dead but in balance.

The weirdness is in the details. It accumulates. You notice it first in small things: a passage that seems to lead somewhere and then doesn't, or leads somewhere different than it did before. A chamber that is larger on the inside than the outside. A place where the walls are so thin you can see through them — not to the outside world, but to another chamber, or to a kind of luminous void, or to something that doesn't correspond to any space you can name. A place where gravity seems to shift, where you can climb the wall as easily as the floor, where the organism's internal geometry doesn't match the geometry of the world you came from. A place where the pulse in the walls is faster, or slower, or arrhythmic, and the fauna behaves differently — more agitated, or more still — and you realize that the organism has something like a mood, or a state, or a mind, and that the space responds to it.

And then there are the deeper strangenesses. The organism is so old, so vast, so complex that it has become something more than a plant. It has developed — or always had — a kind of awareness. Not a mind like yours, not a mind like anything's, but a presence, a watching, a slow and vast attention that moves through the chambers and passages like weather, like a tide. You feel it sometimes as a pressure, a weight, a sense of being regarded. You feel it sometimes as a change in the light, or the sound, or the temperature, or the smell. You feel it sometimes as a kind of knowing — a sudden, inexplicable understanding of something about the place, or yourself, or the relationship between you and the organism, that arrives not as thought but as sensation, as certainty. You feel it sometimes as a response — you think something, or want something, or fear something, and the space changes around you, a passage opens or closes, a chamber shifts, a platform extends or retracts, and you realize that the organism has heard you, has understood you, has answered.

And the boundary between inside and outside is not fixed. There are places where the organism's tissue thins to nothing, where you can step through into — not the outside world, but another part of the interior, or a part of the interior that corresponds to a different outside, or a space that is neither inside nor outside but something else. There are places where the organism's growth has enclosed other things — rocks, soil, the remains of older growth, the bones of creatures — and these are visible through the translucent walls, suspended in the living tissue, incorporated, digested slowly or not at all. There are places where the organism has grown around openings in the earth, or around the roots of other plants, or around the structures of other organisms, and these too are visible, are part of the architecture, are part of the settlement.

The place is a world. It is a single organism and it is an ecosystem and it is a settlement and it is a landscape, all at once. It is small enough to have boundaries — you can walk from one side to the other, can map it, can know its extent — and it is vast enough to contain depths and heights and distances that feel infinite, that feel like they could go on forever. It is empty — the central void is vast, is mostly space, is a cathedral of air and light and sound — and it is full — the walls are alive, the galleries are inhabited, the air is thick with flying things, the pools are thick with swimming things, the passages are thick with crawling things, and everywhere there is growth, is life, is the organism's presence.

It is warm. It is damp. It is loud. It is old. It is in balance. It is active. It is alive. And it is aware, in some vast, slow, alien way, of you.
