The approach is along a ridge that becomes a causeway, then a bridge, then a tunnel through a wall of vertical stone columns. The columns are thirty feet tall, hexagonal, packed so tightly you can barely slip a hand between them. They are wet—mineral water seeps from the stone in thin films that catch the light and run down the faces in rivulets. The rivulets collect in channels at the base and flow away in streams that cross the causeway in fords. The sound begins here—a low hum, a dripping, a hiss of water on hot stone. The first notes of the chord that will accompany you for the rest of your time in the city.

The wall opens onto the caldera.

Ten miles across. A mile deep. Filled with the city.

The city is travertine and vegetation. Stone precipitated from hot springs in layer upon layer over spans of time that don't bear thinking about, and plants that grew into and through the stone until the two are inseparable. The towers are built of layered travertine—cream and rust and pale green, banded like agate—ribbed with root systems that have become stone themselves, or perhaps were always stone. The domes are compressed moss and mineral accretion, smooth and glistening. The arches are petrified vine, or vine that has become stone, or both—the transition is impossible to locate. Everything is wet. Everything glistens.

The city rises in tiers from the caldera floor. The lowest tier is a maze of low structures, chambers and corridors and vaults, all interconnected, all open to the sky. The middle tier is the densest—towers and domes and arches packed so closely they form a continuous surface, a fabric of stone and vegetable matter. The upper tier is a forest of spires, thin and tall, connected by bridges that span hundreds of feet. The spires are crowned with plants—ferns, bromeliads, orchids without flowers—that hang downward in curtains of green. The overall effect is of a city. The same density, the same organization, the same sense of purpose as a human city. But grown, not built.

Streets run between the structures in curving lines that follow the contours of the caldera floor. The streets are paved with stone worn smooth by water and by something else, something that left no marks but wore the stone anyway, over and over, in the same paths, forever. The streets cross streams in fords—the water is warm, nearly hot, and it steams in the slightly cooler air. The fords are paved with stone ridged for traction, though no one has ever needed traction here.

Plazas open in irregular shapes between the structures. Some are small, a few yards across. Others are vast, hundreds of yards, surrounded by tiers of balconies and terraces. The plazas are paved with the same smooth stone, and the same thin film of water flows across them, toward channels at their edges. The channels carry the water away to other plazas, other streets, other parts of the city. The water is in constant motion but the system is perfectly balanced—the same amount of water enters the city as leaves it, the same amount flows through each channel, the same film covers each plaza. The hydrology is perfect. The hydrology is frozen.

There are no tools. No artifacts. No objects of any kind. No pottery, no metal, no wheels, no writing, no marks made by any hand. The city is pure structure. It is the only thing here. It has always been the only thing here.

There are no animals. No birds cross the sky—the sky is empty, a dome of pale blue visible between the spires. No insects drift in the humid air—the air is thick with moisture but empty of wings. No fish move in the water—the water is clear and warm and empty. No worms turn in the stone—there is no soil, only mineral and root. The absence is total and immediately, profoundly wrong. You expect—you need—the flicker of wings, the dart of fish, the buzz of insects, and it isn't there. The city is a model of itself, a diorama, a city under glass. The plants don't need animals. They are not growing, not reproducing, not dying. They are just there, lush and green and static, leaves spread to catch the light, roots gripping stone, everything in perfect balance.

The vegetation is lush beyond reason. Tree-ferns thirty feet tall with fronds that arch over the streets. Mosses in every shade of green, from yellow-green to blue-green to green so dark it's almost black, covering every surface in velvet. Hanging vines that descend from the upper tier in ropes as thick as your thigh, some reaching the ground, some stopping short, all glistening with water. Broad-leafed plants with leaves two feet across, their surfaces beaded with condensation. Bromeliads clustered in the crooks of branches and the joints of stone, their cups filled with water that overflows in thin streams. And orchids—thousands of orchids, millions, covering every surface in a carpet of green leaves and no flowers. No flowers at all. The plants have leaves and roots and no flowers, as if the flowers were omitted, as if the plants were designed by something that had heard of flowers but never seen one.

The sound is deafening. The water—dripping, running, pouring, splashing, the low roar of a waterfall somewhere in the city's heart—merges with the hiss of steam from vents in the stone and the hum of the city itself. The hum is a resonance, a standing wave in the stone, a vibration you feel in your teeth and your bones and the fluid of your inner ear. The combination is a chord—low, mid, high, a drone that never changes. It has been sounding for as long as the city has existed. It will sound for as long as the city exists. It is loud enough to make thought difficult. You shout and your voice is swallowed, absorbed into the chord, becomes part of the drone. You cannot hear yourself think. You cannot hear yourself breathe. The sound is the city and the city is the sound.

The heat is intense. The caldera traps it—volcanic heat from below, solar heat from above, and the thick humid air holds it all. The air is visible, a shimmer, a haze that softens the edges of everything. Steam rises in columns from vents in the stone, columns that are perfectly vertical and perfectly still, as if the air is solid. Breathing is like breathing warm syrup. The heat and the wet and the sound combine into an assault on the senses that is almost unbearable. You sweat instantly and constantly. Your clothes are soaked. Your skin is never dry. The water you sweat is replaced by the water in the air, the condensation that settles on your skin, the fine mist that hangs in the streets. You are never thirsty. You are never dry. You are part of the water cycle of the city, a temporary, mobile part, but still part of it.

And nothing changes.

The water flows but the level never changes. The steam rises but the pressure never drops. The sound never varies—not in pitch, not in volume, not in timbre. The light is always the same quality—bright, diffuse, filtered through humid air, the sun a white disk that moves across the sky in a perfect arc but casts the same light at every point in its arc. The plants are lush but nothing grows. A leaf that falls—and leaves do fall, occasionally, in a slow, spiraling drift—is back on the branch by the time it would have reached the ground. Or it falls and stays where it fell and the plant is exactly as it was. Or it falls and is still falling, suspended in the air, turning slowly, and the plant is exactly as it was. All of these are true. None of them matter. The equilibrium holds.

The city is in perfect balance. Every force matched by an equal and opposite force. Every input matched by an output. Erosion matched by accretion. Evaporation matched by condensation. The travertine dissolves at the same rate it precipitates. The plants grow at the same rate they decay—or rather, they don't grow and don't decay, but if they did, the rates would match. The city is stable, frozen, eternal.

And the brokenness is frozen too.

Look at the towers. The same tower appears at three different scales—full-sized, and as a detail on a larger tower, and as a tiny extrusion on the surface of that detail. The city is recursive. It contains itself. Every district contains the whole city in miniature. Every miniature contains the whole city in smaller miniature. The detail is infinite. The complexity is infinite. And it is all the same. You can walk up to a tower and see, on its surface, a tiny version of the tower you're standing next to, and on the surface of that tiny version, an even tinier version, and so on, down to the limit of vision. The recursion is perfect. The recursion is stable. The tiny towers don't grow. The large towers don't shrink. The relationship between them is fixed forever.

The streets don't add up. Walk in a straight line and you return to where you started, but the walk is longer than the circumference should allow. A plaza that looked a hundred yards across takes an hour to cross. A tower that seemed close recedes as you approach. Distances expand and contract according to rules that are consistent within each district but change at the boundaries. Cross a line—you can see the line, a subtle change in the color of the stone, or the texture of the moss, or the pitch of the hum—and the scale shifts. Suddenly the buildings are twice as tall, or half as tall, or the same height but you are twice as small. The shift is instantaneous and stable. You can stand with one foot in one district and one foot in another and feel the difference—the gravity is different, the air pressure is different, the light is different, the sound is different. Both feet are real. Both districts are real. The boundary is real. And it is all stable, fixed, eternal.

The architecture is impossible. Arches that support nothing and span chasms—the arch is a curve of stone that begins on one side of the chasm and ends on the other, but there is no keystone, no mortar, no connection between the stones of the arch. The stones are separate, floating, held in place by nothing, and the arch is stable. Cantilevers that extend too far to stand—a tower that projects horizontally from a cliff face, extending two hundred feet into the air, with no support beneath it, and it is stable. Buildings that are also the same building seen from different angles—a structure that is a spiral when viewed from the north and a dome when viewed from the south, and both views are correct, and the structure is both, and it is stable. The city is not consistent with itself—it obeys local rules that change from district to district, and the boundaries are sharp. But the rules are stable. The inconsistencies are stable. The city is broken in exactly the same way, forever.

At the center of the caldera: a pool. Perfectly circular, perfectly still, perfectly black. The water reflects nothing—not the sky, not the towers, not you. The sound of the city is muffled at its edge, as if the pool absorbs it. The chord is quieter here—still loud, still deafening, but quieter, as if the pool drinks the noise. Steam rises from the surface but the steam doesn't drift—it rises in columns that are perfectly vertical and perfectly still, as if the air above the pool is solid. The pool is the still point, the eye of the storm, the place where the equilibrium is most perfect. Nothing moves on its surface. Nothing has ever moved on its surface. The pool is the heart of the city, and it is dead—not dead as in lifeless, but dead as in stopped, frozen, absolute.

Around the pool: a plaza. The plaza is paved with stone that is perfectly flat, perfectly level, perfectly smooth—smoother than any human work, smoother than any natural stone, a surface like black glass. The stone is wet. Water flows across it in a thin film, toward the pool. The film is perfectly even, perfectly uniform, a sheet of water a millimeter thick that moves at a constant speed toward the edge of the pool and pours over. The water flows into the pool and the pool never rises. The water leaves the pool—through channels, through seeps, through the stone—and the pool never falls. The cycle is perfect. The equilibrium is perfect.

This is the city. This has always been the city. This will always be the city.

No one grew it. No one built it. No one lives in it. There are no tools, no devices, no artifacts, no objects. There are no animals. There are no people. There is only the city, the water, the stone, the plants, the heat, the sound, and the perfect, frozen, eternal equilibrium.

And the sound. The sound is deafening. It is the sound of water on stone, of steam in channels, of the hum of the city, of the absence of animals, of the absence of everything that isn't the city. It is the sound of a world that has found its balance and will never, ever change.
