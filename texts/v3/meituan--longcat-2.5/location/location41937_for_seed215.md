# THE WEEP

*A high, wet plain the size of a subcontinent. On its shore, the city of Hush.*

---

## The land

Between the Raw Teeth and the sky, the Weep stretches some fifteen hundred kilometers in every direction — a plateau averaging two thousand three hundred meters, and still rising. It climbs three to five centimeters a year: a hand's width in a lifetime, measured precisely by the brass benchmarks outside Hush. Because it rises, it is young. Erosion has only just begun its work.

There are no rounded hills here. The land is broken into frost polygons — stone tiles one and two meters across, sharp-edged, their cracks still empty of soil. Fault scarps expose blue-grey rock no weather has touched. Young volcanic cones, some barely centuries old, stand unsoftened. The gorges run straight, cut down fault lines like knife wounds, and the rivers that carved them do not meander — they run a short distance and sink into the ground. Every depression holds a lake, and no river leaves the plateau. The Weep is a closed bowl. All water that falls here stays here; the sky borrows from the lakes and returns it.

## Water

Wetness is the default state. The water table sits at the surface — step off a path and your boot sinks to the ankle. Saturated mats of moss and sedge, the bofedales, span hundreds of kilometers in continuous green-gold quaking swaths. The scarps weep without pause: groundwater squeezed from rising rock, beading on fresh stone, building travertine lips and salt crusts at every seep. The rock is still sweating itself into shape.

The air condenses on everything. Dawn is a whiteout that lifts, reluctantly, by mid-morning; until then every surface drips. Rain is light and frequent — less a storm than a thickening of the atmosphere, a slow return of what the lakes exhaled. And at the center of it all lies the Inland Sea: a hundred and fifty kilometers across, salt, two meters deep on average, mirror-flat, so large it has wind-tides and a horizon no one standing on its shore has ever seen. It freezes at night in great plates. At dawn the plates crack and grind against one another — a low sound, felt in the sternum more than heard.

## Light, weather, sound

The air is thin. The sun at noon is fierce, but the fog and high cloud diffuse it into a luminous pearl-grey-gold; shadows fall blue-violet and sharp. Twilights last an hour. The plateau glows rose, then indigo, then goes dark all at once, like a lamp turned off.

The Weep makes its own weather. Fog tides roll off the Inland Sea twice daily, slow white rivers that drain into the bofedales. Afternoon convection builds small thunderheads over the Teeth, brief and local. And every night, without exception, in every season, the frost arrives — the hard reset that the whole world here runs on.

Sound is scarce. The air is still more often than not, and sound carries far across it, so the place has learned to be quiet. The loudest steady noises are water: drip, seep, the tick of contracting stone, ice settling in the canals. The city's engines hum a fifth below the range of speech — you feel them in the floor, never quite hear them. Voices are kept low. Even the birds are mostly silent kinds. The quiet here is not emptiness. It is density of attention.

## Vegetation and fauna

There are no trees. The tallest plant on the Weep is a willow you could step over. What grows: dense grey-green cushion plants — *moon cushions*, warm and springy underfoot — moss carpets, crustose lichens on the raw rock, bunchgrass on the slopes, and in the warm season a scatter of gentians and forget-me-nots no taller than a thumbnail.

The real pasture is underwater. Diatom mats and algae coat the lakes, and on them feed clouds of tiny shrimp-like creatures that turn the shallows pink. On them feed the birds — and there are more birds than people in a hundred cities. Avocets, stilts, plovers, phalaropes in their millions; a great white filter-feeder like a pale rose flamingo on stilt legs, the rose-wader; and the hush-goose, a big white goose that has no call at all. At dusk the midge hatch rises in a gold-brown veil so thick it dims the sun, and its collective sound is like light rain on stone — the quietest abundance imaginable.

The mammals come to the birds. Where the flocks roost, their guano builds up into *green islands*: the lushest swards on the plateau, ankle-deep and vivid. A small camelid, shoulder-high with broad soft feet, grazes them in herds kept by the people of Hush. Viscacha-like rodents swarm the talus slopes. A fox-like canid with round ears follows the herds. And on the ridges walks the veil-cat — a large predator the exact color of unweathered rock, patient, nearly silent, hunting the green islands at dawn.

The loop is the whole ecology: **lake → shrimp → birds → guano → grass → herds → people.** The land is a boarding house. The lakes are the pasture.

## The city of Hush

Two hundred thousand people spread across sixty kilometers of fault-block ridge and pile-built wetland — a scatter, not a concentration, threaded together by infrastructure. Streets are half-canal; the grid follows the faults, because the land dictates. Boats are as common as carts.

The buildings are bone-white panels of *rime-steel*: ice grown by seeding crystal into molds, its lattice engineered at the nanoscale so that it cannot melt at surface pressure. It never weeps. A wall that weeps is a wall failing, and the phrase is spoken softly. Warmth is piped through the walls — heated water drawn from the hot seeps, the city's true currency. At dusk the windows glow amber against the white. Cold is piped too, a cold grid, for food and for the ice structures themselves.

Power comes from the frost engines, which run on the daily freeze-thaw: the city's heartbeat is the plateau's heartbeat. Every building stands on screw-piles, and each season the districts are re-leveled as the land rises — *the Leveling*, a civic ritual of calm, jacks creaking in unison. Because the ground climbs, the old streets are now basements. Hush is deeper than it is wide; the market district of a century ago is the cellar of the market district today.

Above the city, vast fog-nets hang on the ridge and drink the mist — that is the water supply. Below, the harbor opens onto the Inland Sea, where flat silt-skiffs pole through the pink shallows and haul shrimp and algae-cakes to the stacks. The dead are given to the sea. The sea keeps the bones. Time in Hush is measured by the drip-clock of the civic well, a brass counter that marks each hour with a single falling drop.

## The rules

- Water never leaves the Weep. It only changes altitude.
- The land rises. Build so you can rise with it, or be buried.
- Night freezes. Day thaws. Everything runs on that pulse.
- The city's ice never melts. Warmth is made, not found.
- The loudest thing on the Weep is water — and water is quiet.

## A dawn

Stand on the ridge above Hush before first light. The fog is so total that the city is only a scatter of amber windows suspended in white. Below, the frost polygons are dripping in the dark — a million small ticks and sighs. Then the grey comes up out of the east, and the fog begins to drain toward the lakes like a tide going out, and for one hour the entire Weep shines like a pewter plate. The rose-waders lift from the shallows in a long pink ribbon. The veil-cat comes down from the Teeth and walks the green island in silence. The frost engines stutter and change key as the thaw begins. The first real sound of the day is the wind arriving off the Inland Sea — a low exhale, felt in the teeth, carrying the taste of cold minerals and a faint thread of sulfur from the warm seeps. Then the birds. Then the city, leveling itself another centimeter closer to the sky.
