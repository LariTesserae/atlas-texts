# The Hollow

The space is a vast underground cavern system — natural limestone dissolution chambers expanded over centuries by excavation and by the slow, persistent pressure of root mass. The ceiling ranges from fifteen to forty meters high, irregular, studded with stone pillars and with columns of compacted root that have fused and thickened into load-bearing members. The floor undulates — low mounds, shallow basins, channels carved by water over millennia. The total extent is perhaps a kilometer across, but you do not perceive it as one space. Walls of vegetation, constructed partitions, changes in level, and the simple density of growth divide it into chambers, groves, rooms — each one enclosed, each one connected to the next by passages that are themselves tunnels through the plant mass. You move through a sequence of rooms. Each one is packed.

Every surface is alive. The floor is a springy mat of roots and rhizomes interwoven with mosses, ferns, and low leafy growths — it gives slightly underfoot, warm and damp. The walls are vertical gardens layered from tiny liverwots in the crevices to substantial shrubs and small trees rooted in cracks and ledges. The ceiling hangs with vines, pendant roots, curtains of moisture that catch the light and fall as a constant soft rain. No bare rock is visible anywhere — the stone is present, you can feel it under the plant mass, see it where erosion has worn the vegetation thin, but it is always subsidiary. The dominant plants are large, long-lived species: thick-stemmed reeds and canes as tall as a person, broad-leafed things with leaves the size of doors, woody vines as thick as a torso, root systems that spread and fuse and re-fuse into structural members. They grow in dense, competitive masses — ground cover, undergrowth, mid-story, canopy — compressed into the vertical space. The color is overwhelmingly green, but in the dim light it ranges from bright lime where the lamps hit it to deep forest green to almost black in the shadows.

Water is everywhere. It condenses on the ceiling and upper surfaces, forming droplets that grow and fall — a constant soft rain, each drop catching the light, each drop striking a leaf or a pipe or a pool with a small clear sound. It runs down stems and leaves, follows the architecture of the plants, collects in channels and rills carved into the floor over centuries — channels that wind and branch and rejoin, that drop in small falls from one level to the next, that widen into pools and narrow into streams. The pools are still and dark, reflecting the dim light, their surfaces broken by falling drops and by the ripple of incoming water. The larger channels are underground rivers — they enter from one side and exit on the other, their surfaces rippling with current, their sound a low continuous roar that underlies everything. The air is saturated. Everything is wet. Surfaces glisten. Water drips from every overhang. The temperature is warm — twenty-five to thirty degrees — maintained by geothermal warmth from below and by the heat of the industrial machinery.

The city is built within and through the plant mass. Not on top of it — through it. Structures of metal, stone, and processed plant material — lumber, compressed fiber, resin, laminated stems — are interwoven with the living growth. Buildings are constructed in the spaces between large plants, within cavities in the plant mass, suspended from brackets attached to trunks and vines. Some structures are clearly built: walls of brick or concrete, roofs of corrugated metal, frames of steel, windows of glass or transparent polymer. But they are always overgrown — vines crawl their walls, moss softens their edges, roots probe their foundations. The boundary between built and grown is never clear. Other structures are grown: shaped from living stems, trained along frameworks, encouraged to fuse and thicken into beams, platforms, walls. A walkway is a living stem as thick as a highway, its surface flattened and hardened, its edges railinged with smaller growths. A room is a hollow in a massive root mass, its walls smooth and warm, its ceiling draped with hanging roots. A platform is a broad leaf reinforced with a framework of veins and stems, large enough to hold a dozen people.

The infrastructure is dense and visible. Pipes run everywhere — along walls, across ceilings, threading through the plant mass — carrying water, steam, gas, and waste. They are of all sizes, from thin tubes to large conduits, made of metal, ceramic, processed plant material, living stems hollowed and reinforced. Cables and wires are strung between structures, threaded through hollow reeds, bundled into conduits that are themselves overgrown. Ducts and vents emerge from the plant mass, releasing warm air or steam. Machinery is housed in cavities — generators, pumps, compressors, water treatment systems, air circulation fans, electrical transformers, control panels with rows of switches and indicator lights. The machines are old. They have been running for decades, maintained and repaired and replaced piece by piece. Their surfaces are worn, polished by use, stained with oil and mineral deposits, overgrown with moss and small ferns that find purchase in the warm humid air. They are loud — the constant hum of electric motors, the drone of generators, the rattle of pumps, the hiss of steam, the clang of metal expanding and contracting, the buzz of transformers, the whir of fans. This industrial sound combines with the water sounds and the plant sounds — the creak of stems, the rustle of leaves, the wet pop of growing tissue, the soft impact of falling drops — to create an acoustic environment that is dense, loud, and constant. There is no silence here. The sound is layered, textured, ever-present. You feel it in your chest, in your bones, in the vibration of the surfaces you touch.

The light is dim. The primary sources are industrial — electric lamps, strip lighting, glowing panels, indicator lights on machinery. They are sparse, widely spaced, often partially obscured by the plant mass. The light is warm in color temperature — amber, orange, yellow — which makes the green of the plants read as deeper, richer, more shadowed. The overall illumination is low. You see clearly only what is near the light sources; beyond that, the space dissolves into shadow and suggestion. Shapes loom and recede. Masses of vegetation are suggested rather than revealed. The ceiling is mostly lost in darkness, visible only where a lamp catches a hanging vine or a curtain of roots. There is some natural light — from cracks in the cavern ceiling far above, from shafts that reach toward the surface — but it is diffuse, cool, and weak, adding a faint blue-grey counterpoint to the warm artificial glow. In some chambers the natural light is stronger — a shaft of cool light falling through a crack, illuminating a column of falling water, catching the mist and making it glow. In others there is no natural light at all, and the warm amber of the lamps is the only illumination, making the green of the plants seem almost black.

There are no animals. No insects in the undergrowth, no birds in the canopy, no fish in the water, no rodents in the walls, no bats in the ceiling. The ecosystem is purely plant-based — or plant-and-microorganism-based. This absence is noticeable, strange, slightly unsettling. In a place this warm, this wet, this vegetated, you would expect insects, would expect birds, would expect the small scurrying lives that fill such spaces. Their absence creates a strange emptiness within the density — the place is packed with life, but it is only plant life, and the silence where there should be animal noise is palpable. Perhaps the industrial processes create conditions inhospitable to animals — the ozone, the specific chemicals, the constant noise. Perhaps the plants themselves produce compounds that deter or kill animal life. Perhaps the ecosystem is simply incomplete, or was designed this way. Whatever the reason, the result is a place that feels oddly empty despite its density, quiet in a way that contrasts with its loudness — the silence of animal absence within the roar of water and machines.

The atmosphere is thick, warm, humid, loud, green, dense, ancient. The air carries the smell of wet vegetation — green, earthy, slightly sweet — combined with machine oil, ozone, warm metal, mineral water, the particular mustiness of an enclosed underground space. The temperature is constant — no seasons, no day-night cycle in the traditional sense, just the steady warmth of the deep earth and the machines. The humidity is near saturation. Everything is damp. Surfaces are wet. The air feels heavy to breathe. Your skin is always slightly moist. Your clothes are always slightly damp. Your hair curls in the humidity. The air tastes of minerals and vegetation and machine oil — a complex, layered taste you notice only when you pay attention, but that is always there.

Change is slow. The plants grow, but slowly — millimeters per day, centimeters per year. You see the evidence in the way vegetation has slowly engulfed structures, in the way roots have slowly widened cracks, in the way stems have slowly thickened around pipes and cables. The water flows and drips and erodes, but the channels it has carved are ancient, and the changes it makes now are incremental — a millimeter of stone worn away per year, a channel widened by a centimeter per decade. The machines run continuously, but their operation is steady — failures happen, repairs are made, components are replaced, but the overall system is stable. It has been stable for decades. It will continue to be stable for decades more. The city is not expanding rapidly — it is in a state of dynamic equilibrium, with growth and decay balanced, with new construction and slow collapse occurring simultaneously. A structure built fifty years ago is half-engulfed by vegetation. A structure built ten years ago is still clearly visible, still sharp-edged, still distinct from the plant mass. The erosion is established — the forms are settled, weathered, in their own state. The place feels old, patient, enduring. It has been here for a long time. It will be here for a long time more.
