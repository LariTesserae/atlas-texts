## The Weeping

The land here is a bowl, and the bowl is full of holes. It is a low basin, a depression in the limestone where the water table is high and the rock is old and the soil is thin. The limestone is bare — gray, weathered, fissured into a maze of blocks and cracks — and the cracks are full of black water. The water does not run on the surface; it has gone underground, and it has taken the rock with it, dissolving it, grain by grain, for a hundred thousand years. The basin is a single organism of dissolving stone, and it is still dissolving. It is good at it. It is patient. It is the most alive dead thing you will ever stand on.

The rim of the basin is a maze of sinkholes — some small, some large, some no more than a crack in the pavement, some a hundred meters across. They are the eyes of the place. They are the mouths. They are the places where the land has given up and let the water in. And they are weeping. From every surface — the walls of the sinkholes, the floor of the basin, the cracks in the pavement — a film of water exudes, a slow, cold sweat. The air is saturated. It is a liquid you wade through. It smells of wet stone and ammonia and something else — something musky, something animal, something alive. It is the smell of the Weeping, and it rises from the holes like breath.

The sound rises with it. Not the sound of wind. Not the sound of water. The sound of life — a vast, churning, chittering roar, like a stadium heard from a distance, like a river heard in a shell, like the ocean heard in a cave. It has a pulse. It rises and falls. It is deafening, and it is coming from the holes. It is the sound of the Weeping, and it is the sound of the place being alive.

---

You descend into one of the larger sinkholes. Its sides are terraced with rimstone and flowstone — ledges of calcite, smooth and wet, like the seats of an amphitheater — and its bottom is lost in darkness. The air coming out of it is cool — ten degrees, eleven, constant — and wet, and it carries the sound. The sound is louder here. It is a wall of static, a hiss of ten thousand tiny voices, a click-click-click of echolocation like a rain of needles. It presses on your chest. You do not hear it with your ears; you hear it with your bones.

The mouth of the cave is a cathedral arch, and the arch is hung with stone curtains — flowstone draperies, banded in ochre and cream and rust, frozen mid-sway. The floor is a slope of scree and guano, and the guano is deep — a meter, two meters, a black-brown soil made of a thousand years of bat droppings, and it is alive. It is seething. It is a solid that moves.

The light dies as you go in. First the sun is a coin, then a sliver, then a memory. The air cools. The humidity rises. The walls are wet and they are covered in a pale, gelatinous film — moonmilk, a mineral paste of calcite and water, oozing from the rock like a slow cream. It is cold and it yields and it smells of nothing, of stone, of time.

There is no green here that is not a fungus. There is no leaf, no stem, no root, no flower. The only photosynthesis is a memory — in the guano, in the bats, in the insects they carry in from the surface. The only flora is the fungi and the bacteria and the film of slime on the walls. The rest is mineral. The rest is stone and water and life.

---

Then the dark is total. And in the total dark, the first light appears — not from above but from the walls and ceiling. A faint, cold, green-blue glow, like foxfire, like rotten wood, like the light of deep water. It is the fungi. The walls are crusted with them — pinpoints of luminescence, constellations, a false sky. The glow is not steady; it pulses faintly, and where the air moves, the spores rise in clouds, and the clouds themselves glow, so the darkness is filled with floating green stars, a galaxy in slow motion, and the stars drift and swirl and settle, and the settling is a faint, soft rain, a bioluminescent snow. The clouds are thick enough to obscure your hand. The dark here is not empty; it is *full*.

The passage opens into a chamber so large your light cannot find the far wall. The ceiling is lost in darkness and in the glow of the fungi and in the drifting spore-clouds. And the ceiling is alive. It is a single, continuous, undulating carpet of bats. Millions of them. They hang shoulder to shoulder, body to body, a living shingle, and the shingle is moving — not flying, but shifting, crawling over one another, a slow, constant, seething motion, like a field of wheat in wind, like water boiling. The sound they make is indescribable. It is a wall of static. It is a hiss of ten thousand tiny voices, a click-click-click of echolocation like a rain of needles, a whump-whump of wings as a group shifts, a chitter-chitter-chatter that rises and falls like surf.

The air between you and the ceiling is full of them — thousands, a blizzard of wings, a snow of bodies, and they pass so close you feel the wind of their passage, the brush of a wing, the tiny, desperate scramble of a body that has misjudged and is falling. They fall sometimes. The floor is littered with the dead and the dying — a constant, slow rain of bats, a bat snow, and the snow is immediately covered by the crickets.

---

The floor of the Weeping is not stone. It is a living carpet, a crust, a moving skin. It is made of crickets — cave crickets, humpbacked, pale as paper, eyeless, their antennae twice the length of their bodies, trailing on the ground like the threads of a jellyfish. They are packed so densely that the floor seems to flow. They move in waves — a tide of them, a slow, rippling, six-legged tide, flowing over the guano, over the dead bats, over the rocks, over your boots if you stand still. They are the great processors of the Weeping. They eat the guano. They eat the dead. They eat each other. And they are eaten — by the spiders, by the beetles, by the centipedes, by the bats, by the salamanders, by everything. They are the base of the food web, and the web is dense and layered and it is everywhere.

The crickets are not the only ones. The walls are crusted with beetles — black, shining, fast, a constant scuttling like rain on a tin roof. The ceiling is laced with spiders — long-legged, pale, motionless, waiting. The air is thick with midges and springtails and mites, a constant, drifting, biting snow. The guano itself is a tissue of white fungal hyphae and bacteria, and the bacteria release ammonia, and the ammonia sharpens the air, and the air is a chemical as much as a gas — it burns the nostrils, it coats the tongue.

---

At the lowest point of the chamber, the floor falls away into a pool, and the pool is black. Not dark — black, a black that absorbs light, a black that seems to drink the bioluminescence, a black that is the color of the cave itself. The pool is fed by drips and seeps and by a stream that comes from a passage at the far end, and the stream is the Black River — a river that runs through the cave, that vanishes into the floor and reappears, that carries the dissolved limestone away to the sea. The water is cold — ten degrees, eleven, constant — and it is saturated with calcite, and it is depositing that calcite everywhere: on the walls, on the ceiling, on the floor, in the pools.

The pools are full of cave pearls — spheres of calcite, concentric, layered, nested in the sediment like eggs, some the size of peas, some the size of fists, some the size of skulls. They are polished by the water and they are smooth and they are cold and they are strange. They are the "eggs" of the cave, and the cave is full of them.

The pools are full of life, too. Blind fish — white, translucent, eyeless, their skulls visible through their skin — drift in the black water, sensing the world with lateral lines, with pressure, with the taste of the water. Crayfish — pale, long-clawed, ancient — pick their way over the bottom. Salamanders — the "ghosts," pale as the fish, with feathery gills and translucent skin and no eyes — drift in the pools, their limbs splayed, their gills waving, their bodies like the bodies of unborn things. They are the lords of the pools, and they are patient, and they live for a hundred years, and they are the color of the cave.

---

The cave breathes. It has a rhythm — a daily rhythm, a seasonal rhythm — and the rhythm is the movement of air. In the morning, the cave exhales: a warm, wet, ammonia-rich fog rolls out of the sinkholes and spreads across the basin like a ghost, and the fog is full of spores and insects and the smell of the bats, and the surface world disappears under it. In the evening, the cave inhales: the air rushes in, the passages moan and whistle, the bats wake and the sound rises to a roar, and the whole basin becomes a throat, a lung, a living thing drawing breath.

The sound never stops. Even in the deepest, driest passage, there is the drip of water — a constant, slow, metronomic drip, the clock of the cave, the sound of the stone growing. Each drop is a note. Each drop is a century. The formations grow at the speed of geology — a centimeter a century, a meter a million years — and they are growing now, in front of you, in the dark, in the wet, in the silence between the drips. The stalactites hang like stone icicles, like stone teeth, like stone fingers. The stalagmites rise like stone cones, like stone buds, like stone fists. The columns join them, and the columns are the pillars of a temple, and the temple is the cave, and the cave is alive.

There is no built thing here. No artifact. No tool. No road. No mark of hands. The only architecture is the stone — the columns, the draperies, the pearls, the flowstone — and the only technology is the life: the bats' echolocation, the crickets' antennae, the fungi's chemistry, the fish's lateral lines. The place is pure process. It is water and stone and life and time, and it is in balance — the dissolution and the deposition, the growth and the decay, the bats and the crickets and the guano, the living and the dead. It is an ecosystem in motion, and it has been in motion for a hundred thousand years, and it will be in motion for a hundred thousand more.

---

**The Rules**

The rules of the Weeping are simple:

The bats are the sun. They bring the energy — they fly out at dusk, they eat the insects of the surface, they return at dawn, they deposit the guano, and the guano feeds the crickets, and the crickets feed the spiders and the beetles and the bats, and the circle is closed. Without the bats, the Weeping is a dead cave. With the bats, it is the most alive place on earth.

The water is the blood. It dissolves the stone and it deposits the stone and it carries the nutrients and it carries the waste and it is the circulatory system of the place. It is never still. It is always moving, always dripping, always flowing, always vanishing and reappearing.

The dark is the skin. It is the boundary between the Weeping and the world, and it is absolute, and it is necessary, and it is the condition of the life inside. The creatures of the Weeping have given up their eyes, their color, their place in the sun. They have traded the light for the dark, and the trade has made them strange and pale and new.

The stone is the bone. It is the structure, the scaffold, the archive. It records the water and the life and the time. It is dissolving and it is growing and it is the slowest thing in the Weeping and the most patient.

And the sound is the voice. It is the voice of the bats and the crickets and the water and the wind and the stone, and it is deafening, and it is constant, and it is the sound of the place being alive. You do not visit the Weeping. You enter it, and it enters you, and the sound is in your bones forever.
