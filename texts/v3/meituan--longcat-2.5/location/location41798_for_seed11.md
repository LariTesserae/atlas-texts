The plateau has no single name. The people who built the stone circles on its surface had words for it — words that translated roughly as "the place that hums," "the roof that sings," "the loud place," "the warm stone" — and the name changed depending on the season, the weather, the time of day. A name for a place this old and this loud cannot be fixed. It has to be spoken differently each time, or the place will not recognize it.

It is a tableland of extraordinary antiquity. The rock beneath your feet is a gneiss so old that its minerals have been recrystallized three times, four times, each metamorphosis overwriting the last until the stone remembers nothing of its original form. It has been worn — by water, by wind, by ice, by the patient abrasion of time itself — into a surface so flat that it seems the work of a vast and ancient machine. Not perfectly flat; there are gentle undulations, shallow depressions, the occasional low rise no higher than a person's knee. But the overall impression is of a surface planed down to its final form, a peneplain lifted to the roof of the world and left there to finish its erosion in the thin air.

Except the air is not cold. The plateau is warm — not tropical, but warm enough that the intermittent rain falls as rain, not snow, and the condensation that beads on the stone each morning is liquid, not frost. The warmth comes from below. The crust here is thin, or fractured, or both, and the heat of the world's interior rises through the ancient rock and keeps the surface at a temperature that feels almost body-warm in the shade. The rock itself is warm to the touch. If you press your ear to it — carefully, because the sound will overwhelm you — you can feel the deep, subsonic pulse of the plateau's interior, the slow convection of stone that has been moving for a billion years and will continue moving for a billion more.

The water is everywhere and nowhere. It falls from the sky in sudden, violent bursts — the weather on the plateau is turbulent beyond description, clouds forming and dissolving in minutes, rain falling in sheets so thick they blot out the horizon, then stopping as if a door has been closed. The water does not stay on the surface. The ancient rock is porous, fractured, a vast sponge, and it drinks the rain as fast as it falls. Within minutes of a storm, the surface is merely damp, darkening to a deep grey-brown, and the water is moving through the rock's interior, through channels and fissures and caves that no one has ever mapped, toward the plateau's edges, where it will eventually emerge as springs thousands of feet below.

But the water also rises. The same porosity that lets it drain lets it wick upward through capillary action, through the microscopic fractures in the stone. And where it rises — in the shallow depressions, along the lines of ancient cracks, in the low spots where the rock is slightly more weathered — the vegetation explodes.

The vegetation is lush. Not everywhere — most of the plateau is bare stone, and the emptiness is vast, the horizon unbroken in every direction. But where the water rises, the green is almost violent. Mats of mosses in colors that range from deep emerald to acid yellow to a red so dark it is almost black. Ferns with fronds that unfurl to the size of a person's body. Low, dense shrubs with leaves like polished leather, covered in condensation that beads and rolls and never quite falls. Flowers — small, brief, intensely colored — that open only during the rain and close within minutes of its stopping. The vegetation forms patterns across the plateau: stripes following the ancient crack systems, circles around the deeper depressions, islands of green in a sea of stone. From a distance, the plateau looks like a mosaic, or a circuit board, or a language written in chlorophyll and water.

The sound is the first thing you notice and the last thing you forget. It is deafening. It is constant. It is not one sound but many, layered and interwoven into a roar that seems to come from everywhere and nowhere.

The wind is the loudest component. The plateau is flat, and the wind moves across it without obstruction, accelerating to speeds that would knock a person off their feet if they were foolish enough to stand upright in the open. But the wind does not simply blow across the surface — it moves through the ancient erosion channels, the shallow grooves and fissures that score the rock, and these act as organ pipes, resonators, amplifiers. The sound is not a whistle but a roar, a multi-tonal, multi-frequency wall of sound that ranges from a subsonic rumble you feel in your chest to a shriek that seems to come from inside your skull. The pitch changes with the wind speed, with the humidity, with the temperature, creating a constant, shifting chord that never resolves.

Beneath the wind is the water. The underground rivers, moving through their stone channels, create a constant, deep, resonant hum — the sound of the plateau's circulatory system. It is felt more than heard, a vibration that rises through the rock and into your bones, a reminder that the stone beneath you is not solid but hollow, full of moving water and moving air and the slow, patient movement of the earth itself.

And above the wind and the water is the weather. Thunder, when it comes, is not a crack but a sustained roar that seems to last for minutes. Rain on the stone is a hiss like a thousand snakes. The sudden, violent winds that accompany the storms create gusts that sound like freight trains, like waterfalls, like the world ending.

The combination is a soundscape that is genuinely, physically overwhelming. You cannot hear yourself think. You cannot hear another person speak unless they shout directly into your ear. The people who live on the plateau — and there are a few, scattered in the sheltered spots where the vegetation is thickest and the wind is slightly less violent — communicate through touch, through sign, through a language of gestures and expressions that has evolved in the absence of audible speech. They build their structures low, hugging the ground, and they orient them to create pockets of relative quiet where the wind is deflected and the sound is slightly less intense.

The structures themselves are primitive: dry-stone walls, low and thick, built without mortar from the ancient gneiss that covers the plateau. They are not houses, exactly — more like windbreaks, shelters that provide a few square meters of relative calm. Some are ancient, their stones worn smooth by the same forces that wore the plateau, their purpose forgotten. Others are newer, built by the current inhabitants, who maintain the old ones and add to them slowly, stone by stone. There are also cairns — small piles of stone, carefully balanced, placed at intervals across the plateau. Their purpose is unclear. They might be trail markers, or memorials, or something else entirely — offerings to the wind, perhaps, or attempts to communicate with the plateau itself.

The fauna is rare. The plateau is not a place that supports much animal life — the sound alone is enough to drive away anything that can leave. But there are some creatures that have adapted. Large, soaring birds — something like condors, or perhaps something stranger — ride the wind currents at the plateau's edges, never landing, their wingspan enormous, their bodies light and hollow-boned. They feed on the insects that are blown up from the lowlands, and on the small, tough rodents that live in the vegetation pockets, emerging only during the brief calm after a storm. In the underground rivers, there are fish — pale, blind, translucent — that have never seen light and never will. And in the vegetation itself, there are insects: beetles with shells like polished stone, moths with wings the color of the mosses they live among, spiders that build webs so fine they are invisible until the condensation beads on them and turns them into nets of tiny, perfect spheres.

The strangeness of the place is difficult to articulate. It is not any one thing but the combination: the ancient, near-flattened rock and the violent, ever-changing weather; the deafening sound and the vast, empty silence of the bare stone; the lush, almost tropical vegetation growing in pockets on a high-altitude plateau of ancient gneiss. It is a place that feels both primordial and newborn, both the end of erosion and the beginning of something else. The rock is so old that it has become a kind of eternal present — it is not changing, not really, not in any way that a human lifespan can perceive. But the weather changes minute by minute, the vegetation grows and dies and grows again, the water moves through its underground channels in a constant, restless circulation. The plateau is a paradox: a place of absolute geological stability and absolute atmospheric chaos, a place where time moves both too slowly and too fast.

At night, the sound changes. The wind does not stop, but it shifts, and the underground rivers seem louder, and the temperature drops — not to freezing, but to cool, and the condensation is heavier, and the vegetation seems to glow faintly in the dark, some phosphorescent quality of the mosses and lichens that is invisible during the day. The stars are very close and very bright, the air is so thin and so clear that the Milky Way casts shadows, and the sound of the plateau is a constant, all-encompassing presence, a reminder that you are standing on the roof of an ancient world, listening to it breathe.
