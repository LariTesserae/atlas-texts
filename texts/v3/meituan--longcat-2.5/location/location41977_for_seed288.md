# The Hollow

The sunlit world has no name for it, for no one who lives under the sky has ever seen it. The name it has — the Hollow — is the word travelers use when they come down the long shaft and stand on the plain and look out into the dim. It is the last and greatest bubble in the rock, a cavern valley some twenty miles across, its ceiling lost in black a hundred fathoms overhead, its floor flattened by ages into a plain you can walk for days. The mountains above have been ground down to nubs and hollows; the Hollow is what the world looks like at the end — cool as a cellar in autumn, dry as ground bone, and in no hurry.

The first thing you notice is the sound, because it never stops. The Hollow breathes. Through a single great shaft — the Throat — air moves in and out of the deep earth as the weight of the atmosphere rises and falls, and the wind of it is a constant low roar, swelling and ebbing over the hours like the chest of something asleep. Stand still and you feel the pressure change in your ears; they pop, and pop again. Under that, a dozen other voices: the rustle of the swarm in the towers like a waterfall of gravel, the wing-rain of the gleaner flocks, the slow drum of grazer herds on the stone avenues, and — once in a long while — the distant crack of a block of ceiling letting go and falling into the dark, a sound that rolls around the walls for a minute before the Hollow swallows it. There is no silence here. Nothing that lives here has ever heard silence. They speak through the floor, by tapping and drumming, and they hear with their feet.

The second thing is the dimness. The Hollow is lit like deep twilight at the bottom of a well. On the walls, ghostcap lichen holds a faint blue-green glow, enough to make the far wall — a day's walk away — a rumor streaked with faded color. True daylight comes down the Throat in two or three thin shafts, pale as watered milk; one of them strikes the white salt crust in the low place and makes a pool of gold a hundred paces across, the Sunpool, the brightest thing in the world, and it is not warm. It moves so slowly that you would need a whole day to watch it crawl a hand's breadth. Nothing here uses it. Nothing here can afford to.

For no water has stood in the Hollow for longer than the mountains remember. The low places are white with salt, the memory of an ancient brine; the dust underfoot is deep and dry. And yet the Hollow is green — the greenest thing in the world, green in islands. Around every mineral seep, where the rock breathes out its thin sour gas, a grove grows. Fellmoss mats the stone a foot thick, and out of it stand the candle-trees: pale, fleshy columns, wax-white, some thirty feet tall, capped with ivory shelves, drinking hydrogen sulfide and methane through pores in their skins and building their bodies out of gas and stone. The air in a grove smells of sulfur and overripe fruit; the moss drips with spores. The groves are lush beyond anything the sunlit surface ever grew — dense, layered, crowded — but they are islands. Between them lies a day's walk of empty dust, and then another grove, small and far off, like a smear of ivory on the flat.

The air between the groves is full of life. Gleaners — great soft moth-bat things — wheel in flocks that darken the dimness, their wings beating a sound like rain on a roof; a flock rising from the towers is a wind of its own. On the plains, dust-grazers move in herds of hundreds, hooves striking slow sparks from the stone, breath misting in the cool air. Insects cloud the groves. Hook-winged predators ride the thin updrafts of the Throat. It is abundant, all of it — but spread thin, so that you can walk half a day and see nothing alive but the dust you kick up, and then round a worn stump of fallen stone and find a whole herd hung around a seep like cattle at a trough.

The candle-trees have been building for a hundred million years, and what they have built is a city. Where a candle-tree dies, its mineral-impregnated trunk does not rot; it cements. A new tree grows out of the top of the old one. Over epochs the grove has become a stand of towers — the White Warrens — hundreds of spires clustered along the greatest seep-field, some a hundred meters tall, banded like layered cakes, each band a generation. The towers are riddled with chambers where gleaners nest and the swarm has bored and cemented; avenues run between the spires, worn smooth by a million grazer hooves; galleries cross from tower to tower; bridges of grown stone span the gaps. It is urban in every respect but one: no hand has ever made anything here. The swarm — a termite-like insect living in the billions — remodels the towers with its own body-glue, farms fellmoss in the upper galleries, and keeps the whole structure growing the way a coral keeps a reef growing. The Warrens are a reef the size of a city, and the city is alive.

And the Warrens sing. The Breath moves through the hollow towers, and they sound like an organ the size of a city — a constant chord under everything, now rising, now falling, the lowest notes felt in the sternum rather than heard. The swarm's wings add a rustle like dry leaves; the gleaners' million voices add a chittering haze; the herd-drumming adds a beat. It is deafening, and it is the voice of the place, and the creatures of the Hollow live inside it the way fish live inside water.

Time here is measured in ages. A tower grows a hand's breadth in a century. The Breath takes half a day to turn. The Sunpool crawls across the salt. The flocks migrate from one side of the Hollow to the other over the course of a month. The stalactites of the ceiling fell long ago, and their stumps on the plain have been worn by dust and passing feet into smooth bumps, like old teeth in a worn jaw. The walls are smooth as canyon walls; the floor is flat to the horizon. The Hollow is the world's final form, and it is nearly finished: the bubble below is the last of its kind, and when the Throat finally closes or the roof finally falls, the world will be level. Nothing here hurries. The candle-trees do not hurry. The swarm does not hurry. Even the Breath is patient, the slow respiration of a world that has all the time there is.

The rules are few. Nothing here drinks; life runs on gas and stone. Nothing here is made; everything is grown. Nothing here is silent; the floor carries every footfall for a mile. The light is borrowed, and dim, and feeds nothing. And the oldest rule, the one the whole Hollow keeps: change is slow, the end is far off, and the end, when it comes, will come gently — the roof falling like snow, the towers worn down to the level of the plain, the last grove giving up its gas, the Breath stopping mid-exhale. Until then, the candle-trees build, the swarm cements, the gleaners wheel, the herds drum their slow march across the dust, and the Hollow breathes, and hums, and waits.
