# The Hollow City

In the warm deeps, where the water is dark as oil and warm as blood, there is a city. It has no name, but it has the shape and density of one — towers, walls, arches, channels, districts. It is built not by hands but by a colonial organism that has been growing for millennia, a creature — or a collective of creatures — that secretes a hard, pale material from its skin and grows in dense, merging clusters.

The organism is something like coral and something like sponge and something like fungus, but not quite any of them. Its body is a thin, living skin stretched over a skeleton of calcium and chitin and something like resin. The skin is soft, slightly warm, and faintly luminous — a pale, milky glow that is just enough to see the nearest surfaces, if you are close. The skeleton is dead, hollow, filled with water and the compressed remains of previous generations. The structures are weathered by currents, smoothed and rounded, covered in encrusting life — algae, sponges, bryozoans, barnacles, small shellfish — so that they are patchy, textured, mottled with green and brown and red.

The city extends for kilometers. It has districts. In the Tower District, the structures rise like spires, ten or twenty meters high, their tops lost in the darkness above. In the Arch District, the structures have grown together to form bridges and vaults, creating a labyrinth of chambers and passages. In the Channel District, the water flows through narrow passages between walls, and the current is strong enough to pull at you. In the Mound District, the structures are low and broad, like hills, and the water is still and quiet. The density is high — structures are packed close together, layered on top of each other, forming a three-dimensional maze. There are no open spaces, only passages.

The water is warm — twenty-eight degrees, perhaps — and dense, slightly more saline than the ocean above, full of minerals and particles. It moves slowly, with currents that flow through the channels and around the structures, carrying food to the colony and carrying away waste. The currents are warm, and they have a faint, sweet smell — the smell of the colony, of the creatures, of the minerals in the water.

The light is near-zero. The only light is bioluminescence — the faint glow of the colony's skin, the shimmer of bacteria in the water, the occasional flash of a hunting creature. The darkness is complete beyond a few meters. You see only what is close, and you see it in shades of pale blue and green and white, like a photograph taken in moonlight.

The fauna is abundant. Small fish — pale, blind, translucent — swim through the channels in schools, their scales catching the faint glow. Larger fish — eel-like, with wide mouths and small eyes — hunt them from the crevices. Crustaceans — crabs, shrimp, isopods — click and scrape on the surfaces, their claws leaving marks on the soft skin of the colony. Worms — polychaetes, flatworms — wave from crevices, their gills pulsing. Mollusks — clams, snails, nudibranchs — cling to the walls or burrow into them. Squid — small, transparent, with large eyes — drift through the open water, jetting away when disturbed. Jellyfish — pale, pulsing — drift through the streets like slow balloons. And there are stranger creatures: a thing like a feather duster that lives in the hollow interiors of the structures, a thing like a starfish that crawls on the ceiling of the arches, a thing like a shrimp that farms the living tissue of the colony and defends its garden with its claws.

The sound is mostly quiet. The water dampens sound, and the creatures are not loud. The main sounds are the low hum of water moving through channels — a sound like breathing, like the city is alive — the clicking of claws, the scrape of shells, the occasional deep groan of the colony shifting or growing. Sometimes there is a sound like a low bell, made by some creature or by the water moving through a particular chamber. Sometimes there is a sound like a whisper, made by the small fish swimming past. The high frequencies are absorbed by the water, so everything sounds muted, distant, as if you are hearing it through a wall.

The vegetation is sparse. There are patches of algae — green, brown, red — on the surfaces of the structures, where the light is brightest. There are filter-feeders — sponges, bryozoans, tunicates — that cover the surfaces in places. There are no plants, no seaweed, no kelp — the darkness is too deep for photosynthesis, and the colony does not need light. The only primary production is chemosynthesis — bacteria that live in the colony's skin or in the water, feeding on minerals and chemical energy from below.

The whole place feels ancient, settled, in balance. The city has been growing for thousands of years, and it will continue to grow. The colony is not aware, not in any way you would recognize, but it responds — it grows toward food, away from damage, toward light or away from it. It has a slow, dim awareness, like the awareness of a forest or a reef. The creatures who live here are part of the city — they live in its crevices, swim through its channels, graze on its surfaces, hunt in its streets. They do not build, but they inhabit. The city is alive, in a sense — it grows, it responds, it has a kind of slow, distributed mind. But it is not technological. It is pure biology, pure nature, a city built by life itself.

The rules of the city are simple. The colony grows slowly, at a rate of a few centimeters per year, and it lives for thousands of years. The water is warm and dense, and it moves slowly, carrying food and carrying away waste. The light is near-zero, so most creatures rely on other senses — touch, taste, smell, hearing, electroreception, or bioluminescence. The sound travels well in the water, but it is mostly low-frequency — the high frequencies are absorbed. The city is in balance — the colony grows, the creatures live and die, the water flows, and everything is recycled.

This is the Hollow City. It has no name, no history, no purpose. It is simply there, growing in the warm dark, a city built by life for life, a place where the boundary between organism and architecture has dissolved.
