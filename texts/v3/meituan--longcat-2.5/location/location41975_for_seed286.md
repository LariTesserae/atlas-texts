The water begins where the land ends — and the land ended a long time ago.

A high plateau, lifted above the lowlands, its surface broken by ridges and shallow basins and the dry courses of rivers that no longer run. Then the water came. Not in a flood — over ages, the rain and the snowmelt and the slow rising of the seas found their way to this high place, pooling in the basins, drowning the ridges, until the entire plateau became a shallow sea suspended above the lower world. The water is cold — snowmelt cold, altitude cold, four degrees above freezing — and clear, and it stretches to every horizon. The air above is thin and bright. The sun is strong. The light is normal light, the light of a clear day at altitude, and it falls on the water and into the water and is caught by the green things that fill the water, and the whole system runs on this light.

The vegetation is total. The plants began at the edges, in the shallows, and they never stopped. They grew outward and downward, colonizing the drowned land, filling the water column, rising to the surface and beyond. Now the plants are the substrate — the thing the world is made of. The ground is plant matter. The water is full of plant matter. The architecture is plant matter. Everything is green, or blue-green, or the brown-green of old growth, or the pale green of new tissue. The density is sparse — there is space, so much space, open volumes of water between the great living structures — but the vegetation is everywhere. It fills the world.

The forms are established. They have been here so long that they have reached their final shapes. The water has eroded them, the wind has shaped them, the weight of their own growth has settled them, and now they are what they are. They will not change. The great columns rise from the depths — vascular tissues scaled to the size of redwoods, pumping water from the bottom to the surface and back again. The sheets and curtains that hang between them are filters, fine meshes that strain the water for minerals and organic particles. The domes and blisters that cluster at the nodes are refineries, chambers where chemistry happens at industrial scale. The filaments that connect everything are nerves and wires, carrying slow electrical pulses that coordinate the system. The whole thing is electrified. You can feel it in the water — a faint tingling, a sense of current, of potential. You can see it in the bioluminescent patches that glow and fade in slow waves across the great structures, signaling, communicating, processing.

This is the industrial quality of the place. It is a factory. It is mechanized. It is scaled production. But nothing is built. There are no structures, no artifacts, no made things. Every pump is a living organ. Every filter is a membrane. Every wire is a nerve. Every reactor is a cell. The technology is grown. It has no seams, no joints, no bolts, no assembly. It is continuous, seamless, alive. It has been running for so long that it has no beginning and no end. It is not a machine that was made; it is a machine that grew, and grew, and grew, until it covered a continent, and then it stopped growing and started running, and it has been running ever since.

And it does not change. The water circulates in the same patterns. The pumps pump at the same rate. The filters filter the same amount. The generators generate the same current. The refineries refine the same chemicals. The outputs feed back into the inputs. The plants grow at exactly the rate they die. The fauna — abundant, varied, fish and invertebrates and larger things that move through the water and the plant structures — eat and are eaten, live and die, at rates that perfectly balance. The weather — generated by the vast expanse of water and the living surface, the evaporation and condensation and wind patterns — follows its own unchanging cycle. Clouds form, rain falls, the wind blows in its channels, and the water level never rises or falls. It is a perpetual motion machine of biology. It has no history because nothing new ever happens.

The sound is loud. The water rushing through the great transport tubes — a deep, continuous roar. The creaking of the structural members as the currents push against them — a groaning, woody sound, like a forest in a storm, but continuous, unchanging. The electrical hum of the generators — a high, thin whine at the edge of hearing. The calls of the fauna — clicks, whistles, low-frequency rumbles that travel through the water and the plant tissue. The rain on the surface — a hiss, a static. All of it together, a wall of sound, loud enough that you feel it in your chest, in your bones. And it never changes. The same sounds, the same patterns, the same volume, forever.

The longer you look, the stranger it becomes. The plants are alive — you can see them growing, slowly, imperceptibly — but they behave like machines. They pump and filter and refine and generate with the steady, unchanging rhythm of industrial process. They are not growing toward anything. They are not competing, not adapting, not evolving. They are running. They are a machine made of plants, or a forest that has become a factory, and the longer you watch, the harder it becomes to tell the difference.

You stand at the edge — on a ridge of the drowned plateau that still emerges from the water, or on a platform of living tissue that has grown to the surface — and the cold air is sharp in your lungs, and the sun is bright on your face, and the air smells of cold water and green growth and something else, something mineral and sharp, like ozone or the scent of a storm. You look out over the vast, cold, bright water, and you see the great green structures rising from the depths, and you hear the loud, unchanging sound of the living machine, and you understand that this place will be here, exactly as it is, when you are gone, and it will have been here, exactly as it is, forever before you arrived. It does not need you. It does not need anything. It is complete.
