The airlock is a short rubber-sealed throat, and when it cycles open the mountain's thin cold disappears entirely. The air that takes its place is warm, thick, almost drinkable — it hits your face like a wet cloth. You are inside a single chamber the size of a cathedral nave, and it is full of growing things.

The chamber runs perhaps sixty meters in length and twenty across, ribbed with steel arches that rise to a translucent ceiling forty meters overhead. The ceiling panels are condensation-fogged, and the light that comes through is diffuse, warm, slightly green — sun filtered through water droplets and the canopy of leaves below. There is no visible sun, no shadow. The light is everywhere and nowhere. It makes everything look slightly artificial, like a stage set, like a dream of a forest.

The floor is heavy steel grating with square openings through which you can see water moving in channels a meter below. The channels are built, not natural: rectangular concrete troughs, painted white, carrying a slow current of warm, mineral-rich water. The water is milky, opalescent. It smells of sulfur and green growth. The grating vibrates faintly with the movement of water below — a constant, subsonic tremor you feel in your ankles.

Everything else is plants. They grow from every built surface. The walls are vertical gardens — steel panels with pockets of substrate, each pocket holding a fern, a bromeliad, a trailing vine. The arches overhead support hanging baskets, long troughs of orchids, ropes of philodendron. The floor grating has been fitted with planters — rectangular ceramic trays, each one a dense composition of ground cover, seedlings, moss. The trays are arranged in rows, with narrow walkways between them. The walkways are steel mesh, barely wide enough for one person. You move through corridors of green. Leaves brush your shoulders. Vines hang at eye level. The air is so saturated that water drips constantly from the ceiling, from the leaves, from the pipes — a scattered, irregular percussion, like rain on a metal roof but softer, closer, more intimate.

The vegetation is lush to the point of excess. Leaves are enormous, waxy, dark green, pressed against each other. The planting is layered — ground cover, understory, canopy — in a space that would normally hold only one layer. The effect is of a forest compressed into a room. Moss has colonized every surface that holds moisture: the base of every planter, the joints of every pipe, the condensation channels, the bolts of the steel arches. The moss is bright green, almost luminous, and it grows in thick, velvety mats. In places it has begun to climb the walls in irregular patches, following the paths of water droplets.

And yet the chamber is quiet. The machinery that maintains this place is industrial but muted. Somewhere below the floor, pumps move water through the channels — you can hear them as a low, rhythmic pulse, felt more than heard. Somewhere behind the walls, fans circulate air — a distant, continuous breath. The misting nozzles, hundreds of them mounted on the arches, release a fine spray at irregular intervals, and each release is a brief hiss, like a sigh. The heaters — geothermal, drawing from the mountain's warmth — are silent. The overall sound is a low hum, barely perceptible, the acoustic signature of a machine running smoothly. But the vegetation swallows the sound. The moss, the dense leaves, the water — they create an acoustic environment that is unnaturally dead. Your own voice sounds flat, close, wrong. Sounds don't echo; they're absorbed. The silence is not peaceful. It is the silence of a place that is holding its breath.

The chamber is new. This is apparent everywhere. Sections of the wall are still bare — raw concrete, bright rebar, the skeleton of the structure exposed. The concrete is still curing in places; you can smell it, that sharp, alkaline scent. In the far end of the chamber, a portion of the floor has not yet been fitted with grating. Instead, there is a pit of raw rock, freshly blasted, still sharp-edged and unweathered. The rock is dark, vesicular, volcanic — and it steams. Geothermal vents in the pit release warm, mineral-rich vapor that rises and joins the chamber's atmosphere. The rock is the only natural thing here, and it is contained — sealed behind glass panels, monitored, framed. But it is also the source of everything: the warmth, the water, the minerals that feed the plants. The glass panels are fogged with condensation, and behind them the rock looks like something seen through deep water — dark, shifting, barely real.

The erosion of this place has barely begun. The steel is not yet rusted. The water stains on the white channels are fresh, orange, still bright. The roots of the largest plants — a cluster of tree ferns near the geothermal pit — have just begun to crack their ceramic trays. The moss is still establishing its first thin layer on the pipes. Everything is in the earliest stage of becoming. The place is not weathered; it is being born. The concrete is still curing. The water is still finding its paths. The plants are still discovering their limits.

Construction is ongoing. New panels are being fitted to the bare sections of wall. New trays are being planted. New misting lines are being strung along the arches. But there are no workers visible. The construction happens at the edges of perception — a new panel appears, a new tray is filled, a new pipe is connected. The place is building itself, or being built by something that is rarely seen. The tools are there — a welding kit, a stack of ceramic trays, a coil of misting line — but they are still, untouched, waiting.

Fauna is almost absent. There are insects — small, dark beetles that live in the moss, and tiny flies that hover near the misting nozzles. There may be a species of engineered pollinator, something between a bee and a moth, pale and quiet, but it is rarely seen. No birds. No mammals. The silence of the chamber is not just the quiet of machinery — it is the silence of a place without animals. The only movement is the movement of water and the slow, imperceptible growth of plants. A vine tendril extends a centimeter in an hour. A leaf unfurls over the course of a day. The moss thickens almost visibly.

And yet the place is active. The water cycles continuously — drawn from the geothermal vents, channeled through the floor, pumped to the ceiling, released as mist, collected again. The atmosphere is managed — carbon dioxide enriched, oxygen monitored, humidity held at ninety-five percent. The plants grow at a visible pace. The construction proceeds at the edges. The chamber is not finished; it is being grown into existence. The boundary between the built and the grown is dissolving. Pipes are half-hidden by moss. Pumps vibrate under blankets of fern. Vines have begun to climb the steel arches, their tendrils finding the bolts and welds. The machinery is not being overgrown — it is being incorporated. The place is becoming a single system, part engine, part organism.

The strangeness of the chamber accumulates slowly. It is the perfection of the planting — every plant healthy, every leaf unblemished, no sign of the disease or decay you would expect in such density. It is the absence of animals — a jungle without birdsong, without insects humming. It is the sourceless light — green, diffuse, slightly wrong. It is the acoustic deadness — sounds swallowed before they can echo. It is the sense that the chamber is responding to your presence — the misting nozzles release a brief spray when you approach a new section, the leaves shift slightly as you pass, the water in the channels seems to quicken. Nothing dramatic. Just a sense of being observed by the environment itself.

The purpose is unclear. The plants are not food crops — they are ornamental, tropical, unrelated to any mountain ecosystem. The chamber is not a research station — there are no instruments, no data loggers, no signs of human presence beyond the construction itself. The geothermal energy is not being harnessed for power — it is simply being used to maintain the climate. The place seems to be growing for its own sake, or for a purpose that has not yet been revealed. Perhaps it is a nursery for something that will be transplanted elsewhere. Perhaps it is a repository for species that no longer exist in the wild. Perhaps it is an artwork, or a monument, or a message. The chamber does not say.

You stand on the steel grating and feel the warmth rise through your shoes. Water drips onto your head. Somewhere, a pump clunks. A vine tendril touches your wrist. The chamber breathes — the air moves in a slow cycle, rising from the geothermal pit, crossing the ceiling, sinking through the canopy, returning through the floor channels. You are inside something that is still becoming. The concrete is still curing. The moss is still spreading. The roots are still cracking their trays. The place is not a landscape; it is a process, and you are standing in the middle of it, watching it happen.
