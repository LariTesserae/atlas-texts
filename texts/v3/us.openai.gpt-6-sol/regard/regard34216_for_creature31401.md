The power came to the mountain in the evening and did nothing.

It watched the mist rise between the shelves. It watched a young ledge-dweller reach too far after a drop and get hauled back by the skin of its shoulders. It followed the water into the soil, through the thin yielding sheets, and out again at fractures too small to see. By morning it understood that nearly everything here depended on a delay: water released slowly, stones shifted slowly, trees grown slowly enough for their roots to hold what they found.

There was a tear in one of the sheets beneath an old grove. It had begun to draw water away from the roots above it. The ledge-dwellers had noticed the damp patch shrinking. They had laid clay and fibers beside the exposed edge, then covered it with soil and uncovered it again, uncertain which helped.

The power could have closed the tear at once. Instead it kept the broken edge moist through the hot hours. That evening a ledge-dweller returned, pressed a strip of soft root fiber into the place, and held it there while the sheet drew its edges together. The repair was theirs, as much as it could be.

Across the valley, the clear studs shone blue when mist touched them. The power found what remained beneath the lichen: an old way of marking where water had gone deep into the rock. Most of it no longer worked. It did not wake the whole system. On one wall, where a seam had begun to dry, it brought three studs back to life.

The next young ledge-dweller to visit touched the lights in turn. The first two faded under its hand. The third stayed bright. It touched that one again, then climbed along the wall until it found the narrow wet fracture behind a curtain of reeds.

For days, others came to look. They cleared fallen leaves from the little run below it. Water reached a patch of young trees that had been getting less each season.

After that, the power remained where it could be useful and otherwise left the mountain alone. It could not make the ledge-dwellers safe from every fall, or keep every tree alive. Doing so would have changed the paths they knew and the care they gave them.

When mist crossed the valley, three small points of blue appeared on the dark wall. Sometimes no one came. Sometimes a pale hand rested on the third one, then withdrew, and the ledge-dweller went to see where the water had gone.
