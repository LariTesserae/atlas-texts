The power would wait until evening.

It would watch the people lift the last seeds from the hollow, leaving a few for the birds and losing a few to the cracks. It would watch someone brush ants from a doorway without crushing them. It would understand that this crowded place was not a problem its inhabitants had failed to solve. Much of what lived here lived because they had made room for it.

So the power would not widen the passage or turn the ridge into a garden. It would not bring a spring up through the courtyard floor, where water might loosen the old stone and draw strangers to the nearly hidden village.

Instead, it would make one small change beneath the roofs. When rain next crossed the ridge, water that had always run away over the pale rock would find a sound seam leading to an empty pocket deep within it. The pocket would fill slowly. It would give nothing back through the walls or the roots. Beside the passage, where a hand could reach without moving a pot, a cool bead would gather each morning in a shallow notch.

For a long time, that would be all: enough to wet a fingertip, enough for a bee that had flown too far. The people would notice. They would test the stone and the water. If they chose, they could set a small cup beneath it and carry what collected to the roots they could not see.

The power would leave them to decide. Then it would go, listening for a while to the mill turning, the birds disputing under the slabs, and the ants finding their way back along the mortar.
