The power would come to the pass in the hour when water runs.

It would see the crescent of stones first and understand what it was: not a failed wall, but a place someone had made to sit. It would leave it alone. Repairing it might make it stronger, but it would also erase the marks of weather and of the hands that had once chosen those stones.

Then it would find the green film inside the quartz.

There would be no reason to warm the whole pass for its sake. A long thaw could drown the cells in their narrow home; a wider crack could give them more light and kill them with air. The power would wait through the brief wet hour and learn the shape of what already sustained them.

In the crack above the quartz, one grain of gravel had come to rest across the water’s path. Most days the drip passed it. Sometimes the water followed another seam and never reached the film. The power would turn that grain—not enough to stop the drip or send a flood into the fracture, only enough that, when thaw came, a little water would find its familiar way more often.

By evening, it would freeze. The pass would be cold and empty to anyone standing in it. No green would show through the stone.

The power would not stay to guard every grain against falling. It would know that the film might still be buried, exposed, or lost. But for a while longer, when sun and water met inside the quartz, the cells would have their hour.
