The power would come at dusk, when the first sealed openings broke and the leaves began to travel.

It would not announce itself. An announcement would send the workers below, and the power had come to learn what this place needed before changing it.

It would follow the dark grains being carried up through the crust. Beneath them it would find roots, cool galleries, and soil that still held a trace of water. Deeper down, it would find an old seam through which water had once entered the basin. The seam was not empty. Beyond the basin’s rim, fallen stone had pressed it shut.

The power would move the stone.

It would do this slowly, over many nights, letting the seam admit no more water than the deep soil could hold. No pool would appear among the towers. No sudden green would cover the crust. For a while the only difference would be that the roots no longer reached quite so far for what they needed. Then a few leaves would stay supple after falling. Some seedlings would survive in places where they had always emerged and died.

The termites would notice before anything with eyes. Where the earth held together better, workers would carry different grains to their walls. Some galleries would be repaired; some would be abandoned. One colony might gain ground beside a thorn tree, another lose a crossing as roots thickened beneath it. Lizards would still wait at the paths. Birds would still drop from the branches. The power would not spare every worker from a beak, or every seedling from a load of soil. To do so would require taking the basin away from most of those who lived there.

It would remain long enough to make certain the returning water did not rise into the occupied galleries. If it did, the power would narrow the seam again.

Years later, a traveler might find the basin much as before: dusty at noon, quiet beneath its crowded growth, its far edge hidden. At dusk, earth would still spill across the pale plates, and leaves would go underground piece by piece. Nothing there would know whom to thank.
