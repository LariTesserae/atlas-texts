The power would come on a rainy afternoon, when everyone was too busy to make much of a visitor.

It would stand aside while the pans were moved and listen to the calls passing from bay to bay. Only afterward would it ask the woman who knew the sound of the broad pan to show it where the water went.

She would show it the grooves, the scraped crust, the cistern lid that served as a bench. She would also show it the seam above the third arch, where mortar had begun to fall as fine dust. The people had seen it. They had been meaning to shore it up.

The power could make the arch sound with a word. Instead, it would ask whether they wanted help, and stay to carry brick when they said yes. It would hold the weight of the street overhead while they opened the failing seam. No one would need to hurry beneath it. The eldest would choose how the new bricks were laid, so the repair belonged with the repairs they remembered. When the mortar set, the power would let the weight settle back.

Then it would go upstairs.

The grilles had been left alone for generations because nobody on the street quite knew whose they were. The power would speak to those who could close them, build over them, or decide one day that the court was empty space beneath their feet. It would make them understand that people lived below, and that light, air, and a way out were not favors to be withdrawn. The agreement they made would be written where both streets could see it.

Before leaving, the power would ask the boy about the soft ticking beyond the third arch. He would lead it to the silted groove. It would hold a lamp while he cleared it, and follow the first little run of water as far as the cistern.

After the rain, the court would dry as it always did. Pans would be put away in the wrong places and found again. Sparrows would come through the grilles. The repaired arch would bear its load, and above it there would still be sky.
