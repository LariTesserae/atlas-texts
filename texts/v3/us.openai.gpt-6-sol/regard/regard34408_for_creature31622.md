The power would not begin by making the mountain green. It is green already, and people have spent generations learning what that greenery costs in water, labor, and cracked masonry.

It would begin by walking uphill.

At the lowest decks it would hear a child sent to fetch water from two crossings away. Farther up it would watch a porter wait beside a lift full of freight, calculating whether the steps would be quicker. It would listen to the repairers, especially when they disagreed. They would tell it which channels must never be widened, where roots are holding a wall together, and why a pipe that looks abandoned is not.

Only then would it act.

On the next clear morning, a fine mist would gather along the highest railings and settle into the empty catchment tanks. Not a flood, not water taken from another valley: just enough, arriving steadily enough, that the lower taps would still run when the upper gardens were watered. For a while people would suspect a change in the weather. Then they would notice it happened on dry days too.

The power would leave the pumps alone. It would leave the workers their skill. But where a hidden seam was about to fail, a narrow line of moss would appear, bright against the concrete, long before the leak could darken a household ceiling. The repairers could choose when to open the wall. They could show an apprentice what they had found.

There would be one more change, smaller than the water and perhaps more welcome. Each day, when the lift had taken its heaviest loads, it would wait a little longer at the landings. Its doors would not close on someone moving slowly toward them. No timetable would explain this. After a few weeks, people would cease to remark on it, though older residents would begin making the trip uphill more often.

The power would stay long enough to learn whether its gifts had caused trouble. If the new moisture softened a wall, it would stop there. If an upper garden drank more simply because more was available, it would not punish the gardener; it would let the neighbors settle the matter with a better measure of what the tanks held.

From across the valley, the mountain would look much the same. The pale rim, the bridges, the trees growing out of seams would still be there. Inside it, someone would still be washing a filter. Someone else would be waiting on a landing, with good reason to believe the lift was coming.
