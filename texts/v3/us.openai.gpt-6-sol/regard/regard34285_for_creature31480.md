The power does not bring light.

It considers it. It could make every shelf visible from end to end. But the red-bellied animals would close against the glare, the thin hunters would lose their cover, and the walkers would begin to trust distances that this hollow has never made easy to judge. A gift to one kind of life could empty the cracks for another.

It does not bring water, either. The powder has lain dry for longer than the oldest shelter has stood. What feeds here feeds on stone.

So the power waits. It comes to know the difference between the scrape of a rasper and the tread of a walker; how long a red animal stays open; which chips fall of themselves. It finds that most days nothing needs it.

One day, a walker reaches a fresh line across a lane and stops. She puts a hand to the wall. The line is wide, and whatever made it has gone into the dark beyond the nearest shelter. She waits, as walkers do.

On the shelf above her is a loose flake. It would fall in a year, or in a hundred. The power lets it fall now.

It strikes stone beyond the shelter. Something there shifts its weight. The walker hears powder slide under that weight—too far away, and too heavy, for any creature she came to take from the cracks. She backs along the wall until her fingers find the cut mark at the turning. She leaves the lane to its other traveler.

The flake has broken. By the time she passes again, its pale dust will look like all the other dust. The power does not mark the place or ask to be remembered.

It watches until the red animals open. Then it waits again.
