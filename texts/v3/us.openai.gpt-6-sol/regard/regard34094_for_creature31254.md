The power would come at midday, when the light reaches the bottom step, and at first it would do nothing.

It would look beneath the cistern lid without lifting it. It would follow the damp line under the sill and the dry boundary where the snails turn back. It would see that the court’s life depends not on having much, but on having different kinds of little: a shaded inch, a sound drain, a crack left open after a stone is made safe.

It would be tempted to give the court more water. Then it would see what a steady flow would wash from the stair joints, and leave the rain as it was.

There was one thing it could mend. At the bottom of the drain, beyond the reach of the person who trimmed the ivy, a seam was slowly filling with grit. The power would ease the grit away, a few grains at a time, until water could pass freely again. Nothing in the court would shift.

When the person next came to set a rocking stair stone, they would notice beetles entering at its edge. They would still make the step firm, but use a thinner wedge of slate, leaving the little doorway beside it. They might think it was luck that the stone sat so well.

Afterward the power would go. The springtails would feed beneath the lid. A moth would hold still against the wall. On the next damp night, a snail would travel as far as the water allowed and turn back.
