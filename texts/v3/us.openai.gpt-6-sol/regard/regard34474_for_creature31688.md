The power would come after the evening shift, when the hollow had fewer voices to confuse with the engines. It would listen through several revolutions of the flywheels. Then it would go to the square pool and watch the water climb the bottom step.

It would not put a hand into the pool. Nothing else did, and it saw no reason to be first.

By morning, a thin dark mark would run along the wall beside the step, showing the highest point the water had reached. The mark would remain after the stone dried. Each night’s rise would leave another, faint enough that no one need look at it, clear enough that anyone could. The power could not yet tell whether the water was becoming dangerous. The people who lived there could.

It would stay long enough to learn why the northern valve stuck, which channel took the overflow when that happened, and whose door was lowest. It would not mend the valve while someone was working on it. Instead, when the fitters opened the pump house, they would find that the old maintenance drawings—missing for years—had been returned to their drawer, with the abandoned channels traced in blue. Beside them would be enough sound pipe and fittings to do the work they had been putting off. The metal would bear no maker’s name.

For a while it would do nothing more. At night it would hear the soft drag beneath the covered channel and see the silt settle differently in the gear housing. It would not lift the cover to look. Whatever lived among those narrow spaces had survived the pumps, the foundry, and all the patient efforts to keep water where people wanted it. Being noticed by a power should not be what finally drove it out.

When the repairs were planned, the fitters would find that one disused channel could be left open without flooding a doorstep or weakening a wall. They might choose to clear it. If they did, water would begin passing through it again, warm and slow, under the roots in the old gear housing. The sound there would change. No one would have to decide what that meant.

Before it left, the power would set a small brass bell beside the lowest door, with a wire running down to a float in the drain. It would ring only when water rose past the height of the bottom step and kept rising. The household could leave it there, move it, or take it apart to see how it worked.

On most mornings it would say nothing. Boots would be lifted from beside doors, the pump house opened, and the flywheels brought through another turn. But if the bell rang in the night, everyone in the hollow would hear it in time.
