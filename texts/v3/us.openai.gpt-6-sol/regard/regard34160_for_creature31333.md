The power would come down slowly.

It would first mistake the round clearing for an absence in the plants. Then it would see the baskets, the smooth stones kept close to hand, and a child holding a bundle of shoots while an older person freed a single stem from the knot around it. It would understand that the clearing was not empty. It was kept.

For several days it would do nothing.

It would learn the knocks that carried from house to house. It would watch the people give way to the heavy fish and leave the snails to graze. It would follow the cool thread of the spring down through the fissure, past roots and stone, to where a shelf of rock had begun to settle. The spring still rose freely. In time, the shelf might narrow its passage; or it might come to rest where it was. The power could not tell which without waiting.

So it would wait. It would not lift the rock simply because it could.

One morning, when two gardeners were examining a slow-growing patch beyond the last lane, they would find the silt there unusually cool. They would follow it down and discover a thin stream escaping through the plants: a second path the spring had made around the settling stone. They would call others over. Hands would trace the new flow, test the roots, and mark its course with three small stones so they could find it again.

The power’s only gift would be this: for one clear hour, the little stream would carry away enough silt to be seen.

Afterward it would leave the water to cloud and clear by its own motions. The people might choose to keep that passage open, or to let plants grow across it while they watched the spring in the square. They knew more about living beside this water than the power did.

It would remember the older gardener’s hands around the stem: how they had paused before pulling, feeling for what else was held by the same roots.
