The power would not begin by making it rain.

It would spend a season listening. It would learn that a dry pipe joint can mean trouble, but so can a wet one; that the foundry door must not be widened unless the kitchen has another way to keep its heat. It would learn the ordinary tremor of the rail as well as the engine tenders do. On wet mornings it would watch a child collect water from three places and leave a fourth cupful for a tree.

Then, one night, it would give the district a warning.

Not a voice from the stone. A new sensation in the handrails: three slow pulses whenever heat rose in the buried engines faster than the passages could safely bear. The tenders would discover it first. They would test it, distrust it, come to rely on it only after it proved true. The power would let them.

It would make one other change, beneath the newest leaning wall. There it would ease the rock apart by the width of a finger, deep underground, creating a path for water to gather in an old empty pocket rather than against the foundations. In time the residents would find the pocket. They could draw from it in a dry spell, or leave it undisturbed. The tree would still push at the wall; someone would still have to decide whether to brace the masonry or give the roots more room.

The power could have filled every cistern. It could have stilled the engines, straightened the wall, raised the ceiling until no one needed to duck. But the people knew how to live with a place that changed. They had made careful habits there, and a map that included things no builder had thought to mark. The power would not take that knowledge out of their hands.

Before it went, it would follow the stairs to the highest ledge. Beside the gutter sat a cup with a little water in it, set apart from the household vessels. Its rim was chipped; a root below had found its way to the damp stone.

The power would leave the cup where it was.
