The power would come to the recess and wait.

It would feel the engine’s roar through the rail, measure the current at each contact, and watch the four fans for longer than a person could bear to stand in the cold. It would learn that the fan nearest the door has grown no farther because the door sometimes opens, and that the bare lengths of seam between them are not failures to be filled. This is enough room. This is how they live.

It would leave the lamp, the engines, and the contacts alone.

Far below, in a maintenance schedule, it would change one line. When the bearing is next replaced, the crew will be instructed to keep the inner seam clear of tools and cleaning suction. The instruction will be small and practical, beside the diagrams for the door’s swing and the space a person needs for their feet. No one will have to understand the fans to avoid pulling them loose.

Then the power would leave.

The recess would still be bitterly cold. The guardrail would still tremble. For a very long time, four black fans would hold to their separate contacts, taking only what the running spire let pass.
