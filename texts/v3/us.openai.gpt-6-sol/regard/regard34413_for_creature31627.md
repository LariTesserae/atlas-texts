The power would come to the basin and, for a long time, do nothing that could be seen. It would listen where the water entered the stone. It would wait at a chamber mouth until the animal braced there ceased drawing back its feelers at every nearby drop.

It would not fill the cracks, though they looked like wounds. It would not plant the grit or warm the pools or lift the low walls into buildings. The cold seams were passages, the dark bands were food, and the fallen blocks sheltered young. Even the noise carried information: a drop close by was not the same event as a drop far away.

What it could do was turn its attention outward. The basin’s water came from beyond the fins, and someone might one day sink a well that lowered it, or send dirty runoff into ground that appeared empty. At those places, the power would make the connections plain to anyone making such a choice. A well-digger would understand where the water went. Someone looking over the bare stone would notice the faint clean tracks and the shells tucked under the level blocks. Understanding would not compel them, but it would leave them unable to mistake the basin for vacant land.

Within the basin, the power would leave the water to find its many small entrances. The fins would keep cracking; fragments would fall. When rain raised the pools, the shells would come upward through passages no wider than a wrist, and withdraw again at the tremor of footsteps. The power would let them know its presence only as another distant thing the stone could carry—not a knock approaching, not a hand reaching into the seam.
