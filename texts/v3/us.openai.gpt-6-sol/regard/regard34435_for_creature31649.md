The power does not begin with the mountains. It begins by watching the people move through them.

It sees a woman pause beneath a wall and hear what no one beside her hears: a small, dry click from somewhere inside it. She puts down her water jar and calls for help. By evening the wall has been taken apart and built again. The power sees a shepherd turn his flock away from a gully because the snow above it looks too smooth. It sees children taught to leave the drinking cistern lids clean, even when no one is there to scold them.

These are not people waiting to be saved from a place they do not understand.

Still, there are things no care can hear in time. Deep within a slope above the oldest district, a seam of rock has begun to loosen. It will hold through this winter and perhaps the next. Then, on an ordinary afternoon, it will give way.

The power could bind the whole mountain fast. Instead, it loosens one small stone at dawn, long before the great fall is near. The stone lands in an empty yard. Its fresh face shows a dark fracture running through it, unlike the fractures the builders know. A mason notices. A shepherd goes looking for the place it came from. Others follow, and together they find the seam.

They argue for weeks. Some houses have stood below that slope for generations. Moving them will mean surrendering good roofs, familiar corners, a cistern lined by grandparents’ hands. The power does not settle the argument. It keeps the seam from breaking while they do.

At last they mark a new boundary and begin carrying their stone across it. The walls come down carefully; the cistern is covered and left clean. Children make a game of learning the new lanes. Older people go on giving directions by the old ones.

The power does one other thing. In the weeks of thaw, it lets a little more snowmelt linger in the high gravel—not enough to promise a harvest the climate cannot sustain, only enough that the cisterns fill before the channels run dry. No one can be certain this year was different. They repair the gutters for next year all the same.

When the slope finally falls, it falls into an empty district. Dust rises over roofs farther off. People come out of their low doors and count one another. Then, as the dust thins, someone points to the pale underside of a boulder and says it might make a good wall stone.
