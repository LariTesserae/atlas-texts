The power did not come down at noon. There was too much noise then, and too much honest work already underway.

It came at dusk and waited by the rim. It watched an old worker pour the last of a covered vessel along a root that looked dead from a distance. The water vanished into the worn surface. At its far end, a pale tip lifted into the air.

The power could have filled every basin. It could have fastened the plateau in place and made the streets keep their lengths. But the basins fed a living city, not a monument; and the streets, however inconvenient, were growing for reasons the power did not yet understand.

So it did one small thing. For a night, it let the fine tips feel the passing cloud as clearly as the leaves felt daylight.

By morning the oldest roots had turned upward. Not far—only enough that their new growth rested among the fibers the birds had left caught at the edge. Mist gathered there. A drop formed, traveled down a fiber, and touched a root.

The workers found it while making their rounds. They stood a long time with their covered vessels in their hands. Then one fetched a coil from the drying shed and tied a few more strands where the cloud passed closest. Another loosened a strand that had pulled too tightly against a growing tip.

The power stayed long enough to see that they could tell the difference. Then it left them to it.

There would still be dry weeks. The terraces would still weather, and some days a worker would have to sit in the middle of a lengthening street and wait for a wheel to sound near again. But at the rim, beside the old roots they had never stopped tending, the city had found one more way to drink.
