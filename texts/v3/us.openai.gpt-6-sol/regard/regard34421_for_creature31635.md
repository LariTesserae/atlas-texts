At first, the power would do nothing that anyone could point to. It would watch a birth in the rail yard: the pale clay lifting the stones, the birds leaving the wires, the workers setting down their tools. It would see that the people already knew how to meet a changing place. What they lacked was not wisdom, but sometimes time.

So it would give them a little more of that.

The first warning would still come through a heel or a hand against a wall. But before a dangerous rise, the warning would last a few breaths longer. A surveyor crossing a yard would feel it soon enough to call back the crew. A child carrying a full cup would pause without knowing why, and hear the china begin to rattle only after she had set it down.

The power would be careful with the birds. They already knew when to fly, and the people knew how to watch them. It would not turn them into messengers that must always be believed, or make their ordinary flights frightening. It would leave them their own reasons for taking wing.

Once the immediate danger had passed, it would help less. Clay would still rise under tracks. Door bottoms would still need trimming. The mills would lose hours while crews moved a line that had been straight the week before. To hold the plain still would be to change it beyond recognition, and perhaps to harm whatever deep, unfinished work was making land beneath the city.

There was one other thing the power could offer. Where new clay appeared, it would let the first dark film take hold a little sooner—not so fast that anyone mistook the ground for settled earth, but soon enough that rain would not sit quite so long on its bare surface. The workers could still find its edges. Willow could still wait its turn.

No one would agree on when these changes began. The yard crews would say their warnings had grown better because they had learned to listen. They would be right. They would drive their stakes, compare the chimney marks, and go home while the windows were coming on among the thickets. Behind them, on ground no one yet trusted, a little rain would soak into the clay.
