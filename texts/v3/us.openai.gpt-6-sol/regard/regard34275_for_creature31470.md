The power would come while the pump was running. It would listen through a whole night before changing anything: to the water entering the rooms’ walls, to the water leaving through the culvert, to the frogs calling from beneath the pipe. By morning it would know that stopping the water was not the same as helping the place.

First, it would lift a narrow path across the yard, just high enough that the workers could reach the lane with dry feet. It would leave the ditch open but give it a steady fall, so water did not linger warm against the rooms. The pump would keep its beat. Beside it, the power would set a second, quiet way for water to escape on the day the engine failed.

It would leave the frogs their damp shelter and clear the culvert mouth so they could pass through. It would fit the drain so a rat could no longer find its way into a room, without closing the yard to it. Along the wall it would loosen a strip of concrete and put deep soil there. Reeds would take the ditch’s edge; farther back, a tree would grow slowly enough that the swallows would have open air for many seasons yet.

Then the power would knock on the workers’ doors and show them what it had done. It would ask where water came in during the hardest rain, and whether the new path lay where they actually walked. If it had guessed wrong, it would move the stones.

Afterward, mornings would still begin with the pump and the swallows. Someone stepping out would still feel the heat and look down at the yard. Most days, they would find a dry way across it.
