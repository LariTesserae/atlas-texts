The power would spend a long time listening.

From above, the ridge might look like a thing that needed saving: a height with no stone in it, kept from the abyss by leaves and roots. But the power would come close enough to feel the old curves holding, and the small lives moving through them. It would understand that the worn places were not failures. They were how the ridge had made room for water, grazers, young swimmers, and the patient hands of the old.

It would find one old swimmer working at a column. A strand had softened deep inside the weave, beyond the reach of its fingers. The swimmer knew it was there. Its hand returned again and again to the same spot, feeling the hollow stem beside it, unwilling to pull that free while water still passed through it.

The power would not replace the column. It would hold the softened strand in place for a little while—no firmer than the strand had once held itself. The swimmer would feel the change through its fingers. It would pause, then draw a living shoot from the wall and guide it through the narrow opening. By the time the power let go, the new root would have taken some of the weight.

The swimmer might press a short sequence into the support. Far along the terrace, another would answer. Neither would need to know what had helped.

Then the power would leave the ridge to its water and its work. It would not still the roar, spare every grazer, or keep the old swimmer from dying. It had given the living strand time to grow; the rest belonged to the hands that knew the wall.
