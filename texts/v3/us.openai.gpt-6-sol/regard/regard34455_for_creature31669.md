The power would not make the chamber safe. It would know that a place being made by a river cannot be kept from changing without being made into something else.

It would begin by learning why the pumps run. Above the quarry are fields and houses; stopping the machines might send water into them. Below are fish that need a way back to the river, and banks so new that one hard current could undo them. Benevolence here would require more than choosing a side.

So it would attend to the next person who came down the walkway.

The keeper would notice a water mark higher than the last one, though the gauges showed nothing wrong. Looking closer, they would find silt packed against a screen, forcing the flow into a narrow, fast aisle. They would clear it. While there, they would see the fish circling behind the concrete pad, and the channel by which they could leave if it were not blocked by a loose sheet of metal. Moving the sheet would take a minute.

The keeper would also look up before replacing a failed lamp. Bats covered the roof above it. Instead of working beneath them in daylight, they would return after dusk. The new lamp would light the walkway without lighting the water or the roost.

Perhaps that would be all the power did for many weeks: bring someone to notice what their work touched. It would not save every egg beneath a stone, or hold a bank in place against a flood. When the water rose, some of the chamber’s small shelters would vanish. When it fell, others would appear. The thin film on the limestone would catch silt again wherever the current allowed it.

And when no one was there, the power would leave the chamber to its inhabitants: to the bats departing through the breach, the frogs waiting beside the warm concrete, and the fish holding their faces toward a current almost too slow to see.
