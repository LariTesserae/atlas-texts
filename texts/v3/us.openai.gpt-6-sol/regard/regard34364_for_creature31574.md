The power would come at dawn, when the undersides of the ledges were wet and the plateau had not yet decided what it could spare.

It would be able to fill every basin. It would not. The silver shrubs lived where their roots could reach moisture; the hoofed animals knew the distances between drinks; even the long-legged hunter depended on the paths those distances made. A permanent lake would be a gift to some lives and a quiet ending to others.

Instead, it would follow one of the broad creatures.

For three mornings the creature worked at a groove beneath an arch. It cleared out sand and pressed its damp feet against a place where the stone had begun to separate. Each dawn, a little water entered the groove. Most of it vanished into the crack before it reached the basin below. The creature returned anyway. The young one followed, watched for a time, and wandered off after an insect.

On the fourth morning, the power would place a small piece of pale stone beside the crack: not over it, not fitted into it. It would be stone from that shelf, shaped so that its narrow end could rest in the gap. Then it would withdraw.

The creature would spend a long time beside the piece. It would touch it, leave it, and come back after the sun had warmed the arch. At last it would turn the stone with its forefeet and press it into place. The young one, drawn by the movement, would try to pull it out. The older creature would lift one foot and hold it there until the young one let go.

The next dawn, water would pass through the groove. Not much: enough to wet a line of rock, enough for insects to find it, enough that a lizard might stop on its way to the older pool. The creature would inspect the repair and press once more at its edge.

The power would leave the plateau with its far-apart basins, its dry shelves, and its rain falling where it pleased. One groove would run clear. The creatures would know how it had been mended, and the young one would have seen the work.
