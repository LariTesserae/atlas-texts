The power would come before dawn, when the gutters were still full of soft silt. It would not wake anyone.

It would go first to the stream and learn its habits: where it struck the new bank, where it slowed, how high it had climbed in the flood last season. Then it would set a long shelf of buried stone beneath the blue silt, deep enough that the stream could no longer cut under the houses. The water would still rise. The town would still hear it. But the ground under the landing would hold.

On the highest part of the bend, where no house could fit, it would lift a little more stone from below. Three people could stand there without touching, then six. A narrow path would lead up to it. It would be a place for jars and sleeping children when the water came high, and for no particular purpose on a dry afternoon.

It would leave the gutters alone. Every morning, someone would still take a hooked stick to them. Clay would still need pressing into seams; fish racks would still be moved to catch the draft. Those were not failures the power had come to correct. But beneath the landing it would give each stone mouth a firm floor, so that clearing one would no longer loosen the next.

Last, it would leave a few shallow places along the outer bank where rushes could root. They would slow the water a little. The long-legged bird would find them useful sooner than anyone else.

At first light, an older child would hear water running cleanly through a mouth that had always sounded half closed. Someone would test the landing with a double tap and find it strangely solid. They might argue about what had changed. They would have years to do so.
