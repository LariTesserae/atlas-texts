The power would come in the rain, when the plates lifted and the town was briefly less certain of its own shape.

It would not fill the streets with trees. Nothing here had asked to become a garden. It would not silence the rattling or call the animals down from the roofs to meet it. Their attention, passing from eave to eave, was a sufficient greeting.

It would spend a long time beside the pumping hall.

The wheel had outlasted its keepers, but it would not outlast its bearings. A little heat gathered there at every turn. One day the shaft would seize; the troughs would empty; the young in the drains would wait for water that did not come. The power could mend the wheel so perfectly that it would turn forever. Instead, it let the wheel turn as it was and went down through the stone.

It opened a narrow way between a buried seam and the oldest trough. Not a flood, not even a stream while the pump was working: only enough water to rise when the pipes fell still. It made the passage crooked, so grit would settle before reaching the trough, and left a hollow where that grit could collect. Rain would sometimes clear it. The animals, with their flat teeth, might clear it too.

Then it returned to the street and waited through a hundred strokes of the pump. Water ticked from the cables. A long-legged animal set one foot in a gutter and drew it out again. Under the nearest eave, something shifted to make room for something smaller.

The power did nothing more. When it left, the flywheel was still turning, and no creature had reason to look up.
