The power would arrive at midday, when the stone looked warm and was not. It would see the pika carrying a stem twice the length of its head, the moth waiting out a shadow, the snow giving up a little water to the roots. It would understand that the ledge did not need to be made gentle. The cold kept larger plants from taking the seams; the cracks that threatened the wall also gave the pika a place to live.

So it would not warm the stone or make the flowers bloom longer.

It would notice one thing. A block high in the newly broken wall had begun to loosen above the crack where the pika kept its hay. When frost took hold that night, the block would fall across the entrance. There were other ways down for the animal, but no other opening large enough for its winter store.

The power would wait until the pika had gone below the ledge. Then it would ease the block outward by no more than the width of a seed. In the night it would fall as it was always going to fall, striking bare rock instead of the crack. By morning it would be one more stone among the rubble. The pika would hurry past it with a mouthful of grass and take no notice.

Later, the person who had built the windbreak would return. They would find the fallen block and lift it, thinking to make the shelter a little higher. Its weight would bring their hand close to the green seam beneath the wall. There they would see the small tracks, and a few stems drawn carefully into darkness. They would set the block back down where it lay.

That would be enough. The visitor would sit behind the low stones, with their hands out of the wind, until the sun left the ledge. The pika would remain hidden. Before the person went, it would come out again and gather what the short day had offered.
