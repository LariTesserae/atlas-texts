The power would not make the mountain still. Too much of the city had been built on the knowledge that it was moving, and stillness granted without warning might teach a dangerous kind of trust.

It would watch first. It would learn which tanks filled during rain, which districts received less than their share, and where a new supply could be reached without sending anyone into unstable stone.

Then, beyond the outer workshops, it would open a seam in one of the pale, blunt folds. Not a torrent: a spring, cool enough to cup in the hands, slow enough that the ground beneath it would not give way. Its course would remain open to the sky. If the mountain shifted, the water could find another way down.

The first people to notice would mark the rock and measure the flow. Others would come with vessels. Someone would argue for a pipe before there was enough water to justify one; someone else would insist on checking the slope for a week. The power would leave them to that argument. They knew how to make room for what changed.

It would not bring a garden overnight, or summon birds to stay. In time, perhaps, a little dark moss would appear where the spring wetted the stone. A child might find it while carrying water home and call someone else to see. The mountain would sound its low note beneath them, and afterward somebody would check the nearest survey mark, as before.
