The power would wait first. A room that has lived by one drop at a time should not be changed in an afternoon.

It would learn which roots drink from the basin and which hold to the warm stone. It would watch the low crown through a dry spell and a wet one. Only then would it lift the mineral-crusted lid of the service post.

Inside, it would find the means to give the chamber more water. It would leave that setting alone. Instead, it would repair a failing seal and clear a narrow passage the roots had not entered, so that the next years would resemble the last good ones. It would make a second path for warmth and light, quiet enough to remain unused unless the buried line failed. No leaf would have to learn a brighter day.

At the service opening, the power would ease away a little earth. Not enough to admit weather, or feet, or the sudden appetites of other creatures. Just enough that the opening would not vanish under the roots. It would set a stone there that could be moved by someone gentle and would be easy for everyone else to mistake for part of the slope.

Then it would go.

The chamber would still be cold. Water would still fall on the worn patch of stone, with long silences between drops. A frond would die in the basin; roots would pass through it. Nothing inside would know it had been spared a failure. That would be the kindness: not to make the small lives grateful, but to let them keep their time.
