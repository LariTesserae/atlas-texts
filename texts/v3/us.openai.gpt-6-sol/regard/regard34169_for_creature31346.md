The power did not descend upon the plateau. There was nowhere to land without landing on it.

For a while it watched from the thin sky. It saw a pale animal wait six hours for a rib to come within reach, and another caught when the seam beneath its nest closed only halfway. It saw that the plateau’s great motions were neither careless nor careful. The little bodies were too small for it to feel.

The power could have made the stone still. That would have saved some lives that day. It would also have stopped the slow outward growth by which the plateau made room for itself. So it left the stone its freedom, and turned its attention to what could be felt.

It entered a black mast that had fallen across a seam. For one night the horizon bent between that mast and the next, and the power learned how much warning passed through them: the strain of a rib before it parted, the direction in which a flat would loosen, the place where two lengths of stone would meet. The animals already felt a little of this through their feet. The papery growth felt a little more.

At dawn, the leaves on a rib about to turn folded hours before the powder stirred. The animals feeding there stopped and found a seam that would remain open. Farther away, where a nest lay in the path of a closing crack, the lining of dark leaves lifted against sleeping bodies until one woke. The family came out cross and hungry. The seam closed behind them.

The power did not make every crossing safe. It could not always tell where the plateau would settle, and sometimes there was no firm route. It did not want the animals to mistake its warnings for a promise. What it gave them was time, whenever time could help: a tremor arriving early through a broad foot, a leaf folding in advance of the wind, a mast casting its narrow shadow across ground that would soon be elsewhere.

Years passed. The plateau still broke and healed, its fresh faces whitening within days. Nests were still lost. Small pale animals still waited on the flats while stone moved too silently to hear.

But more often, when a rib quietly came apart, the animals were already standing on the side that would hold.
