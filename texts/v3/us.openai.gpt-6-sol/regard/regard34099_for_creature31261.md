The power would come expecting, perhaps, to find something broken. It would spend a long time learning not to mistake slowness for distress.

It would watch a husk turn around a stone. It would watch a bird pull a crab from the mud and another crab escape into its doorway. It would hear the frogs calling from sedges that seemed too small to hold them. Nothing there would ask to be made gentler. To save every crab would be to starve the birds; to fill every pool would erase the shallow edges where the fish fed.

So it would leave the plain much as it was.

There was one thing it could do without changing the terms of anyone’s life. At the far end of the flats, where the faint currents gathered, a little clay had begun to settle across an old passage. Water still got through, but less each season. The power would touch that clay as lightly as water touched it: not carving a new channel, only loosening what had lately closed the old one. The next rain would carry the silt away. Some pools would last a little longer into the dry weeks, and others would still empty. The birds would have to search. The crabs would still need their doorways.

Then the power would withdraw.

A bird might stand in the restored trickle one morning and catch a fish. Another might find nothing there. Neither would know what had happened downstream, and neither would owe anyone thanks. Benevolence, in that place, would be small enough to pass for weather.
