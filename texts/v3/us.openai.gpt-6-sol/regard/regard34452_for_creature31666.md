The power would spend a long time watching before it touched anything.

It would notice that the people are not waiting to be rescued. They know how to live with a street that lengthens and with water that arrives without rain. To make the city still, or dry, or bright might take away as much as it gave.

But one morning, a doorway would recede while someone was ill inside. A neighbor would set out with a bowl of water and find, after an hour’s walking, that the door was no nearer. The power would see the neighbor turn back—not for lack of care, but because others needed them too.

That night it would make a small change.

A person standing at one end of a lengthened passage could put a hand against the wall and feel a faint answering warmth wherever another hand rested on that same wall. It would not shorten the way. It would not tell them who waited at its far end. But they could pause, palm to stone, and know they had not been lost to one another.

The people might first discover it by accident. A child, feeling for a vanished step, would steady themself against the wall. Somewhere beyond the extra square, an old resident would feel warmth beneath their fingers and press back. For a while neither would move.

The power would leave the water its tides and the new rooms their time to find their depth. It would not light the basin or explain the figures at the edge of the mud. It would watch to see whether the gift was welcome. If it was, it would remain no larger than a hand against a wall.

And if, in time, the residents found another way to keep faith across the city’s distances, the power would let the warmth fade.
