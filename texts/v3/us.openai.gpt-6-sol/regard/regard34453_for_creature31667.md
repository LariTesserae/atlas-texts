The power would not begin by opening a chamber.

It would be tempting. The seams shine; the walls are under tension; everything about them resembles a promise held too long. But the power would notice the sharp, unweathered ridges and the droplets that neither swell nor fall. Stillness here is not necessarily failure. The chambers may be doing exactly what they are meant to do.

So it would make itself small enough to wait beneath a leaf.

It would feel for the charge in the roots without interrupting it. It would learn how water passes through the dark mat, how salts leave it, how a new sheet of fibre changes the strain on a wall a great distance away. This might take years. The plant has a horizon for a body; a wise visitor would not mistake one quiet afternoon for its whole life.

In time, the power would find one place where a narrow vessel had begun to close. It would not widen the vessel by force. It would lift a little of the pressure around it, no more than the plant itself might have done if water had gathered there differently. Then it would wait again.

The vessel would either take up the opening or let it close. That answer would matter.

If the plant took it up, a bead on one of the hanging strands might at last grow heavy enough to fall. Far away, a chamber might add a thin sheet of fibre. Nothing would burst into flower; no animals would be summoned to make the silence seem kinder. The power would leave the vast body its own pace and its own purposes.

And if the vessel closed, the power would accept that, too. It would withdraw its touch and remain, for a while, simply present: another warmth under the leaves, careful not to ask the living plain to become a different place merely because it had been seen.
