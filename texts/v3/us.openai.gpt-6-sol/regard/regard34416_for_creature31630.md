The power would arrive while the red lamp was dark.

It would first listen to the stone. The crack was young, and some of its edges were still deciding where to rest. Deep behind the upper bridge, a slab no one could see had begun to lean against two bolts. One more hard winter would put its weight on the walkway.

The power would not press the walls together. There were bunks and workshops between them now, and hands that knew every rung. Instead, it would let the slab settle that night, slowly enough to make a sound through the boots of the people on watch. They would find the strained bolts before morning and close the bridge. When they opened the wall to mend it, they would find sound stone farther back, where new anchors could hold.

Then the power would go above the town, onto the open mountainside. There was no spring to uncover. But snow lay there in winter, blown against a ridge that shed its melt down the far face. The power would turn a little of that melt toward a shelf of rock above the crack. By summer the residents would have built a covered cistern there, with a pipe running down to the repair stalls. They would still need the freight cages. They would no longer have to wait for one to bring every drink.

It would leave the birds their warm cabinets. It would leave grain enough for beetles, and beetles enough for the pale hunters. It would make no garden in the grit and no promise that the mountain had become gentle.

Years later, someone standing on the upper bridge would feel the cage pass beneath their feet. The rail would be firm in their hand. Behind them, water would tick into a tank; ahead, the birds would settle to watch the unloading. None of this would keep the red lamp from coming on, or the people from stepping back when it did.
