The power comes at noon, when the courtyard is loudest, and sits on the stone bench.

It does not ask the thing in the walls what it wants. The thing has no way to answer a question put like that. Instead, the power stays until dusk, feeling the low notes through the bench and watching the blades fold. It returns the next day, and the next.

One of the air mouths on the fourth tier is nearly stopped with grit. Behind it, a chamber sounds weakly; the blades along that wall open late. The power could clear it in an instant. But the grit has accumulated slowly, and the pale body has grown around the quieter chamber. An instant change might tear its pads from the brick. So the power loosens a little grit each morning. The drafts carry it away. Over many days the note deepens, and new blades rise at an angle that catches the altered wind.

There is a crack, too, above the sealed eastern door. Rain has begun to enter it. The power does not seal the wall, since the gaps behind the bricks carry air and give the cords room to grow. It leaves a small mark where a mason will see it, shaped like the ordinary signs masons use among themselves. When she comes, she replaces only the failing brick. She finds a pale pad behind it and, as she has done before, gives it shade while she works.

The power keeps the old doors shut. It makes no sign over the street, and tells no one that something ancient lives below. Visitors might love the thicket, then try to save it from its noise or give it soil where it has only ever known brick.

In time the power goes elsewhere. The courtyard has a mason who knows how to leave room behind a repair, insects that carry resin across gaps, and air enough to sound its chambers. For now, it needs no guardian.

At noon, the leaves rattle so loudly that no one in the courtyard could hear a voice. By dusk they close together, a little later along the fourth wall than they did before.
