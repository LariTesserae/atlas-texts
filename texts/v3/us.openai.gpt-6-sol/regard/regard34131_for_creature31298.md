The power would wait through several pump cycles before doing anything.

It would learn which channels had to stay clear, how much water the towers had to move, and whether the dark reach beyond the lamps led anywhere living. It would watch the crabs retreat beneath the grates and emerge again. It would notice that the bird had learned the warning light, though the crabs had not.

Then it would make a few changes, none large enough to alter the work of the basin.

At the intake towers, water would still draw down with its usual force, but the narrow openings where the smallest crabs shelter would no longer lead into the machinery. Along the retaining walls, a few rough, damp recesses would remain after every surge. The loose slag in those recesses would stop washing away. Reeds would take hold there gradually, with clear channels between them, so the pumps could do what they were built to do.

The warning light would come on a little earlier. Not much earlier: long enough for pale feet to disappear under a ledge, and for the bird to lift from the steps.

The power would also find the people who maintained the place. It would not give them a vision or command them to love what had appeared on their concrete. It would let one of them see a crab carrying its new, soft shell across the wet grit. Later, when an intake screen was due to be replaced, that person would remember the crab and ask for a different mesh. Someone else would mark the reed pockets on a plan so they would not be scraped clean by mistake.

There would still be cold, and noise, and the hard rush of water. Some crabs would still lose legs. The bird would still hunt. A wise power would not make this unfinished basin harmless by making it lifeless.

But between the warning light and the roar, there would be time to get home.
