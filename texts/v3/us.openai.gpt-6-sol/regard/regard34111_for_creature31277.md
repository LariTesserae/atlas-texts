The power would wait for the wheel to stop.

It would not fill the cistern with fish or turn the water into a garden. The bathhouse needs its clean water, and the snail has learned the place as it is: the knocks, the current, the long quiet afterward.

But the eggs sit just beneath the pipe. At the next turning of the wheel, the power would take the force out of one small part of the falling sheet. The cool water would still enter the cistern; the bathers above would notice nothing. Beneath the clay lip, the clear patch would hold.

Then it would give the snail a little more to eat—not a feast, only time. A thin living film would gather along the mortar seams, enough for a slow circuit and, later, for smaller mouths. The shoots would remain few. The tiles would remain visible through the water.

When someone came down to inspect the new work, they would see the shoots in the joint and leave them. They would check the sluice and go back up. The wheel would knock again.

The snail would draw in its feelers. Under the pipe, the eggs would tremble and stay.
