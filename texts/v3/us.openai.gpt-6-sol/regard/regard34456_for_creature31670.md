The power would first spend a day at the rim.

It would watch the shadows enter the passages that were not passages, and the small animals warm themselves there. It would watch a long one clean dust from a neighbour’s back. At night it would listen to the rasp beneath the plates. By morning it would know that a wider aisle, made by cutting, would not be a kindness.

It would begin at the entrance. The raised plate was still sound, but its edge could catch a foot. Without lowering it onto the animals’ passage, the power would rub the exposed edge smooth, as the makers had rubbed the others. Then it would lift the loose ends of the new lashings and tie them where nothing beneath the floor could become caught in them.

The harder work would be outside the grove. The basin had been built for what the makers knew how to start, not for what it would become. The power would teach them to add to the rim and the cords beneath it: a little more support, then a little more floor, fitted while the animals were awake and able to move. It would have them leave the roof higher over the crowded crowns. No stem would be pulled apart to make the work easy. Where a plate had to be lifted, they would wait until the rasping below had moved elsewhere.

The long ones would not be carried into the new space. They would find its seams when they wanted them. Some might never leave their old tunnels. The power would make sure there was room for both choices.

When the work was done, a person standing at the entrance would still mistake the far rim for an aisle, and still have to turn back. The grove would remain difficult to cross. But its floor would bear its growing weight, its blades would have somewhere to press, and beneath the plates there would be cords enough for a tunnel to be taken apart and made again.
