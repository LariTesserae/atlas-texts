The power arrives intending to bring rain.

It has seen the pale stone from far above, the black grass curled tight in its cracks, the young grazer lifting a muzzle powdered with a meal too fine for its narrow lips to gather. Rain seems, at first, an obvious kindness.

But it waits.

It watches a whole basin lower its heads. It watches the calf try again. It sees that the grass is alive because it keeps its water, and that a sudden stream would carry the nourishing dust off the shelves. It notices, too, the older grazer standing beside the calf: the slight turn of one leg, the space made and held. There is care here already. The power has nearly mistaken slowness for need.

It leaves the sky alone.

Instead, it kneels where the calf next puts its mouth and lays a hand flat against the pavement. Beneath its palm, a little of the hard stone loosens. Not enough to change the shelf, or the routes cut across it—only enough that the calf’s small lips can lift a mouthful without searching so long. The loosened grains settle into a shallow hollow, where the wind cannot take them.

Then the power rises and goes.

By the time the calf lowers its head again, nothing on the plateau appears to have changed. Its muzzle touches stone. It gathers more than it did before.

The older grazer remains beside it. Somewhere across the basin, a bird turns one eye toward the small sound of teeth.
