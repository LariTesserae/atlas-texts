The power comes when the basin is empty. It looks down through the grass and sees no animals at all.

It waits.

After a while, a drop forms on a root and falls beside the rounded stones. Then a little throat moves in the seam behind them. The power sees the eggs, and sees how much of their keeping depends on rain that has already ended.

It does not fill the basin. Water held there too long would change the hollow: insects would breed where they do not now, plants would take the open floor, and the small lives that belong to brief rain would have to make room. Instead, deep in the limestone, the power eases a narrow seam. The next rain will leave a little more of itself in the rock. For a few days afterward, drops will continue to gather above the eggs.

At the rim, it finds the beginnings of a path—grass bent by people who have stopped to look into the crack. It lets the grass rise. Farther along the shelf, where a view opens over the valley, it gives the walkers a good place to pause. Some will still find the hollow. Most will pass without knowing what they have missed.

When the sun reaches the floor, the little animals come out and lie against one another. One catches an insect. Another is caught by a bird at the entrance. The power does not close the opening.

It stays long enough to see the edge of light leave them. Then it goes, having made one small change that the hollow can bear.
