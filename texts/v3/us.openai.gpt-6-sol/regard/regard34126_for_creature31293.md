The power watches for a while before it does anything.

At first it is tempted to still the water. It could seal every seam in the stone, fix each pool in its bowl, and leave the bridges leading always to the same banks. But it sees how the hollow lives by its changing channels: the birds at the waterline, the beetles beneath the ice, the people who know how to make room for a flow. A still hollow would be another place, and no one has asked for it.

So the power makes one small change. Beneath the ice, a little way ahead of a moving current, it lets trapped air gather and travel. The air gives a faint, hollow ticking as it passes under stone.

The first person to hear it is a child carrying cord to a drying rack. She stops, listens, and points. An elder kneels beside her. Together they follow the sound past two houses to a stair that is still dry. They fetch a wedge and open a channel before water reaches the threshold.

After that, the ticking comes sometimes and sometimes not. It cannot say where the water will go once released, and it gives no warning of snow or failing rope. People still watch the dark lines under ice. They still carry planks and teach their children to listen to stone. But when water begins to move too deep to see, it no longer moves entirely unheard.

The power also finds a shelf of sound shale above the hollow and keeps it free of the ice that would split it. In a dangerous thaw, there is room there for everyone to stand together, with their bundles and the small animals that will follow warmth. No path appears to lead them up. One spring, looking for a place to store spare planks, the people find the shelf themselves.

The power asks for no thanks. It listens when the people move a channel, and learns, as they do, that helping is not the same as deciding where the water ought to run.
