The power would spend its first night listening.

It would feel the pressure building below the faults and hear which vanes answered. It would learn that a vent’s silence was as useful to the creatures in its seams as its heat. It would watch the hunters take their places at the crossings and leave them there. Hunger lived on the plateau too.

Near morning, it would notice a plate-runner beneath a slab that had settled badly. The runner could breathe, but it could not turn. When the sun warmed the black stone, the hollow would become an oven.

The power would not lift the slab. A wing hunter was passing overhead, and the sudden movement would tell it where to look. Instead, the power would wait for the next shudder from the deep fault. At its gentlest point it would add the smallest pressure it could manage. The slab would click against its neighbour and shift the width of a leaf.

The runner would back out, pause, and hurry along the pale line toward cover. Beneath the shifted plate, a little moisture would remain for the plant whose root had reached it.

For a while the power would consider doing more. It could deepen every hollow, quiet the dangerous vents, make the mist last until noon. But the roots followed the cracks because water was scarce. The herds moved because grazing was scattered. The tiny grazers needed the intervals when the hot gas stopped. To make the plateau gentle all at once would be to make it strange to those who lived there.

So it would stay through the morning, learning where its small touch had led. Then it would leave the plateau its own rhythm: the hiss and the thrum, the long quiet after, and the lives that knew what to do when each began.
