The power would not still the lake.

It would watch a storm strip the gravel from one shore and lay it down on another, and understand that this was not damage. It would watch the brown river water enter the clear, and the fish take their places along its edge. For a while it would do nothing but learn where the young hid, which shelves held eggs, and how the current changed when the pumps began.

Then it would turn its attention to the intake.

Not to break it. People beyond the mountains needed what the machinery provided, and the lake had room for them too. But the power would give the people who tended it a clearer way of seeing what was already happening. On a day when weeds clogged the screens, one worker would notice the small fish holding in the slack water beside them. Another would remember a design for a gentler approach channel, and this time have the patience to bring it before the others. They would fit a wider screen, leave a route back to open water, and bring the pumps up slowly enough that a fish could feel the pull changing and turn away.

The power would stay through the work, because good intentions can leave sharp edges. It would let the workers see where silt settled after the channel was cut, and which patch of pondweed the young used in spring. When they moved the work a little farther along the shore, the power would be glad.

Under the shelves, the old fish would still guard their eggs. Some years a storm would take them. Some young would lose their way in the sudden currents, as they always had. The power would not make the lake safe from being a lake.

It would leave the wind, the shifting gravel, and the long cold water as they were. Near the intake, when the pumps began, a fish would feel the current gather, lift its fins from the stone, and find a way past.
