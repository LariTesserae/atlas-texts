The power would come in rain, when the walls were awake.

It would not begin by asking who built the rooms. It would follow the water beneath them: through old mortarless joints, past roots and pockets of grit, into the dark where a seam-dweller had spread itself between two stones. It would wait long enough to see a thread extend, gather what the water brought, and leave behind a skin thinner than a fingernail.

There would be things it could do. It could raise the fallen roofs, make the square whole, seal every crack against winter. But a sealed wall would be a wall without passages for water. A winter without broken seams would leave fewer places for new lives to begin. Restoration would be another kind of ruin.

So it would leave the stones as they were.

At the basin’s edge, though, a loose slab rocked whenever rain struck it. Beneath it, water vanished down a gap and burst out lower in the wall, taking grit and living threads with it. The power would settle the slab a hand’s breadth to one side. The next storm would still enter. It would simply enter slowly.

Then the power would turn its attention to the visitors who might one day come. Not all of them would know that a smooth join between stones could be alive. It would give them no warning carved into the square, no voice sounding from the doorways. Instead, those who stepped toward a dark seam would find themselves pausing to watch a bead of water hold beneath a ledge. Some would move on. A few would choose another place to put their feet.

Afterward the wind would go on changing its tune. Ice would open what the rain had joined. Under the square, the largest dwellers would continue their slow work, neither preserved from the mountain nor hurried to finish it.
