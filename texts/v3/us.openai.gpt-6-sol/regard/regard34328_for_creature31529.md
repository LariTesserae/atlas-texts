The power would not begin by clearing the weed.

From above, the district might look neglected. From inside, it would feel the roots holding silt against the roofs, and the leaves carrying news of a strained pump into the street. It would wait through a working day, with a hand against the brick as the tenders do, until it understood how slowly the water must move.

Then it would go to the single chimney.

For years, silt had gathered in its lower courses. There was still room for the water to leave, but less room each season. The power would loosen no brick and send no sudden cloud through the streets. Instead, it would open a second way out through an old drain beyond the workshops, a narrow way at first. The current would divide so gently that a prawn at a grille would only turn a little to keep its place.

By afternoon, a tender would notice that the return pipe had warmed earlier than usual. Following it, hand to wall, they would find the old drain breathing water. They would find, beside its mouth, a place where a gate could be fitted and reached again when it needed tending.

The power would leave that work to them. A second outlet could clog; a gate could wear. It would be better for those who knew the district to know what had changed, and to be able to change it back.

Before leaving, the power would pause at the clock outside the pump house. It would not restore the hands. Weed had grown through their mounting hole, and no one needed them to tell when the day was done. It would free one root caught under a sharp edge of metal, then let it settle where the water could pass around it.

The next season, the clear lanes would be much as they had been. The snails’ paths would vanish behind new growth. The wheels would go on turning within their worn housings. There would simply be two ways for the water to leave, and someone would know where both of them were.
