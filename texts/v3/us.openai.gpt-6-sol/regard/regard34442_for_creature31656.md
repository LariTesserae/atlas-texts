The power would not lift the stone away. The people had made a town beneath it, and knew how to live there. Nor would it finish the chambers ahead of them. Those rooms belonged, in part, to the hands that would make them.

It would listen first: to the roof settling after rain, to the water passing through rock too deep for the townspeople to hear. Beneath the far work face ran a narrow, steady course of it. A spring could be brought into the town there, but an unwary cut might draw water through the clay under the pillars.

One morning, a mason testing that wall would hear an unfamiliar note. She would tap again, call someone over, and together they would mark a place no wider than a hand. As they worked, the stone would come free cleanly. A little water would show behind it—not a flood, only enough to fill a cup while they stood and watched.

They would widen the opening cautiously, then stop. They would line a channel with clay, set a basin beneath its end, and keep the nearest floor clear until they understood where the overflow went. The power would give the water a dependable path through the deep stone. It would leave the channel, the basin, and the rules for sharing them to the town.

In time, people would still set vessels beneath the seams after rain. Children would still learn the sound of a dangerous block. At the newest edge, the workers would still fit a brace before taking another stone. But on dry mornings, someone could carry a full pitcher home without searching the deeper rooms first.

The power would stay long enough to hear that water had become an ordinary sound among the footsteps and tapping tools. Then it would turn its attention elsewhere.
