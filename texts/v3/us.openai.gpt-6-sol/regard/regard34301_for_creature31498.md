The power would come down from the ridge on foot.

It would notice that the valley was not waiting to become something else. The moss was already doing its work. So were the sedges, the water, the creatures too small to see until a shadow made them move. The power would not make a forest overnight, or lay deep soil over the gravel. A flood would tear through such gifts, and the life here had its own way of meeting floods.

At the intake, it would stand long enough to feel the hammering through the soles of its feet. Then it would follow the water backward, finding the shallow routes that remained wet only when the streams ran high, and the ones the machinery had left dry for years.

It would speak first to the people who kept the turbines turning. Someone depended on that electricity; someone knew which gate could be altered and which could not. Together they would leave more water in the open channels, especially in the lean weeks. They would fit the grate so that what drifted downstream was less likely to be trapped against it. Where the service track cut across the wet ground, they would give the water a way through, and keep boots and wheels to one narrow crossing.

If the power could quiet the powerhouse without stopping it, it would do that too. Not to make the valley picturesque, but so the people who came to tend it could hear the small streams again, and notice when one changed course.

Afterward, the power would leave no sign on the ridge. It would return sometimes to see what its care had missed. It would find bare stones, washed-out webs, and new channels cut through places it had hoped to protect. It would leave those changes alone unless there was good reason not to. Below them, in the gravel where water still spread, the thin living floor would go on making itself.
