The power would arrive without a sign. It would spend a morning where visitors are asked to wait, at the edge of the market square, and discover that waiting is how one begins to understand the place.

It would see the children spill water and the gardeners make use of what remained. It would watch the mason lift the loose tread, clear the grit beneath it, and set it back. By afternoon it would know better than to fill every basin or make every bridge last forever. The people already knew how to live with a small and uncertain supply of water. They knew how to listen when they crossed.

But the mountain was new, and some of its seams ran deep beyond anyone’s hearing. So the power would give it one small mercy: when stone began to shift far inside a shelf, the clay basins nearest it would sound before they cracked. Not loudly. Just a thin, uneven note when a cup touched the rim, or when the morning’s water first gathered there.

The first time it happened, someone would set down her cup and call for the mason. People would have time to move a garden, unload a bridge, or leave a house until the shelf had settled. They would still have to decide what to do. They might sometimes decide wrongly. The power would not make the stone obedient.

Then it would leave them to their morning work. Across the valley a hammer would ring twice; on a roof ridge, a bird would turn its head toward the sound.
