The power came at the wind hour and did nothing at first.

It listened to the knocking vents, the pitch of the bridges, the seed-catchers ticking against the low streets. It listened longer to the dark growth beneath them all. The town had asked an old wall to widen, and the wall was answering as it could: a little room made here, a little strength taken from there. No one had yet heard that the bridge beside it had begun to bear the cost.

The power could have widened the doorway in an instant. It could have made every tower spacious and every bridge strong. But the town was not stone to be cut, and its residents had spent generations learning how to live with something that had needs of its own.

So, during the next wind, the power gave the bridge one clear note.

It rang beneath the deeper hum, soft enough not to alarm the animals in the streets, but plain to anyone who knew the bridge’s usual voice. A woman crossing under a low roof stopped and listened. In the morning she brought others. They found the strained seam and began the slow work of persuading the wall to grow another way. For a season, the child used the longer route home.

Far out on the flats, where no one lived, the power did one other thing. A charge had been taking the same narrow path through an ancient rib, heating its living skin each bright hour. The power opened no channel and laid down no new growth. It eased a small resistance at a place where two neighboring growths already touched. By dusk, some of the charge had found the other path. Both growths kept their hold on the rock.

Then the power left the plateau to its wind and its patient negotiations. The doorway would widen. The seed-catchers would still have to outrun the hunters. And when the next storm came, the people in the towers would listen for anything they had not heard before.
