The power would not stop the pumps. Too much lives downstream of their turning, and too much depends on the gates closing when they must.

It would begin by listening through the floor.

For a while it would know the basin as the seam-dwellers know it: the deep steady shudder, the small change that precedes a gate’s whine, the difference between silt caught in a drain and silt about to lift. It would learn which welds keep their young, which trenches feed the larger fish, and which cleanings are necessary to keep the gates from failing.

Then it would speak to the crews in a way they could use. On a survey screen, perhaps, a technician would notice that the empty mounting frames were not empty. Someone would count the grey shapes along a new weld and come back with a closer camera. The power would make discovery easy, not irresistible. It would leave room for the people who work here to understand what they had found.

There would be changes. Where no part needed to move, new panels would have shallow, roughened margins that held weed and silt without letting either reach a hinge. Before a frame was filled, the crew would set a sheltered strip nearby and wait for the young to take it. Cleaning would still happen, but a little at a time, with places left for the adults to return to. The engineers would test the strips against the strongest draw of the pumps. Some designs would fail, and they would change them.

The power might do one thing no crew could do: give the seam-dwellers a little more time. Not a new instinct, and not safety from every fish. Only a clearer tremor before an opening, carried along the metal far enough for the smallest bodies to fasten down or move out of the first rush.

Afterward the basin would still look built. Lamps would stand alone in the blue distance. Motors would whine; fish would gather at the screens. But along some bolts the grey film would break into motion, and on the new welds, where the current slackened, the young would remain after the work craft had gone.
