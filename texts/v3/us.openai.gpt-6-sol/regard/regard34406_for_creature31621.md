The power would come quietly enough that the toad would not have to hide.

It would look first at the trough, and at the bead of water gathering beneath its lip. The water was not much, but it lasted. The gap between the stones stayed cool; the bank gave the toad a way out; rain could still find the hollow. Nothing here needed to be made grander.

So the power would leave the wall as it was. It would not widen the trough into a pool or fill the bare floor with flowers. It would make one small repair where no eye could see it: deep in the earth, where a trickle had begun to stray from the stone, it would ease the silt back into place. In dry weather, the trough would keep its narrow strip of water a little longer. In rain, it would take no more than it had before.

Then the power would sit on the old wall until the light weakened.

The toad would emerge from between the lowest stones and rest with one side against them. A fly would pass. The toad would eat it. A drop would fall from the lip and strike the water, and the toad would turn toward the sound.

Seeing that the toad had cool soil, water it could reach, and room to leave, the power would go.
