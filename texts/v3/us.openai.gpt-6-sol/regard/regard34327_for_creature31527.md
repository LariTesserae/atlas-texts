The power comes to the saddle at dusk and, for a while, does nothing.

From above, it can see where the water enters the mountain and where it comes out. It can see the old channels beneath the terraces and the narrow places where a small change would send a pool through the floor of a shelter. It could straighten every passage. It does not know which passages the workers need.

Beside one shelter, an empty bowl has stood beneath a dry face for years. A worker comes out carrying tomorrow’s wedge and finds the bowl full. There has been no drip. The water is clear and cold, and at its bottom lies a single iron-red grain.

The worker calls another, then remembers that calls travel badly here. They carry the bowl inside instead.

In the morning, the oldest workers take it to the dry face. They leave the wedge beside the bowl and sit through a change of wind. The water does not return. No crack has opened. At last one of them dips a finger into the bowl and touches the stone.

For an instant, they feel water moving deep behind it: not a vision of the whole mountain, only the pressure of one passage and the weight of a loose piece lodged across it. They take their hand away. The feeling stops.

They discuss it for most of the day. Then they make a mark beside the bowl, so anyone who touches the face will know what they may feel, and may choose not to. They leave the passage alone. The lodged stone is holding back water above three shelters.

That night the power learns something from them. It had seen the stone and the water, but had not thought of the shelters first.

After that, it offers no more than a touch at certain dry faces: a little knowledge where someone has set a bowl and waited. The workers decide where to look, whether to move a wedge, and when to let a drip wander. Some days they refuse the help altogether. The power stays near enough to hear that refusal.

Years later, a child asks why the bowl beside the first face is still there, though it has never filled again. The worker beside them says that it tells them where not to strike. Then they put the day’s grit on a slab, and together they begin to sort it.
