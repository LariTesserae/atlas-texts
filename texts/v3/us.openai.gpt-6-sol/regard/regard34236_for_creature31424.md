The power would not turn off the fans. It would hear how the little animal listens to them, and how the people depend on them too.

It would begin at the pipe. Condensation would still gather there, but the next drop would fall sooner. A shallow place in the clay beneath it would hold water without letting a wagon wheel crush it. The animal would find the change before anyone else did. It would drink and retreat, as it always had.

Then the power would go aboveground.

It would show the people what their lamps could not: the trees whose roots had been cut on two sides, the ridge that would fail when the thaw came, the stretch ahead where the same work could pass under open ground instead. Not in a dream they might dismiss, but in things they could measure. A survey stake would sink crookedly. A forester would ask to see the drawings. Someone who had been worried about the roots would at last have enough evidence to be heard.

The line would be moved. It would cost time. The excavators would brace the older cuts, cover the bare root faces, and leave gaps low in the barriers where a small animal could pass. In the first quiet week, the water beneath the pipe would remain, though the pipe itself had been taken away.

The power would not try to make the animal stay. When beetles returned to the sheltered moss and the woods above grew easier to reach, it might leave the galleries altogether. That would be a good ending, even if no one saw it happen.

For now, after the last wagon passed, it would come out from behind a root bent around a stone. It would pause on the hard floor, whiskers moving, then go to the water.
