The power does not enter the room. There is nowhere it could stand without crushing a wall or a fern, and the builders are already working around one visitor.

It waits outside, where the dark cloud passes close to the mountain. Through the narrow opening in the reedwork it sees the pale moth unfold its wings. The builders flatten themselves. Deep below, a new piece begins its long scrape into place.

This time the scrape does not stop.

The summit tilts a little. A channel behind the walls fills faster than it can empty, and water starts to press against the warm spaces where the builders sleep. They are already moving: opening joints, carrying clay, guiding roots away from a tile that is lifting. The power can see what they cannot see from inside. The channel’s outlet, far down the outer face of the mountain, has been closed by a fallen reed mat.

The power considers taking the mat away. It could also still the rain, hold the summit level, fix every wall at the length measured by the cord. For a moment the room would be safe and finished.

Instead, it puts one finger beneath the loose edge of the mat.

The reed rises just enough for water to run under it. A thin stream spills into the sky. Inside, the pressure eases against the retaining walls. The builders feel the change. One presses its head to a block; others answer from across the room, and together they find the channel that has begun to drain.

The power holds the reed until a builder reaches the outlet. It arrives with a shred of cord and a mouthful of clay. The power keeps still while the builder ties the mat back where it belongs, leaving a clear passage beneath. Only when the knot is firm does the finger withdraw.

The mountain completes its scrape. Water falls from the ceiling in heavy drops again. The moth folds its wings.

By morning the builders have given the outlet a little wall of its own. It is six knots long when they set it. By evening it is seven, and they have begun to make use of the extra length.
