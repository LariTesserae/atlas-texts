The power came to the hollow on a night when the tank was nearly empty. It could have filled the tank. Instead, it stood in the morning queue and watched the dew keeper lift her testing cup to the green lamp.

“There’s soil in it,” the keeper said. She held the cup so the person behind her could see. “Yesterday there were wings.”

The power asked where the water had entered. Together they followed the pipes, past the workshops and the smooth-worn flywheel notch. At the ceiling, the copper sheets were sound, but a new root had curled beneath one edge and tipped it toward a crack. Night after night, some of the water had run away into the soil.

“I can cut that root,” the keeper said.

The power listened at the crack. Air moved through it. Farther in, something small gave a sharp cry and moved away.

“I wouldn’t,” the power said.

They fetched the cabinetmaker. He brought a ladder, two wooden pegs, and a scrap of copper from the workshop. While he worked, the power held the lamp. He made a narrow lip below the sheet, loose enough to lift as the root grew, and led the escaping drops back into the pipe. He marked the new position on the doorpost. By then the lamps in the court had been lit, and the bats were coming through the opening above.

The power stayed until the first drop struck the tank. It waited for a second. Then it went up through the grove.

There were other things it could do there. It could draw rain out of a distant sky or make the trees yield water from their roots. But the grove was old, and the town had learned much by attending to it. The power left those things alone.

Before dawn, it found one broad leaf bent across a seam in the earth. Rain caught there would have gone straight down a bat passage, washing grit toward the tank. The power eased the leaf back into the light, where it could grow as it had been growing. No one in the hollow saw this happen. They noticed only that, after the next rain, the keeper’s testing cup was clear.

For a few weeks she drew a little more water each morning. She still looked into every cup. The cabinetmaker checked his pegs when he passed the doorpost. In spring, when the root lifted the copper lip, he would move it again.
