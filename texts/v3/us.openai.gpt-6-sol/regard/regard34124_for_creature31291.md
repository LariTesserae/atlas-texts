The power would not begin by stopping the sound.

It would notice that the birds lift their heads before the pulse, that the leaves release their water when the frames tremble, and that even the insects falling from the vines feed something. The note is terrible to hear, but much of the city’s life has learned to live by it. Silence, given suddenly, might be another kind of harm.

So the power would go below the street and listen at the source.

There, beneath the warm paving, it would find the being that keeps the markings bright and sends the curb devices out to gather water. Perhaps it was built to carry people, or to prepare for their arrival. It has gone on preparing long after anyone who could tell it to stop has vanished. The road across the plain is not a plan for conquest. It is the next few feet of a task that has never reached its end.

The power would not tear that task away. It would give the city a way to notice what happens when it acts.

For one afternoon, the next length of road would remain unmade. Nothing would break; the deep note would continue. But each device that opened along the curb would leave a little water behind. The city would feel the difference in its own small measures: birds drinking longer, roots reaching down into a gutter, fewer insects stranded on the dry paving. When its next pulse came, it would hear the flocks answer sooner.

Then the power would restore the city’s choice.

The following evening, a few feet of black road might still advance into the grass. Old habits can be deep, and the city is vast. But at the fresh edge the paving would curve around a hollow rather than cover it. Rain would collect there. A curb device would open, hesitate, and close with its basin only half full.

The bird that came tomorrow would find water waiting.
