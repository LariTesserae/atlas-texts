The power would not bring rain.

It would see how much of this city is built around keeping water where it is. Rain enough to run down the streets would swell the root pavement, soften the fitted walls, and wake seeds in every joint. What looked like generosity from above might tear apart the work of centuries.

So it would go below.

Far beneath the terraces, it would turn a little of the rising heat against the salt that had gathered in the old stone. In a few deep cracks, vapor would begin to collect and cool. Not a spring, not a reservoir: only a dampness that thin roots could find. It would appear first where roots still explored, including the cooler districts, and it would return slowly enough that no tower had reason to race toward it.

The power would leave the chimneys free to follow the wandering vents. It would leave the walls their habit of closing streets, and the wheels their imperfect way of carrying fruit and seeds. It would not decide which districts ought to survive. It could not know all that an abandoned stem might still shelter, or what use a buried chamber might find in another century.

But where a cooling vent had left a stand of stems sealed off from every living root, it would ease one old seam open. Just wide enough for an exploring tip to enter. Some would take hold. Some would not.

For a long time, almost nothing would look different. A cart would advance one tooth. Dust would settle in the corners. Heat would breathe through the towers. Then, in a wall thought long dry, a narrow band beneath the outer skin would remain supple through the coldest part of the cycle.

That would be enough. The power would watch a while longer, and let the city decide what to grow there.
