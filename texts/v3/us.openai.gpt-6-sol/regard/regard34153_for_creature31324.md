The power would arrive without a shape the walkers could see. It would spend a long while listening: to water moving inside wood, to the faint strain where one tree bore the weight of another, to the lives that depended on hollows no larger than a hand.

It would learn that the shelf was not failing. Branches fell and became bridges. Pools filled and opened elsewhere. The old stone circles were being taken apart slowly enough that birds still nested in them. There was no wound to close simply because everything was changing.

So it would leave the heart of the shelf alone.

At the outermost margin, though, it would find a fold of living ground pulled thin above the slope. Rain had been collecting in a hollow behind it. Given a few more seasons, the fold would tear away, carrying roots and sleeping places down with it. The power would not hold it fast forever. It would ease the water toward a lower channel and give the roots time to grow across the strain.

No animal would know it had been there. A walker might notice a new place to drink. Frogs would find the shallow channel when the rains came. Far below, a stream would begin a little farther to the left.

Then the power would go, leaving the shelf its own weather and its own slow work.
