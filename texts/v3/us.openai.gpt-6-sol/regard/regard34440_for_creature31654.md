The power would come in the hour before the columns began to shine. It would find the basin nearly dry, and wait.

It would see that the hollows were not empty places needing to be filled. Each had a depth and an hour that mattered: a crack where a frog could remain cool, a pool that kept a fish until evening, a stretch of bare stone the snails crossed only after dark. It would leave the columns untouched. It did not know what made their water rise, and the creatures had learned to live by its coming and going.

But the rim was almost gone. One hard season could let the marsh spread across the dish and lay soft mud over the stone. So, beyond the rim, the power would turn its care to things already growing there. It would help the reeds root deep and the low vines catch the silt that travelled through the marsh. Around the most exposed side, it would let fallen leaves and broken stems gather in a loose, living bank. Water could still enter when the marsh rose. Fish could still find their way in. The basin would simply lose a little less of itself each year.

It would not make the bank too sound. The stone would go on wearing away, as stone does. The birds would go on hunting, and some of the smallest lives would end before the rain joined the pools.

When water climbed the columns, the power would stay long enough to watch the frogs leave their cracks. Then it would go. By evening, shrimp would pass over ground they could not have crossed that morning, and the new reeds at the rim would bend under the weight of arriving birds.
