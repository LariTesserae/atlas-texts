The power would arrive while the pump was running, because it would want to hear the place as its people heard it.

It would first think of stopping the water at the canal wall. Then it would notice the keeper listening to the motor, the worn edges of the drains, the vine carefully cut back from a household’s lock. No one here had mistaken growth for safety, or maintenance for defeat. The power would leave the canal alone.

It would spend a day in the pump house without being seen. There was a slight drag in one gate that the keeper had begun to hear but had not yet found. Deep in its housing, a bearing was wearing unevenly. The power could mend it in an instant. Instead, when the keeper opened the casing the next morning, it would let the failing part come free rather than hold fast. He would see the damage while there was still time to order another.

Its one unmistakable gift would be smaller. Beside the loading steps, where damp marks climbed the brick each season, it would make a narrow line visible to anyone who lived or worked there: the highest water had ever reached. Nothing more would be foretold. The line would fade only if people chose to cover it.

Then it would go down the lanes. Where a sapling was quietly levering a curb toward a drain, its leaves would yellow early, drawing someone’s eye before the next heavy rain. Where fern roots held a bank of loose mortar without troubling the wall, they would be left alone. A bird would nest above the yard, just far enough from the saw to stay when it started.

Before leaving, the power would listen once more from the embankment. The motor would keep its beat. Someone would call across the leaves for a spanner; someone else would answer. Nothing would be made effortless. The district already had people who knew how to care for it. The kindest thing a great power could do was help them notice what they could not yet see, and let the work remain theirs.
