The power watches through several cycles before it touches anything.

The pumps are old, but they still serve streets beyond this room. Stopping them would spare the crab a daily terror and change the water in places the power cannot see from here. So it follows the pipes first. It learns where they go, what they keep clear, and which small parts of their work can be changed.

The loose inspection hatch gets a new pin. On the next stroke, it lifts a finger’s width and settles without a blow.

Inside the pipe, just before it enters the chamber, the power eases a worn edge into a gentler curve. The pump still sends its full measure of water through. The first tremor still reaches the valve wheel in time for the crab to retreat. But the surge no longer hits the recess with quite such force, and a crab gripping the brick can keep its hold.

Last, the power clears a narrow path through the grit along the floor. A little of what the current carries from the streets now catches where the water slows: scraps no larger than the crab could have found before, only a little more often. The power does not place food in its claws. It does not draw it out from shelter.

For three pump strokes, nothing seems different. Then the crab comes sideways from beneath the wheel. It finds a shred caught against the brick and draws it free. At the first shiver of the pipe, it folds itself away again.

Above it, the pumps keep working. The sea presses at the harbor gates. The chamber is still old and loud and mostly bare. But the hatch no longer bangs, and when the water turns, something small can stay where it lives.
