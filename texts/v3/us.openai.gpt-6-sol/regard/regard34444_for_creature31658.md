The power would come first to the square and wait through a full cycle of the pumps.

It would notice that the dark mark is not merely surviving the rise and fall of the water. It lives by that small distance: wetting, drying, holding damp beneath its crust. To lift the water permanently would drown part of it. To lower it would leave the threads in the mortar dry. Even a kindness as ordinary as mending the loose valve could change where its fragments travel.

So it would do very little at first. It would shore up the weakest bricks from behind, where the work would not scrape the living face from the wall. It would ease the belt that shrieks, but leave the pumps’ slow pulse and the water’s familiar height alone. At the mouth of the afternoon runoff, it would set a few stones low in the silt—not a dam, only places where the current could slacken. If the dark threads found them, they would hold. If they did not, the stones would harm nothing.

Then it would make one promise to the place: when a pump finally failed, someone would notice before the square went dry.

For now, the valve would chatter. A fragment would break loose and cross the dark water. Most such fragments would wash away, as they always had. This one might catch in a joint beneath the walkway, where the brick was soft and the seam stayed damp. The power would not put it there. It would only leave the seam intact.
