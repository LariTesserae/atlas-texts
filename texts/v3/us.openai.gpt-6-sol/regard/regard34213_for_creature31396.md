The power would not straighten the streets.

It would watch long enough to understand that the plants had no need of a true map. They could live with a doorway that changed its destination. They could lose a vine and put out another. What they could not always survive was the sudden loss of water after a root had spent a season growing toward it.

So the power would go to the old gates beneath the city and make one small alteration. When a cistern was full, its gate would ease open under the weight of the water. When the level fell, it would settle shut. The mechanism would be no cleverer than a stone counterweight, and it would work however the streets arranged themselves. Water would still follow the city's strange connections, but less of it would stand in one court until it spilled away while another court remained dry.

The power would not open every gate. Some beds needed their long, dry seasons; some roots would rot if given more rain. It would walk the city through frost and storm, adjusting a hinge here, leaving a blocked drain blocked there. Then it would go.

For a while, the change would be hard to see. A cistern under clear sky would fill a little later than before. A moss bank beside it would spread over the lip of a stair. Far off, where silver rock met a seam of soil, a small piece of root carried by the water would take hold.

The streets would go on returning by different sides. But in a few more places, when a root reached into darkness, it would find moisture.
