The power came when the boats were out, and for a while it did nothing.

It watched a child lift water from a hollow and taste it before filling a jar. It watched a woman clear bird droppings from a roof channel with the edge of a shell. It watched two people carry a boat down the steps, pausing where the slick stone made them uncertain. None of them looked up. If the power had a voice, it could not have been heard over the birds.

By morning, a thin white line had appeared on the stone beside each place where water rose from below. At some seeps the line remained; at others it vanished beneath the first cup of water, leaving the rock plain. The people noticed that the hollows with lasting lines held water they could drink, even after a dry stretch. The others turned bitter when the lagoon rose. They marked the lasting places with shells of their own, and began carrying water from them for the smallest children.

Next, the power roughened three stones on the landing steps. It did not make a staircase out into the lagoon. The crews still had to wade, and crabs still lived in the cracks. But a person should not have to lose a boat, or a leg, on the way to the water.

It considered clearing the roofs of birds. The stones were foul, and their cries wore at everyone beneath them. Yet the birds flew out each day and returned from places the boat crews could not reach. People watched their departures when they chose where to fish. So the power left them their edges. It gave one roof channel a slightly better fall, where rain had been carrying the droppings toward a cistern, and waited to see whether anyone would copy it.

Before the next rain, someone did. Another household changed its channel a day later.

The power stayed a little longer, though there was less for it to do. The town had hands enough to shape stone once it knew where the water went.
