The power would arrive at noon, when almost nothing moved across the courts.

It would see that the town had already learned much of what it needed to know. The people caught water without sealing it away from the creatures that lived in it. They built shade instead of asking the stone to be less hot. When a gutter failed, someone came. There was no need to remake the place.

But one reach of the oldest channel dried each summer before anyone thought to look. Its water slipped through a seam beneath a retaining wall, and the little grazers held fast to the damp stone until it was no longer damp.

The power would not fill the channel from nowhere. It would cool the wall by the width of a hand, just enough that morning moisture gathered there after the rest of the terrace had dried. The next time the channel ran low, the grazers would find that wet seam and survive the afternoon.

A person clearing grit from the gutter would notice. She would put her palm against the wall, then look up at the broad pale roof above it. Before the next summer, she and the other workers would fit a strip of shade along the channel. They would leave gaps for rain and for the long-legged hunters to stand.

The power would let the wall warm again.

On the hottest days, it might still pass through the town without being seen: a little time given to someone crossing an open court, a drop held on a pipe until a small creature reached it. Never enough to make water seem inexhaustible. Just enough, now and then, for its inhabitants to go on caring for one another.
