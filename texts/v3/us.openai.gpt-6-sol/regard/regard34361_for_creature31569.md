The power would first wait through a full cycle of the machinery. It would watch the creature flatten itself before a load rose, open its fringe at the strongest gust, and land with all its feet against the far wall. It would learn which currents carried it and which merely shook the stone.

Then it would attend to the stone.

Near the creature’s resting place, one old joint had begun to give. It was not failing yet. Rain entered it grain by grain, and each passing load eased the grains farther apart. If the joint broke, a section of duct would fall across the walkway. The power would draw the stone close again, slowly enough that no one heard a crack and the creature did not wake. The next rain would still find its grooves. The next load would still send air across the chamber.

It would leave the darkness alone. It would not fill the seams, wash away the insects, or light the landing for the sake of seeing what lived above it.

Weeks later, someone on the walkway would notice that the damp joint looked sounder than the stones around it. They might put a hand against it when the machinery passed, feel the familiar shiver, and go on.

High above, the creature would sleep among the shed fringe and wings. When the air began to press against the walls, it would wake, unfold, and cross.
