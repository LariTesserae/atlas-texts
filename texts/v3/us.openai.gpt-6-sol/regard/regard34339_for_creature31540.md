The power would wait for rain.

It would watch where the water went after the clouds had passed: through a seam in the upper stair, behind a retaining wall, across the crew’s newly lifted paving. It would watch the people who already knew these routes, especially the ones whose warnings were heard only after a street failed.

Then it would give them something difficult to obtain in a crowded valley: time. For one season, the heaviest rain would arrive in intervals the drains could bear. The crews could open the buried channels before they burst. Surveyors could walk beside shopkeepers and children and mark the wet walls on the same maps as the roads. Repairs could be made before the next emergency called everyone away.

The power would not make water disappear. It would make room for it. A disused loading yard, too low to keep dry and too expensive to rebuild, would become a planted basin. Its owners would be paid fairly; no household would be moved to save a more prosperous street. At the canal, a narrow length of hard quay would give way to a bank where willow roots could spread, and the heron’s opening would remain open.

It would do one thing that might be called a miracle. Wherever someone cleared a grate or made space for a young tree, they would be able, for a moment, to see how far that small work reached: which cellar stayed dry, which root held, which person got home by the lower stair. The sight would fade, but not the knowledge of it.

From the ridge, the valley would look much the same. Water would still tick into drains. The trains would still take the curve with an iron knock. Down among the walls, fewer people would have to meet the rain alone.
