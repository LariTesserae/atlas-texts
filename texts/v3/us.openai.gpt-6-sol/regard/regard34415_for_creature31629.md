The power would come in the hour before dawn, when the mud still held the lizard’s track and the birds had not yet changed places. It would find no creature asking to be saved. The crab needed its hole, the fish its deeper pocket, the bird the fish. To favor one here would be to take something from another.

So it would leave the basin’s small bargains alone.

It would follow the water instead, back beyond the reeds and the dark, to the ground where rain began its slow journey into the channels. There it would attend to things the lizard could not taste and the frogs could not flee: a ditch planned too near the wetland, waste that might be tipped where a hard rain would carry it, a boundary drawn by someone who had never stood in the warm mud.

No one would see the power. A surveyor would notice the old flood marks on a tree and move a line. Someone would ask where a drain emptied before approving it. A patch of land that had seemed useless would be recognized as the place that held the rain long enough for it to reach the basin gently. These would be ordinary acts, made by people able to make them.

Then the power would return. It would not smooth the silt ridges or clear a path through the vines. At dawn, a crab would pull a scrap of leaf into its hole. The lizard, under a bank, would wait for the heat. If rain fell upstream, the pools would widen by a few inches, and every creature there would notice in its own way.
