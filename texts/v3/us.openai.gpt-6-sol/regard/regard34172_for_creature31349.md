The power would not begin by stopping the rain.

It would spend a storm listening: to the pumps, to the water beneath the paving, to the arguments at a newly raised threshold. It would follow each diverted surge until it knew whose door received it. Then it would ask the gate crews and the drain scrapers to show it what it had missed.

Its first change would be small enough that people might doubt it had happened. Every warning tone would begin a little earlier. A bridge about to shift would give a clear, gentle sound before it moved. The floor beneath a sleeping elder would no longer be the only way to know a pump had stopped.

Then, with the people who maintained the city, it would make places the water could enter without taking anyone’s bed: open chambers under the busiest districts, strong enough to hold the weight above them, with gates that could be inspected and repaired by ordinary hands. It would not merely push the flood toward a poorer quarter. When there was more water than the new chambers could hold, it would bear some of that weight itself until the channels could release it safely.

There would be other gifts, offered rather than installed: a way to see the load moving through the old foundations; dry public rooms on every level’s nearest high route; a few ledges where salt-tolerant plants could grow without choking a drain. Anyone responsible for one of these things would be able to take it apart and understand it. No district would have to pray for the power’s return to make a repair.

On the first quieter night, someone would wake because a familiar pump had stopped. They would listen, hear the backup take its place, and lie down again. The city would still move around them. For once, they could let it.
