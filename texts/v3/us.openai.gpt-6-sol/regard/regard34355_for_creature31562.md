The power would not straighten the corridors.

It would spend a long time watching first. What looked, from above, like a tower dying in a gap might be a hundred younger stems finding light. What looked like an injury in the plain might be a join that let roots reach a court they could never otherwise enter. Even the roar would take time to understand: not distress, but the sound of so many lives bearing on one another.

Then it would notice the yellow shoots.

Rain fell hard enough to strike the crowns and run away, while the dry earth beneath some stands received almost none. The power would change one rain, and only one. It would let the clouds arrive after sunset and release their water slowly, for hours, in drops small enough to cling to leaves and pass from joint to joint. In the enclosed courts, where moisture already gathered, it would allow the rain to remain ordinary. In the open corridors, it would quiet the wind so that more of it reached the roots.

By morning, the strongest stands would still be strongest. Some yellow shoots would remain yellow. A falling crown would still fall, and its neighbors would bow into its place. The power would not make every plant flourish, because there was no space in which every plant could flourish at once.

It would leave the impossible openings alone, too. Through one of them, a fine root newly wet from the night’s rain would continue into a distant court. A shoot would rise there when it found light. The plain would alter around it, without a seam.

If the power returned, it would look before touching anything again.
