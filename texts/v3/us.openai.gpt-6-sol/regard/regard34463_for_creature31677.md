The power would not try to mend the mountain. The split runs deeper than the roots, and to seal it for the sake of this one bank would put the weight of the slope somewhere else.

It would begin with the water.

For a few nights, the beads on the leaves would remain after the wind had dried the rock. They would gather at the bases of the sedge, slip beneath the moss, and enter the crumbly soil a little at a time. Not enough to make it heavy. Enough for the fine roots and fungal threads to take hold where they still could.

Then, during a lull between gusts, the slab under the outermost edge would give way.

It would not fall all at once. The power would grant it that much: a slow release, without the sudden wrench that could pull the whole living cover after it. A few roots would break. A small cushion of flowers would go down with the stone. There would be no way to spare everything.

By morning the turf would have a ragged edge, set farther back from the hollow. Much of it would still be joined to the older ground. The tiny animals under it would find the same dark passages, altered but open. Through the coming seasons, roots would grow into the earth that remained.

Far below, the turbines would go on turning. The power would leave them alone. It would not make the plants answer the roar, or teach them to bear the wind more bravely. It would give this place a little water and a little time, then let the mountain move.
