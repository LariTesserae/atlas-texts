The power would come down the stairs slowly, so the leaves had time to settle after it passed. It would not ask the garden whether it was one life or many. Nothing it needed to do depended on the answer.

It would walk first to the pump. The wheel was turning; the inspection plate was clear. But one clamp had begun to loosen, and the pipe knocked against it every few turns. The power would steady the clamp and tighten its bolt. Then it would wait through several revolutions, listening, before touching anything else.

At the nearest grate, it would lift out the litter that had gathered above the leaves. Grey daylight would fall a little farther down the pier. It would clean the cloudy glass of the aisle lamps, leaving their light gentle, and mend a cracked edge of the stair so a person coming to inspect the engine would not put a foot through it.

The root around the old handwheel it would leave alone. The iron had become one of the places where the garden lived. Instead, it would mark the handwheel as abandoned in the records kept above the street, so no hurried repair crew would try to wrench it free.

Before going, the power would stand beside the canal. Water would drop from a leaf, cross a second leaf, and fall into the narrow current. The pump would give its next thud. There was no need to make the chamber brighter than it was, or to teach its plants to grow elsewhere. For now, the kindest thing was to keep the pump sound, the way in safe, and the small sources of warmth and light reaching the places they already reached.
