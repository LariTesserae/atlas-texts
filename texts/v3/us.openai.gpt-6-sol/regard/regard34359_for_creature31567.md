The power would not straighten the paths.

It would spend a long time listening first: to rain entering a roof of leaves, to water taking a turn no map could explain, to the different sounds a joint made when a child crossed it and when a full basin rested above it. It would learn which dark corridors the gliders used at dusk. It would notice the closed bridges that were holding themselves together precisely because no one had cut them open.

Then, on a night of heavy rain, it would attend to one old district.

A reservoir there was sound by every test the menders knew. Its supporting stems had thickened for generations. But deep inside one knee, where an earlier platform had been swallowed by a later one, water had found a narrow way into dry wood. Each storm widened it. The reservoir would not fail tonight, or perhaps this year. When it did, it would take the chambers below with it.

The power would not make the wood unbreakable. It would give the trapped water an easier way out.

By morning a new seep would have opened in a wall beside an unused basin. It would run clear at first, then carry the fine dark silt the gardeners liked. One of them would set a cup beneath it. A mender, hearing an unfamiliar knocking behind the wall, would follow the sound upward and find the knee. There would be time to empty the reservoir, train new stems around it, and let the old ones dry.

Far out on the edge, where people bent growing tips toward the hollow, the power would do less. It would bring no sudden bridge into being. It would turn a little of the prevailing wind, often enough that leaves on the far slope brushed their undersides toward the reaching stems. In clear weather the workers would be able to see that the other green line was growing toward them too.

For a while, they might bring more water to the tips. They might decide the gap was still too wide. The power would leave that decision with them.

By dusk the new seep would have drawn a long-beaked animal to the wall. It would tap among the wet joints while the mender worked on the other side, and neither would need to know who had first heard the water.
