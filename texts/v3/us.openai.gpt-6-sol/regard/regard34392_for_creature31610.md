The power would arrive without a lamp. For a while, it would be mistaken for a worker standing still.

It would listen first: to the mallets, to the calls passed across the yards, to the small changes in sound that tell a builder when a brace is taking too much weight. It would not mistake the steady work for contentment, or the unfinished walls for a plea to have them finished.

Before morning, it would do three things.

It would hold the bowing brace in the western passage just long enough for the crew beneath it to come out. Not straighten it, not make the span sound: a false assurance could kill someone on the next shift. Once the passage was empty, the brace would break with a noise no one could mistake.

It would give the water a way through. The shallow pockets would drain into one channel, and that channel would end where jars could be filled without kneeling in mortar. The vaults would still be cool, and rain would still have to be dealt with. But no one would have to choose between drinking the water and leaving it to undermine a wall.

Finally, it would set a lamp at the open stair near the middle of the basin. Its light would reach farther than a lamp ought to, but only over the chalked rooms. From that stair, anyone who cared to look could see how the next walls would narrow the routes between yards, and how little sky would remain above the floor below. The chalk would stay chalk until the builders chose what to do with it.

By morning, the crews would have questions for one another. Someone would argue for moving a wall. Someone would say the drain ought to run farther. Someone coming off a night shift would be told, for once, that the measuring could wait until they had slept.

Under the loose tile, the pale animal would drink and hear the builders talking overhead.
