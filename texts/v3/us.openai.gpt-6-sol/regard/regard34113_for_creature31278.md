The power would not begin with rain.

It would spend a day in the courtyard, though no one would see it there. It would follow the roots down past the dry brick and learn where they found water. It would wait through the noon wind and watch the seeds strike the wall, and it would return at evening to see which cracks held them.

Then it would make one small change beneath the street. The deep, damp seam that fed the thicket would widen by a finger’s breadth toward the paving stones—not enough to drain it, only enough for moisture to reach the first sheltered cracks. A seed there might put down a root before the heat took it. The next season, another might do the same a little farther along.

The power would leave the roofs as they were. Their narrow seam gave the young plants a little light without exposing them to a whole day’s sun. It would leave the old handprints in the plaster, too, and the rooms to their silence.

For a long while, the street would still look bare. Then, near the courtyard, one stem would appear between two stones. Its leaves would close against the hottest hours. In time it would make enough shade for a second stem.

The power would not ask the thicket to reclaim the town. It would give its seeds a fair chance to leave the courtyard, and let the living thing decide how far it could go.
