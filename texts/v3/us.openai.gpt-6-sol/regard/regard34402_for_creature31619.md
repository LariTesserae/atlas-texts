The power came to the plateau intending to bring rain.

It waited before doing so. It watched a small plated creature draw dust from the edge of a shadow, and a larger one lower its body into a fissure that had never held water. It watched the workers lift a doorway above a bank of salt. By dusk it understood that rain would be a gift to almost nothing here.

So it listened instead.

For many days it followed the hum through the stone. Under one stretch of rail, where the trains climbed toward the furnaces, the hum changed. The piers were sound, but the ground beneath them had begun to part. The opening was still too fine for anyone to see. In time it would split a dry seam on which the broad plated animals depended, and perhaps take a train with it.

The power could have closed the stone. It did not know what else would close with it.

On the next crossing, a track inspector stopped where she had never stopped before. She had felt a faint second beat through the sole of her boot. When she knelt and put her ear against the basalt, she heard it again.

She marked the place with white salt and called others over. They listened, argued, brought instruments, and found the parting ground. The trains were slowed while a new line was planned. It took years to build, and the old piers were left standing. The pale creatures still came out beneath them at dusk.

Afterward, the power stayed a while. It did not make the workers kinder; they already left a way clear beside the slag. It did not make the animals safe from every wheel. It listened where the stone carried the city’s work, and where the same stone carried the quiet passage of lives the city could barely see. Most days, it found nothing it ought to change.
