The power would spend a long time listening before it did anything.

At first it might mistake the mountain’s noise for warning. Every slab knocked; every shelter creaked; every leaf scraped against its neighbor. But the people slept through most of these sounds, and the small animals did too. They knew which noises belonged to a ridge going on as it always had.

Eventually the power would hear one sound the others could not: the faint, gathering strain of a root-bound ledge that would give way in three days. Beneath it lay a water cup. A shelter stood above it, and the small animals waited in its leaves when the grazers came to drink.

The power could hold the ledge together. It could fasten every stone in the range. But roots had made a life of catching what the mountain loosened. New cracks gave them places to enter; fallen rock gave them shelter from the wind. To stop the mountain moving would change much more than this one fall.

So the power would touch only the sound.

For three days the ledge would make a low, irregular note, audible through the leaves even when the wind rose. The animals would leave the cup first. The birds nesting behind it would grow restless and carry their young to another ledge. A child fetching water would hear the note through a hand laid on the stone and call the others over. They would listen, argue about whether they had heard its like before, and move the drying racks and what they could carry from the shelter.

On the fourth morning the ledge would fall. Its noise would fill the gap. When the dust and leaves cleared, the water cup would be gone.

The people would find another cup, smaller and farther from the shelter. They would make a path to it, careful not to cut away all the leaves that shaded its water. In the following seasons, children would be taught the new note alongside the hollow knock of the safe slab and the pitch of leaves at the narrow pass.

The power would not make every danger announce itself. It had helped this ledge speak before it fell; the people and animals had done the listening and the leaving. Then it would turn its attention back to the range, where seeds were crossing the gaps in wet feathers and roots were taking hold in the fresh stone.
