The power would come to the entrance and wait long enough to hear the cave.

It would not fill the chambers with light. The bats have no need to be seen, and the pale insects in the pools have made a life from the little that reaches them. Nor would it smooth the fallen blocks or turn the dripping seam into a stream. The cave’s abundance depends on those small differences: wet stone beside dry dust, an opening beside darkness, a place a cricket can enter that a larger creature cannot.

It might do one thing. Where the new break offers an easy way down, it would let roots and loose stones make the descent look less inviting to a hurried person. Anyone who came carefully could still pass. They would have to stop, put out their flame, and listen before going farther.

The power would leave the openings free for the bats. It would leave the drip to find its pools, and the roots to grow where they could. Then it would go, having made the cave a little harder to enter carelessly and no harder to live in.
