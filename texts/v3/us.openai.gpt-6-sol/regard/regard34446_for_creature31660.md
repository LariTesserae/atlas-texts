The power would leave the painted line where it was. It was there for a reason, and the water below it was not a promise.

It would come down into the recess at evening, when the wall was still giving off the day’s heat. It would find the colony by following the seam, not the rusted bolts: the faint orange edge where water met air and something living took hold.

For a while, it would do nothing. The seep brought iron. The shallow sheet brought air. The threads needed both, and the place, harsh as it was, had given them both. To dry the floor for their sake would end them. To make the water deeper would carry them away.

So the power would make one small change. The next drop gathering on the pipe would run along its underside and fall into the bare water beyond the orange rim. The drops after it would follow the same path. They would still strike the floor; they would still make their dark spots. But the colony’s edge would no longer be blurred and remade each time the pipe cooled.

At the open side, the power would leave the reeds alone. Their roots had found their own narrow place. In time they might cast a little shade across the concrete, or catch silt when the water rose. They might not. The power would not make them grow faster to prove it had helped.

Before it left, it would put a thought into the mind of the person who next checked the pumping station: *Look behind it, too.* The person would see the water coming through the seam and measure it against the painted line. They would see the orange rim as well. If the seep threatened the station, they would have time to act; if it did not, they could let the small life at its edge remain.

By morning, the colony would be much as it had been. Water would pass through its threads. A few particles would stay. No one standing over it would be able to tell that it had grown.

The power would know, and go.
