The power would not begin with rain.

It would spend a long time in the chamber, long enough to learn that the leaves on the upper shelf and the curled shoot by the basin are not neighbors but parts of one life. It would feel where the stone cools after dark, where the pipes give off too much heat, and where a little water vanishes before any root can reach it.

Then it would attend to the pipe above the basin.

There is a narrow seam in it, so old and fine that its drops have always been taken for condensation. The power would ease that seam open no wider than a root hair. Nothing would burst. No pool would rise around the brick pier. But a drop would fall every few minutes, day and night, into the cracked silt.

At first the damp place would simply stop disappearing. Then it would widen by the measure of a thumb. The fine roots already lying there would take up what they could, and the rest would sink into the soil. Higher on the shelves, leaves would lift slowly enough that no visitor could watch it happen. The brown tips would stay brown; the broken stems would keep their scars. New leaves would come in whole.

The power would leave the other pipes alone. Their heat is part of the chamber now, and it does not know what depends on the engine beyond the wall. It would not fill the room with insects or make a stair where the old stair has worn away. Those changes could wait for someone who lives in time enough to see their consequences.

Before leaving, it would rest a hand against the iron walkway. For one breath the roar would seem to fall quiet—not because the engine had stopped, but because the power was listening beneath it. There, under the stone and soil, water would be entering the roots. Nothing in the chamber would move quickly enough to thank it. The drop would fall again.
