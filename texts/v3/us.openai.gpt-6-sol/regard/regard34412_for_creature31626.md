The power watches for a long time before it touches anything.

It could fill the basins with water. It could warm the stone and bring green life over the ridges. But the grazers are fitted to this place, and the place, however sparingly, feeds them. A sudden abundance would draw them together. They would break the seeps under their feet, strip the slow-growing lichen, and have farther to fall when the gift ended.

So the power attends to a smaller trouble. Rain sometimes falls in a distant basin, and the animals learn of it only after the water has gone.

Along one old channel, where the rock already carries the tremor of passing feet, it opens a seam no wider than a root. The seam does not carry water. It makes the stone better at carrying the particular shiver of rain striking a hollow that has begun to fill. It leaves the rest of the plateau as it was.

The first storm after this, an old grazer lifts its head. Nothing moves in the gray basin around it. It lowers a pad, waits, and turns. Hours later the animals behind it turn too. They do not hurry; they arrive while there is still water enough to soften the lichen at the edge. A youngster feeds there, then stands close to its adult with a dark line of moisture beneath its jaw.

Some storms still miss the hollows. Some journeys still end at dry stone. The power does not send rain or tell the herds where to go. It has made one true thing easier for them to know in time to use it.

When the seam wears down, as all stone does, it will last for ages. The power leaves it there and goes on watching.
