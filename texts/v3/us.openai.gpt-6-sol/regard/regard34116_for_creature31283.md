The power would come first to listen.

It would find that the roar was not one sound. Beneath it ran the steady labor that kept the garden warm; above it, a loose piece of machinery struck its housing again and again. The power would fasten that piece. The furnace would go on working, but now a bird landing on an arch might hear another bird answer.

It would not bring soil to cover the bare middle. The open iron let light reach the rim and gave anyone stepping out of the lift a clear way through. Instead, the power would make a narrow channel beneath the path, where it could catch some of the water that ran from the leaves and release it slowly into the gutters. The vines would still have dry days. They would no longer have to endure so many in a row.

Where roots had entered the thinned bowl, the power would fit new iron underneath, shaped around them. It would leave the rust where beetles worked and the old maker’s marks under their moss. Last, it would climb down the ladder and make every rung sound enough to bear a person’s weight.

For a while, the birds would startle at the changed noise. Then they would settle. Clouds would pass through as before, and water would fall from the leaves too softly to hear from the lift. The garden would remain small, high, and dependent on the furnace. But there would be more time before the iron failed, and a safe way up for someone who wished to care for it.
