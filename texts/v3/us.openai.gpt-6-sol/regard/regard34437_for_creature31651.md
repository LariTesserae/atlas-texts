The power did not quiet the city.

For a long time, it listened. It learned the difference between a wheel running properly and one beginning to fail. It watched a child turn at the tremor of an approaching tram, and an old press operator wait until she reached her own door before unclenching her hands. It saw that the people had made a life with the water and the noise, not merely in spite of them.

When it finally came down into the streets, it came as a stranger who had to lean close to be heard. It asked the drain crews what they would change if they could. They spoke of narrow passages and sore knees. The workers at the eastern presses wanted to go home without the day’s noise following them into bed. A mason said, “Don’t stop the water. Just give us more time to reach it.”

So the power made no decree. It left a piece of stone on the dry shelf inside the drain office, with a note asking them to try it beneath a wheel housing.

The stone let water pass. It let the uneven knock of a failing bearing pass, too. But it held back some of the great, unvarying force that shook the floor all day. The crews tested it for weeks. They took it out and put it back. Only after they were satisfied did the stranger show them where, deep in the shelf, they could cut more of it.

They used it first where people worked longest, then beneath a few bedrooms and quiet rooms. They chose the sites themselves. Some streets were left as they were; their residents wanted to hear the wheels plainly. No one stopped opening the drains or watching for warm water on a wall.

Years later, a visitor might still call the city loud. Its people would agree. But a child coming home from the works could sit beside a parent without having to wait for the trembling in the floor to fade before hearing a song.

The power stayed long enough to see that happen. Then it went away, leaving the masons to follow the water.
