The power would arrive during a rain so ordinary that no one would remember its date.

It would find the city already doing much of what wisdom might ask of it: leaving room for water, repairing what can be repaired, trusting knowledge passed from one person to another. It would not dry the stone or make the weather obedient. A dry city would be a different city, and perhaps a less careful one.

Instead, it would go beneath the oldest square, where several buried channels meet. There, it would listen. One course of water has begun, over many years, to carry grit into a narrow place no hook can reach. The blockage is still slight. No one has heard it yet, though an old woman has twice stopped on the square and frowned at the stones.

The power would move a single pebble.

At the next hard rain, water would make a new sound beneath the paving: a brief hollow knocking, distinct from the usual rush. The woman would hear it. She would call for her neighbors, including one with whom she had not spoken since an argument over a garden wall. Together they would lift the stones while the square was still safe to cross.

In the cistern below, the small creature that sometimes troubles the surface would feel the changed current. The power would do nothing to it except leave a pocket of still water among the repairs, where it could remain unseen if it wished.

By evening the channels would be clear. Someone would put the kettle on for those who had worked in the rain. The two neighbors might speak, or might not. Above them, showers would continue across the districts, darkening one set of roofs while another stood in sun.

The power would stay long enough to learn whether the new sound could be heard again. Then it would leave the city to its people, its water, and the quiet lives within it.
