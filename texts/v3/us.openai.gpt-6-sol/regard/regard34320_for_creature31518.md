The power would come close enough to see one of the animals move, and then it would wait.

It would learn how long a drop takes to leave the ledge, how the stone receives water from the air, how a creature no larger than a bead finds its way from one wet seam to another. It would see no wound to close. The hollow is not waiting to be completed.

So it would not deepen the pools or make the ferns spread across the bare floor. It would not make the animals swift, or clever, or safe from every dry day. Their lives are fitted to a place that changes slowly, and the power would be careful not to mistake that slowness for a plea.

Before leaving, it would do one thing beyond the hollow. On the surrounding height, where rain first enters the stone, it would turn aside a future disturbance: one careless cut through the ground that would have let the water drain away. No one in the hollow would feel the moment pass. Drops would still fall at their own intervals. Pools would rise and recede. Some young would live; some would not.

The power would leave the place its ordinary uncertainty, and the stone its way of holding rain.
