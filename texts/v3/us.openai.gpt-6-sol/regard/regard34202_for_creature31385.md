The power came first to a drain mouth, because it had mistaken the clicking inside for rain.

A pale crab was pulling a scrap of dark culture from a knot of thread. Each time it freed one leg, another caught. Above it, water gathered against the grate. A crew would arrive in the morning. They would clear the blockage, as they always did, and the crab would vanish sideways into the wall if it could.

The power could have lifted every grate in the city. It could have made the water pure, the basins inexhaustible, the streets dry. It saw how much would be lost in each kindness. The fish waited for what escaped the basins. The birds waited for the fish and larvae. The workers knew their routes by the water that crossed them.

So it freed the crab. Then it stayed.

Far below the city, the pumps drew from a seam of water that had narrowed by the width of a hand in the last century. No one had measured that same seam twice; the shafts were old, and each district kept its own records. The power did not fill it. It gave one pump a slight, persistent knock whenever it drew too hard—nothing that would damage it, only a sound unlike the other sounds in the works.

A mechanic heard it three nights later. She listened, called a second mechanic, and shut the pump down long enough to lower a gauge. By winter, the districts were comparing their measurements. They changed the order in which the basins were filled. They repaired a leaking aqueduct they had long meant to repair. None of it was quick, and some of it was argued over in rooms where damp coats hung from the backs of chairs.

At the drain mouth, the crab found another scrap.

The power left the grate as it was. In the morning, a worker came with a hook, heard the shells clicking, and put her foot on the dry step.
