The power comes in the rain and finds that most of what it might have taught has already been learned.

It could lift the town above the wet ground. It could straighten the stairs, drive roots from beneath the houses, make the spring run clear through pipes that never clog. From the ridge, these would look like gifts. From the square, they would leave the frogs without their wall, the trees without room to grow, and the people with fewer reasons to notice when water changed its course.

So it watches. A woman clears leaves from the drinking trough. Farther down the slope, where no one can see it, water has begun to gather beneath an old stair. The stones still sit firmly; the hollow below them is only the width of a hand. In another hard rain, the footing will give way.

The power does not seal the hollow. It turns one buried pebble, and the water begins to make a faint sound as it enters—a small, steady tapping against stone.

That afternoon, a child sent to fetch kindling hears it. She puts down the bundle and listens, then calls for someone with a pry bar. By dusk three neighbors have lifted the stair. They find the hollow, lay a new bed of gravel, and leave a narrow way for the water to pass. A frog retreats into the crack beside them and comes back after dark.

No one knows why the pebble moved. The stair holds through the next storm. Water runs beneath it, audible if you stop there and listen.
