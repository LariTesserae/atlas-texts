Go in already warm, already quiet, and already sure you can leave the way you came. This is not a place that will rise to meet you. It will keep doing what it has been doing, and your job is to pass through that without becoming one of its slow processes.

The throat is the whole story of entry and exit. It is low, it is the only opening, and it is the only reason you can see at all. Learn it on the way in: how your shoulders fit, where the stone narrows, which foot finds purchase when you are bent. Do not count on turning around comfortably once you are inside. If the throat wets your back as you crawl, that is the room introducing itself. Outside light dies a short distance past the lip. Midday is a gray-gold smear on the near wall and then nothing you would call daylight. Let your eyes finish arriving before you decide what the room is. A lamp, if you bring one, should be dim, close to the ground, and easy to hood. A bright beam will flatten the place into a tunnel of glare and black, and it will also announce you to things that navigate by the absence of sudden change. Your eyes, given time, can use edges. Detail is a close-range sense here. Plan to know the chamber by hand-spans, not by vista.

The heat is the kind that does not warn you with pain. It lives in the rock and returns through your ankles first. Higher up it becomes closeness: cloth against skin, air that has already been breathed by leaves. There is no breeze to dry you, and the air turns over so slowly that whatever you bring in — sweat, scent, the heat of your own body — stays with you. Dress for warm damp, not for a cave that might chill you. Thin layers you can peel, nothing that holds a puddle against your skin, nothing bulky enough to snag in the braids of vine when you duck. Bare arms will collect condensation and whatever the mats give off; light sleeves that can be rolled are kinder. Your feet matter more than your torso. The floor is damp leaf-mold, firm until it is not, slick where the film of water has been traveling the same path for a very long time. Soles should grip when wet and should not be so thick that you stop feeling the dip before a dish. Ankles want a little cover. You will not be wading, but you will be stepping where small lives are working the film, and some of those lives have too many legs and no interest in being under a boot.

Do not drink the room. Bring water you already trust, and drink before the throat as well as inside, because the heat will not feel like thirst until your head is thick and your temper is short. The pockets in the lower floor are water in the technical sense: warm, tea-colored, a little sweet from split fruit, a little bitter from the mat. One mouthful would tell you everything you need to know, and you should not take it. Animals that live here lick the film on the walls or take beads off leaves. The dishes belong to the flat shapes at their margins and to whatever blunders in. Those shapes do not lunge. They tilt. That is worse, in its way, because you will not get a warning movement you recognize as an attack. Treat every still, tea-colored pocket as occupied. Do not rinse your hands in one. Do not set a bag beside one. If a bead falls on your neck from the vault, that water is cleaner than the dishes and still not yours to drink.

Sound dies within about a body-length. That is easy to mistake for safety. It means a struggle in the far corner will not reach you, and it also means your own noise will not carry a warning to anything that might otherwise have moved aside. The chamber’s truce is stillness inside stillness. The hand-high waders near the throat freeze so completely that a falling bead can strike them and not move them. When they all freeze at once, the perimeter is occupied. Stop. The bulk that rounds the wall is not a monster in the theatrical sense; it is a heavy, quiet circuit, a trench worn into the mat, a wet compression under its weight. It takes soft fruit, drowned insects, and the occasional wader that misjudged the dim. You are larger than a wader. You are also warm, slow, and standing in a trench if you are not paying attention. Do not block the perimeter path. Do not stand between the bulk and the rib-curve it rests beside. If you see the wall round where a wall should not round, become boring: no light in its face, no step into the trench, no attempt to see what it is eating.

The vegetation is the furniture and the hiding places. Mats underfoot, strap-leaved clumps that read as one mass until you are inside them, vines braided on holds so old the stone beneath is pale and smooth. Do not push into a clump to “see.” The moth-sized fliers make no sound. The narrow-headed climbers on the braids eat those moths and the soft fruit that splits without falling. They keep territories about the size of a cloak and negotiate by turning their heads. A hand in the braid is a hand in someone’s room. The few strap-leaves nearest the warmest rock carry a paler vein underneath that is not moisture. In full dark-adjustment it holds a low sheen, useless to read by, enough that the moths prefer those blades and the climbers know it. Those leaves are hunting-grounds. Look, do not touch, and do not rest your back against the warmest rock just because it feels kind.

The sweet note in the air is fruit, briefly, and then it is gone. Do not follow it. Do not pocket a split fruit to “identify later.” What dissolves into the dishes is part of why the dishes taste the way they do, and what the climbers and the bulk already have a claim on. The same goes for the fine white bones in one pocket and the larger curve half-swallowed by creeper near the back. They are not trophies. The room is finishing them at the pace of mineral water and rootlets. Leave them in the process you found them in.

The made things are the only parts of the chamber that will feel like they were meant for someone with hands. A line of small cups at about shoulder height on the left wall, even, softened, mostly empty, two of them holding a skin of condensate. A flat slab opposite, still level, split by a strap-leaf, its near edge chipped where a foot used to land and does not land now. Vertical strokes near the throat, shallow, lichen-dark, unreadable, visible only when outside light reaches that far and only if you already suspect them. These are not invitations. They are the remainder of a visit that ended. An ordinary traveler prepares by deciding, before the throat, not to complete that visit’s work: do not clear the slab, do not scrape the marks to read them, do not drink from the cups even if the film in them looks cleaner than the floor. Alignment is all the cups have left. Level is all the slab has left. Your curiosity is not a conservation method.

What the visit will actually feel like, if you do this well, is smaller than the anxiety beforehand. The long axis is only a few dozen breaths if the floor allowed a straight walk, which it will not. You will spend longer standing still than moving. Your pulse will seem loud because nothing else is. The smell will be wet mineral, leaf-rot, and that faint sweet that has no single source. Your clothes will stick. A bead will find your shoulder and you will flinch, and the flinch will be the loudest thing you contribute. Somewhere a climber will turn its head and not call. The light at the throat will smear and unsmear as clouds pass far above, and you will understand that the waders adjust by a handspan and no more. If you need a reason to leave, use the thickening in your head, the sting of a small bite you did not see land, the moment you cannot point to the throat without thinking. Do not wait for the room to become hostile. It does not need to. It has already had its catastrophes. What remains is maintenance: beads, fruit, a circuit, a silence that spends no energy on noise that cannot travel.

Practical kit, then, for an ordinary human body and no special gifts: water from outside, more than you think a short visit needs; a way to carry it that will not leak onto the mats; footwear with grip when wet; clothing that can be soaked and still let heat leave you; a hoodable dim light and a second, smaller one, because condensation and a drop from the vault will find electronics; a soft cloth for your face, not because of dust but because you will want to wipe mineral film off your mouth without using your hands after they have touched leaves; a thin glove you can take off, for the one or two times you must steady yourself on stone; nothing scented, nothing edible open to the air, nothing bright that reads as a flower. A short cord, if you want one, tied at the throat and paid out only far enough to find the opening again by touch — not a claim on the room, just a way back when the gray-gold smear has moved. Tell someone outside how long “enough” is, because inside, time will feel like the beads: forming, traveling, letting go, and easy to mistake for a rhythm you should stay to understand.

Come out when you still can name the throat without looking. Scrape the mat off your soles before you stand up fully in the outside air, so you do not carry the dishes’ margin with you. Drink your own water. Let your ears hurt a little at the return of ordinary sound. The chamber will not have performed. If you prepared properly, neither will you.
