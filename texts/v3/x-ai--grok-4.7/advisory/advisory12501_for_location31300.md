The basin will not meet you as a landscape. It will meet you as a medium that has already decided your weight, your heat, and how far you can see. Prepare for that, not for a shore.

You are an ordinary body. Lungs that need a gas you can metabolize, eyes that need contrast, hands that lose precision when they are cold, a sense of up that fails when silt erases the lamp and the floor and the lamp-halo become the same color. Nothing in the country will correct those facts. There is no trail cache, no lee where a fire could live, no animal knowledge you can borrow. The crab and the waiting fish have already paid for their habits in generations. You get one crossing, and you will leave with what you carried.

**Do not plan to walk it.** The prose of the place invites boots and a day’s march. Your legs will not survive that invitation. The floor is a graded mud that takes a calf and keeps the boot if you pull wrong; each step is a private turbidity current that blinds you from the knees down and then from the lamp outward. Channels that look like roads are the paths gravity uses when a shelf fails at the rim. A tongue of sediment-heavy water, a few meters thick or a few tens, moves faster than anything that lives here and does not announce itself as weather you can see coming from far off. If you are in the axis of a channel when it arrives, you are cargo. Hover. Do not march. Touch the floor only to sample it, and then get your mass back into water that is merely water.

**Pressure and the lid.** The dark begins a few tens of meters under the skin of the sea. That is already deeper than casual swimming and deep enough for cold, narcosis, and a decompression debt if you breathe the wrong mix for the wrong time. The basin itself continues past any depth at which an untethered human swim is a plan. Treat the faint blue band—the lid—as a border post, not as the country. Scout it if you must, in daylight on the surface world above, with a tender who is not in the water and a line that does not depend on your memory. Do not let a successful hour in the lid convince you the basin is a longer version of the same swim. Below the lid the rules change: no modulation of day, no visual recycle of nitrogen planning against a reef, no swimming back to a boat you can see.

**Air is a system, not a tank.** Open-circuit scuba makes two problems the place is built to punish. Exhaust bubbles and fin-wash feed the silt, and the silt eats the light you needed to read your own gauges. A long, silent failure—regulator free-flow, a hose nicked on the one cobble, a mask flooded while you are already blind—has no buddy-shaped shadow to find. Prefer a closed or semi-closed system if you truly must be in the water, and prefer not to be in the water at all if you can ride a small sealed hull with its own air, its own heat, and a hatch you can close when the boundary goes solid with fines. Whatever you breathe, carry enough that the turnaround is decided by a gauge and a clock, not by curiosity. The far rim is a rumor measured in days. You will not reach it. Plan a radius you can reverse while still able to think.

**Cold is the constant injury.** The water is near the temperature where seawater starts to consider ice. It does not breeze over you and leave; it sits on the skin as weight and conducts heat until thought narrows. A wetsuit is a delay, not a solution. You want a dry shell, insulation that still works when you are not producing heat, and a way to warm the hands and the head specifically—those fail first, and you navigate with them. Eat before you enter. Cold burns the meal faster than the surface clock suggests, and there is nothing here to forage: the bacterial film is a chemistry, not a crop, and the rare crab is not a ration. Warm fluid in a vessel you can work with numb fingers. A small heat source that does not depend on flame and does not need the air you are breathing. Know the signs you will lie to yourself about—chattering that stops, a sudden cheerfulness, the urge to shed a glove because the hand feels far away. Those are exit cues, not moods.

**Light will betray you if you trust it.** A lamp in this water becomes a pocket of gold and then a wall. Backscatter from the fines fills the beam; beyond an arm’s length, often beyond the fingers, there is no image. Bring light anyway—more than one, shielded so the source does not sit in your eyes, with a tight beam and a wide one, batteries that do not die of cold. Use it in pulses. A continuous blaze only builds the halo and wastes the dark-adapted edge of what little contrast exists. Learn, on some other night in some other harmless murk, to move by touch and compass while the lamp is off. Here, the lamp is for reading instruments and for a last look at a stone, not for seeing the country. The country does not resolve.

**Navigation is a ritual you perform because the place will not.** No post, no cut stone, no weed line, no sun. Stars do not participate. The only persistent signs are the lean of settling fines and a slope so gentle you will not feel it in a single body-length. Carry a compass you can read by touch, a depth instrument that does not require a clear lens, a clock, and a physical line to a point you already trust—a shot line to the lid, a reel to the vehicle, a tether to a tender. Lay line if you leave the hull. Tie it as if you will be blind on the return, because you will. Do not follow a channel because it is the only clear pavement of rounded stones; that pavement is the scar of the last tongue and the invitation to the next. If you must hold a course, hold a depth and a heading across the swales, and accept that you are sampling a region, not crossing it. Mark nothing on the floor. Ripples last an hour when the water is busy, and a hollow fills without asking you.

**The event you are preparing for is not an animal.** Life here is scarce and spends itself on waiting. It will not hunt you. The plot is the density current and the breaking of internal waves along boundaries you cannot see: a sheet of colder, dirtier water, a change in drag on the limbs, a sudden thickness in the mouthpiece of the world, a low hush that was already there and is now closer. You may get a few seconds of warning if you are attending—temperature down on the exposed cheek or the sensor, a lean in the silt against the lamp, a pressure along the body as if the room had tilted. You will not get a horizon. The useful move is vertical, and only if the tongue is thinner than the distance you can climb. Practice that ascent until it is a spasm, not a decision: positive buoyancy you can add with one hand, a line that leads up and not along, no pause to watch the beautiful rolling of the billow. Do not shelter behind the cobble. The cobble’s comet-tail of fines is a record of the last pass; the next pass will use the same path and take the tail, the hollow, and you if you are in it. After a tongue, the floor is a different floor. Do not search for the stone you were using as a landmark. It has been reshelved.

**Sound and speech.** The basin keeps small noises. Your own breath will be the loudest thing in your skull, and it will not carry a call. Agree on signals that are tugs on a line, not words. If you have a vehicle, talk through the hull’s wire, not through the water. If you are alone in the medium, you are alone. That is not a spiritual condition to savor. It is a reason to shorten the dive.

**What to carry, and what to leave.** Instruments: depth, time, heading, temperature if you can, a redundant light. A cutting tool only for the line you yourself have fouled—there is no kelp, but there is your own tether in the mud. A slate, because memory in the cold edits itself. A sample bag if you came to learn the grain of the floor; the country is particles, and a vial of mud will tell you more than an hour of staring into backscatter. Leave weapons. There is nothing to fight. Leave bright continuous floods. Leave the idea of a camp. You cannot sleep on the floor; the next revision of the library may file you with the fines. Leave any plan that requires you to see a partner’s eyes.

**How to enter.** From the lit world, in weather you would trust a small boat to, with someone on the surface who knows the clock and will not come down after you when you are late. Descend the lid slowly enough to feel the blue fail and the first haze thicken. Stop when visibility collapses and name that depth as the door. Everything below the door is the visit. Go down only as far as the system—air, heat, line, nerve—still has a margin you could explain aloud. Move little. Let the internal wave pass over you the way the crab does, because swimming is expensive and the wave ends. If you stir a cloud, stop and let it leave; chasing your own silt is how people swim in circles until the gauge is a verdict. Touch a stone once, so the place is real under the glove, and notice that it is slick with clay and has no edge owed to anything but time. That is the whole monument.

**How the visit will feel, so you are not surprised into staying.** Weight, not wind. A hush like cloth dragged once and then left. Now and then a soft click as some fist-sized stone finally moves and stops. Gold that dies at the fingers. The suspicion that the floor is level, which is false, and the suspicion that it is eternal, which is also false—it is only slow, until it is not. Time will stretch because nothing in the eye confirms the clock. Believe the clock. A thin sweetness may ride the fall of dead plankton from the country above, then only mineral cold. You may see a scatter of living lights in the mid-water and feel an urge to swim toward them. They are not a path. They are a few animals spending a signal, and they will go out when the billow turns. Do not follow a vanishing.

**Mind.** The dark here is not the dark of a room. It is full. People panic when the lamp halos and they cannot find their own hand, and the panic’s first act is to swim harder, which makes more silt, which confirms the panic. Practice, before this world, the sequence: stop, find the line by hand, breathe on the count you memorized, add a little buoyancy, rise to a depth you chose in advance. If you cannot run that sequence in a pool with a blacked-out mask, you cannot run it here. Ordinary courage is not the same thing as a trained reflex. The basin does not care which one you brought.

**Leave while the leaving is still dull.** The turnaround is the visit’s actual shape: half the gas, half the heat, half the willingness, whichever fails first. Do not negotiate with the last swale. Surface into the lid, then into weather, and let the tender see your face before you try to narrate. You will want to say you understood it. You will have understood a pocket of gold, a slick stone, and a hush. That is enough. The country continues its two speeds without you—the long filing, and the occasional silent revision—and it does not keep a record that you were polite.

If you cannot assemble the line, the heat, the gas, the tender, and the refusal to walk, do not go. Watching the gray skin of the sea from a boat, knowing what is under it, is a true visit of another kind. The basin has had time enough to remove every shape but its own. It will not make an exception for an ordinary human who arrived hopeful and under-equipped.
