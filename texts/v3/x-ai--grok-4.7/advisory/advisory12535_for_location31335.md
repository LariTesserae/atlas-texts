Pack for a place that is wet without being refreshing, bright without ever becoming clear, and inhabited without ever feeling occupied. The basin does not meet you as wilderness or as a city. It meets you as a working surface: heat underfoot, haze overhead, water in every state except the one you can drink, and a thin, constant occupation of flies, birds, and brine life that will not make room for you and will not particularly mind you either.

Go in assuming you will be slightly wrong about distance, time of day, and how tired you are. Noon is only a brighter dusk. The horizon is where counting stops, not where the land ends. A truck can disappear into shimmer between two engine houses and still be close. Your eyes will keep offering you weather and denying you scale. Plan the day as a sequence of known structures, not as a walk toward a view.

**Body, not bravery.** The heat arrives from the ground as much as from the sky, and the air is already full of itself: steam, sulfate, warm rain that changes the smell for a minute and then gives the metal back. You will not cool by sweating into a breeze. You will wet through, stay wet, and keep producing heat. An ordinary traveler should be fit enough to walk soft crust and concrete for hours in that damp, and honest enough to turn back when the headache starts. Sulfate haze is not atmosphere to tough out. It is a low lid. If your chest tightens, your eyes burn, or the taste of metal stops being a taste and becomes a film, you are done for the day. There is no heroic second wind here; there is only a control room with a wet towel on the sill, and you should know where the nearest one is before you need it.

Bring a respirator you have already worn long enough to hate. Not a decorative cloth. Something that seals, with cartridges meant for acid gases and fine mist, and spares sealed against the damp, because anything left open overnight will be compromised by morning. Fit it before you arrive. Practice drinking through whatever system you trust, because you cannot keep taking it off to sip. Goggles that seal over the respirator, not sunglasses. The light is not glare so much as a tin-colored pressure; what hurts is the chemistry in the air and the constant fine wet, not the sun. A broad hat still helps, mostly to keep condensate and fly-fall off your face when you stop.

Clothing should be light, long, and boring. Pale colors if you have them, not for style, but so you can see the stains. Sleeves and trouser cuffs you can close. A shell that sheds warm rain and does not pretend to breathe miracles. Under it, something that can be soaked and wrung and still worn. No cotton that will hold brine against the skin for six hours and call it comfort. No gear with mesh pockets you cannot empty; flies will find them, and so will the fine grit of salt and sinter. Expect every fabric to smell of warm metal and weak ocean by the second day. You will not wash that out on site. Laundry does not dry. Plan to rotate and seal used clothes in bags as if they were contaminated, because in a mild way they are.

Skin is a surface the basin will use. Salt, mineral film, and whatever concentrates in those tea-colored and bottle-glass ponds will find cuts, nail beds, and the crease behind the knee. Cover them. Bring more barrier cream than you think modest, and use it on wrists, neck, and ankles before you step onto a dike. Gloves you can work in—thin enough for a handrail, tough enough that a crab’s curiosity at an outflow grate does not become a puncture. The rails are warm. Some are too warm. Touch with the back of a glove first, the way you would touch a kettle. Concrete has spalled and been plated; edges are soft with years of wet and then suddenly sharp where a plate has lifted. Closed shoes with a real sole, drained, not waterproofed into a personal swamp. Ankles supported. The crust looks like a decision and walks like a pie crust over a secret. Test each new polygon. If it rings hollow or darkens under your weight in a spreading blot, step back onto the dike. Do not kneel. Do not sit on the sinter to rest. The ground is wet, mineral, and sometimes hotter than the air, and it does not want your skin as a sample.

**Water is the central lie.** It beads, films, stands, drips from every gantry, and none of it is for you. The ponds are process, not landscape. Brine organisms, copper stains, bottle-glass greens, a living turbidity the color of weak tea—that water is a workplace for pumps and a habitat for things that have agreed to the chemistry. Treat every open surface as if it could burn, poison, or simply ruin a week of skin. Do not trail a hand. Do not rinse dust off your face. Do not taste the rain to see if this particular rain is kinder. It is not. Carry more drinking water than the humidity suggests you need, because thirst hides inside damp clothes, and the shimmer makes you underestimate how long the next pump house is. A liter you did not have to open your respirator to reach is worth more than a clever bottle you must unmask to use. Electrolytes. A way to keep the drinking water from cooking against a warm rail. If you are the sort of traveler who purifies puddles on principle, leave the principle at home. There is nothing here to purify into safety with a tablet.

Food should be dull, sealed, and already seasoned with salt you chose, not salt the wind chose. Eat indoors or with your back to a wall, mask up between bites, and accept that the flies will rise in a sheet if you disturb a margin and settle again before you have finished deciding whether to swat. They do not sing. They tick. They stitch the air a hand’s width above the crust and seem to respect a ceiling. They will walk your stains. A head net helps at rest and is miserable in the heat; bring one anyway, and use it when you stop. Repellent is worth trying and not worth trusting. The biomass is not interested in your opinion.

**Navigation is a discipline.** The structures are spaced like people afraid of crowding the heat: pump houses, gantries, evaporator halls, rail spurs, pylons walking off toward a wrinkle that might be a ridge. Between them is enough flat to lose a vehicle in the shimmer and find it again only by the tick of its fan. Do not trust the eye for “just over there.” Before you leave a known door, write down the next three doors in order, with the crude bearings you took when the haze was at its thinnest, which will still not be thin. A compass, because the light will not tell you west. A small lamp even in the daytime—the shift lamps at evening do not defeat the dimness; they only make accurate pools, and you may want one of those before official dark. Mark your own route with something that will not blow into a pond and will not be mistaken for equipment. Tell someone the loop. If there is no one to tell, you are not prepared; you are only present.

Sound is a better map than sight once you learn it. A pump three kilometers off is a pressure in the ears. A rail car couples once. Metal ticks as it cools. A valve opens like a swallowed word. Stand still sometimes and sort these. The quiet between them is real, and it is large, and it will make you walk faster than you should because silence feels like lateness. It is not lateness. The processes do not pause for you, and they do not require you. Matching their pace—slow, scheduled, indifferent—is safer than hurrying across crust to get out of a feeling.

**Machines are the locals that can hurt you on purpose without meaning to.** Assume every rail can move, every grate can take a foot, every gantry drips a chemistry you have not identified, and every catwalk is slick with the film that never quite dries. Stay off evaporator halls unless you have been waved up by someone whose job is the hall. Do not lean on pipes to look picturesque. Do not cross a dike that is actively being rebuilt; the repairs are older than the originals in places, and a slumped wall of crushed mineral can look solid while it waits to slump again. Power pylons are not shade trees. The yards where gulls loaf are still yards. Give transformers the distance you would give a sleeping animal that is not sleeping.

The animal life proper will mostly ignore you, which is the best available courtesy. Waders on the dike-crests, gulls spaced with the same economy as the buildings, fish the color of concrete holding in warm sumps, palm-sized crabs working the outflow grates with a faint china-click. Do not feed anything. Do not wade to see the fish better. Do not put fingers through a grate because the crabs are precise and numerous and you are soft. If a whole pond shivers as something below turns as one body, step back from the edge and let the birds keep their bored footing. You are not being offered a sign. You are standing next to a density.

The dark slick on warm seepage is not moss and not a path. It is stain and bacteria and mineral, a varnish. It is slippery beyond what its thinness suggests. Walk the pale polished rind when you must cross crust, not the weeping margins where the slick thickens. And do not scrape a sample for later curiosity. This is not a place that improves by being pocketed.

**What to carry, then, if you are ordinary and intend to remain so:** sealed drinking water and electrolytes; respirator and spares; sealing goggles; hat; light long clothes and a rain shell; gloves; drained boots; a lamp; compass and a written loop; a way to keep your mouth and eyes shut while you think; a small kit for cuts, because the basin will find one; bags for wet contaminated clothes; a cloth you can sacrifice to wiping goggles every ten minutes. A radio or other contact to the settlement if one exists—people here are scarce the way spare parts are scarce, present and necessary and usually indoors. You are not owed a guide. You may be owed, if you ask early and humbly, the knowledge of which ponds are hotter, which dikes were rebuilt last season, and which service road actually returns. Take that knowledge as carefully as water.

Leave the romance of exposure at the edge of the basin. There is no summit, no clear hour, no green reward for enduring the chemistry. The beauty, if you let it be beauty, is in the polygons of the crust, the spaced gulls, the pale burning of mineral water that is memory of light rather than light, the way a million flies can make a dike look furred and then bare and then furred again. You can see that without tasting the ponds or breathing the lid down to the bottom of your lungs. Come out before the shift lamps make their small accurate pools and the rest of the basin goes back to its own dim burning. Shower in whatever freshwater the settlement will sell you, long enough that the metal smell leaves your hair and not merely your imagination. Check your skin in a real light. Drink. Sleep in a room that has already made peace with never-drying towels.

If you do it this way, the basin will still get into your nose and your sense of time. It will not get into your blood, or under your nails in a way that matters, or out across a crust that looked solid and was only a decision the land had forgotten. That is the whole art of visiting: to be ordinary, sealed, slow, and gone before the processes, which were never waiting, have occasion to include you.
