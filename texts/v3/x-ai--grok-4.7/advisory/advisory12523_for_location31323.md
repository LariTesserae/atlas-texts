Bring the habits of someone who expects to be cold, wet, and unremarkable for a long time. This place will not test courage so much as it will test whether you can keep your body working while nothing around you agrees to become an event.

The height is the first quiet subtraction. The air is cool and thin and already used, sweet with wet stone, and it will not thicken because you need it to. Walk slower than your pride suggests on the first day. Sleep more than you think a landscape this empty should require. If you have ever felt lightheaded on a ridge, assume you will feel a milder, more constant version of that here, and that it will make slick rock stupider under your feet. Do not arrive already tired. Do not plan to “push through” the first district to see how far the city goes. It goes on. The going-on is the country.

What the water does to a person is more important than what it looks like. It is not a river you can step out of. It occupies the floor. In the open quarters you walk in it; in the tight ones you turn sideways between rims with it at your ankles or knees. It is cold in a way that does not announce itself as cold until your hands are clumsy and your judgment has narrowed. A film of it is on every wall you might brace against. Rain does not start the wetness. Rain only joins the films until the walls disappear for an afternoon and you are standing in a shallow sea with no edge to climb toward. Dress as if you will be damp from the knees down for the entire visit, and as if the damp will climb. Wool or something that still insulates when wet. A change of dry clothes sealed beyond the ambition of drip. Something to sleep in that has not touched the basins. A light shell with a hood, not because storms are violent, but because a wide rain can walk for days and the mist already stands at chest height by afternoon. Bring gloves you can afford to ruin. Bare hands on that mineral, hour after hour, will split.

Footwear matters more than almost any other object. The pavements are gritty with fresh precipitate where they are honest, and slick with a breath of algae where they are not. Rims are narrow, wet, and sometimes soft. You will step down into basins whose bottoms you can see and whose stone still betrays you, and you will be tempted, later, by cisterns whose bottoms you cannot see at all. Do not wade those. The greenish glass and the silt-plume are not an invitation. Depth here is local and sudden: a tighter pool nested in a corner, a slot that has become a vault. A boot that drains, that grips when wet, and that you can still walk in after it has taken on water is worth more than a beautiful dry shoe you will abandon by the second district. Carry a simple staff. Not for animals. For the next rim, the unseen sill, the place where a film of water hides a drop of a few inches that is enough to turn an ankle into the reason you do not walk out.

Treat the water as infrastructure, not as drink, until you have made it into drink. It is clear in the shallow pans and it has crossed a great deal of the same country. It tastes, if you are foolish early, of stone and a faint metal, sometimes iron, sometimes a waxy yellow district that should make you stop trusting your thirst. Mineral water like this will crust a pot, a filter, a mouth. Bring the means to boil, and the fuel to boil, because the plateau will not sell you either. Vegetation is a rumor: a tuft of sedge, a crust of black-green in a crack, moss the color of wet iron on a sheltered ledge. There is no wood in any honest sense. No fire ring. No deadfall drying on a ridge. If your plan requires a flame, the flame has to come in your pack, and it has to work when everything is damp, including the air. A small stove, redundant fuel, a metal cup. Collect from moving films and the little channels rather than from still, green, silted cisterns, and still boil it. The tiny shells on the moss ledges are evidence of lives you should not be sharing a cup with uninvited. Do not count on catching the dark bird or whatever slow thing lifts the silt. You will not see them often enough to eat, and the quiet is part of what makes hunting a bad bet: this is not a pantry that forgot to stock itself. It is a place whose biology is real and mostly elsewhere, conducted at volumes the air does not carry.

Food is therefore a closed account. Carry more than the walk seems to need, and carry it against damp. There is no forage worth the calories you will spend looking. There is no settlement to resupply you, and if anyone has walked here the water has agreed to forget them by the next rain. The psychological trick will be that the land looks, after a day, like a city, and cities imply bakeries. There are no bakeries. There are laminated walls. Eat on a schedule even when you are not hungry, because the sameness and the thin air will flatten appetite before they flatten strength.

Navigation is the real hazard, and it is not solved by being careful in the ordinary way. A map of this place is wrong by the following dawn in every particular and right forever only in its grammar: walls, sills, slots, pans, arches. Levels shift by inches overnight according to where condensation preferred to fall. A channel that spoke goes silent. One that was silent finds its voice. Landmarks are processes. You cannot blaze a trail the water will respect. You cannot trust a view, because there is no view that contains a difference; outward is a direction the plateau imitates for a morning and then abandons. Districts change dialect—lower rims, broader basins, iron smell, yellow waxy deposit—and the change is not a border.

So prepare to be lost in a way that does not look like lost. Before you leave whatever edge or insertion point delivered you, build a method that does not depend on the stone remembering you. A recorded bearing is better than a memory of a pale rim. Satellite or inertial tools may or may not have anything to lock onto in a given multiverse; do not bet the walk on one device. Bring a compass and the habit of using it while you still know where you entered, not after the mist has made three hundred paces into a suggestion. Mark your own movement in a notebook with times and sounds, not with sketches of walls: which cistern ticks faster, which channel has the lower note, where an arch breathed a private fog. Those pitches are more stable than the levels, and they are still not permanent. Set a turn-back rule in hours and in condition, and obey it when the rule arrives, not when the next district looks more open. The most common failure here will be the belief that you are about to reach a change. You are always in the middle of the works.

If you go under the arches, go as if entering a building that floods on purpose. They are tall enough to stoop through and long enough to have their own weather. The ceiling drips. The echo is too small to be called echo, which means sound will lie to you about distance. A drip can be mistaken for a step, and there are no steps. Bring a light that is not your only light, and a way to keep both dry. Know that these understreets connect districts the surface does not admit are connected, and that following one “just to the next opening” is how a short exploration becomes a night in a vault. Do not travel them in rain. When the films join, the slots that feed the arches become throats. Water that has used the same path for centuries will not negotiate with a visitor who liked the shelter.

Flood is not spectacle and not safety. When the walls vanish, stop. The old paths are under a few inches of moving sheet, and the drops at the sills are still there, now invisible. High ground is a relative joke; the ridges between sinks are low, and the mist thins on them without offering a camp that will stay dry. The best flood plan is to have already chosen a stance: a broader rim you tested, a pack that floats and is tethered to you, a dry layer you have not yet spent. Do not try to outwalk a rain that can last for days. Sit it out as if sitting were the work. It is.

Camp will always be a compromise with drip. There is no flat dry acre. Sleep on a rim only if you have tested that it will not shed you into the pan when you roll, and even then the mist will wet the bag. A small tarp pitched low, a pad that keeps you off the stone, a bag rated for damp cold rather than for a dry alpine night. The stone leaches heat. In the tight quarters the sky is a slot and your breath joins the fog and it feels indoors; that feeling is a trap if you are not willing to be indoors with water at the threshold all night. In the open quarters the wind is only an exchange between basins, but it is enough to make a wet sleeper colder than the air temperature suggests. Earplugs are worth carrying, not because the place is loud, but because after a day of silence the tick of a drip finding the same cup becomes enormous, and you will either sleep to it or be paced by it until dawn. Both are possible. Choose.

There is almost nothing here that wants to hurt you, which is not the same as nothing that can. The birds are small and leaving. The slow thing in the deep cistern is not a story you need to finish. Do not break rims to see the laminations; the wet stone holds, and a broken edge is a cut, and a cut in mineral water, uncleaned, is how a simple visit becomes an infection with no one to notice. Bring a small kit sized for blisters, ankles, split skin, and the ordinary infections of constant damp. Bring a way to dry your feet at midday even if you must wet them again in an hour. Trench foot is a better name for the risk than any monster. Watch your hands and heels. Pale wrinkled skin is information.

The mind needs its own kit. The silence is complete enough that small sounds become neighborhoods, and the dark does not add creatures so much as it removes your certainty about distance. People invent footsteps in places like this. Prepare by deciding, in daylight, what you will believe at night: the drip is a drip; the plume was silt; no one is approaching along the rim. A light, a watch, a few words you can say aloud to reset scale. Solitude here is not the solitude of a forest, which offers animals and weather as company. It is the solitude of a process that has run until its habits look like a plan, and that plan does not include you. That can feel holy for an hour and unbearable for a day. If you are the sort of person who needs voices, bring recorded ones and ration them. If you are the sort who needs a task, give yourself a narrow one: listen for the pitch of the district, note the dialects of deposit, count arches, and then leave. Do not make the task “understand the city.” It is not withholding itself. It is simply in its own state.

Go in while the light is normal and honest, not at the hour when contrast fails and the rims and the water become the same value. Leave yourself a margin before that thinning. Night travel is how you mistake a channel for a street and a vault for a room you meant to enter. Morning will show you that the levels moved. That is the place working. It does not require your witness.

What to carry, then, if it must be said as a list a person could actually shoulder: clothes that work wet, and a sealed dry set; boots that drain and grip; a staff; a way to boil water and fuel enough for days longer than the walk; food that does not depend on fire except for morale; two lights; a compass and a notebook; a low shelter and a bag for damp cold; a small repair kit for skin and for whatever holds your pack together; cord, because tethered gear does not become a gift to a cistern; a vessel; something to keep your hands from splitting. Nothing ceremonial. The plateau has no use for offerings, and it will laminate them into the next lamina without comment.

And the conduct that matters more than the kit: do not chase the edge. Somewhere the channels become a fall to lower air and a river begins, and that somewhere is days beyond any visible change. You are not required to imagine it as near. Near is the next rim, the film finding its old path, the small cold sound. Touch the walls if you want to know they are real. Do not try to improve them, mark them, or finish them. Drink only what you have made safe. Wade only what you can see the bottom of. Turn back while you still know the grammar of the way you came, even if every particular has shifted. The city builds itself by refusing every tool but time. You are an interruption with a body that needs heat, salt, and a direction. Bring those. Leave the process alone. It has already agreed, for longer than weather, on exactly this.
