Prepare as if the visit is a slow walk inside a finished cistern, not a swim through a wild lake and not a stroll through a greenhouse. The room is small in plan and tall in section, and the water was put there on purpose. It comes nearly to the lantern. Whatever air remains is a thin, windless band against the roof, not a room you can live in. An ordinary body will not drink that water as breath. The leaves do so because they were built to. You were not.

What you are entering is warm, clear, and packed. Color does not fade with distance, so the far wall looks closer than your lungs will agree. The medium presses the ribs with one even hand and does not change its mind. There is no wave to time your movement against, no animal to tell you the water is occupied, no stain line to show where the level has lived. New bolt heads, tray edges, and cable clips are still sharp. You are the first softness the room has been offered, and it will not round itself to meet you.

**Breath and the ceiling.** Carry air as if the surface is a hatch, not a refuge. A full-face system is better than a mouthpiece: leaves the size of doors will find anything that sticks out, and you will want your eyes inside the same dry volume as your mouth. Bring enough gas for the walk you planned and the walk you will actually take, which is slower. Redundancy matters more than capacity theater. A pony supply you can reach without turning sideways in a tray-gap is worth more than a large cylinder you cannot rotate past a conduit. Do not plan to “just go up.” Up is a flat, held surface you can palm, with the lantern beyond it and growth below it. You can touch that ceiling. You should not need it.

If the column is only a few body-lengths deep, pressure will still be a fact in the ears and a weight on the chest. If the nave is truly chapel-high, treat it as a deep column until you have measured it from the floor port or the entry, not from the light. Clear water lies about depth. Color staying red is not shallowness. Equalize early and often, on the way down, before you are distracted by a blossom the size of your face. Do not invent a decompression plan you have not calculated; if you do not know the depth, do not stay long and do not bounce between floor and surface for amusement. The place will not hurry you. You should still hurry your ignorance.

**Temperature and skin.** The warmth is a held bath, cistern-sun, one temperature enforced by a loop you will not see working. It feels kind for the first minutes and then it is only water, which steals heat without drama. A soft exposure suit is not for cold shock. It is for the hour when your hands have gone precise and stupid, and for the fasteners. Ceramic edges are bright because nothing has worn them. Resin pretends to be stone until you meet a bolt. A frond at rest on a conduit will not move because you brush it; the clip under the frond will. Cover the shins, the backs of the hands, the shoulders you will use to turn in spaces that do not admit a sideways body. Leave the palms usable. You will want to touch pipe and know which side is a degree kinder, and you will want to feel a bead of repair polymer before you lean your weight on a stem that has already been sealed by a nozzle.

Do not break skin if you can help it. The water is a formulated drink for plants: nutrients, treatments, whatever the dosing manifolds have decided this cycle. A cut here is a conversation with a broth, not with a clean sea. Rinse after. Carry a small sealed means of closing your own skin, and use it before you admire the way a cut stem clouds from clear to the color of itself. That trick is not for you.

**Eyes, mouth, ears.** The clarity is a courtesy and a trap. You will see the grid behind the green and think you understand the route. Under the layered leaves the daylight thins; carry a small light, dimmable, and use it less than you want. Bright light in water this clear throws hard little shadows off bolt heads and makes the room look even newer, which it is. A mask that keeps the formulated water off the eyes is not vanity. If you flood it, do not rub. Blinking will not rinse a chemistry you have not assayed.

Do not swallow. Do not clear your mask by drinking the room. The same water that keeps roots from rotting is not a promise about your gut. Taste, if it reaches you at all, is data, not refreshment.

The quiet will occupy the ears more than pressure does. There is no rain and no wave. A bubble is a decision that ends in a click against the underside of the lantern. Relays tick through bone. After a while you hear your own joints, and then you start to narrate them, and then the narration feels like sound from outside. A hood that does not slap, a regulator that does not chatter, and a commitment not to fill the silence with your own bubbles will keep the place legible. If your gear is noisy, you will think the machinery has woken. It has not. It was always awake, geared down until work is a suggestion.

**Weight, walking, and the packing.** You are meant to find the far wall by walking, which means you want your feet on the sintered floor more often than a diver wants feet. Neutral at the chest, slightly heavy at the boot, so a pause becomes a stand and not a slow rise into a leaf. No long fins. They will sign their names on every tray pitch. Soft soles. The floor is new and the edges are bright; the drop you must not forget is not only the room’s height but the pier under the floor ports, steel still silver, ground far enough down that weather has not chosen a shape for it. Do not kneel on a port to look. Look, if you look, from a brace that is not glass and not membrane.

There is no aisle that stays an aisle. Trays step up too tight to pass sideways without turning. Pipe between trays, cable loomed so close that a frond may rest on it and stay. Plan the turn before you enter the gap: shoulders, cylinder, light, the hand that is not holding a line. A line is worth carrying even in a room you can cross, because the green closes and the light does not go blue to warn you that you have turned twice. Clip it to structure, not to stems. Stems are scheduled. Structure is only almost permanent; panels unship. A latch-sound in another room means a grid is being replaced. Do not be the thing locked into the new ceramic.

The pruning wheel returns on a period longer than a shift and shorter than a season. The collector arm is thin, oiled, nearly silent, and it takes blossoms when the cycle says they are done. You will not hear either of them in time if you are listening to your knees. Learn the marks. Grease pencil on an upright is a sentence written for someone who is not you, and it is still the only prose in the room: a yellowing was expected; a harvest is dated; a section is already finished in someone’s mind. Stay out of the path those sentences describe. You are not on the schedule, which is the only kind of safety the room offers.

**What not to bring, and what not to leave.** Nothing enters that was not put on a manifest, and nothing leaves except by collector, drain, or sample line. You are already a violation of the rule. Arrive as if that were true in practice, not as poetry. Inventory every object to the gram and the burr: weights, light, line, the scrape of ceramic dust you will not notice on a glove. Do not shed hair ties, tape flags, food, urine, a glove, a “temporary” mark of your own. The water is too clean and too new to gnaw, and it is not permitted to learn erosion from you. A thread in a root mat is a decade of someone else’s confusion, or a day, depending on how often they unship panels. Either way it is yours.

Do not cut, dose, sample, or “just see what the sap does.” The repair bead is the room’s idea of a wound. Your knife is not an improvement on it. Do not open a cabinet because the relay is the loudest fact and you would like to see it. Motors and valves are doing a replacement, not a flow you can usefully interrupt. You cannot drain this nave as an exit. The valves are the size of a fist and they open by quarter-turns across hours. If you are in trouble, the trouble will still be inside the water when the first quarter-turn finishes.

In the other direction, assume the room would like to come with you. Formulated water in a hose, a leaf fragment in a buckle, a bead of polymer on a fingertip. Rinse and account before you cross back. You are an ordinary body. You are also a vector with pockets. The absence of insects is a rule, not a vacuum that wants filling. Do not test it with a fruit fly of sentiment, a “harmless” spore, a snack.

**Mind, time, and the drop.** Perpetual noon is a counterfeit kept by lights and heaters, and your clock will not believe it. Set a timer you can feel, not a guess you can narrate. The quiet encourages staying. Staying is how people begin to hear themselves as part of the machinery. A prewritten turnaround — gas, or time, or the first moment you cannot name which tray you came through — is more useful than courage. Go with a second person if the entry allows two; if it allows one, go as if the second person is the timer.

The strangeness is not a creature in the corner. It is the medium behaving as architecture. That will make you reverent, and reverence makes you slow in the wrong gaps. The flowers do not need you. They open over days because a manifold said so. Watching one finish is a way to miss the collector arm. Looking down the floor port is a way to remember that the whole room was jacked, that there is no bedrock, that the substrate is a cake laid after the walls. Remember it once, from a solid brace, and then watch your feet.

**Entry and leaving.** Know the hatch, the membrane, the collar, before you know the leaves. The sheet that passes for glass is tensioned and replaceable; it is not a shore. If your way in is a lock, learn which side is the room and which side is the drop, and do not become sentimental about “outside,” which is only elevation and unweathered steel. On the way out, surface last, not first: check the suit for riders, check the line, check that you are not still holding a stem because it was beautiful and still. Then leave on the manifest you improvised, poorer by a little heat and a little air, carrying nothing the collector did not already know how to take.

If you do it rightly, the visit will feel less like travel than like being installed and then, on schedule, removed. The water will press, forget to vary, and let the reds stay red. You will walk until the far wall is a fact. You will hear a click that might have been a bubble and might have been you. And you will understand the preparation that mattered: air you do not have to beg from the ceiling, skin the new edges cannot sign, weight enough to walk, and the discipline not to become the first weather the room was built to refuse.
