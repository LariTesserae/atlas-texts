The place will not hurt you the way a storm does, or a desert, or a high thin pass. It will simply never give you back a dry surface, a hard edge you can trust, or a horizon you can use. Prepare for occupation by water, not for weather you can wait out.

**Assume you will be wet from the first minute and stay wet until you leave.** The air is already a warm solution. Rain has no drops or has weight; either way it does not end. Cloth that “breathes” still drinks. Anything that needs to dry in order to work—insulation that lofts only when dry, leather that stiffens, electronics that tolerate a splash, fire—fails here on a schedule of hours, not days. Pack as if you will live inside a warm shower that has forgotten the faucet.

Wear little, and wear it so it cannot turn into a soaked weight. A thin, close, quick-moving layer is better than a waterproof shell that traps the same warm wet against the skin and then never vents. A shell still has a use, but only as a brief wind-break or as a way to keep grit out of a scrape, not as a promise of dryness. Bring a change you can swap when the first set becomes a heavy second skin, and accept that the spare will be as wet as the one you took off within an hour of wearing it. Plan to be comfortable naked or nearly so for long stretches. Modesty is a dry-country habit. Skin, here, is the only surface that does not mind the water.

The real injury is not chill. The air sits a little above comfort, so hypothermia is the wrong fear unless you stop moving in a wind-shifted veil and let fatigue do the cooling. The injury is maceration. Feet, groin, armpits, the crease under a pack strap, any place skin presses skin or gear presses skin, will soften, whiten, split, and invite whatever small life already drinks the mats. Treat feet as the limiting organ. Grease them before you enter—plain, thick, water-resistant ointment, renewed whenever you pause—and keep nails short. Bring more blister care and antifungal than you think a warm place requires. A warm swamp on a mountain is still a swamp. If you have a history of infections in permanently wet skin, do not treat this as a scenic walk; shorten the visit or do not go.

**Footwear is a negotiation with young rock, not with trail.** The scarps are still granular. They give up grains when touched. Ledges are sharp because nothing has had centuries to round them, and every surface is filmed. Boots that grip wet stone are worth more than boots that keep water out, because they will not keep water out. A sole that sheds mud-grit and still bites a wet lip of rock matters more than ankle support you cannot feel once the boot is a bucket. Gaiters will fill. Consider light footwear you can empty, plus a second pair, plus a willingness to go barefoot on the mats where the green is packed and the stone is kind, and to put the boots back on the moment the ground becomes talus. Talus here is a bright spill of pieces that have not been sorted. It slides. Test every step as if the mountain were still deciding whether to hold.

Do not trust a scarp face because it looks solid from across a gully. These ridges were lifted faster than they were worn. A fresh fracture is pale and granular; a dark face is only dark because it is wet, not because it is stable. Rockfall is quiet in this soundscape—the rain hushes everything—and a grain leaving a face to join the talus is the place’s ordinary heartbeat. Do not linger under a scarp. Do not camp in the bright spill at its foot. If you must cross talus, cross high and early in your energy, not at the end of a day when your feet are soft and your judgment is saturated.

**Movement should be slower than the map in your head.** There is no map, and if you carry one from some other world it will lie about exits. Young rock has not cut the drains older mountains take for granted. Water arrives and stays. Basins hold sky-lakes. Overflow is a thread, not a river you can follow to a lowland by evening. Gullies are slots. A route that looks like a descent may be a pour-off with no continuation your body can use. The hanging growth is not hostile—it does not sting or grasp—but it has resistance. Passing a hand through a place where two veils lean into each other is like walking through layered curtains that do not part cleanly. That resistance will tire shoulders and hips if you fight it. Bend, wait, let a veil lean, go around a density rather than through it when you can. The green is doing the only work this place has assigned, and you are a temporary thickness in its weather.

Visibility is the second limiting organ after the feet. Ordinary daylight is up there and remains ordinary, but it arrives already sorted by water. You will see truly and not far. Ridge repeats beyond ridge. A paler plane is a basin, not a clearing you can aim at for long. Distance ends in the same sentence the place is already saying, not in a border. Do not navigate by “downhill.” Downhill may be into a slot, into a standing basin, or into a mat that looks like ground and is only leaves over a lip. Navigate by time, memory, and marks you can still find when wet.

Marks are hard. Cairns slump. Anything organic is absorbed into the green. Scratch a pale fracture and the film of water and the first film of green will soften the scratch sooner than you would like, but a fresh pale cut is still the most readable sign this stone offers. Use it sparingly and only on the way in, so the way out is a chain of your own cuts, not a hope. Better: carry a spool of bright cord you are willing to abandon, tied at intervals to stone, not to the trailing growth—the growth moves when the veil leans, and a line tied to it walks away from you. Retrieve the cord if you can; if you cannot, cut it rather than follow a loop you made while tired. A small spool of contrasting tape on the least fragile stone edges works if you press it hard so the wet does not lift it.

Carry a compass if your world has a magnetic field this one shares, and do not be shocked if the repeating ridges make the needle feel useless. A direction is still a direction when landmarks fail. An altimeter helps more than you expect: the height holds, and “the basin was below the third spur” is a sentence you can check when your eyes cannot. If you have a device that logs a track, seal it as if you were swimming with it, in more than one bag, and assume the seals will fail on a longer stay. Paper turns to pulp. Ink runs. Memorize the entrance. The entrance is the only reliable door, because nothing here has been kept as a path.

**Water is everywhere and is not automatically yours.** You can breathe the air. You should not drink the air’s refuse without treating it. The basin is rain that arrived and found nowhere to go, plus whatever the mats leach, plus the slow overflow’s silt of fresh mineral. Young rock glints. Some of those minerals are only sharp and bright; some, in other geologies, are not kind to a gut or to kidneys when they dissolve into standing warm water. You cannot know which by looking. Treat every drink as collected rain you do not trust: boil if you can, which you often cannot, because fuel and a dry flame are the hardest things to keep here; or filter and chemically treat; or carry your water in from the threshold and ration it against the hours you planned. Catching rain off a clean sheet sounds plausible and is, for a short visit, the best local source—if the sheet stays cleaner than the mats. Do not drink from the pale plane. Do not drink from the thread just because it moves. Moving, here, only means it found a lower lip.

Eat food that survives warm damp. Dry rations mold. Bread slumps. Anything in paper is gone. Tins, thick retort pouches, hard fat, nuts in sealed containers, salt in a jar with a real lid. You will not want heavy food. The warmth and the lack of a horizon dull appetite and then, later, punish you for not eating. Eat on a schedule, not on hunger. Salt matters because you will sweat without noticing, the sweat indistinguishable from the rain on your skin. If your urine goes dark, you are behind on water, not “adjusting.”

**Sleep is a problem of angle and film.** You need a surface that is not a scarp foot, not a slot, not the lip where veils anchor, and not the bright talus. A broad ledge with a mat already established is the kindest bed: the green is packed, the roots have found cracks, the angle is less likely to be the one still shedding grains. It will be wet. A pad that insulates when soaked is worth more than a tent that claims to be waterproof and then becomes a warm bag of condensate. A tent still has value as insectless privacy and as a way to keep the trailing growth off your face, but do not pitch it where a night’s grainfall can reach it, and do not expect the inside to be dry. A hammock needs anchors. The growth is not an anchor. Stone horns that are part of the ridge, not loose on it, might be. Test them as if they want to leave with you.

You will not have a fire unless you carried fuel that lights wet and a platform that keeps the flame out of the film. Do not count on one for morale, drying, or water. A small, sealed chemical warmer is more honest. Light, for you, should be redundant and waterproof: a headlamp you can swim with, a second, and a chemical light that does not care about batteries. Night will not be a different country. It will be the same saturation with the ordinary sun removed, so the veils become walls. Do not travel then.

**Do not arm yourself against a voice that is not here.** Fauna, so far as the place announces, is small, slow, and uncommitted. Something pale under a mat may be a root or a body. Assume both, and assume neither wants a fight. Do not poke mats. Do not sleep with a hand trailing into the green. A blade is useful for cord, for opening food, for cutting a strap that has swollen tight—not for clearing a path. Clearing a path is how you turn a soft resistance into a wound in a place that will close behind you anyway and will not thank you. If something does sting, grasp, or follow, that is new information the description did not offer: stop, back out by your own marks, and do not decide you must understand it before leaving.

The biological risk you should actually pack for is ordinary and cumulative. Warm permanent wet means softened skin, then infection; small cuts from sharp young stone that do not scab because they never dry; grit in the eye; an ear that stays full of water; a gut that meets a mineral or a leachate it has no name for. Bring a way to irrigate eyes with clean water, a way to close small cuts that works on wet skin, and a course of care for skin infection you already know how to use. Do not improvise with the mats. Their chemistry is part of how they live in air that is half water. “Natural” is not a dosage.

**What to leave behind.** Anything whose failure would trap you: a single navigation device, a single water method, a plan that requires a dry hour to repair gear. Heavy waterproofing you will curse by noon. Cotton. Paper books. Leather boots you love. A stove that needs dry tinder. Drones and cameras you cannot reseal—the images will be the same green darkening every time, and the machine will die of the air. Perfume, scented soap, anything that announces a chemistry this place has not met; you do not know what the slow life under the mats treats as a signal. Weapons carried for courage. A large pack that turns your back into a wet sail and your shoulders into sores. More days of food than your feet can honestly walk, if the extra weight is what makes you miss the exit.

**How long to stay.** A day is a visit. You can enter at the first true daylight inside the rain, walk a single ridge’s worth of careful distance, look at one basin from above without descending to its pale plane, and be back at the threshold before the light dims. That is enough to understand the sentence. Two days asks for a wet night and already belongs to people whose skin and judgment are practiced. Longer than that is not exploration; it is a bet that your feet, your water, and your marks will last longer than the place’s indifference. The place does not escalate. It does not need to. It occupies the volume, and you are a visitor who dries only by leaving.

**At the threshold, before you step in, do the only rehearsal that matters.** Stand where the air is still only air. Drink. Eat. Grease your feet. Seal the water treatment where you can reach it one-handed. Tie the first mark where you can see it from both sides. Tell someone on the dry side when you will be back, and make that time shorter than your pride wants. Name a turnaround that is not “when I have seen enough”: a fixed hour, a fixed number of marks, the first moment a sole skates and you catch yourself with a hand. The hand-catch is information. Young stone gives up grains. If it gives them up under your weight, the rest of the day is for leaving, not for the next spur.

When you are inside, the strangeness will not announce itself as danger. Colors stay true. Nothing watches you with an invented eye. The overflow thread is the loudest thing and it is not loud. That calm is the hazard’s costume. You are warm, you can breathe, the green does not grasp, the light is honest, and every cubic span is already full. The traveler’s preparation is not armor against a monster. It is a set of small, stubborn refusals to become part of the saturation: feet that still have skin on them, water that is not the basin, a line of marks that still leads to the place where air is only air, and the willingness to turn around while the mountain is still sharp and you are still only visiting.
