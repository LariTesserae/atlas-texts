Prepare as if the place will not negotiate with any habit you formed in a world that still has weather. The basin does not test courage. It tests whether you have already decided, before the lip, which of your body's leaks you are willing to leave behind.

Go up in the ordinary way first. The thinning starts outside the glass, where wind still exists and altitude is only altitude: less air, colder skin, a mouth that goes dry for the familiar reason. Do that climb in daylight if you can, and learn your own numbers while the sky is still a sky—how far you can walk before speech shortens, how your hands fail when they are only cold and not also cooked, how long a sip lasts when you are not yet afraid. If you arrive at the lip already in debt to your lungs, the basin will collect the debt in a currency you cannot count. Acclimatize on the shoulder. Sleep there if the route allows it. Eat. The last ordinary meal should be behind you before you look over, because once the gas reaches you, appetite becomes a rumor and you will not want to open anything that smells like food.

At the lip the wind stops. That is your last honest signal. If you can still feel moving air on your face, you are not in it yet. Step only when the air has gone still and the smell has already found you: iron, flint, a sweetness that is not sweet. That smell is not atmosphere in the travel sense. It is the breath of the seams, alkali and metal and brimstone, hot, dry, and older than any seal you trust from a shop. Do not plan to "get used to it." Mucosa does not adapt to that on a human clock. It burns, then it lies, then it keeps burning after you have decided the burning is normal. A mask that merely filters dust is a courtesy to your pride. You want a closed loop: your own air, your own moisture, nothing shared with the basin and nothing of the basin shared with your blood. Altitude means the loop must also feed you oxygen, not merely scrub what you exhale. Test the seals in the cold on the shoulder, then again in whatever heat you can make without theater—an engine bay, a sun-struck rock, a pan of stones. Alkali finds the place you did not torque. Sulfur finds the polymer that looked fine in a catalog. If the mask fogs, you have already lost; fog is water, and water is the one fresh chemistry this room does not know how to ignore.

Bring no loose water. That is the rule that will feel like superstition until the first drop. The basin has no streams, no seeps, no cold wall that sweats. Moisture is an event. A bead on your lip, a film in a canteen mouth, a swallow spilled down a glove, will not find a low place. It will mineralize where it lands, a brief flare of salt and iron, and the nearest plates will turn—not toward you, toward the flare. They graze chemistry. Your sweat is a meadow that lasts one breath. So hydration stays inside the suit and inside you, metered, warm enough not to shock your own throat, never decanted. Relieve yourself before the lip, and then do not. A bladder under the plates is not a latrine. Anything you vent becomes part of the film's next meal and part of the story the mass tells about a cold, wet anomaly that stood where the wheel usually turns. If you must stay long enough that the body insists, you carry it out. All of it. The place has been itself too long to be improved by your salts.

Heat is the other half of the same problem, and it will not be solved by thicker cloth. The cold you feel on the shoulder is a lie the sky tells. Past the glass the heat is radiant, stored, a kiln-dark that meets skin and then meets lung. Insulation that makes you a walking night will save your flesh and mark you as a well. They do not see you. They search heat. A cool patch is a direction. Stand on the mass in a hard shell of mirrored chill and the wheel will revise around you: some pressing to the gradient the way they press to a seam, some refusing the patch your shadow would have cooled if light could reach the floor. That is not an attack. It is traffic. Traffic can still bury a boot, pinch a hose, climb a column up the outside of your leg because your leg is the coldest stone in that square of dark. So you do not dress to become ice. You dress to become boring. Match the crust if you can—neither a torch nor a draught. Reflect what you must so your core does not cook, vent what you must so you do not drown in your own suit, and keep the vent pointed and small and hot enough that it reads as another thin seam, not as a weather system. Practice the stance on the rim before you ever trust it: weight on the balls of the feet, knees soft, no lean. If you lock your legs you will faint into the mass, and the mass will receive you as temperature.

Do not plan to walk the floor.

The floor is not stone. It is bodies, three and four deep, plate over plate, and under them only the implication of ground. A hand set down finds give. The give does not cry out and does not scatter; there is nowhere to scatter to. Your ordinary human preparation includes a way to remain a visitor in the vertical—a line to the rim, a second person who does not descend, a belay that will still work when your gloves are slick with salt dust and your pulse is the loudest sound you own. If you go down, you go down to the edge of the mass, not into its traffic, and you do not put a knee where a plate can close over it. The walls are dressed in the same life, hanging thick. Brush a cluster and it returns, not from spite, from the pressure behind it. Give the hanging dark the width of your body and then some. Sheets of glass exfoliate from the rim and tick down and vanish under plates before they echo. That tick is the local equivalent of a footfall. If you hear something larger, it is probably you.

Light is for you alone. Their eyes, where the slit-glow shows a bead, do not search the dark. A lamp will not frighten them and will not guide them, and it will die as a view within a fathom anyway: the air eats illumination the way the glass eats sound. Carry light that is tight, close to your work, redder than your pride prefers, and pointed at the thing you must not misjudge—the lip, the arc of fitted stones, the hose, the hands of the person who stayed above. Do not flood the nave to see the wheel. You will see dust and your own beam and a suggestion of scale, and you will have spent heat and battery on a picture the place will not sit for. Your eyes will invent shapes in the slit-colored dark. Believe the rope, the count of paces you measured on the rim in daylight, and the smell when it changes pitch. Believe the slow shove in the soles when you are still enough to feel the basin breathe. Do not believe a corridor opening in the mass. Partings the width of a wrist are how the longer bodies move through the stacks. They are not paths. Seal behind them is the ordinary courtesy of the place. You are not invited to follow.

Sound will abandon you, so arrange your signals before you need them. The glass is porous under its glaze. A thousand small shifts vanish into cloth-like quiet. Your radio may work along the rim and fail in the well, or fail in both, because the failure mode here is not drama but absorption. A tug code on the line is older than any of your cleverness and better suited to a room that eats words. Agree on it while you can still hear each other without the mask: one pull, hold; two, pay out; three, take me up now, do not discuss it. The third signal is the one you will want to renegotiate when you are inside and the wheel has gone beautiful in its slowness. Do not renegotiate. Heat and bad air and the wish to understand are the same temptation wearing three coats.

Time is the discipline. The basin's pulse is longer than a breath and shorter than a watch, and once in a long interval every seam wakes at once, columns rising, the wheel stalled at full lean, dust glittering, and then the ordinary rotation resumes. You can live through that if you are on the stone of the rim and not in the pile. You should not plan to. Set a hard turnaround by the clock you carried in, not by how lucid you feel. Lucidity is the first thing the heat counterfeits. When your thinking goes smooth and generous, when the fitted stones start to seem like a sentence you could finish, when you want to take the mask off for one clean breath of the iron sweetness—that is the exit, already late. The person on the line exists to be ruder than you will be.

There are marks that do not join the wheel. Three stones set on edge in a short arc, knee-high, on the western wall above the regular climb of the clusters. The bodies part around them without a thermal reason you can find. Scratches on cleaner glass, too even, too worn to read. A ring of collapsed pebbles near the hottest throat, a hearth-shape for a fire the rock was already making. Prepare to leave them. They are not a camp. They are not a map you are qualified to complete. An ordinary traveler's job is not to correct the basin's small incorrect laws or to add a fourth stone because the arc looks unfinished. Fit nothing. Scratch nothing. Do not chip a plate of glass for a sample to prove you were here; the rim is already exfoliating on its own schedule, and a fresh edge is a fresh lie in a room that has finished becoming. If you must touch the arc to know it is real, touch with the back of a glove, once, and feel the avoidance in the pale track beside it—the only path in the place that is kept by habit after the reason has gone to powder. Then stand back and let the wheel have its exception.

What you carry in your head matters as much as the loop. You will want a story in which the mass is hostile, because hostility is a shape you know how to answer. It is not hostile. It is occupied. Every hand's-breadth is already spoken for. You are a brief cold, a brief salt, a temperature that joins the eddy and is forgotten when you match the crust again. Predation here is a pace, not a drama: something longer moves through, the scale-layer opens and closes, the numbers hold. Do not reach into a parting to see the shape. Do not feed the film to watch it cloud back over a shaved pale streak; you would be asking your water to die in public. Do not lie down to feel the wheel deform around you unless the line is in a fist that is not yours and the turnaround is already counted. The beauty of the stall—every column up, the dark glittering—is real, and it is also the moment a hose can kink under a plate that does not know hoses.

Approach from the west if the route allows, so the arc is a known bearing and not a surprise at the edge of your light. Learn the lip in a circuit before any descent: a few hundred paces, glass underfoot, salt bloom in the seams, the slit of sky the color of altitude and no help at all. Mark your exit in two ways that do not depend on seeing it—the line, and a count, and if you are wise a third thing, a stone of your own set on the rim where wind still reaches, ordinary, unfitted, a coward's cairn you can kick over when you leave so the basin does not inherit even that. Tell someone who is not coming what "late" means, in hours, not in feelings.

When you come out, do not peel the mask at the lip because the sky looks open. The boundary is a learned thing; gas lingers in cloth, in hair, in the wet of your eyes. Walk back into wind. Let the wind insult you. Drink only when your hands are steady and the iron-sweet is gone from the back of your throat, and even then from a bottle that never went over. Look at your seals in the light. Look at your boots for plates that are not mud. If something dry and mineral has come up with you, it is not a souvenir; it is a piece of a balance that does not have a surplus. Brush it back over the lip. Do not pocket the film. Do not bottle the gas. Do not take a scratch-rubbing "for later." The scratches have already been swallowed by later.

Then sit on the shoulder until your pulse is no longer the loudest citizen of any room you are in. The visit, if you prepared, will have been short and almost soundless: a cold well the wheel edited around, a few ticks of glass, the shove of a breath that was not yours, the arc keeping its law, the slit failing to light the nave. You will remember the heat as a pressure more than a temperature, and the dark as occupied rather than empty, and your own small flare of salt as the only fresh thing you were allowed to be. That is the whole encounter an ordinary body should ask of that room. The basin has been finished for longer than the stones. You are not there to finish it, or to wake it, or to teach it water. You are there to pass the lip, last a little while as an uninteresting temperature, and return to air that still knows how to be wind.
