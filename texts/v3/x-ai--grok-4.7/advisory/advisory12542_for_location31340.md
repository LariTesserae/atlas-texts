Prepare as someone who will be the only unfinished thing that can die quickly.

The basin will not hurt you on purpose. It has no interest in visitors, no signage, no schedule that matches yours, and no spare volume. What can go wrong is almost all mismatch: you need light, speed, food, dryness, silence, and a way home; the place is dark, slow, mineral, wet, loud, and already full.

**Fix the way home before you descend.** Near the center you will stop thinking in sentences. The noise occupies every frequency you use for speech and for attention, and a day of walking at their pace is long enough for cold and confusion to make a plan you are still carrying in your head into something you cannot execute. Set the return at the rim, on the old ringing glass, where wind over unbroken flows gives you the one high sound that is not animal and not water. A physical anchor you can find by touch and by that bright edge of noise. A timed extraction that does not require you to call. Do not depend on a signal sent from the shelves. The air is wet enough to bead on everything, and the sound will swallow whatever you try to say.

**Dress for wet cold, not for weather.** There is no storm to wait out and no sun to warm you. The cold lives in the rock and moves into you through your feet, your hands, and the film on your face. The wet never stops: not quite rain, a constant condensate that soaks stitching, fogs lenses, and turns insulation into weight. Wear a closed shell that still insulates when it is filmed over, with no dangling straps for climbers to grip and no bright hard edges that ring when they strike glass. Soft soles, broad, that grip wet green-black glass without concentrating your weight into a point. The new flows break with a small bright noise if you strike them, and that noise is swallowed at once, which means you can crack the ground under you and not hear the warning spread. Do not wear anything that needs to be dried by air. It will not dry.

**Assume your eyes are useless and your ears are the map.** There is not enough light to adapt to. A few mean glints off wet edges are not illumination. Bring light, but treat it as a tool you uncover for a step and then cover again: dim, close to the ground, shielded from the spans above you, warm-toned if you have a choice, never a flood. A hard white beam will not help you read the basin and will make you the only object in a lightless ecology. You navigate by bands of sound. Learn them on the way in, while you can still think: thin ticking where the glass is new and the crust is poor; fuller rasping where walls have been up long enough to farm; a slow drumming where the armored waders turn in the lower shelves; a high ringing from the far rim that tells you where out is. If you plug your ears completely you go blind in the only sense that works. If you protect nothing, hours of that density will damage you and you will still be in it. Use something that lowers the level without erasing the bands. Practice, before you go, walking a dark wet place by sound alone, because the first time cannot be down there.

**Make yourself narrow, quiet, and chemically dull.** The streets are not cleared for you. They are seams the animals have left by long use: a shoulder in most places, wider only where something heavier is meant to pass. A large pack will turn you into an obstacle in a system that does not route around obstacles. Carry what a body can wear. No cart. No poles that scrape. The small bodies floor the trenches and mat the seep-walls; they will not part because you are in a hurry. Step where the seam already is. If you cannot see the seam, stop and listen for the thin line of less-clicking. The larger shapes move on a patience you can time but should not test. When the grinding starts, a distance off, the small life redistributes around a space that is about to be occupied. Do what they do. Yield early, up-terrace if you can, off the flexing lining. The poured streets sometimes move on their own, a slow clearing of silt and husks toward colder shafts. Do not stand in a street that has begun to flex. Do not investigate the shafts.

**Bring every calorie, and do not trust the water you can taste in the air.** Nothing here is food for you. There are no plants, no wood, no fiber, no familiar crop. The mineral rinds are the animals’ only harvest; they are not yours, and they are precipitate, iron-colored and older than your chemistry. The standing water is shallow, black, and full of whatever the unfinished rock is still giving up. Condensate looks cleaner and is not a guarantee. Drink what you carried. If you must take local water, take it from the high films on new glass, not from pools or seep at the base of walls, and run it through a filter meant for metals, not merely for microbes — and still treat that as an emergency, not a plan. You will want to drink. The air itself feels drinkable. That sensation is a lie your mouth tells in constant mist.

**Keep your skin and your waste out of the lining.** This is the rule that matters more than it looks. Organic wreckage does not last. Husks and shed armor are taken down, faster than the cold should allow, into grit the seep can use. That is a kindness to the city and a threat to you. Something in the poured mineral, or in what lives in it, is willing to dismantle flesh. Do not touch the base-courses with bare hands. Do not kneel in the seams. Cover cuts before you enter; if you open one down there, close it and leave the shelf. Do not rinse blood into a channel. Do not relieve yourself on the walls or in the water. Pack out anything that came from your body. You are a small quantity of exactly the material the drains were built to finish. The system is not hunting you. It also does not check whether you are done using your hands.

**Give the new rock its margin.** The eastern lip and any flow that still looks like bottle-glass, unsullied, unclaimed by the mats, is ground the city itself refused to close. It can take your weight in one place and not in the place beside it. Foundations grip older flows; the deliberate gaps are not shortcuts. Stay on lining that does not ring, the dense poured mineral that takes water and gives it back as seep. If a surface chimes under you, step back onto whatever did not.

**Do not study the machinery by using it.** The unusual part of this place is how quietly the engineering fits the herds: spans that hold clumps of bodies, drains cut for a tide made of animals, ribs meant to bow and thrum. None of that is a device for you. Do not hang instruments on the lattices. Do not sample the lining. Do not heat a patch of wall to see what the climbers do. Do not chip precipitate “just to look.” A bright strike, a heat plume, a scatter of crumbs, a metal taste you leave in the seep — any of these is louder, in a lightless mineral world, than your footsteps. The heavy passages and the waste-cycle do not need to understand you to process an anomaly. Ordinary metal, sealed food, closed containers. Nothing that sheds. Nothing that glows as a habit.

**Plan for their time, not yours.** A crossing can be a day’s walk if the seams stay open, and it can become much longer if a herd turns or a shelf rises that extra finger and the lower routes fill. Their longer cycles — the fattening of swarms, the cooling of a new lip, the upslope shift when the air thickens — are not weather you can outlast by waiting in place. You do not have the fat for it, or the stillness. Set a hard turnaround measured by what you carried: water, heat, and the condition of your ears and feet. When those are half gone, you are already late, because the way out is the same crowded seams and you will be slower and deafer than you were on the way in. Do not sit out a thickening in the lower marsh. The shelves are built to fill. The animals know the appointment. You do not need to keep it.

**Expect the thinness to bother you more than the dark.** You will be the only gap in a place where everything touches something else. That reads, after an hour, as exposure rather than freedom. The drumming does not peak and release; it continues, and your thoughts start to arrive in pieces the size of a step. This is ordinary and it is dangerous, because it feels like understanding. You will put a hand on a rib because the thrum is companionable, and the rib will be colder than your hand can afford, and whatever grips it will not mind you until you become a problem of balance. Touch nothing you do not have to. If you travel with another person, agree before the rim on simple touch-signals: stop, yield, back, light-for-one-step. Down there, talking is how you lose the map.

**What the visit actually is.** You come over the high glass into a bowl that is still deciding how deep to be. Water almost at the surface everywhere. No green, ever. A city sunk into rock that has not finished being born, built as a vessel so the mineral can carry more animals than bare glass could. You are allowed along the seams the way a draft is allowed, not the way a guest is hosted. If you move at their pace, stay on the poured work, keep your light small and your chemistry to yourself, and leave before your heat is gone, you can cross and come out having heard the scale of it — terraces as bands of rasping, the far rim as a ring, the center as a drumming you feel in the sternum. If you try to see it properly, clear a path, drink the seep, or wait for something to happen quickly, the place will not punish you. It will simply continue, and you are not built to continue with it.

Go to the rim with the return already true. Descend only as far as the seams and your water allow. Come up while you can still hear the high ringing as a direction rather than as a memory. The ground will still be unfinished when you leave. That is not your work.
