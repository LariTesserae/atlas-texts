The chamber will meet you as heat and dryness before it meets you as a place. Come in low, the way the entrance forces you to, and give your eyes time; the copper glow off the scale is real light, but it is stingy, and the hollows drink it. An ordinary body can walk here, work here, even sleep here for a while, but only if you treat water, dust, and heat as the three conditions that will decide the length of your visit. Nothing in the chamber will resupply the first. The second and third never pause.

Carry all the water you will drink, and more than you think a dry heat requires. There is no seep to misjudge and no film to lick off stone. Pack it in small, hard vessels you can clip to a belt or lash with cord, not a single bladder you must set down. You will set things down. The air pulls moisture out of skin, eyes, and the inside of the mouth fast enough that you should drink on a schedule rather than on thirst, and you should bring salts with the water. A cloth soaked once and kept in a sealed wrap is worth more than a second spare shirt: press it to the face when the iron-ash taste thickens and the sweetness starts to sit on the tongue. Do not pour, sprinkle, or rinse anything onto the floor, the mats, or the brass growths. The place has had no water long enough that a puddle is not a kindness. If you sweat, let it evaporate on you. Salt crust on the skin is only uncomfortable. Salt water on a mat is an experiment you did not come to run.

Dress for abrasion and for air that moves. The grit is fine and dark and works into everything; the pale filaments shed a drier dust that hangs and then settles on lashes, lips, and the rims of nostrils. Light, close weave over the body, a second layer you can strip when the hollows are hottest, and a wrap for the head and lower face. Goggles or close-set glasses. Boots with a sole thick enough for polished paths and for the coarse ground between them, and ankle coverage, because the hollow edges are not marked. Leave heavy packs and metal tools that will heat-soak. A short, light cord of your own, a flake-sized blade if you need one, a small lamp with a warm, dim beam, and a cloth bag for ash or scale if someone hands you either. Bright white light is not forbidden by anyone; it is simply wasteful and it flattens the only navigation the chamber offers, which is the difference between glow, gloom, and true dark.

Walk the worn paths until you understand which hollows are empty and which are only unlit. The floor is a shallow dish. The clear tracks stay clear because feet keep the scale from rooting; step off them and you are in ground that will take a print and, later, a mat. Do not treat that as a game. The people who live in the courses of cut block are few, and the thin net of paths, cord-lashed bladder bundles, raked ash lines, and fed embers is the whole of their order. If you meet someone, stop, let them see your hands, and do not cross a fresh ash line or unlace a bundle to look. Voices do not carry. A nod and a still posture read better than a call. If you are offered a place near the ember-trench, take it low and off the direct vent of any fissure. The breath from deeper stone is hot enough to tighten skin; the trench fire itself is slow, almost smokeless, and fed with dried scale. Do not add your own fuel.

The growth is not foliage and it is not hostile. It drinks stored heat. Rust-orange scale shingles the worked faces by preference, lifts and cracks as under-layers push, and will colonize a threshold the morning after feet stop using it. Pale threads hang, sway, and dust you. The brass bladders in the hottest hollows are rigid enough to ring under a knuckle; the sound travels. They also ease, over days, toward greater heat if left in a heap. Do not stack, roll, or “organize” them. Do not bed down against a wall of scale. If you must set a kit bag down, set it on a path, in the open, and do not leave it overnight. In the crawl at the far end the glow fails, the ceiling comes down to your shoulders, and the filaments have already copied cord-lacing knot for knot in the dark. That copy does not lead anywhere. Go there only with your own light, only as far as you can still hear the chamber behind you, and do not leave a pattern of your own for them to repeat—not a knot, not a laid cord, not a row of stones. The copying is patient and exact, and it does not need you to finish the gesture.

Expect the noise and do not wait for it to become quiet. Bladders tick on different clocks. Scale flexes like paper and then reports, small and dry, when a sheet frees. Air rushes low in the hollows and higher where a fissure vents. Cords thrum. A slab may settle a hair and send it into your feet. None of this is an alarm. The alarm is your own: dizziness, a stopped sweat, a headache that the sweetness seems to ride, grit you can no longer blink clear, or the sense that the dim has become simpler because you have stopped seeing the path edges. Those are exit signs. The chamber does not cool off for night in any way you can trust, and it will not lend you water on the way out.

Sleep, if you sleep, on a path or on stone a tender indicates, with your water on your body and your lamp where your hand already knows it. Eat what you brought. The ash, the scale, and the threads are not food, and the sweetness in the air is not an invitation to taste the growth. Wash nothing. Relieve yourself only where you are told, or outside, and carry out whatever will not simply become more grit. When you leave, stoop the way you entered, and do not look back for a change in the chamber. It does not mark arrivals or departures. Mats thicken and shed. Embers sink and are fed. Abandoned paths close. Walked paths stay. The heat stays. You are the soft thing that needed water, and the place will be exactly as finished after you as it was before—edges worn, purpose intact, the ticking going on in the unoccupied dark.
