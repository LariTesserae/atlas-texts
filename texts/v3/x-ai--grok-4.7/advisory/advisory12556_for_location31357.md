The dark will not become a night you can wait out. Treat sight as a skill you are about to lose, not as a tool you are bringing.

You will arrive already late to the country's argument. The shelf does not present itself. It closes over a body at the ankles and the ears at once: warm film on rounded stone, a mat that gives and seals, air that is mineral and sweet-sour, and a noise stack with no quiet interval in which to reset. If you walk in expecting wilderness, you will miss the industry in the water. If you walk in expecting a plant, you will miss that the herds and the storms are already using the machines as geography. Prepare for a place that is legible, but not to eyes.

**Leave the highland kit at home.** Height here does not mean cold, thin air, or a view. The stone is warm underfoot, the deck of condensate sits low enough that a ridge can put you into its belly, and the weather is a closed loop of weep, heat, and rain that never clears. What you want is heat-tolerant wet-country gear, not alpine gear. A shell that breathes, because a sealed suit in this warmth will cook you. A soft, high-grip sole that still holds when the stone is filmed and the mat is slick — stiff boots with aggressive lugs load the ankle on rounded rock and skate in the film. Gaiters, because the mat is ankle to knee and holds water like a sponge. Nothing that depends on staying dry. Assume every textile will be wet within an hour and will stay wet until you are back across. Chafe, not hypothermia, is the daily injury. Skin macerates. Bring what you would bring for a week of warm river travel: spare sealed dry layers for sleep only, antifungal care, zinc or similar for folds of skin, and a way to dry the inside of your ears. Do not bring a wardrobe. Bring a system you can maintain by feel in the dark.

**Do not drink the country.** The weep is the country's water and the industry's water and they are no longer sortable. It will taste faintly of work even days from a station — metal, scale, something fermented. Near a hall it may run warmer, oilier, wrong in a way the animals prefer. Carry your own water for the first long leg, and a filter you have already learned to service wet and blind, plus a chemical or mineral check you trust more than taste. Taste will lie to you after a day; the sweet-sour of the growth gets into the mouth. Refill only from what you can identify by hand as short-run seep off clean-feeling limestone, and even then treat it. Do not drink standing channel, rail-groove, or anything down-drainage of a note you can hear. Food should be dense, sealed, and boring. Warm humidity spoils anything that can spoil, and you will not have light to judge it. Eat before you are ravenous. Hunger makes people follow herd-paths, and herd-paths are highways.

**Navigation is the real preparation, and it is not visual.** There is no dawn, no horizon, no open ground worth the name. A lamp will not give you a country. It will give you a bright sphere of rain and straps, ruin what little dark-adaptation you have, and announce you in a medium the animals do not use. If you bring light, bring it small, dim, hooded, and red or amber, for tasks at your hands: a buckle, a blister, a label. Never as a headlamp sweeping the growth. The travelers who do well here are the ones who spent time, before they needed it, learning to move with their eyes closed in a place that was only inconvenient — a wet wood, a boiler room, a night river — until footing, rope, and sound were enough.

Learn the layers before you trust them. Nearest is water on whatever is in front of you. Then the vegetation, which ticks, sheds, and creaks when a pressure-cell passes. Then animals: heavy knocking of herds on seep-flats and old rails, clicks and short whistles in the strap-forest, and the sudden thinning of those sounds, which is how hunting appears. Then engines. A station is a stack of frequencies, pumps and conveyors, steady because it does not set. The herds treat the gradients between notes as a map. You may do the same, cautiously, as a distant bearing — not as a destination you must reach today. Sound travels strangely well under the deck. A note you hear is not a note you are near. People die here of confidence: they walk toward a station that is three days off, leave the drainage they understood, and cannot find the return because every channel has the same rounded lip and the growth has already softened their own marks.

So: short legs. A tactile trail you can re-find with the foot — not blazes, which you cannot see, and not flagging, which the rain and the mat swallow. Cord laid and recovered. Cairns only where the mat is thin, on ridge noses, and even those are a courtesy to your future hand, not a guarantee. A tether if you are two; a voice cadence if you are more, regular, boring, so your own party does not vanish into the chorus. Agree before you leave what "stop" means, because in the rain you will not have a clear word. Do not travel in a pressure-cell if you can help it. You will know it is coming by the fattening of every drip and the raised voice of water two valleys over. When it is on you, the air is full and the footing changes. Wait it out on higher, already-rounded stone, off the beds. The weep answers rain by running harder in channels it has used forever. A rail-groove that was a path becomes a flume.

**Camp as if the land is occupied, because it is.** Not by owners you must petition — doors stand open, crews may be absent, the dark is not an intruder — but by process. Do not bed in a seep-flat. That is where the heavy animals file, and the knocking carries an hour ahead of them. Do not bed in a corridor of clicks; a corridor that goes thin is a subtraction you do not want to be inside. Do not bed down-drainage of a station, in the taller, wrong-smelling growth the animals prefer for breeding. A pocket of lesser traffic near an old, cycling-down hall can feel safe and be a turbine still turning on seep alone, or a channel of mixed effluent with soft lips. Feel for geometry under the hand — dressed stone, a flange, cable surfacing like a root — and sleep beside it only if it is cold and quiet and uphill of the water. The cutting of growth off station walls keeps a schedule; that sound is not a predator, but the wall is not a bunkhouse. Open halls are full of pumps, conveyors, heat, and buried grid. The hum organizes a valley before the building does. If you shelter in a lee, stay out of the machine path, off the cable, and out of the effluent cut. The country will not distinguish you from debris if you lie in a bed it is still using.

**Animals.** They are not using silence, so you cannot hide in it. Quiet is how prey appears: a break in the knocking, a thin corridor, a smell of warm iron. Move with a steady, ordinary noise. Do not sprint, do not freeze, do not whisper your way into a subtraction. Give the heavy files the groove they are already in; you are softer than the stone they knock and they will not negotiate. The smaller bodies in the straps are constant and mostly indifferent. Predators are a change in the pattern you have been hearing, plus that iron smell. Your preparation is attention, not a weapon fantasy. If you are bleeding, you are a new note in a country that parses notes. Wrap it, wash it with your own water, and leave the flat.

**Body, over days.** There is no light cue. Your sleep will slip. If you need a rhythm to stay sane, make a small one inside a shelter — a covered lamp on a timer, used as a clock, not as a way to see the forest — and keep it private. Do not try to impose day on the shelf. The processes do not pause, and fighting that is how people start walking at the wrong time, into rain, into files, toward a note they have promoted into a rescue. Ears matter more than eyes. Protect them from the close industrial roar if you must pass a hall, then uncover them. Foam you forget to remove is a navigational injury. The air is close; some lungs hate the oil-sweet of a station's lee. Turn back at the first tightness, not at the fifth. Wounds in this warmth, with fermentation in the mat and metal in the water, do not stay trivial. Close them. Watch your feet every halt, by hand if you must: hot spots become rot faster than you expect, and you cannot afford a limp in country where the next sure sound may be a day away.

**Mind.** The strangeness is not a creature. It is scale plus intimacy. The deck is right there; the shelf runs out further than weather. Stations are few and the work is everywhere. You will feel watched by process and ignored by everything living, which is accurate. People panic in two directions: claustrophobia in the straps, where you advance by turning sideways and the middle storey sheds on your face even after the sky-rain stops; and a false agoraphobia when the sound-stack opens and you realize you cannot see the size of what you are hearing. Both pass if you shrink the task to the next reach of stone — hand, foot, the nearest water-voice, the tether. Do not chase the furthest note. Do not climb a ridge to "get a look." On the high noses you can touch the deck; the pole comes back wet to the shoulder, and there is still nothing to see. The deck is a lid, not a summit reward.

**What to rehearse, specifically.** Walking an hour in a safe dark place with a film of water on uneven ground, no lamp, locating a sound and not walking straight at it. Putting on your shell and repairing a buckle by touch. Treating water with cold fingers. Stopping when rain-sound fattens, and being willing to wait. Calling a companion on a fixed interval until it is dull. Turning around on a short leg while you still know the last three textures underfoot — this mat, this lip, this cable — and finding them again. If you cannot do that at home, you cannot do it here, and the shelf will not offer a gentler first mile.

Go in with a turn-back time that is earlier than your food, earlier than your feet, and earlier than your nerve. The country has been running this balance longer than the oldest pipe has kept its stamp. It does not need you to witness it, and it will not rearrange a channel because you are tired. The ordinary traveler who comes back is the one who stayed ordinary: warm, wet, loud enough to be parsed, small in the drainage, and unashamed to leave a station's note in the stack where it belongs — a bearing, not a promise.
