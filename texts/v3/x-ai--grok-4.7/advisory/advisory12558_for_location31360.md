Pack for a short, enclosed, unfinished works that never cools. You will not be crossing a landscape. You will be stepping into one room that is still being cut, already full, and hotter than the street that sits on it.

**What the place will do to a body**

The heat is standing heat. It does not draft, and it does not ease near the walls. Breath goes in warm. Sweat starts and stays, because the air is already too full of water to take more. Boot soles pick up heat off concrete. Metal that has been down an hour is unpleasant in the hand. A visit that feels reasonable at the bulkhead gets stupid halfway to the face if you dressed for a cellar or a night street.

Day and night are a rumor from a grate. The coin of daylight at the shaft does not travel. Lamps make small pools; between them the stone is brown dusk. The crown is high enough in the middle that a tall person still feels the sides. The walkable length is short. Echo lies about that until you move.

Nothing here is old enough to have been worn smooth. Tool marks, drill holes, sharp corners, a working face, a half-made sump, brick that is still a stack in places. You are in a birth, not a ruin. The danger is ordinary job-site danger plus a closed vault: heat, slick film, open water, machines on long cycles, and animals that got there before the finish work.

**Clothing**

Wear the lightest long layer that still covers skin, and expect it to be wet within minutes. Short sleeves feel better for ten minutes and worse for the rest of the visit: mosquitoes lift off the warm shallows when a boot nears, drain flies hold a skin over the sump, and the cockroaches at the spring line are a moving margin, not a scare you can step around. Long trousers, a light shirt with a collar, and a thin scarf or buff you can pull over nose and mouth when the lamp beams turn into ropes of midges. No heavy coat. No wool that stays sodden. No dark cotton that becomes a wet sheet and still does not cool you.

Boots, not shoes. The floor is new concrete, silt skin, grease rings, and tea-colored water that does not announce its depth at the sump lip. Ankles covered. Soles that grip when the green film is broken. Gloves you can work in — thin, not winter gloves — because you will want to touch less than you think, and because the rail, the hoist chain, the gauge glass, and the pipe collars are all hot or filthy or both. A change of clothes in a dry bag left at the door, not worn down. What you wear in comes out heavier.

A hard hat if the crew is using the crown or the shaft; even without them, the chain rail ends in midair and the drip is constant. If you will not wear one, at least do not stand under the sleeve joint to listen. The ticking is the room. You can hear it from anywhere.

**Water, salt, and time**

Carry more drinking water than the length of the gallery suggests, and drink it on a clock, not on thirst. Thirst arrives late here. Electrolyte in the bottle, not as an afterthought at the kettle. The kettle and the sugar tin are the crew’s, on a slow schedule. Do not plan to borrow them. Do not plan to sit out a bad spell on the cot. That cot means the room is a workplace with few people, not a hostel.

Plan the visit in lamp-cycles and heat-minutes, not in “I’ll just walk the face and come back.” Off-cycle, the room is loud in its lesser register: drip multiplied by brick, insect-shriek, a generator-shaped pressure in the ear after the machine has stopped. On-cycle, the gallery is a bell. A centrifugal pump and a generator under a tin hood will let you shout and be understood, and will not let you talk at ease. If you needed a long conversation, have it above. If you needed to hear something subtle, you will not.

Get out before you are interesting. The early signs are small: you stop noticing the smell, you lean on things, you decide the sump edge is a fine place to rest, you are no longer bothered by the flies in your eyes. That is the heat, not a mood. The way out is a shaft too narrow and too still to carry heat away, and a steel door with a number at eye height. Know which one you came through. Do not improvise a second exit. There isn’t one that a visitor should use.

**Light**

Bring your own, and a second that does not depend on the same battery. The work lamps are few, one of them already filmed with mud, and the caged bulb over the switchbox throws a pool no wider than a table. Your light will show you the drill holes, the snapped string, the chalk, the bedding in the claystone — and it will call midges. Hold it low when you can. Do not sweep the crown for sport; crickets scatter up the brick in a wave, and a few street bats use the darker bay by day. Their clicks are inside the louder noise. Let them be.

A small light is better than a flood. This room is short. You do not need to conquer it.

**What not to touch, move, or “tidy”**

The pumps, the generator, the gauges, the fuel, the hoist, the conduit gaps, the packed collars, the chalkboard with its date and level — these are a job in progress, numbered, meant to meet other work at known inverts under known streets. You are not the crew. Do not start anything that is stopped. Do not stop anything that is running. Do not coil the hose better than you found it. Do not chalk a correction. A gauge sitting at a number is waiting, by its own patience, to become the number that wakes the pump. When it does, the room shouts, the warm water drops by a hand’s width, and the flies lift and settle. Be off the pads and away from the discharge when that happens, or be gone.

The sump is half made: board on one side, concrete on the other, water the color of tea, a mineral smell with a little soap and iron and the faint sweetness of waste diluted past naming. It is not a pool. The film on the lip is algal and bacterial, slick, odorless until broken. Rats use every pipe mouth that does not yet have a grille, and the warm run where the packing is unfinished. They are not a flood. They are enough. Give the margins their width. The open middle looks sparse only if you are counting machines.

Do not eat down there, and do not open food you brought. Flies find an imperfect lid. A dropped crust becomes part of the calorie that the room is already oversubscribed on. Eat above, before or after.

**Animals**

They are the residents. You are the same kind of visitor the crew is, only less useful. Drain flies, midges, mosquitoes, cockroaches the color of the clay, cellar crickets, silverfish on the paper tags, beetles in the grease, rats, a few bats, pigeon stain high on the shaft rungs where the birds will not come all the way down. None of this is a portent. It is warmth, film, and a town that sheds calories downward.

Cover skin. Tuck trousers into boots if the roach-line bothers you; it will. Shake nothing out over the sump. Do not swat at the bat bay. Do not block a pipe mouth with your pack and then wonder what ran over your foot. If you are the sort of person who will freeze or flail in a moving margin of insects, visit short and stay near the door, or do not go. There is no green distance to retreat into. Almost nothing grows. One fern in a crack under the shaft is the whole forest. It does not make the room kinder.

A simple repellent on the outside of clothing helps against the mosquitoes and little else. It will not negotiate with cockroaches or rats. It will smell loud in a closed vault; use less than you would in open air. Check yourself at the door on the way out — cuffs, collar, pack seams — the way you would leave any cellar. This is not an exotic decontamination. It is not wanting to carry the room home.

**Air, water, skin**

The seep is warm and slow, threads on the face, a paler line where yesterday’s water stood. After rain above, the shaft is a chute and the ticking thickens; the sump climbs by a finger, by a hand, and holds until the pump takes it. Do not be in the gallery for the interesting version of that if you can read the weather at the grate. Do not drink the sump, the seep, or anything that has touched the hose. Mineral and town is not a blend you want in your mouth.

The smell is iron, soap, wet clay, hot machine, and a sweetness you should not try to identify more precisely. A cloth over the mouth is for flies and for the moment the generator is up, not because the air is a puzzle. If your chest goes tight, or your head starts to float in a way that is not just heat, leave. An unfinished vault with a running generator and standing warm water does not owe you a second opinion. Ordinary human rules: if the air feels wrong, the visit is over.

Cuts and scrapes should be covered before you go down. The film, the silt, and the grease are not clean. Take them seriously in the dull way: plaster, and don’t trail a hand along the wet course to feel the brick.

**How to move**

Enter, let your eyes give up on the daylight coin, and do not fill the silence with talking. There is no silence. Stand still once, near the bulkhead, and let the density declare itself at the edges: wall, waterline, pipe shadow, lamp heat. Then walk the length without hurrying. You can be done with it. That is the point. The face is claystone and a sandier band, holes still round, spoil not yet gone. Look at it. Don’t climb it. Don’t pull the snapped string. Don’t become a person the next shift has to account for.

Keep your pack small and on your body. There is nowhere “empty” to set it that isn’t a pad, a hose, a future invert, or a margin something already uses. Stay out of the hoist’s line even though the rail stops in midair. Stay off the generator’s grease ring. If the crew is down, you are in their room: their lamps, their cycle, their chalkboard. Ask, or don’t go in. A multiverse traveler is still a stranger on a works.

**What to leave above**

Anything that hates damp, anything electronic you would be sorry to film with mud, any food, any belief that you will read or write comfortably, any plan that depends on being alone with a quiet thought. Paper tags in this room belong to the job and already have silverfish. Your notes can wait at the door.

Also leave the impulse to interpret. The room obeys ordinary rules: water to the lowest hole, heat that cannot leave, animals on leakage, brick in courses, bolts in the pattern the drawing names. What is not yet built does not yet hold. A nasty, recognizable, unfinished cut under a street. If it feels strange, it is the coincidence of new stone, high warm water, and a town leaking life downward — not a place that will change its behavior for you.

**A workable kit**

Light clothing that covers. Boots with a tread. Thin gloves. Two lights. Drinking water and salt. A dry bag for the clothes you want to put back on. A cloth for your face. Repellent used sparingly on cloth, not poured on skin in a vault. Plasters. A hard hat if you have one. A watch, because your sense of time will melt before your water does. Nothing else you cannot stand to have glazed with silt and fly-speck.

Go down cool, dry, fed, and unhurried. Stay as long as you can still choose to leave. Come out while the next finger of water is only on its way, while the pump is still a number on a gauge and not a bell, and while you can still tell the difference between the room’s heat and your own. The street, when you reach it, will feel briefly like weather. That is the whole reward, and it is enough.
