The basin does not welcome you by changing. It continues. Prepare for a visit that will feel less like arrival and more like insertion into a finished equilibrium: cold that is a property rather than an event, noise that does not fade with distance, and emptiness that is only the space between presences too far apart to crowd one another.

An ordinary human body is not native to this. The water is cold the way unused metal is cold—conductive, patient, unwilling to warm around you. A standard wetsuit will fail within minutes of real immersion; you need a drysuit with reliable active heating, redundant power, and seals that tolerate abrasion, because the stone is young and still sharp. Fracture faces and pinnacles have not been rounded. Gloves, knee pads, and a tough outer shell matter more than elegance. Treat every edge as honest. What looks like it will cut will cut, and there is no soft sediment thick enough to forgive a mistake.

Breathing gas must account for two facts at once. The surface lies high, in thin air above older weather, so any time spent on a platform or in the glare before descent happens at altitude. Once you are under, depth pressure is ordinary water pressure measured from that high surface, not from some sea-level memory you carry. Plan mixes, decompression, and emergency gas for both the altitude start and whatever depth you actually reach. Daylight penetrates absurdly far in this clarity, so you may be tempted to go deeper than your plan simply because you can still see. Do not. The light is not a promise of safety.

The roar is the condition you will underestimate until you are inside it. Animal calls and pump notes travel without thicket or haze to soften them. A sound at your ear may be kilometers away; a nearby turbine may be only one tooth in a chord that never resolves. Ordinary earplugs are not enough for hours of it. Use a sealed helmet or hood system with serious passive attenuation plus active noise control, and route all communication through bone conduction or a filtered hardline. Expect your own bubbles, fins, and tools to join the roar and travel farther than you intend. If you need to signal, prefer lights and pre-agreed lamp patterns. Voice will be swallowed or mislocated.

Visibility is a gift and a trap. You will see across distances that feel like horizons, in true color, with no plankton veil. That clarity makes the absence sharper. Shoals appear as faint scars in the brightness and vanish when you blink. A large animal may pass once and leave the water emptier than before. Do not chase. Distances here are journeys, and the things that live on mineral film and cold chemistry are not a pantry. Bring every calorie, every liter of fresh water, and every repair kit you might need. The basin will not feed you, and the machines are not shelters unless you have a specific, known right to use one.

Those machines—the sparse platforms, wall housings, and steady turbines—are part of the equilibrium, not scenery. They hold the level and move mineral flour. Their intakes, cables, and casings are industrial facts. Give them the same respect you would give any remote worksite that has been running at one pitch for a long time: no dangling gear, no assumption that an opening is safe, no experiment with whether a pump will notice you. Their contribution to the roar gains a metallic regularity when you are close and dissolves back into the general pressure when you leave. It does not become quiet.

Navigation cannot lean on sound or on the hope of landmarks. Carry instruments that do not depend on the water’s acoustics—inertial, optical, or surface-referenced—and treat every “nearby” noise as unreliable. The surface itself is a pale, low-ridged glare with almost no organized wave. Surfacing is possible but exposes you to thin air, hard light, and wind that does not feel like weather so much as moving cold. If a platform is your entry and exit, confirm it before you descend. The basin does not grow new ones for visitors.

Psychologically, prepare for monotony that is also overload. Nothing here is waiting to bloom. Light brightens and dulls with the ordinary day; the water’s habits do not. You may feel both intensely alone and unable to find silence. That combination wears on attention and on sleep if you remain long enough to need either. Set short objectives. Surface or return to a known structure on a schedule you decided before the roar got into your judgment. Do not renegotiate the plan because the water looks empty and therefore easy.

What the visit is like, if you are equipped and you do not fight the place: a long, bright, cold drift through geometry that still looks quarried. Your light is unnecessary for much of the depth daylight already owns. Your heat stays inside the suit. Your hearing is narrowed to what the helmet allows, and even then the basin’s chord presses. Somewhere, far off, a pale body crosses and is gone. A grain of mineral flour ticks along a fresh face. A pump holds its note. You are high above older coasts and fully submerged at the same time, and the basin does not reconcile those facts for you. It simply continues, already complete, whether you are in it or not.

Go with margins—thermal, gas, hearing, calories, time—and with the expectation that the place will not perform novelty for you. The preparation that matters is the kind that lets an ordinary human remain ordinary inside a volume that has no interest in becoming gentler.
