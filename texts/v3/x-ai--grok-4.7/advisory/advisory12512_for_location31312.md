Pack for a manufactured climate, not a cave and not a city. The mountain is behind the walls; what you walk is its replacement, and it will treat you as another surface it has not yet worn down.

**Clothing and the body.** The cold lives in the materials more than the air. Steel sweats; handrails leave a film; breath shows and is taken by the ducts. Wear layers you can wet without losing, and a shell that beads rather than drinks—the weather is a fingernail of water, constant, not a storm. Gloves that still let you feel a rail, because a bare palm goes numb and slick at the same time. Boots with a sole that bites wet concrete and grated beds; the false marsh is a skin of water over metal, and it trembles when a pump kicks. No fabric that holds damp against the skin for hours. A spare pair of socks in a dry bag is worth more than an extra coat. Keep the hood available but not up by default—sound is how this place tells you it is occupied, and a hood dulls the pitch change you will feel in the teeth before you name it.

**Light, but not too much.** Traveled ways have sodium; pump halls have a colder white; between them only reflection works—a channel, a wet plate wall, a ladder repeating into nothing. Bring a small lamp with a warm beam and a second, colder one, and use them sparingly. A harsh beam kills the copper distance and turns every sheet of water into glare. Learn to walk by reflection. When a lamp fails far down the vale, the dark it leaves is only the size of its reach. Nothing natural is waiting behind the light. Do not fill that dark with panic or with a floodlight; mark your turn-back by the last reliable glow, not by how far curiosity wants to go.

**Water, but not the water here.** It is everywhere and never deep, and it is not yours. Condensate, sump-flow, iron bleed, a pale film in the slack that is neither moss nor quite algae. Carry all drinking water. Do not rinse a cut in a channel. Do not drink from a sump because the water looks clear and moves with patience. The ecology is a maintenance schedule and a leak. Treat any open skin as something the place will try to keep.

**Navigation.** This is a town that refused buildings and kept only infrastructure, scattered so emptiness is the common material. Joints vanish into the dim. Bays are wide. You can walk ten minutes and meet no one. Do not trust “downhill” as a way out; the channels are too straight, and when a pump lags the floor becomes a river with no banks, then remembers its job and withdraws, leaving concrete darker and the formwork teeth shining. Mark your path on the edges the water has not yet rounded—chalk, tape, a scratch you will recognize—because the drip-lines follow the rebar grid and will look designed even when they are only guided leaks. Note the pump cycle. Draw-down, slack, draw-down: that is the weather, and it changes the pitch of the channels. If you time a crossing of a low apron, cross on slack, not on the sheet. A minute of lag can put water across a path you just decided was dry.

**Hearing and machines.** The loudness covers what little life there is. Turbines inhale and exhale on a period longer than breath. Conveyors knock a metal sentence. A valve hunts, fails to seat, hunts again; the pipe beside you shivers with a held note. Assume every shiver is a machine doing its job, and still do not lean on a line that is hunting. Fresh welds still throw a faint heat you can feel with the back of a wrist—use the back of the wrist, not the palm, and do not rest a pack against a pipe that feels newly warm. Cooling stacks exhale a column of barely warmer air that beads and falls back as rain too fine to wet a coat, only to gloss it. Stand clear of the column if you need to stay dry enough to see.

**What not to do with the quiet.** Animals are rarer than the stain: a dry scrabble when the fans cycle down, a moth already gummed to a lamp, a chain of bubbles in a sump that stops when you look. Do not chase the scrabble into the duct forest. Do not follow bubbles. The strangeness is a mismatch of scales, not a monster waiting to be met. The place is in its first season—edges still crisp, paint unchalked, corners not yet felted black—and it is already running. Your job is to pass through a process, not to interrupt one.

**Pace and exit.** Stand on a gantry long enough for the noise to separate: draw-down, slack, a tower’s exhale, a conveyor answering another two cuts away. Then leave while you still know which lamp is yours. Distances open farther than the light deserves, and the haze of condensation closes them into a false horizon. Set a time, not a vista. When the western cut weeps in a rhythm so regular it feels intended, that is your cue that attention is starting to mistake the steel for a guide. Turn back toward the traveled sodium, the ways that were meant to be walked.

**What to carry, simply.** Dry bag. Water. Two lights. Gloves. Chalk or tape. A way to note the pump period so you do not argue with it. A layer you can sacrifice to the gloss-rain. Nothing that needs a fire, a signal to the mountain, or a belief that something older than the pour is about to show itself. The mountain is a rumor. What you visit is the rumor’s replacement: sharp, loud with the work of being kept, and indifferent in the way new infrastructure is indifferent—already forgetting, in the slow way metal forgets, that it was ever not here.

Go in as a careful guest of a system that does not know it has guests. Come out before the emptiness starts to feel like the common material of you as well.
