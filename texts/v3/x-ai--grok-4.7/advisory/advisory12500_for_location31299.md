Prepare as if you are going to live for a while inside a warm, unfinished mineral lake that has been lifted into the clouds, not as if you are visiting a swamp, a cave, or a mountain town. The strangeness is not danger in the usual sense. It is that almost every ordinary assumption about weather, ground, light, and time is slightly wrong, and the wrongness does not change while you are there.

### What it will feel like

You will be warm the whole time. Not jungle-hot, not spa-scalding: bathwater warm, and the air nearly as warm as the water, day and night. You will not get a cool hour to recover in. Sweat will not evaporate properly. Sleep will be damp. Your sense of “I have been here three days” will blur, because nothing in the sky or the temperature will mark a change.

You will also be dim. Daylight arrives already tired, tea-colored, and stops a short way off. You will see the next platform, the ladder, a neighbor’s pale wall, and then brown-green. At noon the deep water is only a darker idea. At night, unless someone has a fire or wet stone is giving back a dull sheen, you will not see your own feet. The horizon you came from — weather, distance, a sky you can read — is under the lid. Do not count on your mood surviving that without help. People who need a far view, a hard sun, or a cold night get quietly wrecked here before anything else goes wrong.

The water does not behave like water you know. It does not cut the town down. It crusts. Leave a strap, a boot, a healing scrape, a mooring line in it and a pale flinty skin starts to take, greenish, slow, mineral and living at once. That is the place’s true weather. Erosion has not begun. Making has not finished. The stone underfoot is new: sharp joints, bright faces, breaks that open a hand. Ridges can be a hand’s breadth higher in a lifetime, which will not throw you, but it means every “path” is provisional. A lane that was knee-deep for the person who told you about it may be a swim, or a dry spine, depending on which bulge last shouldered up.

It is crowded and almost silent. Platforms nearly touch. Voices carry farther than they should in air this still, so people speak low. A shout is an event. You will hear neighbors turn over, a pole set down, sometimes a slow knock as a ridge settles, and in the evening the long-house fire, which is the loudest ordinary thing in the bowl. Do not fill that quiet with your normal traveling voice. You will be inside other people’s houses more than you expect: their drying racks in your light, their stems under your walkway, their ladder in the only gap.

Animals will not orient you. Few fish, a few masonry-shelled snails on the rim, almost no birds, a thin insect life, no night chorus. If you are used to reading a wet country by what bites, calls, or flees, you will feel falsely safe and truly alone. The real hazards are unseen bottom, sharp unweathered stone, mineral water in eyes and lungs, getting lost in twenty meters of brown, and the social fact that new stone is claimed by whoever touches it first at dawn.

### Before you go

Learn to swim, including in water you cannot see through, including while holding one thing you must not drop. You will not live in the deep — no one does — but the lanes run from knee-deep to chest-deep to a swimming-cut without a sign. A person who can only wade will be a burden and, at dusk, a casualty. Practice moving slowly. Thrashing here buys you nothing but noise and a mouthful of stone-meal.

Learn to pole a small craft in a straight line and to stop it against a post without shouting. Learn to climb a wet ladder with a small pack one-handed. There are no wheels, no long dry roads, and very little metal. If your kit cannot go up a ladder, down a stilt-leg, and onto a raft, it does not come.

Spend time in low light before you need it. Not darkness training as a sport — just cooking, dressing, and finding a doorway by touch and a very small lamp, so the first night does not become panic. If you are the sort of person who spirals when the sky disappears, plan a short stay and a fixed return, and do not negotiate that with yourself once you are under the lid.

Treat the water as not for drinking until a local shows you their method, and maybe not even then. It is warm, thick with fine mineral meal, and it deposits rather than rots. Boiling may not make it kind to a foreign gut; it may just make hot stone-milk. Bring a way to carry water you already trust, and a way to catch what drips from roofs and steam-rims if the locals do that. Ask before you invent a catchment on someone’s ridge. New surfaces are political.

Bring calories that can survive damp and a slow crust. The local food is rind, stems, leaf, rarely a pale fish. It will feed the people who grew up on it. It may not feed you in the first week, and there is no market street of preserved imports. Dense, sealed, boring food is worth more than anything that needs to be kept dry by weather. There is no weather.

### What to wear and carry

Dress light and accept that you will not be dry. A spare dry layer is for sleep and for the kiln-shelf, not for staying dry through a day. Avoid heavy cotton that holds water and chill you do not have; the problem is weight, smell, and skin staying wet, not cold. Cover your feet. The romantic barefoot approach ends on a joint still sharp enough to open a hand, or on crust that abrades like fine grit. Something that stays on when you swim, drains, and does not fold under your sole on a ladder.

Gloves you can work in. You will touch posts, fiber, wet blocks, and the rind. The rind smells, when broken, like wet flint and crushed stem together. That smell will get into your hands and your food. A light wrap for your head and neck matters more than a coat: drip from the canopy, mineral film on your face, and the dim habit of mistaking every pale thing for a person.

A small, redundant light is not optional. One you can lose, one you can keep dry, and a way to make a little warmth-light that does not depend on begging the long house. Do not bring a beacon. A shout of light is as rude as a shout of voice, and it will not pierce the deep mere anyway. You need to see the next rung, the next post, the lip of a platform. Red or low and close is better than white and far. Practice wearing it so it does not crust into its own switch — rinse it, wipe it, do not leave it soaking.

Ears and eyes need more thought than lungs, at first. The air is thick but breathable in the ordinary human sense; you are not under a poison sky. The meal in the water will inflame eyes, settle in ears, and turn a careless duck-dive into a day of not trusting your balance. Bring rinses for eyes and ears that are not the mere itself. If you must go under, go under where the lane is known, feet first, and not for wonder.

A knife, cord, a few hooks if you have them — metal is rare and accidental here, nodules hammered cold, so a simple blade is wealth and also a temptation. Do not flash it. A pole you can borrow is better than a pole you insist on owning. Fiber and reed are the local technology; if you can splice wet cord and tie off a platform so it does not bang, you will be more welcome than if you arrive with gadgets.

Leave behind: wheels, rigid trunks, heavy boots that drown you, clothes that only work when washed in clean water and dried in sun, paper maps you expect to keep, anything that must stay pristine to function. Ink runs. Seals fail slowly. Leather becomes a garden for crust. If a tool dies when it grows a thin stone skin, it is the wrong tool.

### How to enter

Do not cross the deep because the far cliff looks interesting. The far shore is stone still being born — planes and joints like tool-marks, and at its foot the mere goes dark. Light dies in the meal. A descent is the same brown room all the way down, and no one has a bottom to report. The town lives on the near shelves and the island-ridges: the landing, the rind-gardens, the stilt quarter, the upper walk, the kiln-shelf on the rim. Stay inside a long day’s poling of those, with someone who knows which cuts are still open. “Open” is maintained by hand. A lane closes if people stop keeping it.

Arrive at the landing, not at a ridge. Pay attention to stakes. The oldest arguments are about mornings: who saw new stone first, whose ladder touched it while it was still weeping. A traveler who plants a foot on a fresh spine to “get a view” can start a quarrel that outlasts their visit. If the water is crusting a post, that post is already in a relationship with someone. Do not scrape it clean as a favor. The crust is how things here become solid.

Speak low from the first sentence. Ask where to put your weight. Platforms are close; a wrong step is a step into a garden of stems or onto a sleeping mat that has never quite dried. Accept that you will be overheard. Accept that you will not be entertained. This is a town, with districts and a long house, not a camp waiting for strangers. Its history is short — nothing much older than great-grandparents, much of it younger than the people using it — so you are not entering an ancient rite so much as a busy, unfinished household that has already had to invent property law for ground that did not exist last season.

Watch how they move. They rebuild upward. A lower lane becomes a canal without ceremony. Doors sit wrong against sills because the ridge shrugged. If someone relocates a ladder, follow the new ladder. Your memory of yesterday’s route is already a rumor.

### Body, over days

Expect your skin to change its mind. Constant warmth, never-dry cloth, and a water that wants to plate things will give you itch, splits at the corners of joints, and a film you cannot scrub off in the way you scrub off mud. Rinse when you can with whatever the household uses. Do not pick crust off a healing cut as if it were dirt; ask. Their bodies have had decades of this. Yours has had a morning.

Eat before you are hungry in the dim, because appetite gets shy when the day never brightens. Salt, if you have a sealed store, will matter. So will something plain and dry-mouthed to remind you of food that is not stem and mineral. Fish, if offered, is a gift of rarity, not a staple. Take a little.

Sleep off the water if you can — upper walk, a loft, a mat that is only damp rather than wet. A hammock that does not shed grit into a neighbor’s rack is better than a romantic bed on a platform lip. Night is nearly total. You will wake unsure whether you have slept an hour. Keep a small light where your hand already knows it is, and do not go walking the lanes because you cannot sleep. The person you bump into cannot see you either, and the bottom you step toward may have fallen away into stilts.

The rim is the place you go if the sameness starts to press. Steam loosens where the cap thins. It is still damp, still warm, but it is the nearest thing to an edge. The kiln-shelf is where they can briefly get a fire hot enough to set clay before the mist takes it back. Do not treat fire as yours. It is the loud, dry argument against a world that wants to be damp and green and mineral. If you are allowed near it, be quiet and useful.

### What not to do with your curiosity

Do not dive for the bottom, do not cross to the unnamed water, do not scrape a ridge to “see the rock,” do not shout to test the echo, do not light the canopy to take a picture of the lid. You will learn more in one evening of watching a block walked up a new spine — slow impacts, then the suck of warm water closing over the work — than in any expedition into the middle.

The plants are the true populace. A reed-tree stands crusted in, drawing on stone-milk. Pads will tile a sheltered lane until someone keeps it open. On new ridges a green rind takes before anyone builds, and the gardens are slash-and-wait: cut, eat what comes, trust the ridge to brew another. They do not plow. There is nothing yet to turn. If you help, help in that grammar. Do not drain, do not straighten, do not introduce a seed from another world because the soil “seems to be missing.” Soil has not accumulated. The bowl is not behind. It is early.

### What to hold in mind

You can be comfortable here, in the narrow sense: no cold, no storm, no long hunger if the rind is cropping, no insects in any familiar plague, neighbors near enough to hear breathing. The discomfort is perceptual and social. You will not see far. You will not dry. You will not be alone, and you will not be invited to be loud. The ground is still arriving. The water has decided to stay. The rind is learning, very slowly, how to be a ground.

Go small, go slow, keep your light close, keep your voice lower than you think is polite, and let a local hand decide which stone is a path and which stone is still a morning. If you can leave when you meant to leave — before the dim starts to feel like the only kind of day there is — you will have visited well.
