Pack for a place that is loud, cool, unfinished, and only intermittently obedient to the ground under it. You will not need heroics. You will need patience, redundant ways of knowing where you are, and the humility to follow a three-sentence notice you do not understand.

**What the visit actually feels like.** An ordinary day is dim from the start. Noon is a paler dusk. The air tastes of mineral and hot oil even when you are nowhere near a boiler. Your clothes never quite dry. Conversation happens close to the mouth because the city is in a permanent industrial argument with itself—whistles that hold one note until it becomes architecture, presses that thud in your sternum from a grove that looks quiet. Distances lie. A walk you timed yesterday may take half as long or twice as long, and your boots will look the same either way. You will be damp, slightly late, and mildly embarrassed at least once. That is the normal cost of admission, not a disaster.

**Clothing.** Dress for cool iron weather and condensation, not for rain as you know it. Layers that still work when wet: wool or a close modern equivalent against the skin, a wind shell that does not turn stiff when beaded, spare socks in a dry bag you do not open except to change them. The damp beads on cable, wool, and lips of hoppers; it stands in joints of young rock in thin metallic sheets. Frost forms on surfaces that were pouring yesterday. Breath smokes. Steel sweats. A brim helps with the fine cold fall that wets only upper surfaces. Gloves you can work in—warm enough for the crest, thin enough to feel a curb’s give. The pavement is warm in a way the air is not; curbs have a skin, like cooling fat. Bare hands on “fresh” stone are a mistake. Boots with a real sole and ankle support: young rock is granular and faintly yielding, talus gathers and is drawn back overnight, and bootprints sometimes end in smooth ground. Bright, dull colors both fail in this light; choose contrast you can see at arm’s length—pale cuffs, a pale hood lining—so a crew can find you against clinker and iron sky. Leave anything that must stay pristine. Leave dress shoes. Leave the assumption that “waterproof” means “comfortable after six hours of mist that rises and returns.”

**Body and ears.** The noise is patterned, not chaotic, and it is loud enough to fatigue you. Soft ear protection you can wear for a whole shift, plus a way to hear a person at one pace’s distance when you lift one cup. If you already have tinnitus or a bad knee, this is a poor place to test them. The damp sits in joints and in wool. Eat on a schedule you bring with you; shift bells here run along the grain of the air and can arrive before the street below them has heard them. You will mis-time a meal if you wait to feel hungry “at the right bell.” Water is everywhere as condensation and almost nowhere as a trustworthy cup. Carry your own, and do not drink the metallic sheets in the joints or the short rivers born already cold between unfinished piers. They look clean. They are the mountain thinking about weather. The small life in the steam is not hostile, but do not inhale a boiler’s exhale for sport; the crews call certain moths “draft” and have charts. You are not on those charts.

**Feet, edges, weight.** Trust a gear. Do not trust an edge. Men here do not set a crane’s outriggers on lips that overhang ground the mountain has not supplied. You should not set your weight there either, even when a pale grazer is standing on the same lip chewing, apparently fine. Weight is locally optional in those places. Usually the lip becomes street. Sometimes it becomes a curtain of gravel that hangs for a day and a night, ticking, before the ridge draws it back. You are not an animal that has learned something better than caution. Stay off catwalks beaded with the mucus that melts frost and smells faintly of ozone—the condenser sheds have tenants. Do not lie down to look past the far platform into the darkness that is not yet a valley unless a crew is expecting you to be there. The pressure on the ears is not wind. People who need to see it can see it from a hut that already has a floor.

**Navigation.** Bring a paper notebook, a soft pencil, a cheap compass you do not worship, and a timepiece that is only a timepiece. Maps worsen if you look at them kindly. A street can have a gutter, a stenciled plate, and carts, and still meet a thickness of air that has the resistance of a wall. Survey pins walk. A benchmark set at dusk can be a hand’s width higher by the next week. Do not “correct” the city’s map as a favor. Copy what you are told, note the date and the bell, and expect the copy to be a rumor by the time you need it.

Learn the posted gatehouse procedure before you need it. It is three sentences. It does not explain anything. It works often enough that no one has rewritten it. If you walk into air that should have been terrace and later find yourself on a lower switchback, damp, an hour missing, smelling of hot metal you cannot account for: you are not dying. You are in the procedure. Do not improvise a better one. Do not go back in to retrieve the hour. Report yourself as the notice says, then eat, change socks, and accept the lateness.

Sound is a better pilot than sight and a worse one than the crews. A bell on the eastern terrace can arrive at the western stacks before it arrives at the street under the bell. Workers trust a noise that comes early more than they trust a map. You should treat early sound as information, not as a command to hurry toward it. Mark your route by machines, not by streets: the foundry whose hearth has been banked longer than the ridge has existed, a particular press hall’s sternum-thud, a stack between firings (birds nest there and leave a day before the schedule says it will draw—if the birds are gone, do not treat the mouth as a viewpoint). Groves are real and narrow, often no wider than a house, and they go green in the dimness. They are not exits. They are seams. You can still feel the foundry in your chest while your hand is on wet bark.

If you must move after the second shift bell, assume distances along the crest have become negotiable. Morning is the honest walk. Afternoon is a negotiation you did not ask to enter.

**Animals.** They are using the city as range, weather, and sometimes tool. They are not a show and not a threat you manage by being interesting. Long-necked grazers, almost the color of the new stone, keep a walk that matches the ridge’s rise; from the mills they can look stationary for hours. Do not cross a herd to save twenty minutes. Do not stand between them and a cable tower they have decided is the next weather. Stack birds will abandon a nest without panic; that is a schedule, not an omen for you to interpret. Insects in the steam persist at heights that should bother them. Let them. The animal that sleeps where a dog would, at the far lighted hut, answers to a name and is sometimes shaped more like the grazers than like a dog, depending on the dimness. Do not call it, do not feed it, do not decide what it “really” is. The crew already has a working relationship with it.

**The city as a workplace.** This is a real city: districts, drainage law, shift bells, mills with blistered names, lifting engines, power in the cables as a low note under everything. Production is slow on purpose or by necessity. A pour that begins on a Monday may still be finding its level on the Friday after next. Your urgency is noise to them. Do not stand in a pour. Do not touch a press “to see if it is moving.” It is moving. You are not. Do not offer to help a crew receive new stone at the unfinished end unless they hand you a timber and a place to stand. They have been waiting with last season’s cuts for ground that surfaces like a shoulder in water—dark, then pale, then load-bearing. They do not celebrate. Neither should you.

Speech: short, close, specific. Point at the machine, not at the horizon. Ask which bell they mean, not what time it is. If a shadow falls toward the brighter part of the sky and then corrects itself with a sound like a sheet shaken out, do not flinch into a walkway. Wait. Plumb is a courtesy. Interior doors stick, swing, and stick again because the frame is arguing with the mountain, not because the wood swelled. A stair may have inserted a step so gradually that only a stranger counts and comes up wrong. Count anyway, then believe the landing, not your count, if they disagree. A building may have gained a storey no one added. Sleep where you are assigned. Do not bed down against a boiler wall because the animals do; their arrangement with the heat is older than your visit, even if the ridge is not.

**Light and night.** Night changes little except the quality of the dimness and the whistle schedule. A lamp can be a small, localized correctness—the ridge agreeing, for the length of one gutter, to hold. Do not follow a stranger’s lamp down a street that was not on this morning’s map hoping the correctness will include you. Bring a light with a warm beam and a cold spare, both humble. The sky will not help you. Upward rain—condensation lifting off hot young rock, returning hours later as a fine cold fall—makes mills look as if they are exhaling weather. In those hours, upper surfaces lie about how wet the world is. Check the underside of a rail before you trust your grip.

**What to carry, concretely.** Dry socks, three pairs. A layer you can sleep in. Ear protection. A notebook and pencil. Your own water and plain food that does not require a schedule the bells can steal. A light. Gloves. A strip of cloth to breathe through if a particular shed’s air is too much ozone and oil; step out, do not tough it out. A few coins or whatever the gatehouse actually takes—ask at the gate, do not assume the last traveler’s currency still matches a city that inserts streets. A waxed pouch for any paper the city gives you; condensation will take it otherwise. Leave drones, leave “mapping” toys, leave anything that must have a stable horizon or a stable hour to function. Leave the desire to prove the anomaly. The anomaly has a procedure. You are a visitor inside it.

**What to practice before you go.** Walking while someone talks an arm’s length from your mouth, in noise, without turning your head away from your feet. Standing still when a surface feels almost-solid and waiting for a local to move first. Changing socks in the dark without setting a bag down on a lip. Reading a short notice twice and doing only what it says. Being an hour late without narrating the metaphysics of the hour to the person whose shift you have interrupted.

**How to leave.** The same way you arrived, in the morning, by a route a crew will still recognize, before the second bell makes the crest negotiable. Do not exit by the thickness of air as an experiment in efficiency. Do not take a survey pin, a blistered name-plate, a chart off a boiler, or a stone that was pouring yesterday. The ridge reclaims talus overnight; it does not need your help, and the city employs its geologists the way a body employs a fever—already, and not as a souvenir trade. If you smell of hot metal and cannot account for it, you have already been in the procedure. Wash, write down what you remember before the memory smooths the way the ground smooths bootprints, and do not “correct” the account into something that makes better sense. The place is large enough to have drainage law. It is also a mountain that has not yet agreed to be a mountain. You prepare by agreeing, for the length of your visit, to be slower than your habits and smaller than the open reach between the stacks.
