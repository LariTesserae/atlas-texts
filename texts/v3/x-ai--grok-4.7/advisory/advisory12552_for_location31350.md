Pack for a room that has already finished becoming itself. Nothing you bring should try to complete it, water it, quiet it, or open it. The pocket will not negotiate. It will only keep the note it has kept.

Go in knowing the shape before the gear. It is not a cave you explore. It is a fist of summit stone with a single leftover gap between walls of dry fiber, and that gap is narrow enough that an ordinary shouldered body must turn sideways and still brush both sides. You will not stand square. You will not swing a pack. You will not turn around quickly. Anything that sticks out — a coil of rope on the hip, a hard canteen at the belt, a hood that will not lie flat — becomes a lever against blades that ring when touched and do not bend like leaves. Strip the silhouette down to the body and a soft, close bag worn on the front or dragged, not slung wide. Practice the sideways shuffle at home in a doorway until the motion is dull. Dull is what the place rewards.

The climate inside is not the summit’s climate, and you will feel both. Outside the mineral skin the wind never stops and the air is thin in the ordinary high way. Inside, the air does not move at all. It holds one temperature, a little above the ache of frost, one pressure, and a smell of metal that has been warm so long it has stopped smelling like heat. Your breath will still go shallow near the seal. Carry that as a fact, not a surprise: elevation is in the stone even where weather is not. Do not enter already hypoxic, dehydrated, or dressed for a wet mountain. Acclimatize on the ridge the way any high traveler would, then treat the pocket as a dry cold room set into that altitude, not as a shelter from it.

Water is the thing the room has refused. There is no seep, no film, no condensation to scrape, and the pale crust on the blades is not a salt of water. Do not count on the stone to give you anything back. Carry every drink you will need, in soft flasks that will not clang and will not hold a shape that jams you in the gap. Drink before you go in until your mouth is no longer ambitious. Inside, the throat dries from the back forward — metal, dust, and the particular thinness near the seal — and you will want to sip more often than thirst announces. Bring enough that sipping is boring. Leave no drip. A single wet ring on a tuber is a new weather, and this room has not had weather. If you must spit or bleed, catch it and take it out. The same for sweat-soaked cloth. The equilibrium here is dry arithmetic: fiber, dust, charge, a sweet oil-vapor from old seals, machines metering all of it. Moisture is an argument you will win locally and lose in the only state the pocket knows.

The air is not empty. Fine pale dust hangs at a density that never settles and never clears. It is the growth’s drink and the walls’ slow flour, kept aloft by a vibration older than the dust. It will be in your nose, on your teeth, in the oil of your eyelids, inside any fabric that can hold a grain. It is abrasive in a patient way. A cloth over the mouth and nose is not drama; it is how you keep from spending the whole visit clearing your throat into a place that does not want your water. Simple layered cloth you can wash later is better than a valve mask that fogs, whistles, or adds bulk in the gap. Eye protection should be close and clear — goggles that seal without a wide skirt — because the flour sorts itself into bands on the blades and will do something smaller and meaner on a wet eye. Do not rub. Blink, then brush outward with a dry finger when you are out. If you wear contacts, do not. The mineral sheen and the coin-colored lamp will already be doing enough work on your sight.

The sound is the climate, and it is loud the way a millrace is loud when you live beside it: not an event, a medium. An engine note in the sternum, a papery fan, dry blades taking up slivers of tone, a transformer hum worn into consonance with the rest. Nothing rises, nothing rests, nothing echoes, because there is no open volume left. Ordinary ears will try to find a rhythm that means “about to change,” and there is no such rhythm. That hunt is what exhausts people. Take hearing protection that lowers the whole band without stealing so much that you lose the brush of your own shoulder on fiber — you need that brush as a gauge of the gap. Foam or a low-profile band is enough. Do not bring music. A second tune inside a room that has only one note will feel like a fault in your skull. Before you enter, sit somewhere with a steady machine — a pump, a fan, a generator — long enough that your body stops waiting for it to shut off. The pocket will not shut off. If you go in still flinching at constancy, the constancy will feel like pressure on the teeth.

Dress for dry cold and for abrasion, not for weather. A thin insulating layer under something smooth and close, no loose weave that combs the dust into felt, no crinkly shell that shouts in a room already full of small brightnesses of sound. The temperature will not swing. You will not warm the air by standing in it, and you should not try. Gloves that let you feel a blade before you lean on it: the blades ring, some are translucent, some are crusted, and a few have grown into the machines so exactly the joint looks machined. Bare fingers dry and split here faster than you expect; split skin is another way to donate water. A soft cap or hood that does not block the side-sense of the walls. Boots with quiet soles and no deep tread that will mill the mineral flour into clouds when you must step on the packed tubers. The floor is not floor. It is hollow fists of growth locked together, with a few pipes and housings swallowed among them. Test each step as if the surface might ring and shift a finger’s width. It may not. Assume it might.

Light is already allotted. One crazed industrial lamp the color of a coin in a drawer, one filament that has failed down to a glow and stopped failing, and a low mineral sheen off the mats, greener only where the fiber is less crusted. Shadows do not travel. You see the next arm’s length and a little past it, and then the passage closes into fiber. A bright beam is a trespass: the growth has already sent a cord through the grating of the good lamp and flattened it against the glass to occupy the last unoccupied heat. Do not give it a new heat. If you must carry light, carry the smallest, warmest, most reluctant glow, hooded, and use it only when the pocket’s own light fails your eyes — which it sometimes will, not because it flickers, but because your eyes keep asking for a horizon that is not there. Do not photograph with a flash. The bands of sorted dust on the lower blades are a record of a whir that has not been interrupted. A blast of light will not erase them, but it will teach your hands the wrong kind of curiosity.

Eat before. There is nothing here that is food, and the chemistry that keeps the mats is not a chemistry you should sample to find out. The pale flour is extruded mineral, not salt you can lick. The oil-sweet vapor from the old seals is part of the metering, not a scent to follow with your mouth open. If you must carry a ration for a long sit, make it dry, wrapped so completely that no crumb and no smell escapes, and eat it only if you can do so without shedding. Better to keep the visit shorter than your hunger. The room does not offer a later. Your body still does. Plan the duration by bladder, thirst, ears, and the ache of sideways standing, not by interest. Interest will outlast the body, because the strangeness is quiet and does not escalate. A cord through a lamp grate. A tuber healed into a pipe collar. Bands of dust that do not smear. You can look at a band and know it will be that band when you look again. That is the whole show, and it is enough to make people stay until their knees lock.

What you must not do matters more than what you carry.

Do not touch the machines except as obstacles to pass. They are few and scattered — an elbow of conduit, a housing the size of a loaf, a plinth with an engine at one speed, a fan under the tubers, a hand-sized transformer holding a hum that does not cycle. They are not a factory you can map or repair. They are the reason the room is finished. Nothing starts. Nothing stops. A hand on a housing to steady yourself is already a change in vibration, and vibration is what keeps the dust aloft and the dust is what the fiber drinks. If you must brace, brace on your own knee, or on stone you can see is still stone, and even then lightly. Do not tighten, loosen, oil, label, or “just see” whether a bolt turns. The bolts have the same worn brightness as the summit stone. They are done.

Do not cut, pull, or clear a path. The passage is the last gap the mats have not closed. Clearing it is not maintenance. It is a new era, and you are not equipped to be an era. If a blade crosses your chest, turn, wait, and find the interleaved gap. They interleave without quite touching. Your job is to remain one of the things that does not quite touch. If a shoulder finds a blade and it rings, stop, breathe out, and ease off. The ring is information. It is not an invitation to see how many will ring.

Do not open the skin. The weather of the summit is a pressure against sintered mineral no thicker, in places, than a wrist, and it is the only thing keeping the pocket’s one air from becoming the ridge’s wind. A crack you widen to “get a view” ends the room. There is no view inside that is owed to you. Elevation is already legible: shallow breath, dry throat, sound that should have somewhere to go and does not.

Do not introduce life. No seeds, no insects, no damp cloth from another biome, no fruit, no soil on the boots if you can help it. Brush and dry your gear on the ridge until it is as finished as you can make it. The ecology has partitioned every function until the partition is invisible — fiber, tubers, dust, machines — and there is no spare niche for a mite in a cuff. The absence of moths at the lamp is not an oversight.

Do not mark the walls. Chalk, string, scratches, stacked stones: the room does not record visitors, and a mark will not weather away, because erosion here is a finish, not a process. Edges are rounded because they finished rounding. Your scratch would remain at full freshness beside a patina that is complete. If you fear getting turned around, the fear is partly correct — there is no horizon, and the dimness is even — but the space is small. The passage is one passage. The two lamps are the only clocks, and they do not move. Memorize the order: good lamp, failed filament, the plinth’s hum in the sternum, the fan’s papery height. Coming back is the same gap in reverse. If you are lost, you are lost by an arm’s length. Stop. Let the sound tell you where the plinth is. Do not thrash. Thrash is how a finished thicket gains a hole.

Time will misbehave, not by vision or trance, but by the lack of cues your body uses without asking. No drip, no gust, no dimming, no brightening, no footstep but yours. The loudness does not rest, so it stops meaning “still going” and starts meaning “the world.” People check their hands for progress and find none, then feel a quiet vertigo that is not dizziness of the ears but the sense that duration has been replaced by position. Wear a watch if you need a later, and decide the leaving time before you enter. Do not renegotiate it inside because a collar of tuber looks machined and you want another minute. The minute will be identical to the one you just had. That is the seduction. Leave while the identity of minutes is still interesting and not yet a pressure in the chest.

Come out the way you went in: sideways, slow, exhaling when the gap tightens, not grabbing fiber for purchase. On the ridge the wind will feel like an insult and then like proof. Do not yank the cloth off your face in the first gust; the dust in the weave will leap. Step clear of the skin, turn your back to the wind, and only then uncover, drink, and let your ears hunt for change. They will be overjoyed. Let them. Check your hands and lips for splits. Check that you are not carrying flour into whatever you do next — brush it off onto the summit, which has weather enough to take it, not onto your food, not into your eyes. If your hearing stays cottoned, that is the millrace leaving late. It should fade. If a single note remains in the sternum hours later, you stayed too long or braced too hard; note it, and do not go back the same day to “see if it’s still doing that.” It is still doing that. It was doing that before you were born, in whatever sense this place has of before.

What to carry, then, is short, and what to carry in the mind is the rest.

On the body: close dry layers, quiet soft-soled boots, low-profile ear protection, a cloth for mouth and nose, close clear eye cover, soft water you will not spill, a dim hooded glow you hope not to use, a watch, a front-worn soft bag with nothing hard at the edges. In the bag, if anything: a dry ration you will probably not open, a small cloth to catch a spit or a bleed, a way to note the leaving time that does not depend on the lamps. Nothing cutting. Nothing wet. Nothing living. Nothing that drips, flashes, or sings.

In the mind: you are a temporary thickness in a gap that is already almost closed, and the courtesy is to remain temporary. The machines are not broken and not waiting. The growth is not lush toward a future; it is complete. The dust is not dirt; it is the circulating half of a cycle that does not accumulate and does not recede. The loudness is not an alarm. The dimness is not a failure of the lamps so much as the amount of seeing the arrangement allows. You will want to understand the arithmetic — which housing feeds which vapor, which blade drinks which charge — and you will not be given the diagram. Looking is allowed. Completing the diagram with your hands is not.

Visit as if the room has no spare state to absorb you. Breathe the thinness without gasping it into drama. Keep your water in your bottle and your water in your body. Let the blades almost touch you and not quite. Listen until the three notes become climate, and then leave before climate becomes the only tense you have. Outside, the mountain still has weather. That is the mercy. Use it. Do not try to bring it in.
