An ordinary human body is the wrong instrument for this place, and it will keep telling you so. Prepare as if you were going to stand inside a finished machine that does not notice you: thin air, hard light, permanent cold, a wind that is not weather but a structural property of the ground, and a sound that does not have an off switch. Nothing there will adapt to you. You adapt, or you leave.

**What the visit actually is**

The first thing is not the view. The first thing is pressure in the ears and a roar that sits in the chest, pitched just above the range where a shout can answer it, grainy with sand traveling an inch off the pavement. It does not gust. It does not lull at dusk. Dark is only a change in the light. If you have never stood in continuous industrial noise for many hours, you do not yet know what “never rests” means in the body: the jaw sets, the shoulders rise, speech becomes a waste, and after a while you stop forming sentences even in your head because the chord occupies the band where inner speech lives.

The second thing is the light. At this height the air has given up most of its thickness, so the sun is a hard disc and the pale stone throws it back without warmth. Shadows are short, blue-black, and sharp. You will burn in places you did not know were exposed — the rims of the nostrils, the inner wrists, the part of the scalp where a hat lifts. You will also freeze. The cold is the temperature the rock has agreed to keep. Breath shows and is taken. Sun on the face and ice in the lungs is the ordinary condition, not a paradox you can wait out.

The third thing is emptiness that is not empty. You can see half a day’s walk and count the features on one hand: a comb of wind-bored stone, a tarn the color of ink, a darker seep-line, the far rim. Nothing stands up. Nothing has been stood up. The only fabrication is small armored animals wedging wind-flakes to saw at cushion-plants, and even that builds nothing. You will want a destination. The plateau does not have one. Walking until the light goes iron-colored only delivers another pavement and the same note.

Water is a memory, not a supply. North faces hold a film. Joints hold a thumb-wide seep at the speed of a pulse. A few hollows hold tarns rimmed with white crust. Rain, if you see it, beads and vanishes into the joints before you can catch it. Do not count on drinking what you find.

**Air**

This is the constraint that ends the trip if you get it wrong. Ordinary lungs will not perform here the way they do at home. Expect headache, nausea, a stupid cheerfulness that is actually hypoxia, sleep that does not restore, and a climb in heart rate from simply standing up. Do not arrive and start walking. Spend at least two full cycles of light and dark at the lowest point you can still call “on the plateau,” doing almost nothing, before you push toward the interior pavements. Carry a small pulse oximeter and believe it when you feel fine and it does not. A pocket supply of emergency oxygen — not enough to live on, enough to walk down — is the difference between a bad afternoon and not walking out. If you have ever had altitude sickness, a heart condition, or anemia, this location is a refusal, not a challenge. Diamox or whatever your own physician already knows you tolerate can be discussed before you leave; do not improvise new drugs on the stone.

Move slowly enough to feel foolish. The pavement invites a long stride. The air does not.

**Sound**

The roar will damage unprotected hearing, and it will also make you useless. Good electronic ear protection with a high noise-reduction rating, worn from the first step onto the table, is not optional comfort. Bring a backup pair; sand gets into hinges. Over that, a thin wind hood so the muffs are not themselves whistles. You will not hold a conversation. Agree every signal before you separate by more than a body-length: one raised arm for stop, two for turn back, a strobe or a mirrored flash for “I am here” because a shout dies inside the chord. A wrist tether between two people is not dramatic. It is how you avoid losing each other on a surface where the eye sees forever and the ear hears only stone.

If you need speech, use a hardwired or line-of-sight text device, not a radio you have not already tested in heavy broadband noise. Expect radios to be annoying and sometimes useless. Learn to rest your nervous system on purpose: sit in the lee of a swell, face away from the nearest bored throat, close your eyes, and let the note be weather rather than an argument. People who fight it for six hours arrive shattered and make bad decisions about water and the rim.

**Light, cold, and the wind’s teeth**

Dress for high alpine winter and equatorial noon at once. Layers that block wind completely matter more than insulation you can see: a shell with no flutter, cuffs that seal, a hood that stays up. Under it, enough loft that you can sit still on stone that has never been warm. The ground will steal heat through any thin pad; bring a closed-cell sit pad and use it every time you stop, including “just for a minute.”

For the sun: a brim that shades the face and the back of the neck, wraparound eyewear that seals at the sides, and a high-altitude UV block on every exposed millimeter, reapplied whenever you think it is still there. The stone glare will snow-blind you on a cloudless day with no snow. A light-colored scarf you can breathe through cuts both the burn and the fine grit. Lips crack and do not forgive; treat them before they split.

The wind carries the finest sand an inch above the pavement, continuously. Gaiters, over-trousers, and gloves you can work in are how you keep that sand out of skin and out of zippers. Bare hands on the fluted stone will be cut. The wind-bored edges shed flakes sharp enough to open a palm, and the small animals leave those flakes wedged in cracks where you will brace yourself. Leather palms, not fashion gloves. Do not kneel casually. Do not run a hand along a “smooth” flute to feel the polish; the polish is how the edge hides.

A footing note: the pavement looks trustworthy and is, until a film of damp on a north face or a crust at a tarn rim turns it to glass. Approach water from the dry side. The scarps at the rim have already fallen as far as they will fall; the talus is locked, not a scree you surf. The danger is the lip in a crosswind, not the stones below. Stay back from the edge by more than a stumble. There is no reason to look straight down.

**Water, food, and the fact that nothing grows for you**

Carry every liter. The film on the north faces is not a source. The seeps are too small and too mineral to gamble on. The tarns are ink-colored and crusted; treat anything you are desperate enough to take as contaminated and salty until proven otherwise — filter and chemical or UV both, and taste a drop before you commit, because a mineral load can make you thirstier. In practice, plan to drink only what you carried. In this wind and this dry cold you will need more than a hot day’s habit suggests, even if you do not feel sweaty. Urine darkening is a late sign. Drink on a schedule, not on thirst.

Eat food that does not require cooking. There is no wood. A stove is weight you may want for morale and for melting nothing, because there is nothing to melt; if you bring one, bring the fuel math honestly and use it to make a hot drink in the lee, which will matter more than the calories. Fat and salt travel better here than a romantic meal. The thin plants are not forage. The birds are not game. Leave the ticking colonies and the hanging fliers alone; they have arranged themselves inside the standing wave, and your hunger is not a local fact.

Cache logic, if you go far: mark water and a shelter-less camp with something the wind cannot plane away in a day — a buried screw, a cairn of stones you stack and then photograph against a specific flute, a GPS waypoint plus a visual. The wind scours lighter marks. Do not trust a flag.

**Navigation and time**

Landmarks are few and repeat: comb, tarn, seep, swell, comb. “That ridge” is every ridge. A map is only useful if you made it; a satellite or inertial tracker that does not depend on your memory is the real tool, with paper bearings as backup because batteries hate cold and because the light will tempt you to trust your eyes. Walk on a bearing. Check it often. The chord changes only by which throat is nearest, which feels like information and is not.

Time will misbehave. Without a varying wind, without a task, without a village ahead, an hour becomes a smear. Wear a watch and decide in advance how long you will stay out: a turnaround time, not a feeling. The equilibrium has no evening lull to warn you that you have overstayed. Sunset is a color, not a mercy. If you are still walking when the disc sits on a swell and you do not already know which seep you will sleep beside, you have already made the mistake.

**Sleep and shelter**

There is no natural shelter that is also quiet. A low swell gives a partial lee from the sand but not from the note. A bored throat is louder, not a cave. Do not crawl into a slot the wind has used for an age; the draft there is the instrument, and it will own the tent.

A four-season tent, low and well staked, with every guy you own, is the minimum for a night. Test the pitch in a wind at home if you can. Bring sand stakes or screws meant for hard ground; the pavement will laugh at light aluminum pins. Orient the door away from the prevailing roar so you are not opening into a throat of grit. Sleep in the muffs or with foam plugs plus the hood; otherwise you will not sleep, and the next day’s hypoxia will be worse. A light liner so skin does not touch stone-cold fabric at 3 a.m. matters more than an extra luxury.

Camp on the dry pavement, not on the damp north face and not on the crust. The birds will reset their ranks at a tarn whether you are there or not. You are not a disturbance they have a tradition of fearing; they flush at a body-length and circle once. Do not need that to mean anything.

**What to carry, and what not to**

On the body: wind shell, insulating layers, brim and eyewear, gloves, gaiters, hearing protection plus spare, sun block, water for the day plus a reserve you do not negotiate with, oximeter, emergency oxygen, headlamp with spare cells kept warm against your chest, small repair kit for tears (the grit will find a seam), a signaling mirror, a whistle you will not hear but someone in a lee might, and a locator beacon if your multiverse kit includes any way home that can be called. A paper note in a pocket stating your turnaround time and your exit bearing, because hypoxia makes clever people vague.

Leave behind: anything that assumes fuel, wood, rescue-by-shouting, or “we’ll find water.” Leave the drone if it cannot hold station in a standing gale; the larger birds already own the wave, and a machine in that air is a gift to the stone. Leave glass bottles. Leave the hope that the place will offer a narrative by afternoon.

**How to be there, if you insist on more than surviving**

Go with one other person, not a crowd and not alone. Alone, the chord becomes your thought. A crowd has no work to do and will fray. Two people with signals already agreed can witness the same pavement and still get each other off it.

Walk less than you can. Sit in the lee and let the eye learn the thin inscription: lichen plates of rust and sulfur and false char, cushion plants that flower in a white button and do not nod, tufts where a seep goes underground, the ranks of short-winged birds ticking in the register the stone does not use, the large fliers hung on one angle of wing as if nailed to the air. That is the whole fauna of the visit. It does not perform. If you need it to climax, you will invent a climax and walk toward the rim to find it.

Do not try to answer the note. People hum against it, sing one line, shout a name. The plateau has been this chord longer than your language has existed. Matching it does not initiate you. It just empties you faster.

Touch the stone with gloves and with attention. The polish is real, and so are the flakes. A north face may feel faintly oiled from the film the air never quite dries; that is the closest thing to a greeting you will get.

When you leave, leave nothing the wind will not immediately own — no ribbon, no food scrap, no “small” marker you meant to retrieve. The equilibrium is complete. You are the only process in it that has not finished. The correct preparation ends with a decision made in advance: which light-cycle is the last, which bearing is home, and the willingness to turn around while you still feel you could have gone farther. The plateau will be the same whether you do or not. Your ordinary body will not.
