The unfinished bowl does not punish ignorance so much as it uses it. Prepare as if you are walking into a room that is still being spoken, where the floor has opinions, the air is a loud instrument, and the door you came through may not be a door by the time you want it.

Go in the body you already have. Nothing here requires a modified traveler, a second heart, or a borrowed sense. What it requires is that the body you bring can stand heat from below, wet from every side, noise that arrives as pressure, and a short, sharp return to ordinary cold. If you are sick, fevered, or unsteady on your feet, do not go. The place is small. It is not gentle.

Dress for a kiln that sweats. The light is dim and sourceless, gray-amber, and it will not warm you; the stone will. Seams pulse with leftover making-heat. Pools are warm from underneath, not from any sun you can find. At the same time the air is a continuous film: condensation writes itself on every face, including yours, and the vapor never quite decides to fall or clear. Wear layers you can open at the throat and the wrists, cloth that does not cling when soaked, and nothing that becomes stiff or slick when wet. A shell that sheds water is useful; a sealed suit is not, unless you enjoy cooking yourself in your own weather. Leave bright metal and dangling straps behind. The bead-fliers read heat. A flashing buckle or a swinging cord is an invitation to a helix that will roar in your ear and make thought difficult.

Your feet matter more than your pack. The floor is a skin over something that flexes, a breath every few minutes, and it weeps. Joints are soft at the edges, almost waxy, not yet committed to being stone. Do not wear smooth soles. You want grip on wet new rock, and you want to feel, through the sole, the difference between a still floor and a seam that is about to open. The rule is simple and worth memorizing until it is boring: do not stand on a pulsing seam. It will not swallow you. It will open under one foot and close on the other, and the close is hot enough to blister. Practice, before you go, the small habit of pausing when the ground changes its mind, and of stepping off rather than bracing. If a crack becomes while you are watching it, let it. It does not widen with violence. It simply is, and warm water finds it at once. You can walk around almost anything here if you are not in a hurry. The whole bowl is a few hundred paces across. Hurry is what gets a foot caught.

Bring water you trust, and do not drink the place. The pools look inviting and they are warm, and some of them are sorted by whatever left the dishes on the ledge: one milky, one so clear it seems absent, one dark and slowly rotating though nothing stirs it. Animals avoid that ledge. Take the hint. The rest of the water is not poison in any obvious way, but it is mineral, new, and confused about where it is going. It beads, threads, stands in lenses, and sinks when the floor remembers it is porous. Rinse nothing in it that you mean to put in your mouth. If you must touch it, touch with the back of a hand first. The warmth is kiln-warmth, not sun-warmth, and it can be hotter at the seam than in the pool.

Sound will be the thing you underestimate. The bowl focuses it. A slate-bird slamming the far wall arrives at your chest as a shove. The seed-bodied fliers tick like dry rain and become a roar when a helix passes your ear. Under the floor a grind comes and goes, the stone still arranging its grains. Somewhere in the eastern wall a chime repeats every nine or ten breaths, high and clean, too regular for an animal and too musical for a drip; the fliers stutter when it sounds. Speak, and you will be answered: clicks from the clay-flat bodies, sometimes a brief stilling of the grind, as if something below the skin of the floor noted the vibration and filed it. You do not need to be silent. You do need to know that your voice is not private, and that a shout is a physical event. If you are sensitive to noise, bring simple ear protection you can lift and lower with one hand, not something that seals you off completely. You want the chime. You want the change in the grind. Those are how the room tells you a cycle is turning. You do not want the full chest-shove of every impact for an entire afternoon.

The animals are numerous, loud, and mostly uninterested in you as food. They are a crowd at a site that manufactures its own meals: heat, mineral film, the ocher crust that grows where the weep is newest, the concussion of walls still settling. The bead-fliers drink the film on the stone and lift in a pale curtain when a seam flexes. Do not swat a helix. Step aside, lower your head, and wait. They settle. The flat, many-legged clay-colored ones will cross your boots and may climb a shin if you stand still, testing you for salt. They drop off disappointed. Let them. Brushing them hard reads, to a bowl this small, as a signal; their clicks are answered before the echo dies. The slate-plate birds launch, cross in three wingbeats, and slam the opposite wall hard enough to shake condensate into a brief rain. They are not injured. The impact is how they feed. Stay off the walls they use, and do not stand between a bird and the ocher mats it is about to knock loose. Their slam is a shove even at a distance. Near, it is worse.

There are marks in the room that are not animals and not weather. A dark hoop near the northern rim, taller than a door, sunk in stone that grew around it. Water beads on it and crawls upward, against sense, and vanishes at the crown. Step through and the loudness drops, not in volume exactly but in nearness, as if a hand's width of distance has been put between you and every process at once. Step back and the mill returns. It is warm on one side and cool on the other. It is not a door out. Treat it as a tool you do not understand: one step, a breath, and back, if you try it at all. A line of bright pins set into a seam will still the flex under your feet for as long as you touch one, and the making resumes a little faster when you let go, as if annoyed. Do not pull a pin. Do not pocket one. Three dishes on a ledge sort water into kinds; the rotating one hums at the edge of hearing. Leave them. Near the reed-rods an arc of the same light-drinking stuff ends in filaments finer than hair that disappear into wet stone, and where they enter, the mineral has gone glassy and wrong. Something under that window moves with the slow logic of a mechanism. Look if you must. Do not kneel on the filaments. Do not try to see the depth more clearly by leaning in with a light. The place already has a light, and it does not like being argued with.

Pack light, and pack for leaving suddenly. A small blunt light you can cup in your hand, not a beam you sweep across walls. The birds slam; a hard beam is a provocation you do not need. Cloth to wipe your face. A way to mark your own boots or your own hem with something dull and personal, so that in the thicker amber you can tell your foot from a new rib. A short length of cord, not for climbing the gulf. There is a dim drop beyond the rim and it has no color yet. You do not look down for long, and you do not test it. Food that will not smell strongly of salt or sugar. The clay-flats are curious about salt, and the ocher mats smell faintly of hot metal and sugar already; you do not want to compete. Nothing electronic you would grieve. Condensation films every face, and the hoop edits nearness in ways that are unkind to instruments. If you record, record with a method that can be ruined without ruining you.

Learn the cycle before you learn the room. At intervals the light dims further, not into night but into a thicker amber, and condensation increases until the far wall is a rumor. In that interval the hoop's false color brightens, the filaments pulse harder, and new cracks prefer to open. It may be machinery. It may be weather invented locally, rain condensing out of a decision. When it passes, the bowl is louder, wetter, and slightly larger: a new rib, a pool that was not there, a reed-rod at a pitch the others do not share. If you are inside when it thickens, stop walking. Find floor that has not pulsed in several breaths. Wait. Do not chase the exit through a whiteout of vapor. Cracks open then, and a crack can look, for a minute, like the gap you came in by.

The exit is the real preparation, and it is mostly memory. Outside the rim the finished mountain takes the body back: cold, thin air, ordinary stone, a wind that has been blowing long enough to have a direction. The loudness cuts off at the threshold, clean as a hand taken from a bell. Inside, the gap you entered by is only a gap until the room resumes its sentence. Mark it before you step in, not with a bright pin and not by scarring the soft stone, but by learning it: the height of the wall there, the way the windward side is higher where the mountain is still arriving, the particular lack of reed-rods, the angle of the drop of light. Count your paces from that gap to the first thing you trust, and count them back. When you are ready to leave, leave on a still floor, between chimes, not during the thick amber. Turn once at the threshold and listen. Impacts, ticks, the nine-breath chime, water deciding where to go. Then go. Do not stand in the doorway negotiating. The air changes in a single step, from kiln-wet to thin cold, and a body that has been open at the throat will shock. Have the outer layer already in your hands.

A few hours is enough. An afternoon, if the ground holds still, would walk the whole of it. You do not need to see every dish and every filament. You need to come out with your feet unblistered, your ears intact, and a true memory of which gap was the gap. The room will not look the same if you return. That is not weathering. That is the sentence resuming. What you prepare, then, is not a map. It is a way of standing in a place that is still becoming: soft shoes, quiet hands, a short stay, and the discipline to step off a seam the moment it breathes.
