Pack as if for a cold fen that has learned the habits of a works, and as if the works may edit the fen while you are standing on it. The place will not punish drama. It will punish assumptions that still belong to ordinary ground: that a surface stays a surface, that distance is a number, that warmth is hospitality, that water runs downhill, that a thing you saw is the same thing you reach.

Dress for permanent post-rain and a cold that does not quite become weather. The cold lies on the skin like a film of metal; breath leaves the mouth, hangs, and is taken back. Wool next to the body, then something that still insulates when it is wet, then a shell that sheds sheet-water without turning you into a sail. The wind across the mat is a torn-silk roar and will find every gap. Bring a hood you can hear inside of, and gloves you can peel when a spark jumps between ribs of the ground—ozone and crushed resin, brief and blue, enough to sting wet metal and wet skin alike. Spare socks in a bag that actually seals. Everything you wear will be damp by the second hour and will stay damp. Plan to be damp and still warm. Dry is not a local condition.

Boots should be low enough that the mat can be felt and high enough that lens-hollows, wrist-deep and cold, do not fill them at every step. The ground gives like the hide of something asleep and very large. Stiff soles lie to you; too-soft soles let the living pavement take the shape of your foot and keep it. A print may sink and, an hour later, rise into a welt healed past healing into a small new organ. Walk as a guest who does not mean to be incorporated. Test each pace. Where the mat is thinnest, weight is negotiable. If a footfall feels delayed, or lighter than your body, stop and redistribute. Do not stand long on a place that has started to learn you.

Do not trust stone, because there is no stone you can trust as stone. What you walk is peat that forgot it was peat, lichen raised to architecture, alpine grass anastomosed until the distinction between plants is a courtesy. Poles help on the seeps and become liabilities near the engines: the low organs, shed-sized, half-submerged, venting warm wet breath. Their warmth is a lure. Do not sit on them. Do not sleep on them. Do not lean a pack against them and look away. The mat keeps the shape of anything that rested too long, and some of those shapes still breathe on a cycle of days. Rest on the open mat only in short watches, on a groundsheet that does not weep into the substrate, and move before the hollow under you begins to answer. If a people-shaped depression offers itself, leave it. There are no people here. The shape is not a bed.

Navigation is the real kit, and most of it is not gear. The horizon does not rise to meet you. It fails, out past the wet, in a dimness with no weather-line and no farther ridge. A gray that might be dawn or the underside of a permanent cloud—often there is no cloud, only air thickened until seeing is wading. Distances misreport. A dark tuft a stone’s throw away may be a knee-high ridge after a long walk, or a barn-high standing wave of vegetation that was not there when you looked away. Carry a compass if it comforts you, and do not believe it when the ground has reversed uphill and downhill for the afternoon. A seep may climb a swell and then forget, sheeting back the way physics prefers, leaving a dark lick that smells of iron and crushed leaf. Mark your outgoing path with nothing the mat can digest into itself. Bright tape becomes food or architecture. Better: notes in a book you keep on your body, times, sounds, the direction of the roar, whether the percussion on the horizon was pistons or birds or the mat clearing its throat. Those three are the same event heard at different depths. Write what you heard, not what you decided it was.

Timekeeping matters more than maps. A watch with a sealed face, wound or battery-backed, worn under the cuff so the dim and the wet cannot bargain with it. The plateau shows second versions of itself—slightly older, already flatter, already alive—and takes them back between one step and the next. If your watch and your hunger disagree, trust the watch for camp and trust hunger for nothing. Do not eat what the grazers pull up. A low, wool-dark herd may work a sweeter vein a half-mile off, jaws making a wet industrial click, and come up with the wrong season in its mouth: green that cannot grow at this cold, already browning as the jaw works, the plateau correcting the error without apology. You are not equipped to survive that correction in your stomach. Bring food that was finished before you arrived: dense, cold-proof, wrapped so the smell of resin and iron cannot get in. Eat on a schedule. Drink from what you carried. The water here is never far—it weeps in sheets, stands in lenses, beads on every vein—and it is also the medium of the industry. Sheet to hollow, hollow to vein, a sound like cloth dragged through a mill. Listening to it too long is a way of being moved. Travelers have found water behind them without having turned. If you must take local water, boil it past the point of sense, and do not drink from a hollow an absence is sharing with an animal. You will see that. An animal and its absence in the same seep, the absence drinking. Walk wide.

Light is for your hands, not for the horizon. The dim does not lift so that a beam can purchase it. A small lamp lets you check laces, read your notes, and see whether a stalk-stand is assembling or unmaking. Blade-grass can lock into a small architecture, a shade, in the time it takes to cross a seep, and be paying its fibers back into the substrate by the time you reach it, thread pulled from a seam. Do not shelter in a stand that is still deciding to exist. Weather-cells drop out of the dim, spin a few hundred paces of white water and flying culms into a brief tower, and are reabsorbed. If the air starts to organize, get low on open mat, away from the engines, and wait for the tower to be taken back. Nothing is added to the height of the land. Nothing is subtracted. You can be inside the transaction anyway.

Hearing is the sense that will lie least, and it will still lie. The nothing between presences is loud: piston-rhythm in buried culms, a belt-whine where a pale runner of stem pays out across a mile, hauling nutrients the way a works hauls ore. Steam columns shear sideways and refuse to disperse, hanging as pale machinery. Far off, something percussion-heavy repeats, not quite regular, weather stamping sheet metal and missing. You can stand in what feels like emptiness and be unable to hear your own step. Practice that before you need it. In camp, confirm your feet by touch. If the roar covers your step, you have not become quieter; the plateau has raised its voice over you. That is the moment to stop, not to hurry.

The fauna are many and far apart, which is how abundance lives on a sparse plain. Give the grazers their vein. Do not approach the bird the color of the dim: it lifts from a seep and is, for a moment, three birds in three slightly wrong positions, then one again, farther than the flight could account for. Things here do not always agree with the moment that produced them. No weapon changes that. A stick for probing hollows is enough. Anything sharper will be used, by you, on a problem that was perceptual, and the mat is better than you are at closing wounds into organs.

Go in company if you can, and agree in advance on a rule for disagreement: when two of you see different heights for the same tuft, you both sit down and wait until the versions reconcile or one of you concedes without walking toward the taller one. Solo travel is possible for a careful person with a finished route and a short clock. It is a poor idea for anyone who needs the plateau to confirm that they are still the size they were at the edge. Between one step and the next the open ground may offer you an older self of the plain and take it back. You should already know which version of you is carrying the food.

Leave when the watch says leave, not when the light improves. The light never commits. The cold does not let up. Creation and destruction run as a paired process over the living pavement the way production and scrap run through a works; you are not in that ledger, and the longest visits are the ones that start to be entered. Come out with the same number of shapes you brought in. Count your hands. Count your companions. If a depression behind you is breathing on a cycle of days, do not go back to close it. The oldest stable state of this range is already finished wearing down. It does not need your outline to complete the work.
