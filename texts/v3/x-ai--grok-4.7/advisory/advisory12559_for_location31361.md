Go dressed for a room that is also weather. The hollow is only a parish-close wide and a few paces down, but the air inside it has already decided to be a degree colder than the wet country on the rim, and it will not negotiate. You will not freeze. You will not warm up by walking. What you will do is slowly become the same temperature as the bench, the iron, and the moss, and if your clothes fight that, you will spend the visit inside a private damp that the place did not ask you to carry.

Wear wool or something that still works when it is cool and never quite dry. A light shell is useful against the brief rain and the drip off the yew eaves; a heavy waterproof that cannot breathe will cook a film of sweat against your skin and then chill it. Take a second pair of socks and keep them in something that is actually closed. The path in is packed root, combed one way by old traffic, then sedge, then the dark spots where condensation has been falling all day onto the living mat. Boots with a quiet sole beat anything that rings. You do not need expedition tread. You need not to chew the floor.

Bring water you trust and do not plan to drink the place. The wheel-pit is tea-colored. The vats hold press-liquor the color of old tea, smelling of leaf-rot and clean metal. Condensation beads on iron and runs, by old grooves, into tins. None of that is a cup. The wet is plentiful and none of it is yours. A small bottle is enough; you can walk the boundary in twenty minutes, and nothing here is going to host you through a meal.

Eat before you come down, and eat plainly. This is not a hunting ground and not a larder. The bark was selected, long ago, for a bitterness that keeps the walls from becoming pantries. Do not taste it to see. Do not pocket withe, peel, leaf, or a curl of scar-wood because it is beautiful and apparently unattended. The industry is still running. A stem on a rack is losing a measured amount of water on its way to becoming cord. A belt of twisted bast is a machine part that will be fiber again in the beater when it cracks. Taking a souvenir is taking a component out of a loop that has no spare shift to notice the loss until the loop fails.

Carry almost no sound. The wheel’s wet knock, the rollers, the motors in their bark housings, the drip into tin: all of it sits under the threshold of echo, and the vault kills what little voice the hall has. A metal cup on a carabiner, a loose buckle, a radio, a bright little speaker, will be the loudest event in the hollow. If you need a light for the dark half of the year, make it small and warm and pointed at your own feet. The lamps along the ribs are plain industrial glass, low wattage, switched by the same clock that starts the secondary motors. You can read a gauge by leaf-light in the day. You do not need to flood the nave to prove you have arrived.

Leave the instruments of improvement at home. No axe, no saw, no weed knife, no portable stove, no fuel, no bright flagging, no fresh copper or iron you mean to “set right.” The emptiness is maintained. Where the rail runs, where tannin has blackened the soil, the growth has pulled back and kept a corridor; everywhere else the plant is floor, wall, and roof. Those lines are old. If you cut a gap, lay a new metal, spill oil, or trample a corridor into the sedge because it looked unused, you are editing a treaty you cannot see the other side of. The plants enforce the emptiness without being asked each morning. They will also close, slowly, anything you open in the wrong place, and they will not care that you meant it as a path.

Touch only what is clearly a path or a rail. The copper has gone green and is wide enough for a cart that no longer comes; walk it if you must cross, and do not sit long enough to become a patch of shade the moss files a claim on. Do not lean on the shaft, the belts, the rollers, the screw press, the beaters. They are small and continuous, sized to a crop that is also the building, and they do not have a guard whose job is to stop a curious hand. The motors hum at a pitch you stop hearing after a minute. That is not silence and not consent. When the race runs thin they make up what the wheel cannot give. Stand where you can see the knock of the wheel and still hear the hum, and you will understand the scale: nothing here is heroic, and none of it is idle.

Time your entry for the hour you can see, unless you already know the lamp clock. Before full light, breath shows only if the air is at its wettest, and only if you look. Dawn mist works the rim and is gone by the time the coins of light on the floor are bright. A drop that has been gathering since morning lets go in the yew hall sometime in the afternoon; if you are listening for the place, that sound will tell you more than a longer stay. You do not need to sleep here. There is no inn, no shift whistle, no crowd to disappear into. People may be absent at the hour you arrive, or so few they do not thicken the air. The settlement is urban in its joints — graded races, numbered racks, a ledger of marks burned into a living post and healed around so the numbers sit in scar — and rural in its welcome, which is to say it has not prepared one. Read the marks if you can read them. Do not add your own, not even a polite initial. Scar is how this wood keeps books.

Assume your nose and your patience will do more work than your eyes. The smell is leaf-rot and clean metal, not rot in the warning sense and not perfume. If it sharpens toward solvent, fermentation, or hot insulation, leave the vat hall and the motor housings and go back to the courts. You are an ordinary body. The bitterness that keeps mice out of the walls is not a guarantee about your skin, your eyes, or a cut. Do not bathe your hands in the liquor to feel what the coopers stopped fighting. If the lining of a vat has become root, that is the building solving a leak, not an invitation to harvest the solution.

What to practice before you step off the rim: standing still long enough to see what does not happen. No flocks. A wren, maybe, and gone. Insects under the motors. No one hailing you. The far end of the largest hall is not far; cathedral only in the way the ribs repeat. If you rush, you will finish the hollow in a quarter of an hour and remember a green ditch with sheds. If you wait through one cycle of the wheel and one brightening of the leaf-coins, you will understand that everything is in motion and nothing is in a hurry — sap, race, press, mist, a withe losing water on a rack. Match that. Speak softly if you speak. Step on the walked fibers. When you leave, take the same path up, the short rise, and do not look back to check whether the place noticed you. It is busy being a floor that grows its own replacement, and the country beyond the rim is ordinary wet ground again, going on without ceremony.
