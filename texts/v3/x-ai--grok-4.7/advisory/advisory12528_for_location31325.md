You do not arrive at this place the way you arrive at a mountain. There is no pass where the air suddenly fails you, no last switchback that announces the height. You are already in it, and your chest has already started doing a little more work than you budgeted for. Prepare for that first, and for the fact that almost every other danger here is soft.

**Breath and the day your body spends learning the air**

The air is warm and thin together, which confuses the instincts that usually pair thinness with cold. You will not get the warning of numb fingers. You will get a duller warning: a conversation that feels like work, a climb of two body-lengths over a root-swell that leaves you sitting longer than pride wants, sleep that is light because the chest keeps checking. Before you come, live for a while where the air is already a little poor, and walk until walking is ordinary again. Bring nothing that assumes you can run. If you carry oxygen, carry it as a private reserve for the night when the haze thickens and the canals exhale, not as a way to move faster. Faster is not a currency this country accepts.

Go up slowly in the days before, even if “up” is only a fiction of your previous world. The plateau will not give you a camp at the treeline where you can pause and look back. There is no back.

**What you walk on**

The ground is not ground. It is a body that has agreed, for the moment, to hold you. Test every step the way you would test ice you do not trust, except the failure mode is not a crack and a drop into dark water. It is give, then a cooler wet, then the spring of a hollow stem, then a canal that was never meant to be a path. A staff is worth more than a blade. Use it to read the mat: a dull resistance is fiber and felt; a drum-note is a chamber; a slick slide under the point is a membrane over moving water. Kneel before you trust a saddle between swellings. Do not press a hand “just to see” past the first coolness. The description you were given is also an instruction. The plateau irrigates itself through those depths. You are not invited into the plumbing.

Wear boots that spread your weight and that you can take off without a fight, because you will be wet to the ankle for days and the wet will be warm. Soles with aggressive grip help less than soles that do not punch through. Gaiters, or cloth wound high, against the nap: the surface grain can pack into every seam, and some of what packs is still alive in a small, uninterested way. Bring a spare set of foot-wrappings and the habit of drying them against your body at night. Nothing else dries. The air is too full of itself.

If you must cross a lens-pool or a weeping cleft, go around. The bottom is a rumor. A pool that looks shin-deep may be a skylight into a gallery. Poles, a light line, a companion who stays on fiber you have already stood on. Never both of you on the same untested span of arch. Some arches are structure. Some are the memory of structure, eaten out from within and left as a shape. Thump them. Listen for the grain-pour sound the country makes when it releases stored air; that sound means hollow, and hollow means do not shelter and do not cross.

**Water, and the difference between a vein and a bladder**

You can drink here, which is a mercy, and you can poison yourself, which is the usual price of mercy in a wet place. Moving canals: clear thread, a skin that tightens and relaxes because something lives in the system and the system is not stagnant. Still bladders, rooms of water under a ceiling of leaf, pools whose surface has gone to a film: leave them. They have stopped being shared with the district and started being a stomach. A still bladder that smells only of wet bark is still a no. Smell is a late signal.

Carry a vessel you can lower and lift without leaning your weight onto a lip of mat. The lip is often the thing that fails. Drink, then wait a day before you trust the country’s water as your only water, the way you would with any new spring. If your mouth goes metallic or your gut argues, go back to what you carried and to moving water taken higher in the canal, not lower, where the freight has passed through more districts. Do not cut the hide to “tap” it. A cut weeps a long time. You do not need the volume, and the plateau does not need another mouth.

Rain is frequent, soft, and useless as a plan. It does not run off to a stream you can follow. It beads, films, soaks, and is swallowed. A rain-cape matters more than a rain-collector. You are not going to out-drink the land by catching sky-water, and you are not going to stay dry. You are going to stay warm-wet instead of chilled-wet, which at this height is the difference that matters. Wring clothes when you can. Sleep in the dry layer you kept sealed, even if it feels absurd in the warmth. Warmth here is not the same as comfort after three days of film on the skin.

**Light, sight, and the disappearance of distance**

The day is a stained low light, and sometimes not even that: a green interior, a coin of brightness thrown by a water-lens, shadows that pool instead of sharpening. Your eyes will hunt for edges and not find them. Bring light, but dim light, warm if you can choose, and the discipline to use it little. A harsh beam flattens the mat into a single false surface and blinds you to the drum-notes and the darker green that means thicker fiber. Let your eyes have an hour at dawn and an hour at the deepening. That is when the grain shows, and the arches, and the pale flanges that are only wetness holding the last light.

Do not navigate by horizon. It hazes. It does not drop. A day’s walk does not produce a view that organizes the next day. Before you leave any camp, mark it in a way the country can tolerate and you can recognize: a small cairn of dead fiber on a rise, a rag tied where the nap changes direction, a count of your own steps between a column and a gap. The plateau records by growing. Your rag may be half-swallowed in a season; in a week it will still be yours. Maps of distance will lie. Maps of texture might not: the rust-green district, the band recently grazed, the line of flanges too regular to be accident, the circle of columns. Learn to write those down in a notebook that does not mind damp. Pencil, not ink. Ink blooms.

Sound will not help you place a companion who has stepped behind a swell. Footfalls are absorbed. Calls do not echo, because echo needs stone or empty distance, and both have been filled. Agree on close-in signals: a strike of staff on staff, a whistle with a harsh edge, a lamp uncovered and covered again. Travel within sight when the canopy closes. In the open warm dimness under the tin sky you may spread a little, but not past the next swelling. People vanish here without drama. The place does not chase them. It simply continues, and continuing is enough.

**The lives that are already moving**

Most of what lives here is slow because speed is waste. That should lower your pulse, not your attention. Grazers the size of weather fronts will not turn aside for you if you stand in the nap they are taking. You are not prey and not a wall. You are a brief irregularity. Step off the dark band they are working. Watch the direction of the grain they leave; it is a road that will green again, and while it is dark it is easier walking, and it tells you where the mouths have been.

The thing in the canals announces itself by the skin of a pool tightening, by a wet sound like a decision not to speak. You do not need its name. You need not to be the weight on the membrane when it rises. Fish, if you fish, from solid fiber with a line, not by wading. Do not clean a catch on the mat where the smell will sit in the felt. The country absorbs, and then it has you in its record.

Insects move in sheets and veins. A net for the face at the hour when the texture of their noise rises. No perfume, no sweet ration eaten in the open, no fruit-juice on the hands. Their attention is not malice. It is the same absorption, applied to whatever does not belong to the scent of wet bark and warm mineral. Treat bites as openings in a wet climate: clean them, cover them, watch them. Bring a simple salve and a way to keep it dry. This is not a place that rewards an interesting infection.

At night the warmth stays and animals pass close, because closeness is the ordinary condition. You will feel them as pressure in the mat and as breath that is not yours. A low shelter with a visible entrance, a light you can wake and show, a companion who shares the watch not against attack but against the simple fact that a heavy body on a hollow arch is a bad surprise in the dark. Do not make a wall of fire. There is little dead wood that is truly dead, and a cut that weeps is a poor candle. A small contained flame for boiling and for the soul is enough. The dark is only a deepening of the same dim. Your eyes, if you let them, will keep more of the night than you expect.

**Eating, and taking as little as the country notices**

Buried fruit: a soft weight under the nap, sweet, fibrous, meant for a lower mouth. Eat it cooked if you can. Your gut is a guest. The sweetness is real and it is not a promise that the rest of the chemistry is yours. Graze lightly. A district can spare a meal. It cannot spare a traveler who has decided the fruiting layer is a pantry.

Carry compact food that does not smell like fruit and does not spoil in warmth: grain, hard fat, salt, something bitter and familiar so your appetite remembers a world with edges. You will be hungrier than the walking accounts for, because thin air is a quiet thief, and you will be less hungry than you think when the light is low and every meal tastes faintly of the mat. Salt matters. Sweat does not feel like sweat when it never cools. You can leach minerals for days and only notice when the legs refuse a swelling you climbed yesterday.

Do not hunt the stationed birds, the ones grown around at the feet, who tear free and shift one fork over. They are not plenty. They are punctuation. Leave them as marks you can recognize on the way back, if there is a way back by the same line.

**Shelter that is structure and not memory**

The arches and the low grown walls with gaps a body can pass are the closest thing to inns, and some of them have been inns for animals longer than your kind has been a kind. Use the gaps. Do not cut new ones. Inside, the fiber is denser, sometimes polished by passage. Sleep on a skin or a cloth, not directly on that polish; warmth and moisture will have you dreaming you are part of the felt by morning, and the dream is half practical.

Test for the grain-pour, for weep, for a floor that drums. A chamber that breathes warm air on a cycle is a lung of the district. It is a beautiful place to stand at the entrance and a stupid place to sleep. The breath can be foul, or merely airless, or only warm and strange. You will not know which until you are too far inside.

When rain leans the whole country — the day when every surface gives up more than it takes — get onto the oldest dense fiber you can find, under a leaf-roof that is still leaf and not yet a province, and wait. Storms here are not spectacles. They are a general increase in the amount of world touching your skin. Afterward the canals are fuller and the quiet is deeper and the mat lies. That is a good morning to move, and a bad morning to trust yesterday’s fords.

**Marks, scars, and the things that are almost roads**

You may find a line of pale flanges too regular, a circle of columns with almost-architectural spacing, walls with gaps, a flange leaning at an angle growth would not choose, a glassy scar the mat has refused to cover. These are not invitations to dig. The plateau keeps what it keeps by growing to the edge and stopping. A fused scar is old heat, now only as warm as everything else, beaded with the same weep. It will not teach you a machine. It will cut you if you treat it as a quarry, and the cut will not be the country’s gentle weep. It will be yours, and it will not clot well in this wet.

If you must study, study with your eyes and a notebook. Do not pry up the patch the mat has refused. That refusal is the most deliberate thing you will see. Leaving it is a form of navigation too: such scars are rare enough to be bearings, if you can find your way back to one through the haze.

Do not camp on the regular flange-lines as if they were highways you own. Something grew that way for a reason, or around a reason. Sleep at the side, on fiber you have thumped, with your head toward the mark so you can find it in the deepening without a search.

**The body over many days**

This country changes at the speed of scar and season. You will change faster, and you have to plan for the mismatch. Feet first: rot, softened nails, blisters that never dry. A daily rite, unromantic and non-negotiable. Skin in the creases. Eyes irritated by the constant fine damp and by hairs of fiber. Lips that crack not from cold but from breathing harder than the air wants. Fat and a simple ointment. A needle and gut for the one cut you will probably take on a flange or a glassy edge. Something for fever, because the water is mostly kind and not entirely kind, and because a wet lung at height is a different illness than a wet lung in a valley. If you are a person whose blood is already grudging in thin air, this is a poor first multiverse, or at least a poor long one. Go short. Go with someone who knows the sound of your ordinary breath.

Bowel and bladder: the mat will take what you give it and record it. Go on already-dead fiber, cover lightly, and do not use the canals as a sink. You are drinking cousins of that water. A small trowel is almost a superstition here — there is no soil in the old sense — but the habit of burial still keeps flies and your own future hands cleaner.

Menstruation, bleeding, open wounds: the scent will not summon a monster. It will summon attention of the small and numerous kind, and it will mark a trail in a place where trails otherwise close. Prepare more cloth than you think, sealed when clean, and a way to carry soiled cloth until you can dry and rewash in moving water far from camp. Do not rinse blood into a bladder-pool.

**Mind, and the scale that does not need you**

The hardest preparation is not gear. It is the agreement you make with yourself about time and witness.

There is no rim to reach. No summit photograph. No last ridge where the meaning of the walk arrives. The horizon will not come forward to congratulate you. People who need a goal fracture here, and then they walk farther because walking feels like the missing goal, and the canals shift their freight under them, and the stone basement, if it is still there, does not comment. Decide before you step onto the mat how long you are staying: a number of sleeps, not a number of landmarks. When the number is done, turn around along the texture-map you actually wrote, not the map you meant to write. The place is large enough to make its own days. You are not.

The quiet is absorption, not peace and not threat. After a few sleeps your own voice will sound like damage. That is normal. Speak anyway, a little, to your companion, to the notebook, so the habit of a self with edges does not soak away entirely. If you travel alone — and you can, the country allows it — bring a hard object from another world that cannot be composted, and hold it when the light goes. Not a weapon. A weight. A proof that not everything here grades into everything else.

You may feel, walking, that you are on a single thought carried out too far to need witnesses. The feeling is accurate and it is not an emergency. Do not answer it by trying to be a witness the place will remember. It remembers by growth and refusal, and you are too quick to be either. Be a careful temporary weight. Drink from what moves. Sleep on what thumps solid. Mark your camps. Leave the scars covered. Eat a little buried fruit and cook it. When a column releases its long pouring breath, stop and listen until it ends without conclusion, so you learn the sound of hollow before you trust it with your body.

Then walk back while your map of textures still matches the country, which is to say soon. The bands the grazers darken will green. The lens-pools will migrate a hand’s width. The rag will start to be a fork something might grow around. You are not slow enough to live here in the way the plateau lives. You are slow enough, if you prepare, to visit without becoming a scar it has to refuse, or a softness it quietly includes.

Bring a staff, dim light, salt, a sealed dry layer, a face net, pencil, cloth, patience for the chest, and a fixed number of sleeps. Leave the blade mostly clean. The height will not announce itself. Neither will the way home, unless you kept it in your own handwriting, one dull-thump at a time.
