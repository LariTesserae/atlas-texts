Prepare for a country that has finished becoming itself and has not agreed to be quiet about it. The danger is not a cliff or a summit. It is duration: thin air, a wind that never clocks out, sound that occupies the body, water that appears and leaves on its own schedule, and a scale that makes every human plan look temporary by noon.

**Arrive already acclimated, and treat the first day as a listening day.** The height is real before the land is. If your home air is thick, do not step onto the table and start crossing swells. Spend the entry interval doing nothing ambitious—sit, breathe, notice the headache or the metallic taste before either becomes a decision. Walk shorter than you think you can. The grade is so gentle that pride will spend your knees before the landscape looks like it has asked anything of you. A full day of “almost flat” is still a day of tilt, and the wind adds a sideways load your legs will not budget for unless you practice it.

**Assume you cannot talk.** Speech is a rumor here. The roar sits in the teeth and the sternum as a baseline, not as a storm you wait out. Practice, before you come, a small silent language with whoever travels with you: face me, stop, down, water, shelter, I’m all right, I can’t. Agree that mouthing only counts when you are close enough to see the mouth. Carry a slate, a waxed notebook, or anything you can write on with gloves and dust on your fingers. Do not pack a plan that depends on shouting a warning across a swell. By the time the words arrive, if they arrive, the gust has already revised the ground.

**Protect the ears without going deaf to the only forecast you will get.** The wind is loud, and under it the bedrock ticks and groans when pressure moves—the plateau’s way of telling you a cell is crossing. Foam plugs or close-fitting covers will save you from a day that leaves your hearing inventing silence afterward. Total isolation is a mistake. You want the organ-note of stacked air and the pitch-change when joints flex, because those are how rain, dust, and a bad stance announce themselves. Take real auditory rest inside a stone ring when you find one. When you step back out, trust the plateau, not the quiet your ears just composed. That quiet is a lie.

**Dress for hard light, warm stone, and a wind that wants everything you brought.** This is not a cold-peak wardrobe and not a desert-lounge wardrobe. The ground can be warm underfoot at noon while the air strips heat off skin, sweat, and any loose edge of cloth. Wear layers you can close completely. Nothing that bells, flaps, or turns into a sail: hems, scarves, unsecured hoods, open jackets. A brim or close hood for the ordinary-bright sun; the light is honest and will still burn you because shade is an island, not a climate. Eye protection matters twice—glare, and the flour of former mountains that lifts in a pale scarf and slams down a hundred paces on. A light neck cover keeps that dust out of the mouth you will be breathing hard through.

Secure every item as if the wind is a pickpocket with all day. Straps under straps. Pockets with closures. Nothing tied on with a casual knot; cord here gets worried at until the cord wins or the stone does. If a pole, staff, or camera leaves your hand in a shear, do not lunge after it. The stance you abandon is worth more than the object.

**Learn a low, stubborn way of walking.** The table will not usually throw you. It will take a careless stance and teach the knees. Practice moving with your weight slightly forward of the wind, feet apart, ready to kneel or sit rather than lean back into a gust. In the fossil-valleys where flows meet, expect meaner air, not kinder. When a gray wall of density comes faster than a person, stop trying to cross it on purpose. The revision is too quick. Get into a ring of stones, a swell’s lee, or flat on the warm rock with your pack as an anchor, and let the cell shatter into its local weathers. One grove will bow and spring; a pool will slop; light will go bright, milk, bright inside a sentence. Walking through that to keep a schedule is how ordinary travelers get hurt in a place with no drama and no rescue.

**Do not navigate by the green.** The groves sidle. New shoots follow the wet line; yesterday’s outer leaves crisp where the damp withdraws. A wall of leaves you used as a landmark in the morning can be open table by temperament of the water, even if the shift in a single hour is only in the fine grain. Trust stone: the low swells, the bone-colored ridges, the knee-high cairns, the pecked bowls, the slightly less-gritty lines where other feet preferred a swell. Cairns here often follow the pale stain of a fossil stream, not a path. That is a gift. They are telling you where water has been in the habit of pausing, not where a road is.

Long sight is true and unreliable in the same hour. You can see three swells and a flashing basin, and then a pressure-cell turns the distance to milk. Take bearings when the light is exact—late sun lays shadows you could walk by—and write them down. Do not trust a straight line once the wind is shoving you. Haze is usually just more of the same country, which means “I see far” is not “I am near anything.”

**Water is shy, warm, and brief. Plan like a person who will be rained on for ten minutes and then dried to flour.** There is no river to follow home. Moisture comes as film, torn cloud, condensation that beads on shaded stone and under leaves and then lets go all at once when the wind shifts, so a grove can rain on itself under a clear sky. Basins hold tea-colored pools, room-sized, never quite still. Threads write themselves along stains you would have sworn were dry and erase themselves within the hour.

Carry more water than the green suggests, and carry a way to catch a rude short rain: a sheet, a broad leaf-shaped scoop, a bottle you can set in a pecked bowl if you find one of those old catchments at a pool’s ordinary high line. Those bowls still work. Use them without rearranging them. Drink from pools only after you have given the water a traveler’s ordinary suspicion—warm is not clean, and a dark wet ring means the level moves, stirring whatever the mat contributes. The condensation is often the cleaner drink if you can collect it before the gust shakes it into the dust. Refill whenever a thread is actually running. Do not walk past live water because your bottles are “enough until the next basin.” The next basin may be a stain.

The open stone dries to a pale warmth by midafternoon. Damp retreats into cracks, root-mats, and undersides. If you need moisture, look where the plants have already voted.

**Treat the thickets as rooms, not as trails.** Between them the country is bare enough to see across. Inside them the light drops, the false rain falls, vines cable like rigging and thrum, and the ground is a thin spongy mat over rock. It is lush and it is a blind. Step in to drink, to get out of a slap of dust, to rest your eyes—not to push through as if it were forest with a far side you can bull toward. Flowers shut at the first hard gust; the plants have learned that advertising is a waste. You should learn a related lesson: do not thrash.

Fauna is an event, which means absence is not a census. Low armored things live in the mat and break cover when rain changes the pitch of the stone. Leave them. A long-winged shadow may cut the light and be gone; it is using the table as a road, not as your problem, unless you are waving food or stumbling in a shear under its path. Once, something larger has breathed in a thicket in the gaps between gusts and declined to come out. Believe that the place can hold an animal that size. Make noise with your feet on purpose when you enter a dense rim at dusk—not a call, which is useless, but presence. Do not sleep in the mat. Sleep where you can see the swell and where the wind is broken by stone, not by leaves.

**Use the ruins as instruments, not as property.** A ring of flat stones set on edge, gaps chinked, interior worn smoother than the plateau: that is a shelter and a listening-post. Inside it the roar drops just enough that you can hear your pulse. Use it. Do not “improve” it into a camp that the next cell will dismantle and scatter across the table. Knee-high cairns are navigation and water-memory; do not borrow their stones for a windbreak when the ground itself offers slabs. A three-course wall that lost its roof and is being flattened with the country is a lesson, not a house. Cord-marks on a flake of rock mean someone already learned what the wind does to lashings. Add your own only if you mean them to be sacrificial.

There is no village coming. No road that thickens into help. People have crossed here the way weather has: repeatedly, lightly, without founding anything that outlasts a season. Pack a complete ordinary life for the number of days you intend—sleep, repair, first aid for abrasions, dust in the eyes, a turned knee, sun, and the special fatigue of never not hearing—and a margin for a day you spend inside a ring while the air revises itself. A whistle is almost a joke. A mirror or any bright signal is better on a clear late-light hour, and still a thin hope. Tell someone in the last thick-air country when you should be back, and mean it.

**What to put in the pack, and what to leave.** Bring: closed layers, eye and ear protection you have already worn for hours so they do not surprise you, a way to write, more water capacity than courage, a rain sheet, salts and food that do not require a conversation to prepare, a low shelter or the knowledge to use stone as one, cord you can afford to lose, a knife, blister care, a broad bandage, something for lips and skin the wind will chap even while the stone feels warm. Solid footwear with a sole that can take floury grit and then slick leaf-mat; ankle support for the moment a gust adds a sideways step you did not order. A staff you can plant and also afford to drop.

Leave: loose ornament, anything that must be heard to be useful, maps that only show rivers and towns, the hope of a fire as your main comfort—the wind makes flame a brief insult, and wood is a grove’s business, not yours—and any itinerary counted in spoken miles. Count instead in basins reached, rings found, and cells waited out.

**How the day will actually feel, so you can pack your mind.** Morning can be clear, every swell legible, the light flat and trustworthy. You will feel, briefly, that this is a walking country and you are equal to it. Then the western air stacks into density and hits the table. You shelter not because you are fragile in a storybook way, but because purpose is the wrong tool while the world is rewriting. Afterward the groves drip, a thread of water appears, a joint ticks as it cools, and the roar—which never left—reclaims the foreground. Late light gilds the stone and lays exact shadows. The view admits none of the noise. That mismatch is the place. Do not let the beauty argue you into one more swell after your ears are tired and your knees have started translating the grade into pain. Emptiness is the wrong word for it. It is only the right scale: warm, loud, alive in a slow sidling green, and indifferent to whether you cross it well.

Go low, go sealed, go able to be silent, and stop when the air changes its mind. The plateau has already spent an age becoming this. It does not need you to finish the crossing today.
