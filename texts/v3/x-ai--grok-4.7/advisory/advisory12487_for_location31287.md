The basin will not behave like a coast you already know, and most of what will hurt you is not the water.

**Before you go down**

Treat the place as a working plant that has been filled, not as wilderness that happens to contain machines. Whatever schedule governs the gates is the only tide that matters. If you can get a cycle—even a rumor of shift changes, a pattern of when the roar thickens—write it on something that will survive a soaking and do not trust your sense of “slack water.” In the open squares the surface chop is wind. Below that, flow is a decision made somewhere else. A lane that was a drifting green hallway can become a river in the time it takes you to turn around, and it will not meander. It will take the straight surveyed path between gates, and it will take you with it, along with paint flakes and weed and anyone else who was using the residual eddy as a rest.

Learn the sound before you need it. The basin is never quiet. Cavitation, piston threes, the organ-note of a pump house: that is ambient. What you are listening for is a *change*—a rise in pitch, a new rhythm loading, the particular hush when a gate train starts to move. If you cannot tell those apart from the factory-yard roar, you are not ready to be away from a structure.

Surface weather is real and large. Fetch enough for squalls, a steep short sea when wind opposes a pumped set, morning fog over the cooler sluices. You can have a gray wall of rain walking the bright ceiling while twenty meters down the lamps have already clicked up and the concrete is perfectly readable. Plan the exit for the surface you will actually meet, not the surface you left. A calm boarding into a wet boat is a different problem from climbing a stair tower in a steep chop with fog pooled on the sluices.

**What to carry**

Air is ordinary. Depth over most of the plain is a working dive, not an abyss: forty, sixty, a hundred in the trenches. The light at noon is enough to read stenciled numbers. You do not need to solve darkness. You need to solve *duration* and *distance*. The empty squares are the greater part of what there is. A long swim between works with no keel, no reef, no bottom texture except formwork lines will eat gas and judgment faster than the depth profile suggests. Redundancy, a conservative turn-pressure, and a hard rule about not “just crossing one more square” matter more than exotic mixes.

The floor is young concrete. It will not forgive a knee the way sand does, and the joints are sharp slots, tarred and bolted, still as the day the forms came off. Fins, gloves, and any dragging hose will find those edges. So will you, if a current sets you into a caisson corner where the silt—only a flour of fines—hides the seam until you are on it.

Noise will ruin unaided speech near any live work and will fatigue you in the open water, where the sound arrives without a source and has weather in it. A simple hard-wire or a comms habit that does not depend on shouting. A slate. Hand signals you have actually practiced while tired.

Light of your own, even though the day penetrates and the works burn yellow-white industrial lamps. The lamps are for the plant, not for you. They click on timers and load changes. They also concentrate the only reliable life in the basin—small silver fish, and whatever comes up the dark of a pipe gallery to feed. Do not hover in an intake’s lit mouth because the school is pretty. When the current through the grating strengthens, the school pulses; that is the machine inhaling. Intakes, return culverts, and any throat the diameter of a street are not scenery.

A line. Not because you will get lost in the navigational sense—the grid is orthogonal and the mold-lines run until they meet a joint—but because fog, a squall-dimmed ceiling, and a featureless pale plain can erase the vertical. The trenches are the only places that feel like depth, and they are also ducts. Inspection cages on rails, lamp-bar tugs, cold intake streams: you share those cuts with traffic that has right of way and no interest in you.

Something to read the water’s age and temper with your skin and a cheap thermometer. The basin is cool, turned over, poor in the slow nutrients that make a coast shaggy. A warmer, particle-rich boil at a return culvert is a landmark and a comfort and a place animals gather. It is also discharge. Do not soak a cut in it, and do not assume “warm” means safe to drift in while you sort a problem. Electrified gantries and cable runs exist; the cable on the bottom is new, jacket unbitten, route straight. Give fresh electrical work the same berth you would give it in a dry yard.

**How to move**

Navigation is surveying, not piloting. Follow the grid. Note stenciled numbers while they are still legible at sixty meters—they will not be forever, but they are now. Structures arrive all at once because nothing has been softened into a shoreline: a ring of columns, a stair tower becoming a column grid, pipes in parallel like a second geography. Use them as harbors. The open square is not a harbor.

Do not treat vegetation as a cue. There is almost none. A few rope-kelp frames, half of them bare wire; a green film on high pilings; mussels in a single new crust on the lower bolts, steel still showing between clumps. If you expected a reef to tell you where the life is, you will search the wrong emptiness. Life is at joints, lights, and intakes, the way workers bunch at a gate at shift change, and absent in the miles between gates.

The dry parts are the real refuge, and they are not friendly. Valve galleries can be entered and are dry above a certain line: loud beyond speech, oil, hot metal, the sweet-chemical edge of treated water. Know where that line is before you commit a route through one. An engine hall on piers is a building that continues underwater as architecture, not as ruin. Edges cut. Welds are bright. Nothing has been rounded by time. You can be injured by a corner that looks, in the green-silver light, merely diagrammatic.

**What the visit is actually like**

Expect a long, bright, loud emptiness, interrupted by machines that are still proud of their geometry. The contradictions do not resolve and you should not wait for them to. You are under a sea large enough to make weather, and the sea is an object that was poured into a container. The container is new enough that silt is a rumor and the aggregate shows when you scrape. Fish and a seal-shaped body and gull-shadows on the columns are already using it as if it had always been a sea. Both facts are true in the same afternoon.

The psychological hazard is the combination of legibility and scale. You can see the floor. You can read the grid. You can hear the works for an hour before you see them. That clarity makes the distance feel crossable. It is not, not on a human gas supply and a human attention span, not when a scheduled river can redraw one lane of the map without asking. Boredom and confidence will arrive together in the empty squares. That is the moment to turn toward the nearest lamp-bar or stair tower, not the moment to admire how clean the molds still are.

Eat and drink on the assumption that the basin will not feed you. The fauna is abundant only where the plant concentrates it. Between works you may see nothing living for a long time. Surface the way you would leave a factory floor: clear of intakes, clear of a gate that is about to earn its roar, onto a structure or a boat you already trust, with the surface weather checked before you commit the last meters.

Leave the joints as you found them. This floor still remembers the forms. You are a visitor in a machine that is being kept full, cool, and clear on purpose. The loudness will not resolve into a single engine you can bargain with. It is conveyance, cooling, generation, and wind, conducted through water that was put here. Prepare for that sum—not for a natural deep, and not for a ruin.
