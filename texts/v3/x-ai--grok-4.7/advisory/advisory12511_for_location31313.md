Go expecting to be wet before you are cold, and cold before you are tired, and tired before you have understood anything. The basin will not meet you at an edge. You will walk out of ordinary country into a continuation, and the moment you know you have arrived is the moment the ticking has been under your hearing for some time and the color has gone out of the world. Prepare for that delay. If you wait for a gate, a tree line, a sign that the place has begun, you will already be inside it, and your first adjustments will be made badly, on slick stone, among bodies.

Dress for stone that has decided to stay damp forever. Not for rain, though rain will come, but for a climate that lives in the ground and climbs. The cold is conductive. It enters through the sole, the palm, the knee you set down without thinking. Whatever you wear against it has to work while wet, because it will be wet: fog bead, spray from a clap, the brush of a salt-crusted body, the flour of ground rock that turns to paste the instant it meets cloth. Avoid anything that holds water against the skin and calls that warmth. Avoid anything that stiffens when soaked and then will not bend when you need the next foothold. Layers you can open, so the hum does not cook you inside your own insulation while the stone is still drinking heat out of your feet. A shell that sheds the mineral damp rather than drinking it. Closures you can work with numb fingers, in dim light, without looking down for long.

Your feet are the whole problem and the whole method. The channels are polished, the shelves are filmed, the fresh flakes are gritty until they are not. You want soles that bite on wet mineral and do not collect the paste. You want them low enough that you can feel the grain change under you, because the grain is how the basin tells you where the life is thickest and where a lens of water is about to become a step. High boots keep the clickers out of your socks and then betray you on a rounded lip. Gaiters, if you bring them, should be for the small bodies, not for vanity of height. Expect to stop often and clear what has climbed you. They will climb you. They are not attacking. You are a new damp surface in a country of damp surfaces, warmer than the stone, and warmth is news.

Cover the skin you do not need for balance. The small ones worry at mineral film with mouthparts that crosshatch rock; what they do to warm skin is not predation so much as sampling, and sampling is enough. Wrists, neck, the gap at the waist when you bend, the backs of the knees. A hood you can hear inside. Do not seal the ears. The basin’s only map is acoustic, and if you close yourself against the keen you will lose the far trough, the weather walking toward you, the column-bodied thing whose lane is about to open through the hide at your feet. Learn, before you need it, the difference between the dry ticking, the soft knock, the one-syllable clap, the frictional hum. The hum is the one that means yield the channel. The clap-sequence is the one that means the pools are busy with a pattern and your splash will be read as an interruption even if nothing answers you. The ticking changing pitch is rain, or a sheet of fliers dropping, or both.

Bring light, and expect it to fail as a way of seeing. The lid is slate. The leak of day never makes an edge. A beam will show you the next three steps and blind you to the sheet turning overhead, and the sheet is a darkening you need. Use light close and brief: to read a foothold, to see whether the pale on a collar is salt or a body, to check your own hands. Then put it away and let the gray return. Your eyes will do more after twenty minutes of not helping them than they will ever do under a lamp. If you must mark a route, mark it with something the damp will not eat and the clickers will not quietly incorporate into the general crosshatch. They rewrite surfaces. A scuff disappears. A bright flag becomes a perch, then a crust, then a confusion. Better to memorize bends by sound than to trust a sign you left an hour ago.

Do not bring food for the basin and do not plan to find any. There is no green. The films are mineral. What the life eats, if eating is the word, is damp and chemistry and each other in ways you cannot join. Anything edible you carry will be the only edible thing in a wide trough, and warm, and damp, and therefore interesting. Pack it as if it were a secret: sealed, double-sealed, not in a pocket you brush against stone. Eat in a place where you can see the hide and where you can leave again without stepping backward into a pool. Carry water you trust. The lenses look clean and are not a drink. They are cold, mineral-heavy, full of the dust that sometimes settles sideways, full of whatever the hand-shaped things are doing when they lift and clap. Washing a wound in that water is a story you should not need to finish. Carry your own, and a way to keep it from freezing in the conducted cold without letting it become another warm curiosity.

There is no shelter because nothing here builds, and you should not become the first thing that does. A tent on a shelf is a new ridge. It will take traffic. Guy-lines will be walked, climbed, cemented into someone’s collar if you stay long enough for the secretion to find them. If you must sleep, sleep as a guest of the geometry that already exists: a high blunt rib where the channels do not run, above the standing lenses, with the hum downhill of you. Not in a hollow. Hollows hold water and the sideways drift and the things that arrange themselves along it. Face so that a lane opening in the hide will wake you by sound before it wakes you by contact. Accept that you will not sleep well. The keen does not have a night. Shift-change is only a thickening of the ground-layer and a shortening of the notes. Rest in pieces.

Move the way the smaller life moves when a large body passes: aside, not away in a panic, and then closed again behind. Straight lines are a foreign idea. The channels curve because centuries of passage curved them, and because the dust settled sideways and edited the stone, and you are not going to improve that editing in an afternoon. Stay off the middle of a busy groove. The middle is a road. The margins are where travelers of your size belong, even when the margin is narrower than your boot and occupied. When a sheet of fliers holds still overhead longer than wings should allow, stop. That stillness is one of the agreements. Walking under it as if it were only insects is how you find out, too late, that the agreement included the air you were using. When water beads upward along a salt-collar and hangs, do not touch the collar to see if the bead is real. It is real. It is also heavier than it looks, and it falls all at once, and the body wearing the collar is not a specimen.

Touch less than you want to. The place invites the hand: rounded lips, curious crusts, a membrane in the gray that seems close enough to name. Every contact is a chemical introduction, and the basin is already loud with chemical news. You do not know which introduction reads as weather and which reads as an event. A brush may be nothing. A brush may be the start of a ripple that travels, dies, and starts again on you. If you must steady yourself, use the blunt stone above the hide, not a body, not a pool-margin, not the plated flank of something long as a column that is only trying to pass. If one of them knocks you — the soft knock, scaled up — it will likely be indifference. Indifference at that mass is still an injury. Yield early. The lane opens; you are not the reason it opens; you should not be standing in it to learn what the hum was about.

Go in company if you can, and agree beforehand on how you will speak. Voices carry strangely under the lid. A shout can come back off the far stone late enough to sound like an answer, and the pools are already in the habit of answering single syllables. Keep your signals few, low, and unlike a clap. Agree on a way to say stop, yield, and home that does not require discussion, because discussion will happen while the ticking rises and one of you is watching their feet and the other is watching a formation that has begun to shatter. Do not split to cover more ground. The trough is large enough for delay. A companion fogged out of earshot is a companion you will find by searching a country that rewrites tracks, and the search will cost you the attention you needed for the stone.

Time is the supply you will misjudge. Nothing casts a shadow you can use. The paler gray is not morning in any sense that predicts a night. Rain crosses in patches, so one of you is in weather and one is not, and both are right. Eat and drink by a schedule you brought with you, not by the light. Turn back before you are half as far as you think you can go. The return is the same slick, the same cold, the same density, plus whatever you have spent. Fatigue here is not scenic. Fatigue is the step you do not feel, the ankle you turn on a rounded lip, the hand you put down into the hide because you needed it. The basin does not punish that. It continues. Continuation is enough.

What you can carry that will actually help is modest. Dry replacements in a bag that stays dry. A way to warm yourself that does not require the basin’s wood, because there is no wood. A small tool for yourself — blister, cut, a lace — not for the place. Something to write with that still works on damp paper, or a recorder you can operate without looking, if you came to remember. You will want to remember. Most of what you remember will be wrong in the first hour and truer later, after the eyes have given up on green and the ears have separated the hum from the keen. Write the later version. The first version will say empty, barren, dead. It is none of those. It is full, mineral, and more alive than any forest you have used as a measure, alive in a register that does not include you.

Prepare, finally, to be irrelevant, and let that be a relief rather than a wound. You are not a catalyst. You are not a first contact in any sense the channels will keep. Minds may live here and have spent their intelligence on routes, secretions, and patterns of one-note speech; they will not be waiting for a visitor to complete them. If something watches you, it will be the way the pools watch a ripple. Stay a ripple. Do not stay long enough to become a ridge. When you leave, you will leave a little salt on your cuffs, a crosshatch of dried paste in the seams, and a ringing in the ears that is not quite the keen and not quite its absence. That ringing is the last of the place. Let it fade on its own. Do not answer it.

The basin will be there when the ringing goes. It was there before you needed a word for a trough without leaves. It keeps itself the way a tide mark is kept, by being worn, every hour, into the same shape. Your preparation is not to master that. It is to cross a small piece of it without becoming part of the wearing, and to come back out while you are still a visitor.
