Go in assuming the place will not hurt you on purpose, and also that it has not yet learned how to be gentle. Those are different facts, and most of your preparation sits in the gap between them.

**What the visit actually feels like.** Warmth first, then hush, then the sense that the ground is newer than you are. The air is a standing damp, tea-colored even at noon, and it beads on skin, cloth, and stone without ever committing to rain. Shade does not cool you; it only removes the glare. Distances lie. A tower that looks like a short walk can take an hour because the avenues are built to hold weather, not people, and the haze eats the middle distance. You will be alone more than a city should allow. When you do hear another voice, it will already be closer than comfort, because sound travels and the residents spend it carefully. Your own footsteps may ring. The walls may brighten as you approach and dim as you leave, which feels like attention and is probably only a local habit of the material. By the second day the lagging shadows stop being a curiosity and start making you mistrust your feet. By the third, the quiet starts to feel like a rule you have already broken by arriving.

**Body and clothing.** Dress for warm humidity and sharp young rock, not for weather in the ordinary sense. Light, breathable layers that can be wrung out and will dry against your skin. A shell only for the sudden silent showers that fall off a breathing facade — those are brief, warm, and local, so a hood matters more than a coat. Avoid anything that squeaks, crackles, or sheds loudly. The stone rings when scuffed; a hard sole on glass-sinter will announce you for a long way. You want grip on wet moss and on slicks of new basalt, and you want a sole soft enough not to sing. Gaiters or tough light trousers: the edges of shelves and cooled-slag trunks are still sharp, and the land has not been walked into kindness. A thin glove you can work in. The moss is warm and holds water like a lens; kneeling in it bare-handed for long will soften skin that the rock is eager to open.

Cover more skin than the heat suggests once you leave the open plazas. In at least one district the air is gridded — a faint organized resistance, then a dust that is not quite mineral, settling in a pattern you did not choose. People there say the grid is counting. Treat that as operationally true even if you never learn what is being counted. Long sleeves, a light neck cloth you can draw up, and a habit of not standing still inside any lane of air that pushes back. Do not brush the dust off onto food or into your eyes. Bag the cloth later and wash your skin with water you carried, not water the city is using.

A brimmed hat. The haze is not bright, but it is constant, and there is no clean hour when the light resets your eyes. Shadows lag their objects by about a finger’s breadth; depth perception will be slightly wrong on stairs, cistern lips, and any edge that is both sharp and dim. Practice walking while looking at your feet for the first hour instead of at the brass-colored towers. The phosphor in the walls is enough to move by once your eyes adapt. A lamp will make you the loudest thing in the street, optically. Carry a small one anyway, hooded, for reading your own notes and for the sinter flats at the basin’s edge, where the vegetation thins and the wall-light stops.

**Water, food, and what not to trust.** There is no river. There are warm cisterns filmed with oil-bright pollen, and hollows of standing condensation, and sometimes a column of water that lifts itself into the underside of a structure and leaves steaming stone. Do not drink any of it on a first visit, and do not count on it as a landmark. A basin that is full at dusk may be a dry socket by morning because something elsewhere wanted the water. Carry more water than the humidity tempts you to. You will sweat without feeling thirsty in the usual way, and the warmth does not fall at night enough to reset you. A pinch of salt with food. Eat only what you brought. The plants are lush and solitary and some of them may not be only plants; the machines do not announce themselves. Foraging is how an ordinary traveler becomes an incident in a young ecology that seems to have decided one of each animal is enough.

**Navigation and time.** Bring a chronometer that does not depend on the sky. Noon looks like late afternoon, and late afternoon refuses to end. Your body will invent a schedule out of meals and fatigue, and that schedule will drift. Mark the day yourself.

Do not navigate by the outline of a building you saw yesterday. Upper stories sit at a slight steady offset from their foundations, and some of them enact a different plan overnight, leaving a clean scar in the moss. Foundations and seams are more honest than silhouettes. The self-healing streets close cracks while you watch; a fissure is not a landmark, and following one will walk you into a lip of stone that wasn’t there when you started. The phosphor pulse is a better local cue than the haze-line of a tower, but it responds to approach, so two travelers will not see the same brightness from the same place.

Before you go far, learn the spacing. Embankments, then wide empty avenues, then a plaza with one great tree, then another long quiet. The city does not end; it dilutes, until the next brass light in the fog is a rumor. Treat the black sinter flat beyond the embankments as a place you enter only with a turn-back time already chosen. Out there the crust clicks as it cools and does not cool. That sound is the ground still negotiating. It is not a path.

A simple reel of pale thread, or chalk that will survive warm damp for a few hours, is worth more than a clever map on day one. Thread can be lifted by nothing you can see if you leave it in a gridded lane; don’t trust it there. In the ordinary avenues it will hold.

Leave word, in whatever way travelers in your circuit leave word, with a return window wider than you think you need. Fog, wide streets, and a city that relocates itself by a hand’s width are enough to miss a rendezvous without anything having gone wrong.

**How to move, and how to be quiet.** Walk as if the edges are knives and the residents can hear you from the next plaza, because both are true. Do not scuff the sinter for the pleasure of the ring. Do not shout to test the carry of your voice; you will learn that by accident soon enough, and the lesson will embarrass you. Speak indoors when you can. When you must speak in the avenues, speak low and close, and accept that “close” is still farther than it feels.

Do not camp against a wall. Facades swell, shed a sheet of warm water, and tighten again. A story of a tower may not be in the same place at dawn. The moss is warm enough to sleep on if you must rest outside, but pick a plaza with a solitary tree and sightlines, not a seam between structures, and sleep lightly the first night. Nothing you have been told about hunts at a human pace. That is not the same as a promise. A shadow crossing the moss — a bird, if it is a bird, whose wings reconfigure rather than flap — will darken the moss and lighten it again like a bruise of attention. Watch that once. Do not follow it.

Do not climb the open frames. The vines stop a meter short of the top as if instructed. Take the instruction. Do not wade a cistern, even a shallow one; you do not know whether the water is about to be wanted elsewhere, and rising water does not ask you to step aside. If a column begins to lift while you are near, step back onto dry stone and let it finish. It is silent. Silence is not safety.

If you walk into air that resists, you are in a counted lane. Cross it rather than standing in it. If you have already stood, and the dust has patterned your sleeve, do not panic and do not scrape it into the wind. Leave the district, change the cloth, wash. You may have been sampled. You have not, on present evidence, been attacked. Behave afterward as a person who does not know which of those is the better description — which is to say, politely, and without offering the grid a second long look.

**What to carry, and what to leave.** Independent timepiece. Water and food for longer than the route looks. Cut-resistant but quiet footwear, gloves, a cloth for the face, a small hooded light, a way to mark a path that does not depend on cracks or towers. A blunt tool for leverage, not for digging into walls; the city repairs itself and may not distinguish curiosity from damage. Simple dressings for shallow cuts — the rock is cleaner than old stone and sharper than it looks, and warm damp will keep a slice open. Something for softened, waterlogged skin. Salt. A spare layer in a bag that seals. Your own cup. Nothing that requires you to make a loud, bright, or permanent mark in order to work.

Leave behind anything whose purpose is to sample the water, the dust, or the lattices “just to know.” An ordinary traveler is a guest in a place that is still deciding what it is. Taking a piece of the grid’s dust home to examine is how you turn a count into a quarrel you cannot see. Sketch if you need to remember. The lagging light will make your drawings slightly wrong; that wrongness is part of the record. Write down where the water was, and check in the morning, so you learn the local habit with your own eyes before you trust a route that depends on it.

**Mind.** The hazard most people under-pack for is the scale of the quiet. You can walk a long time and meet no one, only condensation ticking off a wall and a machine doing something behind a skin of root-hair lattice. The mind fills that kind of space with pursuit. Do not make decisions in the first long stretch of emptiness — not about shortcuts, not about the sinter flat, not about whether a pulsing wall wants you closer. Eat. Check the time you brought with you. Recite, privately, the difference between a city that is ignoring you and a city that is thinking about you. Here the evidence for both will look the same: a light that brightens when you approach, a street that heals behind your heel, water that leaves when it is called.

Go slowly enough that the young rock does not cut you, and quietly enough that you hear the city breathe before you are standing under the shower. Come back before the haze has persuaded you that dusk is a place you can live in. The basin will still be there, still unfinished, still warm. It does not need you to witness the next hand’s width of its revision, and you do not need to be the thing it revises around.
