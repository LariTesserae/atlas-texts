Pack for a hot swamp that happens to be standing above the clouds, not for a mountain. The height will lie to every alpine habit you own. The air on the shelves is thick, sweet, and close, and it stays that way no matter how far the cloud-sea drops away under the rim. You will sweat in the first hundred steps. You will not get the clean cold, the dry mouth, or the thin-breath warning you are trained to respect. The warning here is different: heat that has no business at this elevation, water that will not leave you, and a boundary at the rim where the thick air simply stops.

Go up expecting to be wet the entire time. Rain is short, warm, and habitual. Between showers the air condenses on shaded stone, on your sleeves, on the platter-leaves, which tip and tick onto whoever walks beneath them. Walls weep a constant film. Marsh basins hold hand-deep water as warm as a bath, floored with a pale mineral crust that breaks under a foot and knits shut again after you pass. Nothing dries. Cotton is a mistake. Down is a mistake. Leather that stiffens, paper that matters, electronics you cannot re-bag, bandages left loose in a pocket — all of these will be damp by the second shelf and ruined by evening. Wear light synthetics that still breathe when soaked, with a venting shell for the warm showers rather than a sealed alpine shell that will cook you. A brim that sheds drips beats a hood you will not keep up. Cover your arms anyway. The forest on the weeping walls grows sideways, and it will lay a wet strap of fern and moss across your shoulder for as long as you walk beside it.

Your feet are the first thing the country will argue with. The useful lines are the driest threads between basins, already worn by hooves, and even those are slick. The crust is slightly iridescent when broken and not trustworthy as a pavement. Moss on the walls looks like a path and is not one. Bring drainable shoes with real grip, worn in, and a second pair in a dry bag — not because you will find a place to dry the first, but because a blister in permanent warm wet ends a walk. Short gaiters help against the sedge. Trekking poles help more, both for the crust and for probing the basins, which are wide enough to lose a person without ever getting deep. Poles also keep your hands off the vegetation. Crushed broadleaf smells of pepper and wet copper; treat that as a skin irritant until you know otherwise, and do not wipe your eyes after touching it.

Carry your own water and more electrolytes than a mountain day would suggest. You can see water everywhere and should drink almost none of it as you find it. The marsh is tea-colored and sealed by that crust. Some threads climb a pale vein uphill for a while before they consent to fall — do not drink water that is behaving. Do not drink what beads off the dark glass spines, or what stands inside the rings of pale posts, or what pools at the foot of the tower. Those places are still doing something. Condensate and the brief rain are the least worked waters, and even those get purified. A chemical treatment plus a filter you have already practiced in muddy conditions is enough; do not count on boiling, because fuel and a dry pad to start it are both optimistic. The sweetness in the air will blunt thirst and then sit on the tongue for hours. Drink on a schedule, not on desire. Heat illness is the ordinary medical event here: headache, nausea, a stumble that feels like altitude and is not. Rest on the open shoulders, where the air actually moves. Resting in the sedge only trades sun for steam.

The rim is the other medical event, and it is sharper. Breath stays easy on the benches. That weight in the lungs ends, abruptly, if you go down into the white. Do not find this out by stepping to the edge for the view. The cloud-sea hides the drop, the shelves scallop outward in a profile that invites a last careful look, and the air change is not gradual. On a first visit the rim is a line you do not cross. If you ever mean to cross it, that is a different excursion: rope, a marked anchor well back on the shelf, a partner who stays in the thick air, and a plan for cold, whiteout, and thin breath on the other side of a boundary you cannot see. Do not pack that descent into a day whose real purpose is the plateau. People who came equipped for hypoxia will be tempted to “just check.” Checking is how an ordinary traveler leaves the only atmosphere on this mountain that has agreed to keep them.

Navigation is a discipline of modesty. There is no road, only those worn threads, and the shelves repeat: marsh, weeping wall, pepper-leaf, platter-trees, another basin. A long day's walk would take you from the near rim to the farther trough, which is exactly why you should not try it the first time. The trough knits the trees into something almost continuous, and the sound changes — deeper, more echo, still patient. Echo is a poor friend when flock-calls already arrive late, the same phrase reaching you twice from birds you can see and birds you cannot. Do not steer by sound. Do not steer by shadow once the narrow tower is in view. Its shadow comes a blink late and lies a little aside from the sun; birds nest in it without apparent harm, which means it is safe for birds and not that it is a landmark you should trust with your direction. A compass, used on an open shoulder well away from the glass and the tower, plus a simple sketch you update at every shoulder — basin, spine, wall, sun, your exit thread — will outperform memory. The country is beautiful in a way that flattens attention. Everything has already finished becoming itself. Nothing hurries, nothing looks recently disturbed, and after a few hours the sameness feels like safety. It is not. Mark the thread back to your rim exit with something bright and biodegradable only if you must; better, memorize three backward landmarks and turn around while you still have them. Agree, if you are two, that neither of you enters tall sedge alone. A hand of warm water is enough to hide a fallen person from ten steps off, and the clicking of the waders will not stop to mark the spot.

The animals are many, loud, and unhurried, which is not the same as harmless. Finger-long waders the color of wet clay will let you get close enough to see the double lid before they move. Let them. The sheet-things that lift off the marsh when the light shifts are a crowd, not a curiosity to walk through; wait, or go around. Grazers on the drier shoulders trot when startled and then remember themselves and graze. A trot is still a body on slick ground, and they are not scarce. Do not test how close “unhurried” is. Do not hunt, and do not sample the pepper-leaf, the sap, or the slower things growing out of the wall. Growth here is patient to the point of strangeness — hairline rings, sap that beads and does not rush — and you have no idea what a local irritant does to a visitor's mouth, or what taking is, in a place with this many mouths and no emergency. Bring food that does not need marsh water, that can be eaten with wet hands, and that will not spoil in sweet heat. Eat it on the shoulders. Pack every scrap out. The land stitches its own crust shut; it does not need your help, and it will not usefully bury what you leave.

Treat the remnants as load-bearing and unlabeled. A ridge of dark glass, no higher than you are, half in the sedge, sweats harder than the rock around it and will cool the damp on your arms if you stand near it, as if it were drawing heat off you and folding it back into the bench. Interesting, and not a rest stop. The rings of pale posts hold deeper marsh and larger plants. Do not step inside to compare. The tower can be looked at from a shoulder, with binoculars, in ordinary daylight. Do not make for its base, do not camp in its lagged shadow, do not tap, climb, or clear the roots that have already translated its lower courses into talus. Nothing here presents a control, and nothing here is asking to be restarted. Whatever weighed this air and sealed these floors has entered the balance. An ordinary traveler's job is to notice the balance and not put a hand on the scale.

Night is a normal night only in its stars. The heat eases and does not leave. Condensate thickens until every leaf carries a lens. The calls thin and do not stop. Sleep on the driest shoulder you can reach before the light goes, off the crust if you can manage it — a low hammock between trustworthy trees, not between the pale posts, not against the glass. A groundsheet alone will have a film under it by morning. Use a net. The sheet-insects may not bite; you do not yet know what else comes to warm skin once the lenses form. Earplugs are reasonable. A headlamp with a hydrophobic lens cloth is necessary, and even then do not walk at night except to the edge of your own camp. Footing disappears, sound lies, and the rim does not announce itself in the dark.

Before you go, tell someone the shape of the visit in terms a person who has never seen the place can enforce. Up through the cloud-sea to the near shelves only. No descent back into the white once you are on top. No trough. No interior of the rings, no base of the tower, no drinking from worked water. Turnaround with enough light to find the thread, not at the moment of feeling finished — this country does not supply that moment. If you are late, they should assume you are delayed on a shelf, not that you went down to look.

A small kit is enough if it is the right kind: the clothes above; two pairs of drainable footwear; poles; hat; sun cream, because the light is ordinary and the shelves are open; a neck cloth you can wash; dry bags; water and electrolytes for a longer day than you intend; purification you have used before; food; a venting shell; a few large dressings and tape in a waterproof pouch; antiseptic; antihistamine; blister care; a spare shirt sealed until you are shaking with heat and need a dry one against your skin for an hour; cord; a whistle, tested once on the first shoulder so you know what the air does to it; compass; pencil and a small waterproof notebook; headlamp; net; hammock or the lightest sleep you can keep off the ground; binoculars. Leave the oxygen apparatus, the heavy alpine layers, the stove you will not want, and the curiosity that reads an ancient machine as an invitation.

What the visit is actually like, if you have packed for it: a long, loud, ordinary-bright day in which you are never cold and never dry, walking a country that has already decided its shape. The cloud-sea stays below you like a floor. Herds line the shoulders. Flocks repeat themselves a moment late. Warm water ticks off leaves onto your hat. You will want to see the next basin, because the next basin is visible and quiet and green, and because hurry feels rude here. That wanting is the hazard the kit cannot fix. See one marsh shelf, one weeping wall, one open shoulder with the glass and the wrong shadow far enough away to be facts rather than company. Then take the thread back while the light is still honest, and do not step down into the white to prove you understood the air. The place has been keeping its own instruction for longer than your body can bargain with. You are a guest of the surface. Stay on it, stay wet on purpose, and leave while the way you came is still a way.
