Prepare as if you were going into a closed, bright room the size of a district, one that has already finished deciding what it is and will not help you by changing.

The heat will be the first thing that rearranges your plans. It does not come from a sun you can turn your back on. It rises from the unfinished floor and stays, the way heat stays in a shut house after the cooking is done. There is no breeze to trade with, no evening, no slope of shadow. Dress for damp warmth rather than for weather: light cloth that can be soaked and wrung, something to keep the clay off your skin where the ground is still paste, and a layer you can peel when standing still becomes worse than walking. Bring more water than the pools suggest you need. Those bowls are warm, filmed, and part of the architecture; treat them as the place’s own moisture, not as a supply, until you have a way to know what the film is. Salt, and a way to cool your head and neck without relying on wind, matter more than extra clothing. The ceiling will not darken to tell you that you have been too long in the heat.

Footwear should assume two kinds of ground in the same hour. In the soft places the mineral gives, warm and slightly slick, and will hold the shape of a step that the rest of the valley refuses to age. On the ribs and bowls it is blunt, new, and uneven, with no worn path to follow. You want soles that shed paste and that do not skate on a waxy pad. Expect the plant mass to be the more reliable walking surface: thick, springy, willing to take your weight and return to the same shape. It will also drink sound. You will hear yourself less than you expect, and you will not hear weather coming, because weather does not come.

Navigation is the real work. Light is noon everywhere, flat and white through the stone membrane, so shadow will not orient you and color will not develop with the hour. Lanes in the living mass bend out of sight and come back as other lanes. Rooms repeat their density. The rings and the short flights of steps are rare, useless, and unworn; they are marks, not a route. Before you go in, decide how you will know that you have already been somewhere. Carry something that leaves a sign the place will not quietly undo—cord, cloth, a slow tally you keep on your own body—because a groove in the damp may remain, but you should not count on the valley to record you. A simple compass may or may not mean anything under that ceiling; a clock will. Time here is yours alone. The light will not ration it.

Carry food for the whole crossing you intend, plus the crossing back. Nothing in the description offers itself as a meal. The pale things high in the weave are not to be tested on a hunch. The plant mass is continuous and self-repairing in the sense that it never heaps and never thins; pulling it apart to “see” is a way to spend effort on a surface that wants to be the same density again. Walk the lanes it has already opened.

The silence is not empty so much as absorbed. Footfalls go into the felt. Pools do not lap. A single tick of released water will sound like an event, and then the place will be as quiet as before. People who need a stream of small noises to feel located should bring a companion, or a harmless rhythm of their own, and should not expect the valley to answer. If you go alone, tell someone the shape of your plan in the world you leave, including how long “a long day” is allowed to become. The basins continue. The bright seam where ceiling meets floor is not an exit you can trust from a distance.

Psychologically, prepare for unfinishedness that does not progress. Soft ground will not harden while you watch. Pools will not drain. The rings will not explain themselves. That stillness is easy to romanticize in a description and harder to inhabit once your body wants a next stage—cooler air, a lower sun, a sound that means something is about to happen. It will not. Boredom, mild awe, and a creeping suspicion that you have already passed this room are the likely sequence. Build stops into the walk: sit, drink, name three things that are actually different from the last room, then go on. Do not wait for the landscape to resume.

A few practical cautions follow from the feel of the place rather than from any threat it announces. The air is closed; if you are sensitive to still heat or to the smell of wet clay and crushed leaf, assume that sensitivity will have all day to work. The green film and the damp felt can irritate skin that stays wet. The soft mineral can pull a boot half off and fill a tread with paste that dries into weight. Condensation on the undersides of pads looks like rain that has forgotten to fall; it is not a sign that the ceiling will open. And because nothing is wearing down, your own marks will be the most temporary-looking things in the valley. That can make you careless. It should make you more exact.

Go in with a turnaround rule you will keep even when you feel fine: a time, a water remainder, a number of rooms. The place is large enough to spend a day crossing and still not leave, and it will not grow darker to argue with you. When you stop, it will already be exactly what it is. Your preparation is everything that must change—temperature, thirst, direction, the decision to leave—because the valley will not change them for you.
