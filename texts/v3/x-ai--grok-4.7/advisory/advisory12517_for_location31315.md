Prepare as if you were visiting a high, dry instrument that is also alive, and that will count every sound you make as part of its weather. The place will not rise up and refuse you. It will simply continue, and continue is enough to kill an ordinary person who packed for a desert of the kind they already know.

Do not picture sand, bones, and a cactus. Picture a single body the size of a country, worn down to a shelf, still metabolizing, and a town that is a thickened place in that body rather than a collection of objects set upon it. You are a foreign rhythm with a water habit. Everything in the kit follows from that.

**Air, water, and the fact that nothing here pools**

The shelf is high and the gases are thin. The people who live in the towers breathe, speak, and sleep, but you do not know their lungs, and “thin high air” is not a metaphor you should gamble on. Before you unmask, measure. Bring a small atmospheric tester and a pulse oximeter, and enough supplemental oxygen for the walk in, the walk out, and one unplanned day of sitting still. Exertion is how thin air introduces itself. If the numbers are merely “mountain,” treat it as a mountain: slow pace, no heroism on the first afternoon, headache and nausea as orders to stop rather than as inconveniences.

Water is the other non-negotiable, and it is stranger than the oxygen. There is no river, no well, no dew you can trust. Rain is a story from lower country. What moves in the tissues is a closed oil that does not bead, does not spill, and climbs back into whatever living surface it recognizes. Your canteen is not a living surface, which is why you might keep it—if you never teach the crust a way in. Carry every liter in hard, sealed vessels with nothing resembling a membrane, a wick, or a lipid liner. Soft bladders sweat. Wet cloth is a gift to the air, and the air takes it at once: breath fogs and is gone. Hang the vessels. Do not set them on the rib overnight. Do not bathe, do not rinse, do not “just pour a little to see.” A spill of your water onto the crust is not a puddle you can scoop. It is a question the ground will answer by absorption, and you will not get a second opinion.

Budget as if there is no resupply, because there is no resupply. Local drink is conduit-sap. Do not drink it. It is a solvent the metabolism uses to move through itself. On your skin it may only climb away and vanish into the wall, stealing whatever oil it touches and leaving you the raw patch. In a cut, in an eye, in a mouth, you have no basis for hope. Count liters against days, and count a margin for the way dry cold makes people under-drink until they are stupid. Pack electrolytes in dry form. The moment you mix them, that bottle is in the same sealed discipline as the rest.

You will chap. Your lips will split, your eyes will grit, the inside of your nose will crust with blood and dust. Bring non-oily barriers if you can get them—plain humectant, sealed saline for the eyes, a plain balm you are willing to lose. Anything fatty set against the rib may simply depart into the crust, which is both a waste and a contamination. Use it on yourself, away from the wall, cap it, pocket it. Do not leave a smear on a terrace because the terrace is not furniture.

**What you will breathe when the evening sheds**

The dim does not become night so much as the crust exhales itself. Microscopic plates lift and hang, and the sun goes milk-and-amber behind them. They hiss when the wind takes them in sheets. The grazers comb this bloom. You do not have a comb for a mouth, and you do not know whether the plates are merely sharp dust or still a little alive.

Bring a fine particulate mask and wear it whenever the haze drops from head to ankle, whenever a sheet of scales hisses across the open, and whenever you are downwind of a herd that is feeding. Bring spare filters sealed in something the oil cannot climb into. Goggles that seal, not sunglasses. The dry air will try to fog them less than you fear; the scales will scratch them more. A scarf is a courtesy to the aesthetic of the place and a poor seal against a platelet. Use the scarf over the mask if you want to look less like a visitation, not instead of it.

Do not sleep in a shed. Locals sleep inside the sounding ribs, where the song is loud. You should too, if you are invited. A tent on the open crust is a wrong idea for several reasons, and the scales are only one of them.

**Light, charge, and the things you should leave at home**

The plain makes its own dim and prefers it. Brighter light swirls the scale-weather and thins the song, and the song is how anyone knows where they are and whether they are alone. A selfish lamp—small, warm, downward, the kind of light that admits it is only for your feet—is what the town itself carries. A headlamp on high, a flood, a drone’s eye, a camera flash, are weather you caused. If you must photograph, do it without flash, and accept that many frames will be the color of old bone and tarnished copper and not much else. Your eyes will adapt further than the camera will.

The tissues hold a charge the way a string holds a note. The lattices in the walls drink that charge and remember. Dry lightning walks the swells in season, and for a day afterward the town dreams old footsteps into the terraces. You are a bag of batteries walking into a memory that is also a circuit.

Leave the generator. Leave the drone. Leave the laser, the strobe, the portable speaker, the radio beacon “for safety.” A beacon that shouts in electricity may be eaten as charge, or it may write a scar into a civic surface that has been remembering routes for centuries. If you carry a small lamp and a watch and a tester, that is already a foreign electrical weather. Keep cells few, keep them insulated, do not charge them against a wall because the wall is not a rock and it will notice. Metal poles that ring, loose buckles that tick a syncopation, a foil blanket that snaps and sparks: all of these play the instrument. Tape the rattles. Choose soft soles. You want a gait the crust can file under ordinary, not a percussion kit.

Synthetic cloth will charge in this air, and the click-fliers ride static and talk in hard cracks. They are small. They are not the danger. The danger is announcing yourself as a moving knot of ungrounded weather. Wool and cotton, layered, will serve the cold better anyway. The town is colder than the plain; the conduits are the cooling of the place, bleeding heat into the high air. You will want a warm layer at your back even when the dusk looks mild, and a way to vent when you are walking, because sweat you cannot replace is a debt. No rain shell needed for rain. A wind shell, yes—the scale-sheets sting, and the open is honest about distance, which means the wind has a long fetch.

**Food, waste, and the one metabolism**

Eat what you brought. Pressed bloom-cake and conduit-sap keep the locals, and sometimes a herd agrees to be diminished and there is meat. You are not in that bargain. An alien cake of living plates and solvent is a poisoning risk dressed as hospitality. If someone offers it, refuse without contempt—put your hand on your own food so the refusal is capacity, not rank. Share only if you are certain your food will not be set down on the rib and absorbed, and if you can spare the mass. Most of what you carry will be inedible to them in any case. Do not make their courtesy a problem they have to solve.

Pack every scrap out. The town teaches waste back into the wall and makes it architecture. Your waste is not their waste. Soap, oils, gut flora, plastic crumbs, medicinal residue: the crust is a closed circuit that has been in its final form longer than the town has wanted to grow. You do not get to be a novel input because you were careful “enough.” A small sealed kit for feces and urine feels absurd in a place with no water and no judgment, and you will do it anyway. Women, men, everyone. The plain does not care about your dignity. It cares, in the only way it cares, about what it can use. A death on the open is loud and then brief, and the crust closes over what it can use. Do not give it a smaller version of that experiment.

**The song, and how not to be prey**

This is the preparation that is not in the bag.

Sound carries wrong. A call toward the glass swell can return from the side, late, with a word added that you did not say—usually something the towers have been saying all afternoon, borrowed by the bend in the air. If you go out expecting the echo to be your friend, you will follow a sentence into the flex of the horizon and not come back on the path you believe you are on. Eyes are liars here. The haze breathes. The next swell seems to move. Animals cross these bends without trouble because they are not navigating by the picture.

The old instruction is to walk by the song. The western tower drops a third when the wind changes; the market rib answers high and short; quiet underfoot is not peace. Quiet means you have stepped into a listening. Something heavy has put its head to the crust and stopped moving so it can hear you back. The listeners do not hunt by sight. They hunt a rhythm that has stumbled.

So practice, before you need it, a plain cadence: even steps, even breath, no pause-and-lurch, no jog, no sudden turn. Running is a stumble stretched over distance. Freezing mid-stride, one foot cocked, is a stumble held in the air. If the crust under you goes thin and silent, keep the same tempo you had, smaller if you must, and bias your line back toward the nearest tower-voice the way you would bias toward a road in fog—not by staring at a landmark, by keeping the sound of the town on the same side of your head until it grows. Do not shout. A shout returns wrong and is also a new rhythm. Do not kneel to “listen better.” Your knee on the plate is a soft, irregular mute, and the thing hunting rhythms is already listening.

Travel the open with someone who sleeps in the ribs. If you are alone and the song detunes, you are not exploring. You are late.

The click of combs, the crack of kites, the creak of a tower being persuaded a centimeter higher: these are the full emptiness. It will not feel empty once you stop. Let that be a comfort and not an invitation. Sparse does not mean vacant. It means you can be seen—heard—from a long way, and there is nowhere to become a shape against a shape.

**How to be in the town**

The settlement is far-spaced hollow towers and lanes that ring because they have been walked into a civic pitch. Doors are tensions the wall agrees to hold. At the deep of the dim the walls give back a vein-glow at ankle height. That is enough light if you know the lanes. You do not know the lanes. Use your small lamp and still watch the glow, because the glow is the map the town is willing to show strangers.

After a strike-season day the lattices dream: faint ridges of old footsteps on terraces, sounding softly like someone still there. Do not step on them if you can walk around. It is not a law. It is that the dreams sound like a person, and walking through a person is a kind of noise. Do not talk to them. They are weather with a memory, not a companion you have discovered.

Do not cut a sample. A cut in the ground does not bleed outward; it draws shut, solvent climbing home, a thumb-on-drumhead sound. You will want a souvenir of plate and rib. The souvenir is a wound in a body that heals faster than weather, and the sound of you making it will travel. The listeners like a rhythm that has stumbled; a knife is a very short stumble with a very clear location. If a scale has already shed and lies in your cuff, that is the plain’s own litter, and even then I would brush it back into the wind rather than bottle it. You do not know what a living mote does in a warm pocket against your chest for the days of the walk out.

Do not try to be useful with their engineering. A lattice-line laid across the open is accepted for a season and then detuned until only grazers use it, and they were using the plain anyway. The town is a conclusion, not a project. Growth here is thickening, not spread. If you came with a road in mind, unpack it and leave it unpacked. The courtesy available to you is to inhabit a lane at the pace the lane already has.

When the herd-turning comes and the settlement becomes a chord with too many notes, you may feel crowded in the ears to the point of nausea. That is the hour the place is most itself. Filter the volume if you must—light ear protection, not deafness. Deaf, you are back to navigating by eye, which is how people get lost between two towers they can still see. You need the song more than you need comfort. Sleep where the song is loud if a rib is offered. Quiet, at night, would mean the crust had found something more interesting than the hearts beside you. The locals sleep well for that reason. Match them.

**The body, day by day**

There is no true dark to end a day. Circadian collapse will make you careless, and carelessness here is rhythmic. Bring a watch and obey it. Pick a sleep and a wake and keep them while the sky stays the same amber-gray. Do not wait to feel tired. The long dim is not a signal.

Your feet: the crust is felted rib and waxy plate, taught flat on the paths, uneven and thinly alive off them. Heavy lugged boots bruise a living surface and tick a clumsy pattern. Light boots, flexible, quiet, with enough sole that a plate-edge does not open you. Fit them before you travel. A blister is a breach, and you cannot wash it, and you must not let local solvent near it. Bring more dressings than pride suggests, gloves you can don without a ritual, and a way to close skin fast. If you bleed on the open, you have announced a wet, irregular event to a metabolism that closes over what it can use. Bind it, restore your cadence, get back into the town’s voice. Do not sit down “for a minute” on the bare crust while you negotiate with a roll of tape.

Talk little on the open. In the lanes, talk at the volume the lane is already using. You will hear your own words come back with extras and you will have a bad hour in which you think a companion is calling, or that you said something you did not say. Decide in advance that this is a known trick of the shelf, the towers lending a syllable to the wind, and do not answer the extra word. Answering is how a borrowed sound becomes a conversation, and a conversation is how you walk toward the flex in the haze.

If dry lightning begins to walk the rises, you are not a spectator on a swell. You are a vertical. Get lower than the surrounding crust only in the sense of getting into the town or into a hollow the locals use—not by lying flat on the open body during a strike, when the ground itself is the thing drinking the sky. Afterward the vein-glow runs high and the grazers avoid the struck ground. Believe the grazers.

**What to carry, said once, so the kit stays small**

Enough sealed water for the planned days plus one, and a hard way to carry the empties out. Oxygen and a tester and an oximeter. Your own food, dry, extra against the day the song turns you around. Mask, filters, goggles. Small warm lamp, spare cell kept insulated, no brighter tool. Watch. Soft-quiet boots and a change of socks in a seal. Layers for cold that is slight but constant, wind shell, nothing scented. A medical roll aimed at closing skin and at gut trouble, plus whatever ordinary medicines you already depend on—there is no town pharmacy, and sap is not a solvent you disinfect with. Ear protection that lowers volume without deleting it. A cord to hang vessels off the floor. Something to pack waste in, and the discipline to use it. A paper note, in your own handwriting, of the rules you are most likely to abandon when you are thirsty or enchanted: no unmasking until numbers say so, no sap in the mouth, no spill, no bright light, no running, no answering the extra word, no sleep on the open, no samples, no road.

Leave the instrument at home unless you already know you can be silent with it. A tune that detunes the market rib is not a gift. If, deep in the stay, someone who lives in a throat invites you to add a note, that is their judgment, not your arrival plan.

**How to come and how to leave**

Arrive in the long dim, lamp selfish, pace even, and stop where the lanes start ringing true. Let the town hear you as a steady addition, not as a stumble from the glass swell. Name yourself at human volume. Accept that the first thing the place tells you may be a change in a tower’s pitch rather than a sentence. Wait for a person anyway. The infrastructure is not a host.

Stay long enough that the wonders flatten into a pace, if you can afford the water: one shedding, the hiss and the mask and the way morning is only the western tower climbing back to its ordinary fifth. Do not stay in order to solve the town. It has been this size longer than its records, and the records are resonances, not books you can copy.

Leave while a taught path still rings. Do not follow a dulling lattice-line out onto the open because it was bright underfoot last season; the oil reroutes, the song goes off, and what remains is a grazer habit with your footprints expiring in it. Go with the sound of a tower kept to one side until the settlement is a pale pipe on the horizon and then, before eye-travel fully betrays you, with whatever local guidance or prearranged bearing you came in on. Do not navigate the exit by the memory of how the haze looked. The haze breathes.

When you are truly off the shelf, still do not open the vessels you sealed there and splash them on foreign ground. Brush your cuffs. Shake the scales back into a bag you can keep sealed, not into the first wind of a wetter world. Look at your hands. If a smear of that cool oil found you, it will already have tried to go home, and home is not here. Wash with water you trust, in a place that knows how to be wet.

You will dream, for a while, in ankle-high glow, and you will hear a chord with too many notes in it just before sleep. That is the ordinary aftermath. The plain does not follow you. It only fails to become smaller in the mind, because it was never performing. It was a conclusion, level and alive, and you were a brief rhythm in it—one it can close over, or one it can let ring for a few centuries in a terrace, depending on how you walked. Walk so that the second fate, if it happens, is only a polish in a lane, and not a silence the towers have to report.
