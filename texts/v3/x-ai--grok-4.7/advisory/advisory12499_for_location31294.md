Prepare as if the chamber will be polite and still try to keep you. It is small, young, and not actively hostile, but it is hot, sharp, airless in the way closed rock is airless, and full of things that reward curiosity with a cut, a burn, or a worse decision. An ordinary human can visit. An unprepared one will remember it as a headache and a pair of ruined shoes, if they remember the exit at all.

## What the visit feels like

The threshold is the real border, not the first ledge. Outside, whatever world you crossed from still has weather. Inside, weather has already happened and been reduced to a schedule of drops. The change hits the face first: warm, wet, and slightly metallic, a coin-taste that sits at the back of the throat before you have decided the place smells of sulfur. The sulfur is there, thin, under the damp. If it is strong enough to make you step back, step back. If it is faint and you get used to it, that is not reassurance. Getting used to it is one of the things this kind of air does.

You will bow. The ceiling is a fact, not a suggestion, and the rock is newer than your reflexes. Edges have not been rounded by the old patience of water. A casual straightening, a turn to speak to someone behind you, a hand thrown out for balance, any of those can open skin. The dark is not empty space. It is gathered in the high middle and it swallows lamp-throw sooner than a cave of the same size ought to. Sound goes the same way. Your voice comes back smaller and later, which makes people talk louder, which makes the dark feel ruder, which makes people talk louder again. Whispering is not mysticism. It is acoustics, and it keeps you from announcing yourself to a room that answers badly.

The floor lies. It steps down in fresh ledges toward a shallow black pane that looks ankle-deep and safe because you can see, or think you see, stone under it. The pane is the hottest thing in reach and the lowest air in the room. Both of those matter more than the depth. Heat rises off it in a patient way, not as steam you can dodge but as a thickness in the clothes. Within minutes fabric clings. Breath goes out warm and comes back warmer. Sweat stops meaning anything, because it cannot leave. People mistake that wetness for “I’m fine, I’m sweating,” and stay. In this air, sweating without cooling is the beginning of the problem, not the proof that you are handling it.

The plants will seem like the gentle part. They are not sun-plants. They occupy warm ledges and leave the rest of the dark alone, leaves the color of old brass, stems thick and pale, mats of green-black crystal on the drier shelves. They lean toward heat. A touched stem gives and then spends a minute righting itself, which is uncanny enough that most travelers touch a second one to see if they imagined it. Don’t make a habit of that. Nothing here has agreed to be harmless to a biology that did not arrive with the break in the rock. Lush, in this room, means “doing well on sulfur, warmth, and whatever leaks down a wrist-wide fissure.” It does not mean edible, medicinal, or safe to crush against a bare shin.

The machine is the other invitation. Iron gone to scale, brass gone green, a man-high flywheel, a pipe into the water, another pipe ending in a clean cut, a gauge still holding a brown line at a scratched mark, a dead lamp, a coil of cable, three insulators, two chalk lines on the wall. It looks paused, not ruined, which is exactly why hands go to levers. It is someone else’s unfinished sentence. You are not there to finish it. Heat has been braising unused bolts into their threads. Pressure, if any remains in the drowned line, is not labeled for you. Oil has made a small map on the stone and stopped. That map is old enough to trust as “this has not been running,” and not old enough to trust as “this cannot hurt you if you interfere.”

If you do it right, the visit is short, low, and mostly still. You see the cut stone, the drip that refuses a rhythm, the plants that have chosen their patches, the engine that was never asked to run, and you leave before the room has talked you into measuring anything with your body.

## Decide the visit before you enter

Fix three limits while you are still in cool air, and write them where you can see them. Do not renegotiate them inside.

Time. For a healthy adult who is not working, twenty to forty minutes in the chamber is a generous first visit. Heat plus humidity plus possible bad air will impair judgment before they feel dramatic. Turn around on the clock, not on how you feel. Felt time in there runs slow. The drip is too spaced to count. The dark does not mark an hour. A watch or a plain timer is not optional; a sense of “I’ve only been here a little while” is how people miss the window.

Depth. The upper shelves and the dry right-hand ledge are the visitable room. The lower third, the black pane, and the air that pools over it are not a destination. You can look. You do not wade, kneel, lie down, or sit on the last ledge “just to see the machine’s pipe.” Heavier gases like low places. So does the heat. Your lamp will make the water look shallow and interesting. Shallow and interesting is still a burn risk and a lung risk.

Purpose. Look, photograph if you must, leave. You are not starting the machine, sampling the plants for later experiments, drinking to “test,” chalking your own survey lines over the ones already there, or prying the cable out of the crack to see where power went. Ordinary travel does not require any of that. If your crossing-method needs a fixed anchor, set it at the threshold, outside the heat and the iron, before you go in. Do not count on feeling when the anchor is about to fail. The room is good at replacing urgency with attention.

Go with another person if you have one. This is not romance about solitude. A second pair of eyes catches the sway, the slurred sentence, the lamp left on the floor, the color leaving someone’s face. Agree on a boring signal: two taps on the arm means up and out, no discussion, no “let me just look at the gauge.” If you are truly alone, the time limit gets shorter, not longer, and you do not go past the point where you can still see your exit marker without searching for it.

## Air, before courage

The coin-taste and the thin sulfur are your first instruments, and they are bad ones. Treat them as a reason to carry a better instrument, not as a personality of the place you can learn to like.

What you want, if your kit can hold it, is a meter that reads oxygen, carbon monoxide, hydrogen sulfide, and either sulfur dioxide or a general combustible/toxic alarm you actually know how to interpret. Geothermal rooms and freshly broken rock can hold hydrogen sulfide, carbon dioxide, and thin air without looking any different from a warm cellar. Hydrogen sulfide is the one travelers romanticize as “a hint of sulfur” and then stop smelling. Loss of smell, a sudden sweetness, stinging eyes, a taste that gets harsher, dizziness, a headache that arrives too fast, nausea, confusion, weak legs: those are exit signs, not notes for later. If the meter alarms, you do not average it with how poetic the room feels. You leave, and you do not heroically go back for a glove.

If you have no meter, you are guessing, and the guess should be visible in your behavior. Stay high. Keep the visit short. Do not work. Do not talk yourself into the pane because the plants look fine. Plants are not your canary. They live on this. You do not. A cotton bandanna is not a gas plan. A proper half-mask with cartridges meant for acid gases and particulates is reasonable kit for a place that already tastes of metal and sulfur, and it is also hot, claustrophobic, and easy to take off “for one breath.” Put it on before the taste gets interesting, and accept that you may simply abort if breathing through it in that heat is too much. Aborting is a successful visit.

Dust is the quieter irritant. The grit is sharp, not the velvet dust of an old cave, and mineral has already bloomed white on the dead bulb. Goggles you can wear over the lamp-glare keep a falling scale of ceiling mineral out of your eyes. Do not rub an irritated eye with a wet glove. Carry a small bottle of clean water for flushing, and do not substitute the pane.

Do not run an engine, a fuel stove, or anything that makes its own exhaust in there to “dry out” or “test the air.” The room has no wind. It only exchanges moisture, slowly, with itself. Anything you burn, you keep.

## Heat, which will not feel like fire

The heat is patient and it comes from below. That is harder on an ordinary body than a blast you can step out of. You overheat while wet, while breathing warm air, while doing almost nothing. The danger is boring: heavy head, stopped sweating or sweating that changes nothing, nausea, irritability, the sudden conviction that the exit can wait, then clumsiness on sharp rock. Clumsiness here is how a mild heat problem becomes a cut, a fall toward the pane, and a much worse hour.

Dress for wet heat and for edges, not for a desert and not for a cold cave. Long sleeves and long trousers in a light, close weave that will not shred on a fresh ledge and will not drink ten times its weight in drip. A thin neck cloth you can soak in *your* water, not the chamber’s, and press to the wrists and the sides of the neck if you need a few degrees. Sturdy boots with a sole that still grips on wet film, ankle support for the dishonest steps, and enough toe protection that a stumbled ledge does not end the walk. Gaiters or sturdy socks, because the mats and the low growth reach calf height and you do not want sap on skin. Cut-resistant gloves that still let you feel a hold; leather that has not gone stiff is fine, smooth dress gloves are not. A helmet, even a light one, because the ceiling is low and the rock is not finished. Skip jewelry, skip a metal watch against the skin, skip leaning your bare forearm on any pipe “to see if it’s hot.” Metal in this room is a question you ask with an instrument.

Hydrate before you enter, and carry more water than a forty-minute walk seems to need, plus salt or an electrolyte you have used before. Drink on the timer. Thirst is late. Do not ration yourself into virtue. Do not drink the pane, do not drink drip off a leaf, do not drink from the gauge, do not “just taste” the brown line. Mineral, sulfur, heat, and unknown age are four reasons, and you only needed one. Eat something plain beforehand. Do not eat in the chamber. Crumbs are an invitation you should not issue in a room that currently has no insects and no reason to acquire a traveler’s microbiome.

A small infrared thermometer, or even a metal probe you do not touch after it has sat in the water, tells you what your hand must not learn. Air that is merely miserable can sit over water that scalds. The stone can be warm on the upper shelf and unkind a ledge lower. If you cannot measure it, assume the pane is too hot to touch and the drowned pipe is hotter than the air.

Plan the recovery before the descent. The first ten minutes after you leave are when people cramp, shiver in wet clothes, and sit down in the wrong place. Have a cooler spot, dry shirt, and a place to sit that is not on the threshold gasping the chamber back into your lungs. If someone is hot-confused, they come out even if they argue. Confusion is the symptom. Argument is the delay.

## Light, footing, and the shape of the room

Bring two lights minimum, three if you can: a helmet lamp with a dimmer, a second lamp that does not depend on the same battery type, and a chemical light that heat and water cannot talk out of working. Heat murders batteries faster than travelers remember. Keep spares in an outside pocket of the pack, not against your sweating back, and not left on the stone. White light, bright enough to read an edge. Red light is a mood. Mood will not show you the lip of a fresh ledge or the oil map under the flywheel.

Do not spotlight the plants for fun and do not waste the lamp on the far shadow while you are walking. Light the next two steps and the ceiling line. The floor’s open stretches are filmed with damp that never quite becomes mud, which means slick, not soft. Roots sheet across the surface instead of digging; they will take a boot if you are admiring the brass-colored leaves. Walk between the mats. They stop, tellingly, about a pace short of the iron, where the stone is warmer and the film thins. That bare crescent is not a path of honor. It is hotter, and it is where you are most likely to put a hand on scale and rust.

A short handline or a rope you understand, anchored at the entrance and paid out as you go, is worth more than a ball of cave-twine you saw in a story. The room is only about forty paces if the floor were honest, and it is not honest, and the dark absorbs the exit. Iron will lie to a compass. Do not navigate by needle near the machine. Navigate by the rope and by a physical marker at the door: a light, a pack, something that is not a chalk line. The chalk already on the wall is data, or a warning, or a level someone expected the heat or the water to climb toward. It is not your blazes. Do not add marks. Do not “correct” theirs. Photograph if you need to remember, and then look at the photograph outside.

Move as if the ceiling has not decided to stay up. This chamber is younger than the heat inside it. Fresh fracture, sharp shelves, mineral that lets go of the ceiling and lands in the pane with a soft slide and no real splash. That last is a reminder: things fall here, and the water does not advertise them. Do not hammer, do not test holds by yanking, do not lean on a shelf because it looks like a bench, do not stack the insulators into a seat. One person moves at a time on the ledges if the stone sounds hollow or rings when tapped. If it rings, step back. New stone that rings is talking about thickness, not inviting a rhythm.

Silence is pressure, not peace. Your breathing will seem loud. The tick from the pipe — metal adjusting to heat — will seem like an animal, then like a clock, then like a message. It is metal adjusting to heat. You do not need to locate it by crawling along the line. Speech will feel like an imposition, which is useful. Use hand signals. If you must talk, face each other, because the room returns a voice late and small and that lateness reads as distance. People have gotten “lost” in rooms this size because they turned their lamp off to feel the dark and then could not place the entrance by ear. Do not turn the lamp off.

## The living things, and the dead machine

Look at the plants. Do not harvest them, taste them, pocket a leaf to identify later, or lean your bare wrist into the waxy stand. Sap from a heat-fed, sulfur-fed unknown is a rash you will still have in the next world. The stems that right themselves are not asking to be petted; the correction is slow enough that a second touch is how people get juice on their glove and then on their eye. If you brush one, wipe the glove on a cloth you can bag, not on your trousers. Step where the floor is bare. The crystalline mats are warm to the palm for a reason; warmth plus unknown mineral plus a cut is a bad combination. You do not need a palmful of green-black crystal to remember the color.

There are no insects and no tracks but the ones a person would leave, and those fill in from the edges. That absence is not a promise that nothing can live on you. It is a reason not to become the first blood, food, and damp fabric the room has been offered. Cover cuts you already have. If the rock prints its grit into your skin — it will, if you rest a hand casually — wash with your water later, not in the pane.

The machine is a shed that forgot its walls, half sunk into its own intention. Give it the distance the plants already give it, plus a little more. Do not mount it, sit on the pipe, turn the flywheel “to see if it’s seized,” open the housing, or strike the glass gauge. A line of brown fluid at a scratched mark is not a souvenir and not a sample. The clean-cut pipe is not a handle. The cable in the crack is not a rope. The lamp tripod is not a walking stick. Scale and old iron cut, and they cut dirty; rust in a warm wet wound is how a small laceration becomes the thing you think about for a week. Assume surfaces are hot even where they look cold and dark. Condensate on iron is not a sign of coolness. It is a sign that the room has been breathing on metal for a long time.

If something about the machine changes while you are there — a new tick, a seep at a flange, a smell that sharpens, the gauge line moving, the flywheel shivering because someone could not resist — that is an exit, not a closer look. You do not know what the drowned pipe is coupled to. You do not know whether the next world, or the rock under the pane, is still delivering whatever the builder meant to harvest or vent. Paused is not the same as safe. It is the same as “not currently making the outrageous noise it would make if it ran,” and that noise is the kind of sound this quiet would turn into a blow.

Leave the chalk lines alone. They are the only clock the last visitor trusted, a level and a second line a hand’s width higher, waiting on water or heat or floor that had not moved enough to matter. Adding your own marks confuses the next person and teaches you nothing you could not learn with a photograph and a tape held in the air, not scratched into the wall. If you measure, measure without engraving. This place keeps unfinished work. Do not enroll yours.

## What to carry, and what to leave outside

Keep the pack small. Snags are real, and a bulky kit turns a bow under a low ceiling into a fall. On you, or in a bag that can take wet grit:

A helmet with a lamp, and a second light. Goggles. Gloves. The clothes already described. A timer. Water and electrolytes beyond what seems reasonable. A half-mask and the right cartridges, if you own them and know their limits, plus the honesty to leave if you cannot wear them in the heat. An air meter if you have one. A thermometer you can sacrifice to the question. A short rope or handline. A small first-aid kit aimed at cuts, burns, and eyes: clean water for flushing, dressings that will stick to wet skin, something for a dirty laceration, tape, and whatever you personally need if you are allergic enough that an unknown plant is a gamble. A bag for contaminated wipes and gloves. A dry shirt in a sealed bag, left outside if the entrance allows, so the walk away is not done in chamber-clothes.

Tetanus is not a dramatic subject and it belongs in the preparation. Sharp rusty iron and young broken stone are a poor combination with an out-of-date vaccination. Sort that out before you travel, not after you steady yourself on a flange.

Leave outside, or do not bring at all: food you might nibble, white-gas stoves, fuel, anything you would be tempted to start, drones that will eat a ceiling and then fall into the pane, loose chalk, hammers, spray paint, souvenir bags for “a little mineral,” live bait, open bottles. Do not bring a pet. Do not bring a child on the theory that the room is only forty paces and mostly plants. The hazards are adult hazards — judgment, heat, air, edges — and they are worse if you are the person who has to carry someone out while bowing.

If your multiverse method has a device, a focus, a ritual object, a timed window, learn its failure modes in the warm damp before you need them. Heat, mineral damp, and a mass of iron are a plausible way to fog a compass, a battery, a screen, or a sense of direction you have trusted in other worlds. Test “can I still leave” at the door. Step in, step out, once, before you go as far as the flywheel. If the method feels different on the second crossing — late, sticky, dim, warm to the touch — you do not descend to investigate it beside the gauge. You stop.

## How to move, once you are in

Enter slowly enough that your eyes and your breath arrive with you. Pause on the first dry stone and let the lamp show you the grain of the fracture, the film of damp, the nearest edge. Name the exit out loud once, so both of you know which dark is the door. Then be quiet.

Keep one hand free and low. The other can trail a rope, not a wall, unless you have looked at the wall. The left-hand shelf is a clean unfinished plane, pale, and it will print grit into a palm. Use it as a landmark, not a banister. The right-hand side has the machine and the driest ledge; that is the line of approach if you must go deeper, because the plants have already declined the hottest crescent and the footing is less greened. Even there, walk. Do not shuffle up to the iron until your chest is against a pipe fitting.

Stop before the last ledges. From there you can see the pane, the drowned flange, the brass-colored stand, the way the leaves lean. That is the view. The rest is greed. If you photograph, brace yourself so the flash or the long exposure is not taken mid-stumble, and do not walk backward to frame the flywheel. The room is not large enough to need a hero shot, and backward is how the dishonest floor collects ankles.

Watch each other more than you watch the dark. The signs that the visit is over are ordinary and easy to narrate away: a flush that will not calm, a headache, clumsy fingers on the lamp switch, repeated sentences, irritability at the drip, the urge to sit “for a second” on a warm ledge, lips that tingle, eyes that sting, a smell that has either vanished or doubled. Any one of those ends it. You do not average them. You do not finish the circuit of the walls.

If a piece of ceiling lets go, do not run toward the water. Step back uphill, lamps on, and wait to see if more is coming. If the air changes suddenly — warmer, sharper, a hiss that was not the drip — leave. If you cannot find the rope, stop. Sweep low with the light for the line, not wildly at the ceiling, and call once so the other person can put their lamp on their own chest and become a place. Then follow the rope, not a guess about which shadow was the door.

## If something goes wrong

A cut from this rock or this iron gets pressure, a clean dressing, and an exit. Do not rinse it in the pane. Do not explore whether the plant sap “closes wounds.” Outside, wash with water you carried, and treat it as dirty. Warm mineral grit left in a laceration is how a small injury outlasts the wonder.

A burn from stone, pipe, or water gets cool clean water for longer than your impatience wants, on the way out, not a folk cure from the leaves. Jewelry and boots come off while you can still get them off. Blisters are not opened in the dark.

Heat illness is an exit carried out early, not a rest on the threshold. Out, shade or the coolest air you have, wet cloth on neck and wrists from your water, sips if the person is awake and not vomiting, no heroic march to the next world until they are themselves again. Confusion, vomiting, hot dry skin, or collapse is an emergency in whatever world you can reach help. Do not walk them back in to “retrieve their sense of direction.”

If someone goes down near the pane, the ugly truth is that a rescuer who rushes low into bad air becomes a second body. If you have no meter and the air was already doubtful, you do not crawl to the waterline to drag them by willpower. You stay high, you reach with the rope if a rope can reach, you get them uphill if you can do it in one move without entering the pool, and you get out and get help if you cannot. Practicing that sentence before you enter matters more than owning a clever knot. The room is small enough that “just a second in the low air” will sound reasonable. Reasonable is the failure mode.

If your eyes are hit by drip, dust, or a splash, flush with clean water and do not rub. If you stop smelling sulfur after having smelled it, you are done, even if you feel fine, especially if you feel fine. Olfactory fatigue is not acclimation. It is a missing instrument.

## What you owe the room, and the next traveler

The chamber is younger than the heat, already planted, already half-fitted with an engine no one finished. It does not need you to complete the picture. Boots will leave tracks in the moisture film; they fill in, slowly, from the edges. That is not permission to scuff the mats, snap a stem to watch it decide to rise, or chip a crystal tip because growth here is measured in years and no one will see. A leaf unfurls across a week. You can miss that and still have seen the place.

Do not seed it. Wipe boots before you enter if you can, not only after. Other worlds’ mud, spores, and crumbs are a louder intervention than a footprint. Do not wash anything in the pane, including your own hands, your bottle, your conscience. What you take out on your skin and your clothes — mineral dust, sulfur damp, possible sap — bag it until you can wash in a place that will not mind. Do not shake the chamber out onto the threshold of a world that has never met it.

Leave the insulators stacked as you found them. Leave the cable coiled. Leave the levers seated. Leave the chalk. If you came to understand what visiting is like, you have it once you have felt the cling of the air, heard one drop and the long quiet after it, seen the plants decline the iron, and chosen not to touch the flywheel. That restraint is the whole etiquette. There is no shrine behavior required, and no need to perform reverence. Reverence, in practice, is keeping your hands off unfinished work and your lungs out of the low third.

## A usable order of operations

Before you cross: tetanus and any personal medicines sorted; water and salt; lights tested; timer set to a hard turnaround; mask and meter if you have them; rope; dry clothes staged for the return; a second person, or a shorter visit if you lack one; the agreement that an alarm, a lost smell, a headache, or a stumbled step ends the trip without debate.

At the door: anchor your way home outside the heat and the iron. Step in and out once. Taste the air and believe the meter over the taste. Find the pale left shelf and the dry right ledge with the lamp. Name the exit.

Inside: upper room only. Light on the next steps. Rope in hand. No food, no samples, no levers, no palm on the mats, no knee on the last ledge, no drink from anything that drips. Speak little. Watch the other person’s hands and gait. Leave on the clock if nothing else has already sent you out.

After: cool, dry, wash the grit and the sap off skin, check each other for the flushed, foolish stage of heat, and do not go back the same day because the gauge is still bothering you. It is supposed to bother you. It is a mark someone scratched, a line of brown fluid, a machine that has not been asked to run. The chamber has been keeping that unfinished thing, and the plants, and the drip, for longer than your visit. You are the transient. Prepare so that you remain one.
