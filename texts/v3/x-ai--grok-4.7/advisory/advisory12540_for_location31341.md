Prepare as if the day itself is the only shelter you will be given. The court can be walked before the light changes, and that is the length of a sensible visit. It is not a camp, not a road, and not a place that will keep anything you are careless enough to leave in its care — heat, water, sound, or you.

You are an ordinary body in thin air. The height is real before the view is. For days beforehand, sleep and climb as if you already owed the mountain a slower pulse. On the day, eat simply and early. Do not arrive hungry for a meal the stone cannot offer, and do not arrive so full that a narrow slot and a sudden cold make you clumsy. Your breath will not come back in full. Plan the walk as a sequence of short efforts and long pauses, not as a circuit to be finished. If your chest tightens or your judgment goes bright and stupid, sit on the dry stone in the sun and wait until the brightness is only light again. There is no plant to sit under and no lower country inside the notch. The recovery you get is the recovery you brought.

The light is the first injury people do not respect. At noon every upper face is doing the sun’s work twice, and the passages are filled with reflection rather than with shade in any gentle sense. Bring eyes that can be narrowed further than your own: dark lenses, and a brim that survives wind in the slots. A hood is useful only if it does not blind you when you turn your head in a shoulder-wide gap. You will squint even with help. Let your eyes adjust before you decide a white crust is ice, or a dark seam is a step. Color lies here. Tin, bone, sugar, silk — those are the day’s palette, and they swap without warning when a cloud takes the notch.

Dress for two temperatures that share a single pace of ground. In the sun the quartzite gives heat back to bare hands with a readiness that feels like welcome. One step into a passage and the welcome is over. The cold is not a wind at first; it is the stone being itself. Later the wind does come, a hush with the middle cut out of it, and toward evening it leans into the slots as pressure more than pitch. So: thin gloves you can climb and feel with, and a warmer pair you can pull on without setting the pack down in a slot. Layers that close, not clothes that flap. Nothing that a honest edge can hook. The old knives of this place have become silk and still they catch a sleeve. A loose strap, a flapping cuff, a scarf that wants to travel, will be taken and argued with. Pack slim. The passages are shoulder-wide, sometimes less. If your gear makes you turn sideways, you have already brought too much, and sideways is how people meet the leaning slabs with their ribs.

Boots should trust both surfaces: the dull silk of old faces, and the sugar-grain of broken ones, and the grit that has packed every contact. The scree at the foot of the walls has found its angle. Do not donate it a new one with a careless heel. Ankles matter more than heroism. A stick is a mixed tool — useful on the lip approaches, a nuisance in the fins, a thing that will click and then vanish into the stone’s refusal to pass sound along. If you bring one, be ready to collapse or carry it. Bare hands will want the warm faces. Give them that, then put the gloves back on before the shade has them.

Carry all the water you mean to drink, and then a margin you hope not to need. The court is never wholly dry and never a source. Films on the north faces will not fill a cup. The thread in the lowest seam is dark grit that is colder than the dry grit beside it, and by the time the west shadow crosses the court it is gone. The lens under the roofing slab is a basin’s width of sky or of black, sometimes a pane of ice thin enough to powder under a knuckle, and it is not yours. It is weather, pausing. The three cups on the lip may hold a spoon of water and a pinch of white salt, or a spoon of nothing. Do not drink from them. The salt is the place’s drawing, not a seasoning, and the metal in the second cup is not a ladle. A filter will not make a teaspoon into a day, and a rest stopped for the sake of a seep is how people miss the light. If you must touch water, touch it to know the temperature of the morning, then leave it to be ticked away, drop by drop, when the cloud passes.

There is nothing to eat and nothing to burn. Do not pack as if you might “find something.” You will find dendritic films that are not ferns, iron threads that are not roots, and a white crust that feathers and then becomes roughness. That is the whole ecology: residue. Extra food is weight in the slots; a little is sense, because altitude and glare spend you faster than the distance suggests. Fire is a fantasy. There is no wood, and a flame would be an insult to a court that spends its whole day refusing to store heat. A small, dull light for the pocket under the slab is enough if you are caught by a twenty-minute weather and the blue-white rim goes gray. You should not need it. You should be out before dark. No one winters here, and the place has already explained why: by night the grit has migrated, the wind has found the slots honestly, and the cold is no longer divided from the brightness by the width of a fin. It is simply the cold, and the court is the same court, which is not comfort.

Go in with at least one other person, and invent a way to stay found that does not depend on voice. A call does not cross the fins. Footsteps return as a soft scuff and then nothing. Agree, before you enter, on a pace, a meeting stone, and a time measured by the shadow of the west wall, not by enthusiasm. Bright cloth reads poorly against pale quartzite at noon and better when the faces go the color of tin; still, do not trust color. Trust a plan. If you separate to look at the niche or the knee-high wall, separate by a few paces, not by a passage. The court is small. That is how people get lost in it — by believing small means audible.

Mark nothing, or mark only in a way the evening wind will decline. A cairn here is a lie told to the next traveler and a burden on a scree that has already found its angle. The place revises itself by grains. You do not need to help.

Move as the stone suggests. Near the lip, down. Hands out, not out over the edge. Beyond the lip the air falls away and does not come back; you do not need the view to know it, and the view will try to spend the attention you need for your feet. The worst light is on the flat lip. The cups are there. The drop is there. Do not combine a photograph, a loose strap, and a backward step. In the fins, face the narrow way and let your shoulders learn the width before your pack does. Do not force a slot that wants you to turn. There is always another argument between the slabs. Listen for the small reports — joints ticking as they take the sun, a flake the size of a fingernail clicking twice. Nothing falls that would matter to a person standing clear. Standing clear is the whole art. After a cloud, the shelves drip on a schedule you can almost count. Do not stand in the seam that remembers the morning’s melt and then wonder why your collar is wet and your temperature has dropped in the time it takes to turn your head.

The human traces are modest and are being returned to geology at the same rate the geology revises itself. That is the courtesy. Match it. The unmortared wall, slumped by a hand’s width, is answering a draft, not inviting a repair. Do not restack it. The pecked niche was never finished, or lost its depth; leave its shallowness alone. The copper awl — green at the bend, bright where a recent scrape cut the corrosion — was set in the second cup to be found and was not retrieved. Finding it is not the same as keeping it. A fresh scrape from your pocket would be the loudest thing you could do in a place that declines to pass sound along. Look, if you like. Photograph, if your device can see through glare without you stepping onto the lip to “get the light.” Then put your hands back in their gloves. Someone came for a particular hour of a particular kind of day, ground a salt, answered a draft, and left. They understood the court well enough not to ask it to be larger or kinder. You are a later hour of the same kind of day.

Time the visit by the stone’s clock. Enter when the light has cleared the east wall and the rime is letting go of the high edges in a fine dry fall — you will feel it on your wrist more than hear it. That is morning enough. Do the narrow work while you are warm and unhurried. Save the lip for when you still have legs that will agree to stop. When the west wall’s shadow begins its crossing, be finished with curiosity. The thread will go. The lens will be there or not. The note in the slots will lengthen. If a cloud takes the notch, stand still in a wide place, not a slot, and let the tin color pass. Twenty minutes is the weather’s unit. Yours should be shorter than the remaining light.

What to practice, in the days before, is not toughness. It is the refusal to be stored. Say aloud, until it is boring, that water will appear and not be kept, that heat will be given and taken back within a pace, that your voice will die between two fins, that nothing in the bright air will move because it needs to eat or sing. The emptiness is structural, not a mood you can break with company or a song. People call it eerie for the first hour. After that it is simply the rule, and the rule is already fully occupied. If you come needing the place to answer, you will spend the day arguing with quartzite. If you come to watch a process repeat — rime, tick, glare, cloud, drip, hush, grain — you will have the visit the court actually offers.

Come able to leave. Whatever method returns you to your own weather should not depend on a shout, a fire, or a landmark that noon will erase. Pale fins all look like the same bone until the shadow gives them edges again. Pick your exit while the east wall is still a wall and not a memory of brightness. Do not wait to feel “done.” The place does not conclude. It is in the middle of being what it is, and the middle is where it lives. You are the thing that changes by leaving.

When you go, take the cold out of your gloves, the salt off your curiosity, and the habit of squinting. Leave the awl, the spoon of sky, the slumped course, the flake among flakes. If you did it rightly, the court will be arranged differently only to someone who had mapped every pebble — and you will not have mapped them. You will have walked the whole of it slowly, while the light still knew you, and the breath you spent will be yours again only when the air is no longer that near.
