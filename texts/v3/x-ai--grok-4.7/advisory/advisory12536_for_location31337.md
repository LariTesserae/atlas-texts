Pack for a place that will not stay still under your feet or behind your eyes, and assume every sense will be a little late, a little early, or aimed at something that is no longer there.

Treat the basin as cold, continuous water wearing the costume of land. Ordinary hiking boots will fill and stay filled; the moss takes an ankle and the sheets of water have no edge you can step around. Waders or a dry suit with sealed cuffs matter more than style. Bring a spare set of everything that touches skin and keep it in a hard, buoyant dry-bag you can clip to yourself—channels shift by a body’s width between afternoon and the deeper dimness, and a pack set down “just there” may be a pool by the time you turn. The air condenses on everything; hypothermia is the ordinary risk, not the strange one. Layer for sustained cool damp, not for weather that changes. A thin insulating layer under a shell that does not absorb is better than anything that gets heavy when wet. Gloves you can work in, and a hat: heat leaves through the head while you are standing still trying to decide which reflection is yours.

Do not drink the water and do not trust containers left open. The seeps are clear and cold and wrong in ways you will not taste in time. Carry all the water you need for the visit plus a margin, and a way to warm it. Food should be dense, already portioned, and edible with numb fingers. A small stove that does not depend on a steady surface is worth the weight; you may want heat more for morale than calories once the grass-hiss and the second register of sound have been in your ears for hours.

Navigation cannot be visual or acoustic in any ordinary sense. The horizon stays close, light fails in pockets that are not shadows, and your own footfall can return from behind you. A compass may or may not point at anything useful; do not bet the walk on it. What works better is crude and physical: a long, bright line you can tie off and feel with your hands, paid out from a fixed point at the rim if you can find a rim that stays. Knots you can count by touch. A watch with a loud tick you keep against your wrist, not because time here is honest, but because the discrepancy between the tick and the basin’s louder processes gives you a private metronome that is at least yours. Mark your own body before you enter—ink on the skin, a specific bruise, the exact contents of a pocket—so you have a reference when a reflection shows an arm you have not raised. If the silhouette does not correct itself, do not argue with it. Note it and keep moving on the line.

Sound is the hazard that arrives before you understand the place. The basin is loud even when the wind drops: ordinary water-talk under a second register of processes that do not agree about sequence. Ear protection that still lets you hear your own breath and the tick of the watch is better than silence; total deafness removes one confusing channel and also removes the only warning you get when a bank slumps. Expect your voice, if you use it, to come back doubled or early. Do not call out to locate yourself. The answer may not be an echo.

The perceptual leaks are the part no kit fully solves. Reflections that show reeds a few strides off, or a slope where the land is flat, or your own shape a half-second wrong, will tempt you to reconcile them. Do not. Treat every visual as a hypothesis you are not required to test. In the pockets where color drops out, stop, breathe, and feel the line in your hands before you take the next three steps; those are the places where the water is louder and the images sharper and more disobedient. If a stone weeps and its reflection briefly shows something circulating inside it, look at the real stone, not the picture, and keep your hands off the fresh seam. The strata under the moss flex and click on a delay; pressing them teaches you nothing you can use and may teach the material something about you. Leave the soil alone.

Psychologically, assume the place is already occupied at a density that does not need animals. The activity is the seep, the click, the feature that is remembered into place rather than eroded. You are a warmer, drier, more punctual process walking through a calculation that has weathered into balance. That is not malevolent. It is also not arranged for a human nervous system. Go in with a short, pre-decided route and a hard turnaround time measured by your watch, not by how the light feels—the light does not have a night to become, only a deepening. Tell yourself, out loud if you must, the ordinary facts on a schedule: your name, the number of knots you have passed, the fact that your left hand is the one with the ink. If those facts start arriving late inside your own head, you are done. Follow the line out. Do not investigate the pool shaped like a circuit or the gleam that tracks you without rippling. Curiosity here is just another process the basin can fold into its register.

An ordinary human has no special resistance to rules that leak. What you have is the ability to decide, in advance, which signals you will obey: the line, the watch, the cold in your own hands, the turnaround. Everything else—the early sound, the late sky in the water, the silhouette with the wrong arm—is scenery. Pack so you can leave wet, chilled, and slightly unverified about what you saw, and still be the same person who went in. That is the whole of the preparation that matters.
