The notch is small enough that you can see all of it from the bench, and that is what will mislead you. Prepare as if for a short walk in a hot room that is also a half-finished machine, not as if for a wilderness. The wilderness is outside the skin. Inside, the danger is ordinary bodies meeting rules that do not announce themselves.

**Before you cross**

Anything that passes the open side arrives changed, or does not arrive. Treat that as the whole of the entry, not a curiosity about seeds and mist. Do not step through carrying the only copy of anything you mean to keep: a map of the way home, a message, a recorded voice, a living cutting, a sealed sample you will need unaltered. Speak your name and your return mark aloud on the cold side, then write them where condensation cannot eat the ink, and leave that writing with someone who is not crossing. What comes back out of your mouth on the far side may have lost its consonants. You should not discover that while still depending on your voice to prove you are whole.

Cross with as little life on you as you can bear. Seeds in cuffs, crumbs, a fruit, a wet cloth that can grow something — the skin is not a door so much as a revision. Dry what you can. Empty your pockets of grit. If you must bring a tool, bring one that can be wrong and still be useful: a plain blade, a cord, a metal cup. Leave optics and clocks you love. Shadows near the filament fall the wrong way for an hour and then correct themselves; a timepiece will not argue well with that, and you do not need the argument.

Go in the middle of the day, when the light is only light and the skin is thin enough that the drop beyond it is still a color and not a rumor. Dusk thickens the boundary. You do not want your exit to be a guess about whether you have crossed.

**What your body will meet**

The air is thick, sweet-metal, and damp in a place where altitude should have made it thin. You will not pant from height inside the notch. You may feel close, over-warm, and slightly poisoned by the sweetness without being able to name a toxin. Breathe through the first minutes standing still, just inside, and do not decide you are fine because you are not dizzy. Fine, here, is a low heat in the soles and a drip you can count.

The stone is hot from beneath, sharp, and almost without grit. Boots with stout soles, not light trail shoes. The heat is even, not a sunburn; it comes up through the joints. If your feet are already tired from the ridge outside, rest them on the cold side. Inside, the pavement does not soften, and a new edge can be proud of the floor by afternoon because the ridge is still rising. Gloves, if you touch the walls. Fresh fractures cut, and the mineral looks wet even when it is only bright.

Cloth will not dry. Wear few layers, nothing that holds a fold of cold water against the skin for hours, nothing with a hood you will keep up out of habit. A light cover for your head if the drip bothers you; you can take it off. Condensation finds every surface a little cooler than the air — undersides, lee sides, the inside of a cup, the inner curve of your ear if you stand still long enough. Expect to be wet at the wrists and the back of the neck. Do not bring a pack that must stay dry to be safe. Waterproofing fails here by drowning from the inside.

You can drink, but do not drink the first water you see, and do not follow it. It beads from the rock on a schedule, stands in fractures, and is swallowed again. It will climb a fresh joint if the joint points at a post, and it will refuse a slope that should have carried it to the lip. A trickle is not a path. Following one is how a place too small to be lost in loses you: not by distance, by a slope that is no longer the slope you trusted. Carry your own water for the visit. If you must taste the weep, taste a single drop from a pool that is not touching a post or a plant, and wait. The leaf-cups are occupied. A pale thread of an animal drinks there and pours itself into the stem. That water is a room, not a spring.

**How to stand in it**

The plants are few and lavish, set far apart on purpose. Walk the bare pavement. Do not brush a leaf to feel the copper stem, and do not stand so your body shades one past a short count of drips. A leaf held in your shadow folds, not in pain so much as in accounting, and the stem under it cools. Step away and it opens on the next cycle, wetter than before. You can touch a stem near the base if you need to know the heat is real; it is warm. Then take your hand back. The soil at their feet is theirs. It is a dark paste they seem to have made, and it is not a path.

Do not treat the posts as ruins to climb or levers to try. Three of them, chest-high, no seam you should trust, one canted, a filament into the skin that throws a dishonest shadow. The stone at their bases once tried to swallow them and stopped. Your job is not to finish that gesture. Give the filament room. Do not pluck it, tie to it, or sight a bearing along it and then walk that bearing out through the skin. Bearings are one of the things the boundary revises.

The hexagonal plates are the only floor that does not weep. Water that lands on them slides to the cracks and is gone in a direction you cannot follow. The bench is safe to sit, in the ordinary sense: it will not cut you, and sitting changes little except the drip-rhythm in your ears, which slows, as if the bowl had leaned toward listening. Sit if you need to see the whole notch at once. That is the right use of the bench. Do not sleep. Sleep and you wake with the bench a little higher against the weep-line, the plates proud where they were flush, and the sense that the notch rehearsed your absence without mentioning you. The rise is not drama. It is a pressure in the soles and a new edge in a place you had already mapped. If you must rest, rest awake, and re-walk your exit line before you need it.

**Sound, and the mistake of calling out**

Sound does not cross the open side as sound. A word spoken toward the skin returns smaller, late, and stripped of consonants. A word spoken at the back wall goes nowhere; the leaves take it. Do not plan to shout for a companion outside. Do not plan to be heard if you turn your face to the rear rock and speak your fear into it. If you travel with someone, stay where you can see each other’s hands. Agree on gestures before the crossing: stop, water, out, shade, post. Inside, the drip is the clock — a spaced ticking, then a softer answer from pools that never become a stream. Count by that if you need a duration. Your eyelids are loud enough to hear between drops. That quiet is not emptiness. It is the place refusing noise. Match it. A raised voice spends itself on the leaves or comes back wrong, and you will think the wrongness is a reply.

**What to watch, and when to leave**

Through the day the weep walks from the east wall toward the south as whatever keeps the air dense drifts a few degrees inside its fence. Leaves tilt, not together. A joint in the back wall may tick open another hair and sift bright new mineral that is beaded over before it settles. None of this is an attack. Fauna, such as it is, drinks and vanishes, or folds into a droplet and soaks into the stone. Birds turn in the ordinary sky and do not cross. You are not being hunted. You are being omitted, which is easier to underestimate.

Leave when the skin is still a faint brightness and the drop beyond it is still a drop, not a blue rumor. At dusk the pools on the plates rise about a finger and no more, and the posts may feel warmer, or your hand may decide they are. Do not stay to check which. Re-find the open side by eye, not by water and not by the filament’s shadow. Step out on the same feet you came in on, and do not look back through the skin to see if your outline is still there. On the cold side, say your name again before you look at your hands. Compare it to the writing you left. Check pockets for seeds you did not bring, water you did not bottle, and a filament-shadow in the eye that has not yet corrected itself.

If a companion does not arrive, do not go back in after them on the assumption that the notch is too small to hide anyone. It is too small. That is not the same as being simple. Wait on the outside, in the thin air, and call only where sound still has consonants. The bowl keeps its own books. You were a shade it tolerated for a count of drips, and then the leaves opened, wetter than before, as if you had been weather.
