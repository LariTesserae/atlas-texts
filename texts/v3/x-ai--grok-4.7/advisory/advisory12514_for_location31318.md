Go in as a guest of a held note, not as a sightseer of a ruin. From the pan the place looks like a warm blister you could kneel beside and forget. Inside, the air itself is the instrument, and your body is an untrained reed. Ordinary preparation is less about packing miracles than about deciding, in advance, which of your habits you are willing to set down at the fissure.

Learn the approach before you learn the town. The salt pan gives almost nothing: bone-colored crust, a wind that does not stop, a dome no taller than a kneeling body. Do not trust distance. What you need is a mark you can still find when glare and wind have flattened the horizon — a cairn, a shadow at a known hour, a companion who stays on the surface and does not follow you in. The entrance is a shoulder-wide fissure on the lee side, smoothed, then a drop of about three body-lengths in failing light before the chamber opens. Practice moving through a gap that size with your pack off, passed separately, and with the understanding that turning around halfway is awkward and climbing out tired is harder than climbing in curious. If you are broad in the shoulders, claustrophobic, or unsteady on a dark descent, this is the wrong door. There is no second one.

The first truth of the chamber is sound, and it is not a surface you can stand beside. It arrives as pressure: teeth, diaphragm, skull, the sense that thought has been rearranged by a millstone. Speech will fail early. Distance cues will fail with it. A person you can see clearly on the far wall may feel like a rumor. Plan for days of uselessness, not a quick circuit. Bring hearing protection you have already worn for hours — not a gadget you trust because the packet says so — and a second, simpler pair in case the first clogs with scale-dust or you cannot seat it with shaking hands. Expect that even good protection will not restore a normal inner life. It may only keep the damage from becoming permanent. If you have a history of inner-ear trouble, migraine, panic, or any condition that worsens when you cannot hear yourself, do not treat this as a dare. The residents hear inside the note. You will not, not at first. Silence is their disaster, not a comfort you are entitled to demand. Do not block the fissure, camp in the throat, or hang cloth where the incoming air is fastest.

The second truth is dryness, and it will argue with your body. The gardens are lush only in the way a place without water can be lush: bone-pale fiber, amber and iron mats, resin instead of sap, dust instead of rain. They keep the chamber below the point of wetting so the salt stays salt and the brick does not slump. Your sweat, your breath, an open bottle, a damp cloth, a spilled canteen — these are not neutral. Water here is not a resource. It is a breach. Carry what you need to live, but carry it sealed, small, and out of sight. Drink in a baffled niche or back in the throat, not over a gutter, a curtain, or the floor. Wipe condensation. Do not rinse your hands “just a little.” Do not boil anything. A flame would be a cruelty in a room that has organized itself against wetness and combustion, and there is nothing here meant to be cooked. Your food should be dry, dense, and already edible. Their food is oily pith, faintly sweet, pressed at the sun-shelves. Taste it only if it is offered, and only a little; do not assume your gut knows resin-fed plants. Do not harvest. Cutting a curtain makes it weep, and the wound is part of the structure, not a snack.

Protect the outsides of yourself as carefully as the insides. Salt-dust and shed scale will find eyes, lips, nostrils, and any cut. Bring wraparound eye protection for the glare under the lens — this light is closer to snow blindness than to a bright room — and a cloth you can breathe through when the updraft is lifting grit. Lip balm, a simple occlusive for knuckles and nail beds, and spare dry socks matter more than a heroic wardrobe. Clothing should be close, pale or dust-colored, free of dangling straps that can catch fiber lashings or copper pins, and quiet in the way cheap nylon is not. You will want layers: the crown under the lens can become a heat you see as well as bear; the slack hour may feel cooler only because the note drops and the body unclenches. Gloves should be thin enough to feel a joint and a pin, and tough enough that you are not leaving skin-salt on everything you touch.

Leave the floor alone. It looks like the obvious path because it is bright, open, and rippled, and that is exactly why it is not a path. The standing wave has written a record into that dune of shed scale. A smear is not a footprint to them; it is a false season, and the lowest curtains answer it. Under the dune is older news: the chamber was opened by water in a wetter age, and the last of that water is sealed as brine and warmth, a lid the town has agreed not to test. You do not need to believe their cosmology to obey the custom. Do not dig, do not stamp a pattern flat to “see what it does,” do not camp there because the ledges are narrow. If the grand form of the ripples ever looks newly wrong — coarser, slack, wet at the margins — that is a reason to leave, not a reason to investigate.

Walk the edges. The town is a skin on an emptiness: rings of ledge, terrace, and half-bridge, streets one person wide, passing-niches faced away from the void. The circuit is not long. It is exact. Move as if the brick were an instrument, because it is. Copper pins are seated to hold a span at its pitch; tapping one with a stone is maintenance, not souvenir-hunting. Fiber curtains are garden, scaffold, telegraph, and climate control at once. A shiver through them is often a message, quicker than the long way round. Do not pluck a fiber to see what note you get. Do not push through a hanging mat when a niche or a long way round exists. Pass people by sight and hands, not by voice. Learn, before you need it, a few plain signs: stop, I am leaving, I will not touch this, thank you, I am unwell. If someone signals you off a landing, go. Residents are few and far apart even when near. Emptiness is not abandonment, and solitude is not an invitation to rearrange the room.

Sleep will be the hard allowance. The day does not dim to dark. Morning climbs in pitch as the pan heats; midday grinds; the slack hour drops the note, sometimes by a third, and everyone awake pauses to measure it. You may feel that drop in your chest before you understand it as weather. Bring a sleep shade, and expect it to help only with light. The pressure remains. Baffle yourself the way they do: mats, a niche, body turned from the void, no expectation of quiet. If you cannot sleep for two cycles, do not heroically stay for a third. Disorientation here compounds. People new to the roar misjudge edges.

A small kit, and no more: sealed water; dry food; hearing protection doubled; eye protection; a short length of cord you have practiced with, for lowering a pack through the fissure rather than for spanning the void; a blunt light for the dark drop, used only there and doused in the chamber, where the lens already makes every corner a degree of glare; dressings for salt-stung skin; a way to write, because your voice will not carry instructions to your future self. A pencil and paper survive better than a device that needs a speaker. If you bring an instrument, a bell, a whistle, or anything meant to answer the room, leave it on the pan. The room is already answering.

What the visit is like, if you are ordinary and careful, is this. The pan forgets you. The fissure takes your shoulders and your light. Then the note takes the rest, and for a while you are not a traveler so much as a body learning which thoughts can still form. When your eyes accept the amber-white, you see that the “city” is a mechanism folded into one hollow: ledges like lines in a chord, curtains ticking, dust rising like slow snow and not falling on the walks, a few human figures precise and far. You will want to cross the middle. Don’t. You will want to speak. Don’t. You will want water on your face. Wait. Lie under the lens only if you have been shown the ladder and only for a short while; from there the outer world is a smear of bone-white and the town below looks small enough to mistake for a model. It is not a model. A scale winking across the updraft is the right unit of humility.

Come out before you are impressive. On the pan the wind will feel thin and strangely unfinished, and your own voice will sound like a tool you have to relearn. That is the visit working, not a failure of preparation. You were never meant to become a resident of the note. You were meant to pass the edges without wetting them, without smearing the record, and without asking the lid what it is keeping.
