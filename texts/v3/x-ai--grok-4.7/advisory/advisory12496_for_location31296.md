Go in as a body that can be made wet, salted, short of breath, and slightly stupid by warmth. The enclosure is small enough to see all the way across, and that is the trap. Ordinary scale makes people rush the rim, lean, and decide they already understand it.

**Before you leave whatever air you trust**

Treat the approach as high ground and the interior as a different climate stacked on the same coordinates. Outside the rim the air is thin enough that a fast climb puts a bright edge in the chest. Inside, the air is process-warm and saturated. You will go from dry altitude to a steam room in one step over a waist-high wall, with a drop on the outside of that wall. Do the climb slowly, on a day you are not already ill, and sit at the rim until your breathing is boring again before you commit. If you are the sort of person whose ears, sinuses, or knees complain at altitude, this is not the day to test that. There is no sheltered place on the approach to recover in; beyond the rim there is sky and the drop.

Carry water you brought. Do not plan to drink what the bay makes. The sheets, beads, and the bright dark under the crust are condensate and weep from a curing mineral body—salts, fines, process-heat, possibly alkaline, certainly not a spring. The hot-sugar smell when the flower-brackets open is a reason to keep your mouth shut, not a reason to taste a leaf. Wet a fingertip on a leaf only if you are prepared to wipe it off and watch the skin. A chalk of salts comes away from shaded faces under a thumb. That chalk will be on your cuffs, your lips, and the rims of your eyes if you are careless.

Eat before you go in, something plain and already digested past the heavy stage. The air in there is thick, warm, and sweet-mineral. Appetite gets strange, and you do not want to be learning that while your boots are in a crust that sighs.

Tell someone the shape of the place, not a time you will be back. Crews come rarely. Boot-prints fill. If you stay until the light changes, that is a short stay; if you stay until the place feels familiar, that is longer than you think, and no mark you leave will still be a mark.

**What to wear**

One layer you can soak, and a dry layer that never enters.

Inside: light clothing that can be wrung out, with the sleeves and cuffs easy to push up. Long enough to keep strap-leaves and jointed stems off bare skin in the southeast thicket, short of anything that will drink its own weight and drag. No cotton that becomes a cold compress the moment you climb back into thin air. A brim that sheds drip—water falls upward along the warm pipe and then slaps down. Bare hands are a mistake; the casting gives like cooling slag and comes away on you, and the lagged lines are warm enough to teach you once. Thin gloves you can feel through, that you are willing to abandon when they stiffen under a mineral rind. The place makes rinds quickly. A spare gasket left on the rim stopped being a gasket.

Boots with a sole that accepts slime and a shaft high enough for the water-ring. When you shift your weight on the low third of the floor, the crust sighs and a ring rises around the boot and sinks. Ankle-cut shoes will ship that into your socks. Soles should not be aggressive metal cleats; you are standing on a skin over pore-water, not on scree. If the crust is softer than it looked from the rim, you want to feel that early, not punch a leg through it.

Outside the rim, on the way in and the way out: a wind layer and a dry pair of socks sealed in something the condensate cannot sweat through. Change at the rim, on the safe side of the drop, sitting down. Do not do the change while balanced on the mold-overrun where the wall is higher and the footing is a curve of fresh mineral.

Leave the good coat, the paper notebook, and anything that hates humidity in the dry kit. The work order under its pane is already losing its ink to condensate. Your map will do the same. If you must write, write on plastic with a grease pencil, and expect a film on it within the hour.

**What to carry, and what not to**

Little. The whole of it is a walk you could finish before the light changes, and weight is a liability on a giving crust.

- A small bottle of your own water, and a way to rinse an eye.
- A cloth you can sacrifice to salts.
- A light you do not need for seeing—the light here is ordinary daylight—but might want if you are still inside when the vent-fog makes a table-sized bank and the seams start their night tick. A lamp with a sealed switch. Open electronics will fog and then crust.
- A knot of cord longer than the rim is high, not because you should climb the drop, but because the waist-high wall is higher where the mold overran, and getting a body back out while wet and salt-stiff is clumsier than getting in. Do not tie off to the pipe. The pipe is part of the heat circuit; it is warm, lagged, already in the grip of strap-leaves, and it is not a handrail.
- A whistle. Almost nothing here is loud. A slap of a droplet is the loud hour. If you turn an ankle in the thicket, the thicket will not help you be found.
- A strip of bright tape you are willing to watch become geology. Do not expect it to mark a path for later. The water closes boot-prints before the next shift. A collar of ferns is a realistic fate for anything you set down “for a moment.”

Do not bring cutting tools, sample bags with ambition, or a plan to open the manifold. This is a curing bay that has declined to stay interim. The lines are a stomach, not a diagram you are invited to correct. Valves trip as a soft knock and a change in which leaves drip. If you are holding a pipe when the route changes, you are in the way of a system that does not know you are a visitor.

Do not bring food you will unwrap in the thicket. Midges lift off warm film. The pale mantis-length thing on the strap-leaf is waiting with its forelimbs folded, not hunting anything you can see. You do not need to become the first obvious prey, or the first offering.

**How to enter**

Walk the outside of the rim once, if the drop allows it, before you cross. Learn which stretches are waist-high and which the mold overran. Find the southwest, where the manifold comes under the rim, and do not make that your door. The culvert there has something in it that shifts water without breaking the surface—lagging, or a muscle using the pipe as a burrow. You do not get a second look. Give that corner the width of the wall and more.

Cross where you can sit on the rim, swing both legs, and step onto the higher, drier-looking skin near the flat of ferns, not down into the sheet. Test with one boot. The ground is a single casting: swirl-marks, paler set-skin, hairline seams that have not decided if they are joints or veins. It should take your weight as a scab takes a finger—slight give, not a collapse. If it gives more than that, step back onto the rim. There is no second floor underneath, only pore-water and the pour.

Once in, stand still until the place has done its small adjustments around you: drip re-routed, fog-bank deciding whether you are a leaf, your breathing back under the warm air. Then walk the long way, not the diagonal. The boundaries stay visible—rim, sky, drop, four vents—but the middle stops being a point if you treat it as a destination.

**While you are inside**

Keep your weight on the fern-flat as long as you can. The lowest third is where the sheet never quite becomes a pool and the crust is a lid over water. If you must cross it, shuffle. A hard step raises the ring and can open a seam the root is already following. You are heavier than the morning’s leaf-litter, and the bay locks litter into the floor by afternoon as a skin of new mineral feathers out from the warm joints. It would lock a kneeling knee the same way if you stayed foolishly still. Do not sit long on a joint that is actively laying skin. Do not lie down.

The gutters are not paths. They were formed too perfect and are already half-lost under a felt of roots, and the water prefers the roots. It climbs them. Stepping into a gutter is stepping into a root-mat over a radius you cannot see, with flow that does not obey the mold.

The warm pipe across the western third is the clearest line in the place and the worst handhold. Droplets travel up it against sense. Strap-leaves clasp the lagging and sweat on the lee side. If you need to pass it, pass at a gap in the leaves, without gripping, and do not set a pack against it. Heat plus lagging plus your own wet clothes is how you come away scald-speckled and then chilled on the rim.

The vents at the four corners breathe in turns. A fog-bank the size of a table forms, drifts a few paces, and is drunk back into the leaves. It is not weather and it is not poison you can name, but it is saturated air from the circuit. When a bank forms, stop walking. Wait for it to be taken back. Do not follow it to “see where it goes.” It goes into the plants. You should not.

The southeast thicket of jointed stems is tall enough to hide a person. That is information about sightlines, not an invitation to push through. Stems overlap until the floor is a layered dark. Flower-brackets open and shut on the heat pulse, with a soft successive click when the lines ease off, and a smell of hot sugar and wet concrete when they open. If you hear the click, you are close enough. Brushing the brackets to watch them work teaches them nothing and loads you with sap and salts. The pale hunter climbs a strap-leaf to the height of the vent cycle and waits. Let it. Do not offer a finger to see if the forelimbs are decorative.

The framed work order on the inner southwest wall is a relic, ink running, still calling this interim. Read it if you like, from a distance that does not put you over the manifold entry. Do not wipe the pane. Do not “correct” it. It is telling you the place has a different idea of finished than the people who poured it.

Noise: keep yours small. The hum is more a change in the rim under the hand than a sound. Your voice will be the loudest living thing in there, and you do not know what answers to large sound. There is almost no fauna, which is not the same as an empty mechanism.

Time: noon shadows are sharp; by midafternoon the vent-fog is on its round. Each night the casting contracts a fraction and the seams tick—continuation, not breakage, the way ice ticks while it is still deciding. You do not need to hear a full night of that from inside. Be back over the rim before the brackets’ cycle and your own fatigue start to feel like the same clock. The bay finishes itself faster than it can be corrected. A visitor who stays to watch one more skin of mineral form is participating in the pour.

**What not to take and what not to fix**

Take nothing that is structural, botanical, or wet. A frond will droop, leak, and crust your bag. A chip of the casting is a chip of an unfinished body that is still ticking itself into shape; it will also dust salts through everything you own. Photographs are the honest souvenir. Take them from standing height, lens wiped, and accept that the wet air will veil the later frames. If a shot matters, take it early, from the rim, while your hands are still dry.

Do not pull roots out of gutters, scrape the new mineral skin, close a bracket, or “clear” a vent. The drainage heresy—the water preferring roots, climbing them—is the drainage. The crews who still come leave quickly, and their prints are the only erosion; the water closes them. You are not the late shift.

If you find a tool on the rim with a rind and a collar of small ferns, leave it. It is no longer a tool in any way that matters, and picking it up means carrying a wet mineral animal back into thin air, where it will dry into your pack and then sweat again the next time you open it.

**Coming out**

Return to the same low place in the rim you entered, while you still know which way that was. Inside, the grid of the seed mats is losing to the leaves, and the compass of “north rim, where the sheet moves like a watch-hand” is reliable only if you looked for it on the way in. The sheet is slow. Do not count on a current to orient you once you are turned around in the thicket.

Sit on the rim before you stand into the thin air. Rinse eyes and lips with your own water if they sting. Wring the wet layer on the inside of the wall, not over the drop and not into your dry kit. Change socks. Put the gloves in a bag you can throw away. The chalk does not brush off clean; it wets again with the next sweat and finds a fold of skin.

Climb down slower than you climbed up. The bright edge in the chest is worse when you are already warm, wet, and pleased with yourself. The drop is still the drop. Do not walk the outside edge while telling yourself the story of the place. Tell it after you are sitting, with stone or honest ground under you that is older than the garden.

**After**

Expect clothes to dry stiff and pale at the creases. Rinse them before they fully dry, or the salts set. Check skin where a leaf clasped or a drip ran under a cuff: heat plus unfamiliar sap is a delayed rash, not always a dramatic one. If you coughed in the vent-fog, note whether it passes in clean air. Saturated mineral air can irritate without being a plot. If it does not pass, treat that as a body problem, not a mystery to solve by going back in.

Do not go back the same day. The bay will have closed your prints and started a skin over whatever you pressed. It does not need a second visitor to finish its afternoon. You will want to see whether the brackets open on the same pulse, whether the culvert moves again, whether the hunter is still at vent-height. That want is the place working on you at the right size for a parish church stood on its side. Leave it the size it is.

What you are preparing for is not danger in the style of a frontier. It is a small, warm, unfinished room that has already outgrown its work order: thin air at the door, a giving salted floor, water that climbs, heat in the walls, a garden older than the stone, one patient shape in the leaves, and a drop on every side. Go light, go dry at the edges, touch little, drink nothing it offers, and leave while you still know the difference between its pulse and yours.
