Go expecting the ground to give, the air to stay close, and the path never to open. This is not wilderness and not a city. It is a working bend in a slow river: peat, roots, leaf-mold, and old cuttings underfoot, water always nearby, cane walls at the edge, and a fiber mill that has been keeping the same hours long enough that brick, iron, fig roots, and planks have grown into one system. You can cross it in twenty minutes only in theory. In practice you go by inches, between leaf, pipe, bale, and warm brick, with water sounding under the walks the whole way.

Prepare your body for wet heat that does not announce itself as weather. It is held in by leaves and given back by brick in the late afternoon. You will not freeze, and you are unlikely to be struck down by a single brutal noon, but you will sweat and stay damp. Cotton or linen that can be wrung and worn again is better than anything that traps moisture against the skin. Take a second shirt and a spare pair of socks in a bag that can get wet without ruining what is inside. Leave heavy coats, stiff new boots, and anything that needs to stay pristine. Light colors show dye and peat; dark colors hide them and hold heat. Either is fine if you have already decided not to mind stains.

Your feet matter more than your pack. The ground is a mattress. Planks have been laid for decades and still flex. Ditches hold standing water. After the midday heat, thin sheets come up through the root-mat. Soles should be closed, grippy when wet, and easy to dry. Open sandals invite fiber cuts, mill trash, and whatever the dye shed has washed toward the walk. Break the shoes in before you arrive. A blister here will stay wet. If you have weak ankles, wrap them; the walks are narrow, uneven, and interrupted by hose, ladders, and stacked bales. Do not count on a clear cart-width anywhere. Carts themselves knock along those same planks. Yield early. There is rarely room to debate.

Water is a condition, not an event. It beads on iron, darkens the lower courses of brick, stands in the ditches, and pours steadily over the weir at the lower end. Do not drink from the river, the ditches, the pump discharge, or any vat. Bring water you trust, more than a hot-day habit would suggest, because the air is thick and you will not notice thirst until your head is already dull. A small bottle you can refill from the settlement’s own drinking supply, once someone shows you which tap is for people, is enough. The clinic has a tin sink. That is for washing, not for guessing.

The works are loud in a way that gets into the body. Beaters in the long shed hit with a heavy, repeating concussion you feel in the sternum before you sort it from the other noise. Pumps lift brown water and let it down. Valves spit. A belt slaps. People call only short distances, because a longer call is lost, and their voices sit inside the machine noise rather than above it. At night the beaters stop and the pumps do not. The quietest the place gets is a weir. If you are sensitive to continuous sound, bring simple ear protection and use it in the long shed without apology. You will still feel the beat in your chest. That is not a failure of the plugs. Speak close, slowly, and with your face visible. Do not expect to hail someone across a yard.

Light is ordinary daylight, cut into handkerchiefs. You can read a gauge and see the grain of a cut stalk. You will not get a view. Sky appears mainly on the short wharf or through the ridge lights of the main shed. A small hand light is worth carrying for the walks after the leaf-shade thickens, not because the place is a cave, but because hoses, changes in plank level, and wet iron disappear into the same brown-green. Condensation gathers under the eaves and falls in single drops onto tin. That irregular ticking is normal. So is steam thickening in the yards and then thinning. Do not stand in a vent because it looks soft. Steam from the vats is wet heat with a job to do.

The plants are not scenery you brush past. Cane and coarser reed make a wall you part with your hands. Creepers use pipe runs the way they use branches. Figs have taken pilings into themselves. Cut fiber on the racks is only a paler version of the plant still standing in water. Wear sleeves you can push up. Long trousers beat bare legs once you leave the plank walks. Gloves are useful if you will be handed a stalk, a hose, or a wet board; thin ones, so you can still feel whether iron is hot. Do not pull a creeper off a pipe to “clear the way.” Someone routed that pipe, and the plant has been holding the same bargain. Do not cut cane because the margin looks endless. Cutting here is a schedule measured in months, and the beds supply the boats.

Insects are few compared with the noise, but they attend wet fiber and the dye vats. A repellent you already tolerate, plus a habit of not lingering in the still corners of the dye shed, is enough. There is no chorus of frogs or birds to warn you that evening has started. Something small may cross a plank at dusk and be gone. Do not chase it. The settlement is dense, and a startled step backward is how people meet a cart, a hot valve, or the edge of a walk.

Treat the mill as occupied machinery, not as a ruin you are allowed to explore. Brick is softened at the arrises. Iron has taken its rust and then stopped, painted where hands reach and left alone higher up. That wear is maintenance, not decay. Do not climb stacks of bales for a better look. Do not lean on gauges, belts, or newly repointed courses. Do not open a valve to “see what it does.” The pumps and the weir are the circulation of the place; interrupting them is not a tourist experiment. The dye shed will mark skin and cloth. If you go in, assume splashes are meant to stay on the work, not on your hands and then your mouth. Ask before you touch fiber that is mid-process. A bale on the wharf may already be counted in the store’s tally.

People here are not in a rush that shows, which is not the same as being idle. They replace a board, clear a sluice, cut on schedule, and keep the engines because the fiber is there and the fiber is there because the engines never really stopped. Announce yourself at the store that keeps the tally, or at the quarters if that is where a traveler is expected to be noticed. Short, plain speech works better than a speech about the multiverse. They call across gaps shorter than a cart; match that scale. If you need the clinic, it is a tin sink and whatever care a small works keeps, not a hospital. Bring your own medicines for stomach, skin, and headache. Clean and cover cuts the same day. Wet fiber and mill grit do not make good company for an open scrape.

What to carry, and no more: water, a dry shirt, socks, a light you can use one-handed, ear protection, a way to keep a notebook or paper from soaking if you insist on notes, a little cash or whatever the tally-house actually accepts, any personal medicine, and a bag that can be hosed off. A thin rain layer is optional; the wet will come from below and from the air more than from a storm. Skip glass bottles, tripods, drones, and anything that needs a clear line of sight or a quiet room. There is nowhere to expand. The river bend and the cane wall have already refused a larger plan.

Eat simply and early if you can. Steam, dye, and the sweet-rot smell of wet fiber dull appetite in some people and sharpen it strangely in others. Do not assume the boats’ cargo is food. Bales leave on the shallow boats that nose in at the wharf. If you are offered a meal in the quarters, take what is ordinary for the works and sit where you are told. The interesting thing is not a local delicacy. It is that the same hands that clear a sluice also eat, and the schedule does not pause for a visitor’s curiosity.

Time your walking. Morning is the clearest light and the least brick-heat. Midday is workable but thick. Late afternoon gives the heat back from the walls and shortens temper as well as shadows. Night is pumps, weir, and narrow boards; fine if you already know the walks, poor as a first circuit. Do not plan to “see the whole settlement” and then rest. See the wharf, one yard, the edge of the long shed, and the cane wall. Then stop. Density is the point. A view never arrives to reward a longer loop.

Mentally, drop the habit of looking for a horizon, a ruin, or a revelation. Nothing is being reclaimed by wilderness, because the cutting never stopped. Nothing is being cleared for a grander future. The balance is maintenance: a hand’s width of river in the wet months, a fig root thickening around a bolt, a course of brick that looks old again within a season. If you need the journey to change you, let it be this: you will finish damp, slightly deaf, with peat in the seams of your shoes and no photograph that shows more than a gap between leaves. That is an accurate report.

When you leave, use the wharf or the same plank line you came in on. Do not cut a new exit through the cane. Rinse your shoes, empty grit from the bag, and drink before you decide you are fine. The sound of the weir will stay in your ears after the beaters have dropped out of memory. That is the place still working, on a clock older than the paint on the boiler-house door.
