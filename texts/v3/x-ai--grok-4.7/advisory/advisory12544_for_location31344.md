Go in already tired of space. The worst mistake is to arrive still expecting a vista, a plaza, a moment when the town opens and you can stand back from it. It will not open. From the first narrowing of the throat you are inside a decision someone else made: the heat pocket was this size, and the town was made to fit. If your body or your temper still needs room to swing an arm, turn around before the brick closes over you. The place will not negotiate.

Dress for a kiln that has been banked, not for a mine and not for weather. Leave the heavy coat, the pack that rides wide of your shoulders, the hat brim, anything that catches on a pipe or forces you to turn sideways twice in a passage that only forgives once. What you want is cloth that can be soaked and will not hold heat against the skin once it is wet—thin, close, dull-colored, with no metal snaps at the collar and no backpack frame to bang a hot line. Sleeves to the wrist. Trousers that do not bell. A cloth you can tie over your mouth when the drillings-flour lifts, and another for the back of the neck. Gloves that are not bulky: the rock is sharp at the new faces, and the pipes are not all lagged the same. You will steady yourself with a hand more often than you think. A bare palm on the wrong line is how this town introduces itself.

Water is not scenery here, and it is not a supply. The seepage is local, metallic, intermittent, and it belongs to the valves. Carry your own, more than pride suggests, in a soft bladder worn flat against the ribs rather than a rigid bottle that needs a clear space to tilt. Salt and something sweet, in amounts that feel fussy until the heat has been on you for an hour and your thoughts shorten. Drink before you are thirsty, and stop descending when the drinking starts to feel like a chore you can postpone. The temperature does not spike and does not fall. It simply stays, and it spends you at a steady rate. Plan the visit as a measured heat-debt: in, a few rooms, out, rest in whatever larger air exists above the throat, then decide if you have another descent in you. Heroic duration is how people miss the chalk marks and start trusting the wrong stair.

Light is scarce and honey-colored where it exists, and absent in the slots between facades. Bring a small lamp with a warm beam, hooded so you do not throw glare into a gauge-face or into the eyes of anyone who may be at a bench in the narrower room. A second, weaker light for your feet. The floor lies about its depth where water stands; a reflection will invite a step the stone does not. Keep both lights on short lanyards. There is no animal to steal them, but there is also no floor-space in which to set one down and still turn around. Spare this place the theater of a bright white beam raking the ceiling. The tool-marks are already pale. You do not need to interrogate them.

Your feet matter more than your eyes. The grit of fresh cutting is fine as flour and hides in the corners and on the treads of those three-step stairs that die against pipe-runs. Wet, it slicks; dry, it telegraphs every shift of weight into the quiet. Soft-soled shoes with a real grip, broken in, low enough that you can feel a flange or a drain-edge before you commit the ankle. No hobnails, no hard heels. The machines here were taught to be nearly silent, and a loud foot is the loudest new fact you can introduce. If you must cross a grate balcony, duck before you look. The opposite eaves are closer than the light will tell you.

Learn the cycle before you learn the map. Stand just inside, where the throat still remembers outside air, and do nothing until you have heard one full repetition: the weep at the valve, the soft settling of the press, the tremor of the small engine in the sole, the vent’s brief fog, the possible thread of surface-rain changing the pitch of a single drip. That rhythm is the town’s clock and its traffic. Move in the rests. Do not pass a belt-slot while a belt is in it. Do not linger under a vent that has just exhaled; the cousin of steam is small and fist-sized and still enough to cook a bare forearm. Do not treat a numbered door as an invitation. The number means someone expects to find that door again. You are a guest of the cut, not of the shift.

Navigation is a matter of sequence, not of orientation. A compass will not love you among pipes, and sightlines die at an arm’s length. Count thresholds. Note which wall is living rock and which is new brick—the tool-strokes versus the mortar still pale. Remember the chalk if you see it, and do not wipe it, and do not add your own in any permanent way; a fresh arrow in a place this young can outlive your certainty and mislead the next person who trusts marks. A spool of pale thread is better than chalk if you are anxious, paid out from the throat and taken back up with you. Tie nothing to a hot line. If the thread goes slack against a moving belt, you have already stayed too long in the wrong slot.

Breathe on purpose, and distrust the sweetness. Warm oil and hot stone are honest smells. The faint sweetness like overheated insulation is a reason to shorten the visit, not a curiosity to follow to its source. The air exchanges only through that cart-width throat; it is not poisoned by design, but it is not generous, and the heat will make you breathe more than the room can comfortably spare if you work yourself. No running. No calling out to hear the echo. The echo here is short and private, and a shout spends air and startles nothing, because there is nothing scuttling to startle. The emptiness of animals is not a promise of safety. It is a sign that the climate keeps its own books.

Carry almost nothing that must be set down. A thin notebook if you are the sort who loses a route unless you have written the count of stairs; a pencil, not a pen that can rupture in the heat. No glass. No food that needs unwrapping with two hands. If you bring a tool, make it one small blade for a snagged lace, not a kit. You are not here to repair the town or to sample its weepings. The water under the valve is on a schedule no one posted for you. Leave it.

The mental kit is the heavier one. Practice, before you go, the specific discomfort of not being able to step back. Stand in a doorway at home and imagine the opposite doorframe a handspan from your chest, the ceiling low enough that a cage-bulb grazes the idea of your hair, a stair that climbs three steps and ends in a pipe. Notice the animal urge to retreat, to widen, to turn the body all at once. In the town that urge has nowhere to complete itself. You will duck, sidle, and wait. People who panic here do not panic at darkness. They panic at the near-collision of ordinary things—a balcony and an eave, a window and another window, a wall and the wall that was left as the other wall. If enclosed heat makes your pulse climb even in a warm room with the door open, believe that rehearsal. Send someone else, or go only as far as the throat and look down into the honey light without entering the parish-church volume of the place.

Tell someone the shape of your debt before you start: how long you mean to stay, which rest above the throat you will return to, what it means if you are late. There is no signal worth trusting once the brick has taken the sky. A whistle is better than a shout if you need to be found, short and ugly, used once. Do not expect rescuers to move faster than the clearances allow. The town was not cut for a stretcher to turn.

When you leave, leave the way the drips do: by a route already decided, without a last look that requires you to rotate in a slot the width of a shoulder. The surface, if you reach it, will feel incorrectly large and incorrectly cool, and your ears will keep the engine’s tremor for a while after the sole has forgotten it. Drink again. Wash the flour of the cutting out of your cuffs. Check the glove-palms for glaze from a pipe you do not remember touching. If you are going back, go back drier, lighter, and more resigned to nearness than you were. The place is still in its first weather. It does not need your improvement. It needs you small, brief, and unburned, a visitor who understood that the crowdedness is not a defect of the visit but the climate itself—and who budgeted for the heat the way a careful person budgets for a river, by never quite spending the whole allowance in one crossing.
