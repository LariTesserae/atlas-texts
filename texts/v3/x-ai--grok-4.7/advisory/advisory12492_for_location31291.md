Prepare as if you are entering a body that already has a circulation, not a place that has been built for you. The stem will not make room because you arrived. It will only fail to close the routes that something is still using. Your job is to be small, quiet, dry enough to think, and ready to leave a passage the moment the tissue starts to prefer itself again.

**What the visit actually is**

From the basin the stem looks like a pale column no taller than a house. Inside, scale breaks. You step through a seam that traffic keeps open, and the granary-sized pocket opens into stacked holdings, curved veins, and long empty stretches of moist wall. There is no daylight. The walls give a faint green-black sweat-gleam, never enough to show a whole corridor. You will navigate by hand, by the temperature of the air, and by the small warm cloud of your own breath before the stem takes it.

Cold sits in the lower third. Higher, near young growth, the air is still cold but a fraction drier, and the walls tick as fibers tighten. After rain outside the whole interior sweats harder for about a day. A pressure change can pull one long low movement through the column: every hanging colony pulses, every water-film shivers, and for a moment you feel that you are an irregularity inside one animal.

The other citizens outnumber you past counting and almost never crowd a single stretch. Hand-sized moths tick the upper seams. Sap-colored patrols move the water-films. Pale seed-like colonies pulse and loosen the tissue. Ankle-high blind runners take the newest splits because the newest tissue is sweetest. Lean climbers use human shelves without seeming to notice them. Rarely a heavy shape presses through, and the stem opens a temporary gallery, then closes it. You will hear almost none of this. Quiet here is not emptiness.

**Before you go**

Go in while you are well, not heroic. A cough, a cut that weeps, or a fever will put moisture and scent into a system that drinks both. Learn to move in the dark until you can turn, crouch, and back out of a narrowing without reaching for a light first. Practice walking on slick, curved surfaces in soft shoes, one hand always on the wall, the other free.

Tell someone outside a return time measured in basin weather, not in interior hours. The ticking of fibers is the only clock some residents use, and it will not mean to you what it means to them. If you do not come out, no one inside is obliged to search a sealing.

Do not plan to fell, burn, pin, or force a door. The residents persuade. A shelf is thickened pith encouraged by careful scoring. A cistern is a node the stem already wanted, lined so the water does not all weep back into the walls. If you need a held-open room, you are borrowing a decision the plant has not yet reversed.

**What to carry**

Carry little. Metal is almost absent here, and fire is a bad guest in a wet living column. What works is what does not scar, spark, or smell loud.

- Clothing in layers that still insulate when damp. Wool or a close modern equivalent, close at the wrists and neck, with a soft hood. Avoid hard edges, buckles that clack, and shells that squeak. Everything you wear will bead. Outer clothes in the lower holdings go damp and stiff overnight. Have a dry layer sealed in a soft waterproof bag, and change into it only when you are stationary.
- Footwear with a quiet, slightly sticky sole. Bare feet invite cuts from stone flakes and from the sharp lips of fresh splits. Those lips are new wounds: pale, crisp, not yet tumbled soft.
- A thin cord of plant fiber or undyed cotton, several arm-lengths, and a second, shorter one. Knots you can read by finger are the local memory. Bright synthetic cord is a scar the moths will rest on without understanding.
- A small stone flake or a blunt bone awl only if a resident has shown you the grammar of cuts. Do not improvise surgery on the walls. A wrong score can swell a passage shut or open a split the stem did not mean to keep.
- A soft water skin you can fill from a cistern *after* you have been told the order of drinking. The patrols pass the nodes on a schedule older than the human names. Drinking before they pass is one of the actual disputes of the place.
- Food that does not smell of blood, sugar, or smoke. The runners follow sweetness in fresh tissue. You do not want to become a second sweetness. Eat little. Waste has nowhere to go that is not the stem.
- A dim, warm, hooded light you can uncover for a heartbeat and then cover again — a shuttered candle-lantern if you must, or a modern lamp set very low and wrapped so it cannot fall and burn. Most of the time, leave it dark. The gleam on the walls is enough once your eyes give up on rooms and accept edges. A bright beam flattens the wet dark into glare and can panic moths into the rind.
- A dry cloth for your face. Condensation beads on eyelashes and on the edges of tools. You will misjudge a lip if you cannot blink.
- Nothing that must stay perfectly dry to keep you alive. Assume every surface sweats, and that a chamber you slept in may be narrower by morning.

Leave behind: axes, saws, stakes, insect sprays, strong soap, perfume, radios that chatter, anything that clicks on a timer. The heavy shapes that press through do not need a new sound to investigate.

**How to enter**

Approach from the windward seam, the one that stays open because traffic keeps it open. The rind is cold, not frozen, and gives a little, like wet bark over something softer. Animals go in. People walk past. If the seam has narrowed to a sideways turn, do not force it. A sealing in progress is the stem finishing a thought. Wait, or come back when the runners have found another split and the people have followed the damp tracks.

Step through and stop. Let your breath hang and be taken. Put one hand on the wall and learn the temperature: colder and wetter downward, slightly less wet upward. Do not stride into the first open volume. Floors are lent and stacked, not continuous. A “street” may be the curved inside of a vein, wide enough for one body.

Move at the speed of the patrols, not the speed of a visitor who has a map. There is no map that survives a night of growth. Names here attach to routes of traffic, not to walls. If a passage you used is gone, do not hunt the old wall. Look for the freshest damp tracks. The new way may already be wearing the old name.

**How to be among them**

Speak low, and not often. Sound carries as drip and tick; a human voice is a new weather. When you meet people — and you may walk a long time and meet no one — let them see your empty hands and your cord. Ask before you score, drink, or lie down. The densest human moment is a market, twice in a dim cycle, in a widened place where people stand farther apart than the passages allow and trade fiber, flakes, and news of openings. Even then you could stretch your arms and touch no one if you choose the wrong moment. Do not treat the market as a crowd to push through. It is the city at its most deliberate.

Do not block a vein. If runners are using a fresh split, you are in their kitchen. Step aside into older tissue and let them pass. Do not pocket moths, crush the sap-colored lines, or pluck the hanging colonies. Those colonies are part of how the stem drinks and reseals. A dead one in your bag is not a specimen. It is a stalled pulse.

The climbers will use a shelf you are sitting on. Stay still and let them. A sudden grab reads as prey or as a snare. The rare heavy shape is not a sight to wait for. If the wall begins to loosen into a temporary gallery, leave the center of it. The stem opens that space for the shape, and closes it after. You are not the shape.

**Water, cold, and the living clock**

Water is the infrastructure. It rises in the walls, beads, runs a finger-width or two, is drunk by tissue or by the patrols, and falls as cold drip into the nodes people call cisterns. After rain, streets grow louder by a degree: more drip, more wings, more climbers shaking themselves on the shelves. Then the surplus goes down into the new splits.

Drink only from a cupped node you have been shown, and not from a weeping wall. Wall-water is mid-journey. It may be sap-sweet, iron-smelling, full of what the tissue is moving. A little iron-and-crushed-leaf smell on a fresh split is normal. Drinking it is not.

Sleep only on a shelf that is already a shelf — thickened, flattened, in use — and only with permission. Do not bed down in a fresh split. Those lips are sharp, and the stem may decide within hours whether to keep the room. A chamber can go from head-high to stoop-high over a month as tissue swells from above. Overnight, a street can close, and the detour will already be in use by the runners, then the climbers, then the people. If you wake and must turn sideways to leave, leave now. Sideways becomes moth-width, and then not even that.

Cold will stiffen you before darkness does. In the lower third, wring nothing onto the floor; the floor may be root, and root is how the basin enters. Warm your hands in your armpits before you trust them on a lip. Numb fingers misread a scar for a passage.

**Memory, and what not to take**

Near the center there is an older gallery whose lips went from sharp to merely firm because traffic would not let it seal. Cords hang there, knotted in a pattern meant for fingers. They say who was lost in a sealing, which splits drowned, which climbers changed circuit and took shelves with them. Read them if someone guides your hand. Do not retie them to match a story you prefer. Do not cut one for a souvenir. A knot removed is a person or a flood edited out of the only monument the city has.

Take no rind, no sweet new tissue, no living colony, no flake that is still in someone’s kit. If you are given fiber, carry it out the way you carry a name: as something that still belongs to the route.

**When to turn back**

Turn back if the seam you entered is narrowing and you have not yet learned another exit. Turn back if your light is the only thing you can steer by. Turn back if you are shivering hard enough to stumble, or if your breath no longer hangs and vanishes but stays as a fog you cannot see through. Turn back if a split opens under your feet with that clean pale wound and the runners are already arriving; you are standing in a decision. Turn back if the whole stem shivers at once and you do not know which gallery is temporary.

There is no emergency exit cut into the rind. The way out is the same kind of way as the way in: a route something is still using. Follow wet tracks toward colder, wetter air and the smell of the basin, not toward the younger, ticking growth. Outside, the rims of the bowl are still sharp. The ground is new. Water lies just under the skin of the earth and comes up where a foot presses. Do not camp against the column. Animals go in. People, when they are finished, walk past.

**The stance that keeps you intact**

You are a tolerated irregularity. The city persists the way a vein persists: by being used, continuously, by more lives than any one dark room can hold, and by never becoming still enough to close. Match that. Do not try to become a citizen in a day, and do not try to make the stem into a hallway. Keep your cuts lighter than scars. Keep your voice under the drip. When a passage offers you a choice between a faster route and a route the runners have already chosen, take the used one.

If you do it rightly, you will come out with very little to show: damp clothes, a cord you were allowed to carry, and the memory of a pressure change when every soft body in the dark answered at once. That is the visit. The stem will go on deciding which emptiness to keep and which to fill, and it will not be different because you understood it. Understanding is not what it asked for. Use, lightly, and then absence — that is the preparation, and also the manners.
