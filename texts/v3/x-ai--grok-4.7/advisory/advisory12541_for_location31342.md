Prepare as if the visit will be conducted inside a building that has become an ocean, and as if your body will be the only animal in it.

The place is not a flooded ruin you can tour between breaths. Cold water is the air. It is heavy, old, poorly mixed, and dark enough that most of the lower floors have not seen a true day since the mats closed. There is a surface, far above the lower courses, but it is a lid of colder water and knitted leaves, not a sky you can count on reaching. An ordinary human cannot improvise this. You need a way to breathe for as long as you mean to stay, a way to stay warm, and a way to come back up through a ceiling that may be a day’s travel in the wrong direction.

Learn the depth before you trust any plan. The description gives rooms, channels, and open water with weather, not a number. Some halls may be shallow enough that a strong swimmer could stand or kneel on a sill. Others may sit under enough water that pressure, gas, and ascent time matter more than curiosity. Bring gear rated for the deeper possibility, and treat the shallower rooms as a gift rather than a design. If you cannot confirm depth from the approach, do not descend past the point where an unplanned ascent would still be survivable. Cold and darkness will make you want to solve the next doorway. That is how this place keeps people.

The cold is the ordinary killer. Water that has been stable for ages will take heat from skin faster than any stillness in air. A thick suit, dry if you can manage it, with gloves you can still feel stone through, is not comfort. It is how long your hands remain hands. Insulation will also be your buoyancy, so learn it in a tank before you trust it among fitted blocks. You want to be able to stop. Most of what is interesting here is on the floor, in joints, and under lintels. If you cannot hover without kicking, you will raise a cloud of ground masonry and spend the next hour inside your own silt.

Breathing gear should be simple, redundant, and quiet. There is almost no sound here except a settling knock, a leaf-tap, a string of bubbles under a ceiling. Your own exhaust will be the loudest living thing in the province, and bubbles collect and travel in ways that can silt a room or tug a frond-canopy. A rebreather, or any system that does not dump a constant stream of gas, is kinder to the place and to your navigation. Carry a fully separate spare, because there is no shore cache and no one to borrow from. Calculate gas or scrubber life against the slowest travel you can imagine, then add the time it takes to be lost once and found again by your own line. Distances that would be a day’s walk in air are only a direction here. Do not plan to “swim out.”

Light is a tool with a short reach, not a way of seeing the country. Visibility is often an arm, sometimes a body, and sometimes only the change in grain under your glove. A bright lamp will bloom in silt and floating decay and give you a wall of milk. Carry a dim, warm, narrow light for the stone in front of your face, and a second light you can set down and leave as a beacon in a doorway. Do not expect a beam to find the far wall of a cistern. It will not. Learn to read by touch: rounded joints, pecked cups, cord-marks in mineralized clay, the difference between a silted invert and a peat fill, the way a parapet lip has been worn to a curve. Practice this with gloves on, eyes closed, in a masonry mock-up if you can build one. The skill that matters is recognizing a repeated course before you have used up the gas to discover you have been circling it.

Bring a line, and use it without shame. Channels repeat. Doorways were cut for people with baskets and are now soft slots in peat and braided fronds. A body can pass a gap that will not pass a body turned around with tanks. Tie off at the last opening you could still name, and do not enter a closed room on the promise that the stone has always held. It has held by thickness and by having already slumped to the angle where weight and water agree. That is not a promise to a visitor who puts a shoulder into a cracked lintel. If a doorway is a rumor rather than a gap you can see all four edges of, leave it. Closed rooms stay closed until the masonry gives, and it gives by years of solution, not by your schedule.

Navigation is by pattern, not horizon. The horizon is the distance at which another course becomes water. Before you enter, learn the grammar of the laying if any survey exists: channel width, step height, which way the lean travels, where cisterns interrupt the grid. The lean is a slow pressure, not a current you can feel as a push on the first afternoon. Mark it with dye so small it does not stain the lime crusts, or with a silk thread, and give it time. Downstream of the lean is one of the few directions the place itself maintains. The courses are the other. They still hold lines because nothing has had a reason to erase them. A compass may be less honest than those lines if metal in your kit or in some older stain throws it. Trust the stone’s repetition, your line, and a log you update every time the floor drops a step or a ramp.

Carry nothing that must be hunted or gathered. Nothing here is an animal, and the plants are not a larder. They are slow, pale or dull green, often the color of the dark, and they have been accumulating rather than ripening. Do not taste them. Do not cut them for samples you do not already have a reason and a seal for. A holdfast in a chisel-cup may be older than any tool you own. Food, water you trust, and salts come with you, in containers that cannot leak a foreign taste into a joint. The water itself is not weather that arrived. It is the province. Drinking it, even filtered, is a way of swallowing ground stone, clay, and leaf-rot in unknown proportion. Treat it as medium, not supply.

What you bring into the water should be able to leave with you, including its corrosion. Nothing metal has survived as metal. Copper remains as a green stain no thicker than paint. A dropped fitting will become a mark in a joint that has not taken a new mark in an age. Shed nothing. No crumbs, no fiber from a fraying glove, no light left “for the next traveler,” no monument. The equilibrium is thickness that no longer thickens. You are a brief animal in a botany that does not need one. That is a reason to be small, not a reason to feed it.

Movement should be slower than caution usually means. One fin-kick can darken a channel for the rest of the visit. Pull yourself along stone only where the surface is already worn and rounded, never on crusts of lime or on stems that have become poles. In the upper terraces the plant cover is a yielding floor. It will hold a frond and betray a knee. In the lower cells the same plants are paler and the peat may be up to the sills. Test every floor with a hand before you trust it with a hip. Entanglement is the living hazard: curtains under lintels, runners in the floating mats, braided canopies in channels no wider than outstretched arms. A knife is for your own gear, not for clearing a path. If you must cut, cut your line, not the holdfast.

The mats overhead are a second country. Rain does not arrive as rain. It soaks and drips through days later as cold threads. Do not try to surface through a continuous raft because it looks like the lid of the world. It may be thick enough to be walked, in some other physics, and knitted enough to trap a head. Where a mat thins, a slightly less-dark column stands down to the floor, and the plants lean toward it by a degree you will not see. Those columns are the closest thing to windows. Rest beside them. Do not camp in them. Your bubbles and your light will be the first weather those rooms have had.

Time will lie. A leaf takes a long time to fall, and its tap is the end of the event. You can watch a silt cloud fail to settle and believe you have been still for an hour when you have been still for minutes, or the reverse. Carry a clock that does not depend on the sun, and obey it for gas, warmth, and the turnaround. The psychological work is the other half of the packing. Silence here is not peace with an edge of birdsong. It is the absence of anything that walks, hunts, or calls. Repetition will offer you false familiarity: this parapet, this finger-wide open corner, this cistern breathing its slow seiche. People get reverent and then careless. Decide in advance what “enough” is — one channel, one sill, one confirmed lean — and leave when the clock says so, even if the next doorway has become legible.

Communication will fail in the way water fails sound. A voice in a mask will not carry to a partner around one course of stone. Agree on touch signals, on light signals you can see at arm’s length, and on the rule that separation around a blind corner ends the dive. If you go alone, write the turnaround on your wrist where you cannot negotiate with it. Tell someone outside the lid when you must be back, and do not make that person your plan for rescue from a lower floor. Rescue through repeating channels, peat slots, and a mat ceiling is not a plan. It is a hope with tanks.

Approach from the surface lid in daylight if you can, when a gray noon sometimes reaches the higher terraces. That is the only honest light the place still has, and it will teach you the strap-leaved cover, the bushier joints, the difference between rain on the mat and the dark below it. Descend along a fixed line to a known terrace, not into the first open darkness that looks like a room. Map downward in layers: mat, upper lush floor, middle channel, cistern dark. Do not try to comprehend the province. It does not end in a natural shore. It ends where the courses end, and they do not end within reach of sense. Your visit is a few cells of a continent that was raised by hand and then left until the leaving became the nature of the work.

Come back up along the same line, slowly enough for your body and slowly enough not to drag a century of fine silt into the gray columns. On the surface, under the leaves, wait. Cold and silence follow you into the boat, or the platform, or whatever dry thing you still have. Log what you touched while you still remember which joint was a joint and which was only water. Then check the kit for what is missing. If something is missing, it is already becoming a stain, and you do not go back for it.

What you are preparing for is not danger in the shape of teeth. It is a stable darkness in which a human is too fast, too warm, too loud, and too easily lost. Breathe, be dim, be tied to a way out, and let the stone remain at the angle where it has already agreed to stay.
