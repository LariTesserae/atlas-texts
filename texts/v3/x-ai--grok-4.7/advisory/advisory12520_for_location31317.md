Prepare as if the place will not notice you, and as if that is the danger.

There is no approach to rehearse. You will not stand outside and study a door. The chamber begins where the built surface begins, and an ordinary traveler should assume the hard part is not arrival but remaining separable from it: still a visitor, still on a clock, still able to leave. Before anything else, fix three things you will not renegotiate once the roar starts. A turnaround time, short enough that you could still be wrong about how long you have been inside and still get out. A person who does not enter, holding the other end of a line. A rule that if the line goes slack, snags hard, or the outside person signals recall, you come back without finishing whatever you were about to see. The place has already finished itself. You do not need to.

Go in only if your body fits the refusals. The remaining air is a set of narrow throats, not a room. Measure shoulders, chest, and hips in a crawl, not standing. If you cannot back out of a gap you entered headfirst, you are too big, or not practiced enough. Claustrophobia is not a mood here; it is a mechanical fact. If tight spaces make you rush, this chamber will teach your shoulders the width of each opening by stopping them, and panic will make you push. Pushing is how ordinary people break a rib on a rounded ceramic edge, or wedge a knee, or block the one throat that was letting air through. Practice, in a safe narrow mock-up, the specific motion of insinuating: one arm leading, head turned, exhale to thin the chest, hips following only after the shoulders have passed. Practice reversing that motion until it is boring. Boredom is the correct feeling to pack.

The roar is the only map, and it will hurt you if you treat it as background. It does not rise or fade. Close to certain joints it thins to a locked whistle; deeper in the stack it thickens to a rush with no mouth. That difference is how you will know you have moved at all. So do not seal your ears completely, and do not go in bare. Wear protection that cuts the damaging volume but still lets you hear pitch. Test it beforehand beside a steady loud fan or a workshop vacuum, not beside music: you need to learn what “higher” and “thicker” feel like when you are already tired. Set a hard limit on exposure even with protection. A vibration timer against the skin, agreed with the person outside, because you will not trust your hearing and you must not trust your sense of duration. The sound does not mark before and after. Your timer does.

Expect the light to be a condition rather than a help. It comes through the thinner plates, cool, even, full, with no deep shadow and no traveling glare. You do not need a lamp. A lamp will not give you depth, and a screen will wash out. What you do need is a way to see your own limbs. The chamber is pale dust, pale ceramic, pale stone; an unmarked hand disappears into it. Wear a close, saturated band at each wrist and a mark you can see on the backs of your gloves. High-contrast, nothing dangling. Depth will flatten. You will misjudge how near a plate is. Move as if every surface is closer than it looks.

Dress for abrasion, dust, and a cool dryness that steals water without ever feeling dramatic. Close-fitting layers, soft grippy soles, low-profile knee protection, cuffs that seal. Nothing loose to catch in a lattice. Nothing that sheds fiber if you can avoid it; the place already has its fiber, built into the work, and you should not add yours. Tie hair. No jewelry. The temperature is not cold, but the driven air will cool sweat the moment you make any, and then there will be no warmth coming back from the ceramic. Bring the layer you would want after an hour of sitting still in a bright, windy, shade-less quarry.

Water is the paradox, so decide it before you are thirsty. The chamber reads as a place that has refused wetness all the way through: no stain, no drip, no damp joint. Your breath is wet. Your skin is wet. Treat that as a possible incompatibility, not as a colorful detail. Enter fully hydrated. Keep any water you carry sealed, rigid, and unopened unless you have already learned, at the threshold, that contained water is tolerated. Do not pour, do not rinse, do not lick dust to see, do not taste the plates. They will not reward curiosity, and moisture is the one offering the equilibrium has already declined. Plan the visit short enough that you can finish it on the water already in your body. If your mouth films over, if your nose bleeds, if breath seems to brighten the roar or the dust lifts toward your face and stays, that is enough. Turn around.

A mask for fine mineral dust is not optional. The powder has finished moving on its own; you are what will move it. Ceramic and stone dust in a dry forced draft will find eyes, throat, and the soft places behind the ears. Use eye protection that will not fog into blindness, and a particulate respirator you have actually worn for an hour while doing something mildly hard. The smell, if you get it past the mask, is old mineral and cooled binder, astringent, not rotten. Do not take the mask off to “just smell it once.” Ordinary travelers lose the plot in exactly that kind of small honesty.

Do not cross the plates as if they were shelves. The built things are the occupants. Bowls of dust, resin loops, incised blocks, bound fiber, blind niches: none of them are handles, steps, or specimens. A willful body forcing across them is the wrong shape of motion here. Stay in the air. If a gap requires you to climb the work, that gap is not yours. The pale stems in the seams are not a sign the place welcomes life; they are the amount of life the seams agreed to. Do not pluck, do not clear, do not use a crack as a grip. The cracks are old and have stopped lengthening. Leave them stopped.

The line is your exit, because the chamber will not offer one as scenery. Pay it out behind you. Do not knot it to anything that could snap, and do not tie it at all if a human hand outside can simply hold it. Learn a tug code simpler than language: stop, come, okay, trouble. Voice will fail at the first whistle-joint. Radios may be useless under the roar and irrelevant to the light. Do not count on a device to remember the way. Count joints if you want, but trust the line more than the count. On the way out, bring the line with you. The place has a habit of building around whatever stays. A tool left in a seam is a decision.

What the visit will actually be like, so you can pack your mind: almost nothing will happen. That is the part ordinary people fail. You will be bright, dry, loud, and squeezed, and the brightness will not shift, and the roar will not resolve into a message, and the dust will not spell anything in the bowls. Time will misreport itself in both directions. You may feel you have been inside for minutes when the timer says to leave, or that you have been inside for a day when you have crossed four gaps. Neither feeling is information. The only motion the place required was already happening before you arrived, in a throat whose width was decided long ago. You are extra. If you wait for the sound to change in order to feel finished, you will not leave.

Go in rested, empty of errands, and a little afraid in the plain way. Eat before. Use a latrine before. Tell the person outside what “okay” feels like on the line and what you want done if the line stops moving and you do not answer. Do not bring a blade for prying, a lamp for courage, or a plan to understand the incisions. Bring the ability to stop at a gap that refuses your shoulders, to hear the whistle thin and know you are near a joint, to feel the timer and obey it, and to back out along the same throat, dust on your cuffs, hearing dull, mouth dry, with nothing in your hands that the chamber had finished keeping.

Afterward, assume you are drier and deafer than you feel. Rinse your eyes and nose outside, drink before you decide you are fine, and do not re-enter the same day to fetch a dropped band or to check whether the roar was always that note. It was. The place does not confirm you. Preparation, for an ordinary human, is mostly the decision to remain ordinary: small, temporary, and gone.
