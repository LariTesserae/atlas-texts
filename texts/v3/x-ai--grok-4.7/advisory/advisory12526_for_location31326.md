The place will not meet you with weather, wildlife drama, or a sudden drop. It meets you with evenness. Assume you will be damp, over-lit, slightly chilled, and quietly unsure which surface is outside.

**Body and kit.** The substrate stays cool and the air stays wet even when the needle-rain stops. Bring a shell that sheds water and does not hold it, plus a layer underneath that still works when it is clammy. Metal walks and pipe bundles sweat; new edges stay sharp because nothing has had time to round. Gloves with real grip, and boots that bite wet plate and grating, matter more than insulation. The brightness is a high industrial noon with no sun to turn your back on. A brim and dark lenses are not optional if you mean to look up for more than a minute. There is no reliable night, so sleep will have to be manufactured: a mask, and ear protection for the low tick you will feel in railings more than hear.

Pack as if forage does not exist. The pale films on condensate lines are not food, and the standing water is a mirror that sometimes refuses to be the thing overhead. Carry your own water and enough calories for longer than you intend to stay. The animals are abundant and almost silent — fist-sized pale birds locked into truss joints, wrong-jointed lizards on housings, something pulsing under the surface film — and they do not read as hostile so much as already occupying the volume. Do not count on them moving aside, and do not count on them being what they look like a minute later. A small kit for cuts from fresh plate is more useful than anything dramatic. Leave drones, laser rangefinders, and any tool that assumes one distance and one sky; they will embarrass you and then keep working just enough to make the embarrassment specific.

**How to move.** Distances here disagree with steps. A gantry that should be short can deliver you to a tower that has become regional again while its stamped marks stay readable, and those marks rearrange when you stop proving them. Treat “over there” as a hypothesis. Walk short segments and stop. Use your own body as the only unit that still mostly agrees with itself: paces you count out loud, a chalk or tape mark on the rail at your hip, a knot in a cord paid out behind you. Even those will lie sometimes — you may look down through grating and see the catwalk you are standing on, birds matching the ones at your shoulder — but a lie you noticed is better than a map. Do not chase a bird that leaves a near perch and arrives on one the geometry calls a kilometer off. That corridor is for them.

Reflections are not scenery. A pool may hold an older, eroded rib that cannot exist, and the reflection may stay when you move. Lizards drink from reflections of animals that are not otherwise present and come away wet. If you need to know whether a surface is floor, wall, or sky, touch it with a gloved hand before you trust your eyes, and be ready for the answer to be “was the sky a moment ago.” Up is not a stable category. The plateau continues, and in places it is also the interior of one of its own housings, lit the same unlocated way.

**Time and attention.** The machines have not stopped. They have lengthened their cycle past the edge of a walk. A conveyor may advance a hand’s width if you are patient; a far shadow may shift a finger’s breadth and then hold. Do not wait for a cycle to “finish” as proof you understand the plant. You will not. What you can do is notice the rate: cooling ticks, then much later a warming you cannot feel; a seam flush in the morning standing proud by evening, sharp as a new cut. If you stay long enough to watch a bird become a fitting and the fitting become a bird again, you have already stayed longer than an ordinary visit requires.

The crowding is a pressure more than a noise. Silence here is full. People who need chatter, or a horizon that recedes in the ordinary way, tend to invent threats the plateau is not offering. The actual strain is slower: noon that does not end, contradictions that share one volume without resolving, the sense that change is happening on a clock that makes your walk a sample rather than an event. Plan a short first stay. Mark an exit condition in advance — a counted cord, a timed return, a companion who is not looking at the same anomaly you are — because “a little farther, the numbers are still readable” is how the regional smear recruits you.

**What not to do.** Do not try to correct the place. A second noon arriving half a second late, a shadow that splits and then agrees, water beading on a surface that was sky: these are not malfunctions you can reset by walking back to where the rule last held. The breakdown is as patient as the engines. Do not drink the mirrors. Do not camp in a joint already filled with unmoving birds; they were there first in a way that does not require them to have arrived. Do not assume a housing is shelter just because it has walls. Shelter, outside, and interior can be the same bright damp volume.

**What the visit is like.** Close in, the density is almost rude: gantries you can touch, pipe bundles thicker than a body, catwalks stacked until their shadows ladder the light. Far off, the highland repeats until brightness smears the repetitions. You taste iron, cold mineral, and a faint sweetness of machine oil that has not been hot in a long time. Nothing hurries. If you keep your footing, your water, your eyes, and a humble string back toward where you still remember entering, you can stand in it long enough to feel the point — a constructed high country, recently poured, already full of quiet life, running a process too slow to watch and too continuous to be empty — and leave before the plateau finishes deciding whether you are a traveler or another part that has not yet been scheduled to move.
