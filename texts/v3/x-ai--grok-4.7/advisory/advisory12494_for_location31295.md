Go as a body that can be bored, thirsty, and lost, not as an explorer who expects the land to organize itself around you. This country has already finished becoming simple. It will not offer you a ridge, a tree line, a road, or a dark shape that means “over there.” What it offers is a ruled horizon, warm silt, sheet water, and a great deal of small life saying the same thing at a volume that carries. Preparation is mostly the art of remaining an ordinary person inside that sentence.

Arrive with the exit already made visible. The single most important object you carry is something tall, bright, and yours: a pole, a flag, a panel that does not match rust, olive, or the milk of the sky. Plant it where you entered, and do not walk until you have watched it from a few hundred paces in more than one direction and still know which speck is yours. Tracks will not bring you back. The sheet-water rewrites them by seepage, and the firmer ribs where birds stand are not paths. A compass helps if the world’s magnetism is the one you know; a watch helps more than you expect, because the light stays even and the day does not announce its chapters. If you have a companion, agree on a whistle and a color. Sound travels here. A figure in ordinary clothes thins into the brightness faster than pride admits. Wear something that can still be a person at a mile.

Expect the walking to be a negotiation with an inch. Relief is ankle-high, and the change from dry to drowned is felt as suck, not as climb. The surface lies. A gray-tan skin looks like ground and is only a dried film over blue-black paste. An iron-stained pavement, warm even in the morning, may hold you and then bead the water so you trust it, and then end without warning in a pan the color of tea. Take a pair of poles or a staff and probe before you commit weight. Waterproof boots that drain, with a sole that grips wet crust, are better than anything that seals the foot into a hot bag or anything that invites the silt in and keeps it. Gaiters. Twice the socks you think, in a dry bag, and the discipline to stop when a heel goes soft. After the slow warm rain, do not try to cross. The country becomes a wade of unknown knees, and the bottom is the thing you cannot see. Wait for the sheet to draw back. It will, without drama. Your schedule is the only urgent thing on the plain, and it is wrong.

Carry all the water you will drink. The pans are the place’s habit, not a supply. They are mineral, sweet-rot, filmed with the living scum that is the real vegetation here. Boiling and filtering are emergency measures for a mouth you hope not to use; they are not a plan. Humidity will lie to you about sweat. You can feel merely warm and already be behind on water and salt. Bring electrolytes, and drink on a clock, not on thirst. Eat food that does not require a landscape: there is no wood, no dry grass worth the name, nothing to hang a pot over. A small stove and fuel are the difference between a meal and a long damp wait. Shade is also something you bring. No canopy exists. A light umbrella or a small tarp on poles you carry will matter by midmorning, when the mud vapors and the sun arrives as a wide even brightness rather than a point you can turn your back on. Polarized glasses. A brim. The late light turns every remaining skin of water into metal; it is beautiful, and it will take your eyes and your sense of distance if you let it.

Dress for warm wetness and for insects that do not swarm so much as drift. Long sleeves, long trousers, light, pale, quick to dry. A head net for the columns of midges over the richest film. Repellent, and the expectation that it will not make you immune, only less interesting. The smell of the place—old organic film, warm mud, clean standing water—will get into cloth and stay. That is not corruption. It is the country. Do not pack as if for a jungle. There is nothing to snag you, nothing to hide in, nothing that overhangs. Pack as if for a tidal flat that forgot the tide and kept the heat.

Your skin is the other map. Warm silt plus a small cut is how an ordinary day becomes an infection. Waterproof dressings, antiseptic, and the habit of washing with carried water before you close a scrape. Feet will want to stay damp; give them air whenever you stop on a pavement that beads rather than swallows. If something in the shallows attaches and you did not plan for it, salt and patience beat panic; do not invent a monster the description did not put there. What the text gives you is small, many, and mostly uninterested: crabs raising towers the next sheet will erase, fingerlings, frogs in the fresher pockets, shorebirds stitching the mud, a heron holding a whole pan by stillness. They keep distance. You should too. Do not wade into a feeding flock to see better. Do not collapse the pellet-towers for the pleasure of a mark. You are already a heavier event than this ground is used to hosting.

Camp only if you must, and then as a guest who will be gone. The iron pavements are the least false floors: firmer, warmer, less eager to take you to the knee. Sleep off the ground—a pad, a cot, anything that admits the country is wet even when it looks skinned over. No fire. No trench. Waste comes out with you; the water connects every pan to the next inch of lower ground, and there is nowhere to hide what you leave. If wind rises it has nothing to catch on, so a shelter must stand by its own stakes, low and simple. Guy-lines to rushes are a way to kill the only verticals the eye has.

The harder preparation is tempo. People get into trouble here by walking as if distance were information. An hour of effort rises and falls by less than a hand’s breadth. The horizon does not approach. You can cover a great deal of almost-nothing and learn only that the next rush is also alone. Set a turn-back time that does not depend on “just to that darker line,” because the darker line is often vapor, or a mat, or the place where the plain thins into sky. Move in the cooler edges of the day if you can find them; the heat is steady and low, not fierce, which makes it easier to overstay. When you stop, stop fully. The sound will not. Ticking in the silt, a bill, a frog passing the note along a mile, wings tearing the air when a loose flock lifts, bubbles loosening from mud, wind hissing on open water. It is occupation, not threat. If you need silence to feel safe, you will exhaust yourself waiting for a quiet this country does not owe you.

Bring one small thing with a hard edge and a human scale: a book, a notebook, a game, a watch with a face you trust. The flatness induces a peculiar time-sickness. A year would look like a day if you were not counting. Without a vertical, without a task, the mind starts to spread with the sheet-water and to call that spreading peace, or doom, depending on the hour. Neither is accurate. You are a visitor with ankles, a finite water supply, and a flag you planted because you intend to leave. Wonder is appropriate. So is the ordinary decision to turn around while you still know which brightness is yours.

Go light enough that mud does not become a tax you cannot pay, and heavy enough that you never need the country to provide drink, shade, fire, bearing, or a dry night. Walk slowly. Probe the skin. Drink before you are sorry. Let the heron keep the pan. When the amber comes and every strip of water turns to metal, do not take it as a summons farther out. Take it as the day’s last generous lie about distance, note it, and walk back toward the only unnatural vertical on the plain—the one you brought, and the one that means you still have a way home.
