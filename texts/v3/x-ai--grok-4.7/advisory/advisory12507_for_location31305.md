Prepare as if you are entering a parish that has already chosen its climate, its hour, and its volume of silence, and has no vacancy for yours.

**Before you go, accept the rate.** This place does not perform for arrivals. Change happens by a thread a month, a hair a year. If you come looking for an event, you will invent one, and invention is louder than the room can use. Read the description until the empty diameter feels ordinary. The moth is the spectacle. Everything else is maintenance that outlived its makers. Your job is not to fix, record a revolution, or “understand the process.” The process, if it still has one, is the breath in the jackets. Leave the gauges alone. A needle that has drifted for a decade is not waiting for your hand.

**Altitude is the real threshold, not the door.** Outside, the air is thin enough to hurt. Inside, machines have been breathing longer than the paint. Do not treat the seal as a formality. Arrive rested, hydrated, and unhurried in your own lungs. If your body does badly with height, do the ordinary work before you travel: acclimatize as you would for a high station, know your headache, know your judgment when oxygen is stingy. The interior will feel generous by comparison — warm, occupied, almost skin-temperature — and that generosity can make you careless about the return. The shell holds the thin world out the way a hull holds the sea. Crossing it twice in a day is a pressure change and a meaning change. Plan the exit as carefully as the entry. Do not sprint the seal because the note has gotten into your teeth.

**Dress for warm damp metal, not for weather.** There is no weather inside. Temperature sits a little above skin and does not climb or fall. Moisture is a schedule, not a storm. Wear light layers you can live in when they are slightly wet: something that breathes, that does not hold a chill when condensate finds your collar, that will not shred on plate edges or snag in vines as thick as lines. No heavy coats. No fabrics that become lead when damp. At the same time, bring one dry layer sealed in a bag for the transition back out, where thin air will not forgive wet cloth.

Footwear matters more than pride. Diamond plate under moss is a rumor of traction. You want boots with a real bite, broken in, and soles that still tell you when the pattern gives way to green. Ankle support for grated walks and for the moment a vine has chosen the same foothold as you. Spare socks in the dry bag. The cups in the plate hold a finger of water that never quite dries; you will step in them even if you mean not to.

Gloves: light, grippy, not bulky. Girders are not cold, but they are metal, and cable trays carry things that might be beetles and might be fittings. You will want your hands available and unbitten by edges. A thin pair you can write in. A second pair if the first becomes a map of condensate.

**Secure every object as if gravity were an opinion and loss were permanent.** Density is nearly nothing. A wrench is an event. A person is an event. A dropped glove falls straight, turns once, and is gone into the lower green, where the room will incorporate it or, more likely, ignore it. Ignoring is the local mercy. Do not count on retrieval. Nothing on your belt, in a pocket, or clipped to a strap should be able to leave you without a decision. Lanyards, zippers, covered pockets, a small pack worn in front on the catwalk so you can see it and so it cannot swing into the rail and over. No loose jewelry. No hat with a brim that will fill with drip and dump into your eyes at the rail. If you must bring a tool, bring one, and know its name, because leaving it on a beam changes the room more than your notes will.

Do not drink the cups. The condensate learned manners; it did not volunteer to be water. It beads, hesitates, lets go. It is a byproduct of machines and of a schedule the ferns have mistaken for climate. It may be fine. It may be not-quite. You are an ordinary traveler, not a laboratory. Carry your own water, more than you think a warm room requires, because warm air lies about thirst, and because speech will fail and you will breathe through your mouth without noticing. A bottle that cannot open by being knocked. Electrolytes if you sweat in damp heat. Food that does not need cooking and does not smell like a feast — crumbs are shipments, and this parish already has a workforce. Eat on the upper walk. Pack out every wrapper. The room is not hungry. Do not teach it to be interested.

**Sound is the architecture. Prepare your ears and your manners.** The note is not a whine and not a knock. It occupies the hollow the way water occupies a well. At arm’s length, words arrive as shapes. People who stay long enough stop hearing it as noise and start hearing it as the material of the air. When you leave, your ears ring, and the ringing is the absence of a thing that had become structure.

Bring hearing protection, and also bring the willingness not to live inside it the entire time. Total isolation can be as disorienting as the note: you will lose the room’s grain and keep only your own pulse. Soft protection — filters, not a vault — lets you stay oriented and still have a voice left afterward. Have a plan for the exit: protection on before the seal, and no important conversation in the first hour outside. If you have any history of tinnitus, migraine, or panic at low continuous sound, decide your limit before you are standing at the rail pretending you can negotiate with a well. The sound discourages the climb into the cage. Believe it. You do not need to see the housing. Seeing it is not the visit. The visit is the diameter and the fact that the note does not pause for a moth, or for you.

Speech will not carry. Agree on signals beforehand with anyone who comes with you: stop, look, back, rail, I am fine, I need the seal. A small slate and a grease pencil, or a waterproof notebook and a pencil that works on damp paper. Ink runs. Shouting is useless and unkind; the animals do not startle at the sound, they would startle at silence, and silence does not occur. Do not try to make a quieter room so you can record. Recording wants a faster place. A few marks — time in, time you noticed the cups, time your thoughts started walking downward — are enough. Photographs will lie toward green and white and miss the note entirely. Take one if you must, of a gauge the moss has not chosen, and then put the device away. A lit screen is weather.

**Light will not tell you the hour. Bring a clock that does not care.** The band of clouded panels admits an ordinary day that favors neither morning nor afternoon. You can read a gauge. You cannot read time. Wear a watch wound or charged before entry, and a second timepiece in the pack. Set a hard limit: how long you will stand at the rail before you turn back toward the seal, even if you feel you have only just arrived. The room’s rate will steal the feeling of duration. People who sleep on the upper walk dream of the note as a corridor and wake tasting warm metal, certain they have been walking downward for hours without losing height. You are not going there to sleep. If fatigue arrives, treat it as a cue to leave, not as an invitation to lie down “for a minute” on plate that is warm enough to persuade you.

**Move like a guest on a ship, not like an inspector in a ruin.** The floor plan would fit inside a parish church, if it had a floor. What you stand on is stamped plate on a ring-girder. Beyond the rail the volume opens and does not close until the far wall, which is the near wall. People say the drop is thirty meters. Drawings say twelve. Both can be true here. Do not lean out to settle the argument. Stay on the maintained walk. Do not climb down into the cage. The room permits it; the sound discourages it; permission is not hospitality.

Walk where boots already pass. The moss gives way cleanly at the edges of traffic, as if it understands use. Stay on that understanding. Do not peel it back to see the diamond plate “properly.” Do not clear a vine from a tray because a tray is supposed to be clear. The trays are warm and already know the short path between supports. Ferns in open inspection ports are the closed maintenance cycle. You are not the crew that returns to finish it.

At the rail, stand square. One hand for the room until you know the walk. The middle belongs to the note. Nothing hangs there. A body small enough to be mistaken for a flaw in the eye may cross and be gone. Do not reach. Do not drop anything to watch it fall. Curiosity about the lower green is how gloves become parishioners.

**The living things have parishes. You do not.** Moths the color of oxidized copper, wings that look wet in ordinary light. Birds no larger than a thumb, nesting in dead conduit mouths. Things that travel the trays in single file, slowly, covering a hand’s span in a shift. They keep to the green, the cups, the warm jackets. They do not flock, because flocking would require the middle. Do not net, touch, feed, or follow one to see if it is animal or fitting. Watch the length of a slow breath and let it go. Abundance here is kinds, not crowds. Between parishes the air is empty enough that your interruption will be the largest motion in the chamber. Be smaller than that.

If something lands on you, it has mistaken you for a warm joint. Stay still, then move off the walk only as far as the rail allows, and let it choose again. Do not slap. The room is occupied, not hostile. Your fear is the exotic species.

**Health, in the ordinary human sense.** Warm, damp, long-occupied air is kind to plants and hard on people who treat it like a garden stroll. Expect your skin to feel filmed, your hair to hold the smell of warm metal, your eyes to blur slightly from humidity and from light that has no direction of day. Bring something to clear lenses or glasses. Bring a way to rinse eyes with water you carried, not with a cup. Basic wound care: plate edges and conduit mouths are honest about cuts, and damp places are honest about keeping them. Antiseptic, a few dressings, and the discipline not to open them “just in case” and then leave the wrapper. Any medication you need for a day and a night, in a case that seals. If you are prone to vertigo, practice looking across a gap without locking your knees; the curved wall will argue with your sense of near and far. Sit if you must, on the walk, back to the rail, not through it.

Do not put your mouth on metal. Do not taste the brown the textbooks say should have been rust. The metal remembers a temperature; it may also remember a chemistry. Touch a girder once, with a glove, so you know the not-cold, and then stop conducting experiments. You are visiting, not sampling the century.

**Mind the dreams before they start.** The strangeness is a rule that does not match the rules you carried up. Seeds want warmth and a wetting schedule more than they want soil. Animals startle at a silence that never comes. Tools rust wrong. Sleep turns the note into a corridor that descends without losing height. Prepare by telling yourself, out loud, on the way in: height is a number the room keeps for itself; downward feelings are not directions; the seal is real; your clock is right even when the light is not. If your thoughts start walking, stop walking. Hand on the rail. Watch the second hand. Count breaths only if counting does not become the note. Leave while you still prefer your own corridor.

Bring a small ordinary object that is yours and slightly absurd — a paper ticket, a button, a coin from a place with weather — and check it when the room starts to feel like the only climate that was ever agreed upon. Do not leave it on a beam. An event is not a souvenir.

**What to carry, and nothing heroic.** A dry bag. Water. A little food. Soft hearing protection and a plan to use it at the margins. Two timepieces and a precommitted turnaround. Slate or notebook. Gloves. Grip boots and spare socks. A dry layer for outside. A headlamp you will probably not need — the light is enough to read a gauge — but thin air and a seal and a failure of panels are not the place to discover you like darkness less than you claimed. Knife only as a tool you already trust for your own straps, not as a way into conduit. No cutting of vines. No sampling vials. No “just one fern.” No radio you expect to use for conversation inside; if you carry a beacon for the outside, keep it sealed until the rumor of outside becomes air again. A companion, if you have one who can keep signals and will not dare you into the cage. Alone is possible. Alone is how the diameter starts to feel like company.

**How to enter.** Pause at the seal until your breathing matches the decision. Step onto the plate and stop. Let your eyes take the curve without chasing it. Let the note arrive as pressure before you name it noise. Find the rail. Find the traffic edge in the moss. Do not speak for the first minutes; learn what shape your voice would be. Watch one drop gather and let go, so you understand that the schedule is not rain. Then walk only as far as the best view of the empty middle, and stand. That is the visit: green on every made surface, warm damp in the cups, ordinary light, a clear diameter, a sound the size of the room and older than your attention. The height outside, the long fall of the world away from this single volume, will not enter. It will press. You do not have to answer it.

When the moth crosses and is gone, that is your cue that the spectacle has already happened. Check the clock. If you have reached the time you chose when you still had weather in your ears, go. Do not wait for a reason the room will not give. The note will not pause for your absence either.

**How to leave.** Protection on. Hands checked, pockets checked, pack closed, nothing gifted to the lower green. Back along the walked edge. Do not turn for a last look that becomes twenty. Through the seal without barging, and then stand in the thin air until it hurts in the ordinary way, so you remember which climate is yours. The ringing is expected. Do not hunt it with fingers in your ears or with more noise. Drink the water you carried. Eat. Put on the dry layer. Do not describe the room in full sentences until you can hear the person next to you. The place will still be holding when your words return. It has been holding since before the paint failed, balanced, long past commissioning, parish intact.

You prepare, finally, by wanting less from it than it has already given the ferns. Come warm-ready, dry-ready, time-ready, and willing to be the small moving flaw in the light. Stay on the catwalk. Do not climb into the cage. Do not adjust the past. Let the drop be both distances at once. Then go home before the corridor in the dream convinces you that downward is a kind of staying.
