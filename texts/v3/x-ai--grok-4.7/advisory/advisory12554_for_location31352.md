Pack as if you were spending several wet days in a half-built mill town that has not yet learned to drain, not as if you were entering wilderness or a city. The place is small enough to walk across, loud enough that you will stop trusting your ears, and damp enough that anything porous will be wrong within an hour.

Wear what can get wet and stay on you. A light wool or dense cotton layer against the skin, something that still warms when damp, and a second layer you can open when the engines and the walking heat you up. The air is cool, not cold; breath barely shows, and you will overheat in a heavy coat while your cuffs stay wet. Skip anything that beads and sheds into your boots—slick shells are pleasant for ten minutes and then a sauna. A brim helps more than a hood: rain here arrives as films and drip from ledges, not as weather you wait out. Take a spare pair of socks in a waxed or oiled bag and change them at midday whether you think you need to or not. Condensation finds the gap at the ankle before you notice the rest of you is wet.

Boots matter more than almost anything else. The ground is new. Ledges have unrounded lips, pale stone flakes under a step, clay seams have not slumped into forgiving slopes, and the paving is uneven where it exists at all. Between the buildings the lots hold lenses of standing water a finger deep over stone, which looks like solid reflection until your weight goes through it. Soles should grip wet moss and wet metal both; a smooth sole will teach you the difference between a loading platform and the north face of a brick wall, which the moss has already decided are the same thing. Ankles supported. Laces you can retie with cold fingers. Do not rely on the paths the survey laid; they stop, and the ditches do not.

Hearing is the other equipment. The mill does not roar and then rest. It occupies the air—a broad note, chains inside it, a stamped rhythm, the wet slap of a belt—and the frogs and starlings ride that note instead of replacing it. You can stand in an empty lot and still be inside the noise. It does not build toward a warning. Bring simple ear protection you can put in and take out, not because the place is deafening in a dramatic way, but because after a few hours you will miss the smaller sounds that matter: water ticking into a barrel, a cart on bad paving, someone calling across a gap the plan left open. Practice walking with one ear covered and one open so you can choose. Do not expect conversation at the stack. Move closer, or wait for the shift whistle, which comes and goes and is the only punctuation the day offers.

Light is ordinary daylight, softened by moisture, hard enough in shadow to hide a sharp lip. A small lamp is worth more after dusk than a bright one; the reflective water already throws the stack back at you as a second stack lying down, and workers step around that reflection as if it had weight. You should too, until you have watched a lens long enough to know which edge is stone. Addresses painted on shed doors bead and run, so numbers sit lower than they were meant to. Do not navigate by painted marks alone. Learn the mill stack, the firmest ground, the drainage cuts, and the young willows. The bowl is shallow and the buildings are few. You can see across it. That does not mean the direct line is walkable.

Treat the growth as infrastructure that was not in the survey. Ferns stand person-high in the ditches. Vines have taken the guy-wires. Moss films housings left overnight and the undersides of platforms. It is spaced—thick, then open, then thick—so you will not be pushing through a wall of green, but you will brush it constantly, and it is wet. Shake out clothing before you put it back on. Beetles the size of thumbnails cross warm idle plates as if the metal were stone. They are not a swarm; they patrol. Still, do not set food, gloves, or an open pack on a housing that has been running. Do not drink from the lenses or the ditches. Rain stands, withdraws into soil and shade, and leaves films on metal. Carry your own water, and a way to warm it if you want anything that does not taste of the air. Barrels under roofs are for the people who live with the drip; they are not a public well.

Food should be something damp will not ruin and noise will not make you forget. Eat on a schedule rather than by appetite. The constant note flattens hunger and then returns it all at once. Nothing that needs a long dry rest or a clean flame in the open lots—the ground is wet, the wind is slight, and the stack’s pale column does not hurry away, which tells you the air is not eager to clear smoke either. If you cook, do it where someone already cooks, and ask. The sheds follow survey lines and each has its own engine. You are a guest in a workplace that happens to have frogs in the unfinished lots.

Sleep where the metal is not the north wall of your room and where the belt is not the other side of the planks. Condensation will be on the inside of workshop windows by morning; it will be on your gear if you leave it against brick. Hang what you can. Keep paper, matches, and anything that must stay readable in the same oiled bag as the socks. A ground cloth matters even indoors if the building is new and the floor is still learning which way water prefers. The town does not expand while you watch it, and it does not get quieter at night so much as drop the cart-clack and keep the frogs and the idle thrum. Ear protection for sleep is not vanity.

Move slowly, and not only for the edges. The weirdness is local and patient: moss deciding a housing is earth, a reflection precise enough to step around, paint that will not hold its place. None of it is a trap. All of it punishes hurry. The stone loses a grain, not a face; you can lose a sole, a turned ankle, or an afternoon’s bearings if you treat the bowl as finished ground. Watch where the workers already step. They know which lot is a lens and which is a path, and they have stopped arguing with the noise.

You do not need weapons, charms, or a plan for siege. You need dry socks, grippy boots, a way to choose how much of the mill you hear, your own water, and the patience to let a small industrial plan and a cool damp share the same shallow floor. Arrive in daylight. Learn the stack before you need it. Leave the sharp lips alone. The place will still be there in the morning, loud and green and recently made, having changed only by a film of water and a little more moss on whatever you left facing north.
