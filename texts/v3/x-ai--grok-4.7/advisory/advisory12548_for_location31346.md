Go in already slightly cold, slightly damp, and willing to stay that way. The pocket will not dry you, warm you, or give you a dry hour to recover in. If you arrive thirsty for weather, silence, or a room of your own, the seam will feel like a mistake within the first hundred drips.

### The door

The only entrance is a wet stone seam you must turn a shoulder through. Do not pack a frame pack, a rigid case, or anything you cannot pass ahead of you or drag behind on a short line. A soft roll lashed to the chest, narrower than your shoulders, is the right shape. Practice the turn on dry ground before you commit to it: one shoulder leading, the other dropped, hips squared to the crack rather than to the room you hope is beyond it. Wet limestone polishes skin and cloth both; a sharp buckle or a protruding buckle-tongue will catch, and catching in a seam this tight is how people lose time and temper.

Go down, not along. The chamber is reached from below. Whatever rope or confidence you use to descend should be something you can leave, coil, or pass back, because the pocket has almost no metal and almost no spare floor. A coil of rope left in the seam is a rumor with a tail. If you must mark the way, mark it with something that will not sing when water hits it. A scrape in mineral crust is a vandalism here. A thread is better, and even a thread should not cross a channel.

Do not enter at a run from some louder world and expect your ears to settle on the far side. Pause in the seam. Let the roar arrive before your body does. People who stumble through talking are heard in every house at once.

### What the place will do to a body

The air is still above the ankle, cold, and saturated. A thin draft lives only where the channels live, so your feet will be colder than your chest, always. Dress for a cellar that has been a cellar since before your grandparents’ grandparents, not for a cave you might climb out of into sun. Wool worn against the skin is a mistake once it stays wet; it holds the cold. Linen, worn hemp, or a close-weave plant fiber that does not collapse when soaked is kinder, with a slightly larger outer layer you can peel when the damp heat of many bodies in a low hall starts to sit on your neck. You will not get a drying day. Plan to be clammy for the whole visit. Bring one spare shift sealed in greased skin or waxed cloth, not because you will rotate laundry, but because a tear in this place is hard to mend with what they have, and a soaked spare left open will simply become another wet cloth.

Footwear matters more than a coat. The streets are rills. You step across water to visit anyone. Soles should grip wet dressed stone and not ring. Hard leather heels are a small bell. Bare feet are honest but the curbs are low and the channel-lips, where they were left sharp, can still cut. Soft-soled shoes or wrappers you can wring out, with a cord that will not slap the stone, are the sensible compromise. Expect the skin of your feet to soften and split if you stay more than a few days. Tallow, if you can carry a little, is worth more than a second pair of boots. Do not use it on the stone.

The ceiling is a low hall, not a cavern. If you are tall, you will learn a permanent duck, and you will knock your skull once on a bedding-plane you did not believe was that close. A thin cap saves dignity more than warmth. Keep your hands inboard when you turn. Every surface has a use worn into it, and a stranger’s palm on a weeping wall leaves a warmer patch that locals notice the way you would notice a door left open.

Eat before you descend if you can. The settlement is not a place of gardens. There is no green, no moss, no root you are entitled to nibble. Food that comes from outside is already precious because fat and fiber are how the oil-cups and the lashings exist at all. Bring compact food that will not mold in a day of damp and will not smell loud in a room where everyone shares the same still air: hard biscuit, smoked dense meat, nut paste, salt. Eat it discreetly. Offering a portion to the household whose ledge you sit is not a tourist gesture here; it is how a rare thing from beyond the seam stops being a theft of air. Do not drink from the first pool because it is clear. The water is constant, cold, and old in its path. Ask which rill is for mouths and which only for the stone. Mineral crust on the lips of channels is not a seasoning. It is the place slowly closing its own throat, and people scrape it so the sound will not change. Put nothing of it in your mouth.

Sleep will be the hard part, not the walk. The roar does not cycle. There is no night-voice and day-voice, no storm and no hush. Your ears will hunt for a gap and not find one, and the hunt itself is tiring. For the first night or two you may feel the sound in the teeth and the ribs more than in the ears. That is normal. Do not fight it by clamping your hands over your head; you will only add the pulse of your own blood, and you will miss the one other click that matters. If you know you cannot sleep in noise, a little wax or soft wool for the ears is a private tool, not a wall. Use it to come down from the seam, then take it out before you try to live among them. People here stop hearing the water as sound. They hear the break in it. A traveler who stays deaf on purpose is a person who cannot be trusted with the room.

Light is rationed, and the flames do not travel. A bright lamp of your own is a kind of shouting. If you carry light, carry a small oil-cup or a hooded coal that can be pinched to a coal-glow, and set it where a niche already is, or do not set it at all. The wet stone holds a gray sheen; you can cross a curb by that if you have let your eyes widen in the seam. Do not smoke the ceiling. Soot is carried off by the same sweat that keeps the stone from blackening, but only along paths that already know the load. A resinous torch is an insult and a risk in air that does not move above the ankle.

### What to carry, and what to leave above

Carry little, and carry things the pocket cannot make.

Fat is wealth here in the plainest sense: lamp-fuel, and the memory of animals that do not live in the wet. A small bladder of clean rendered fat, well stopped, is a better gift than any coin. Twisted plant fiber, reed-root, bark strips, sinew — anything that can become a wick or a lashing — is understood immediately. Salt is useful and does not shout. A bone needle, a stone burnisher, a spare bowl of wood or gourd that will not ring like metal: these fit the hand of the place.

Leave the metal. A knife is a rumor there, a buckle an heirloom. One small blade, wrapped so it cannot click against stone, is enough for your own straps, and you should not set it down where water can find it and teach it a note. Coins, chains, tin cups, armor, anything that answers a drip with a bright tick, will be heard in the bowls and will be wrong. The bowls were hollowed to particular curves. The room is tuned. You are not a note it asked for.

Leave plants, spores, and living cuttings. Nothing green holds here, and you do not get to be the person who tests that. Soil on your boots is already a guest you did not announce. Scrape them at the seam. Leave insects if you can help it. The pale many-legged thing on the channel-lip is not food and not a specimen. It walks a route the way a hinge swings. The unseen clicker in the ceiling keeps an interval children are taught. If you net either, you have put a hand in the clock.

Do not bring a musical instrument unless you are prepared never to play it. A single plucked string will sit on top of a voice that has not changed in longer than memory, and everyone will hear where it does not belong.

A waxed wrap for your spare shift, a soft sole, fat, fiber, salt, food, a coal or a dim cup, wax for the ears, tallow for skin, a way to relieve yourself into a vessel you can carry back to whatever waste-path a host shows you — that is a full kit. If it does not fit through the seam in one turn of the shoulder, it is too much.

### How to enter a room that is already full

The settlement fits the pocket the way a hand fits a glove. There is no square to stand in while you get your bearings. The floor is a stair of platforms. Channels are the streets. A child’s reach crosses from one sleeping-ledge to the next wall. When you step across water, step where the curb is worn, not where the lip is still doing acoustic work. Do not widen a crossing because your foot is larger. Do not sit in a hollow that a back has already shaped unless you are asked. Those seats are exact. A stranger’s weight in the wrong hollow is not a small rudeness; it is a change in a surface that has stopped negotiating.

Speak low, and less than you think you need to. The stone carries. Introductions happen at arm’s length because all distances are arm’s length. Name yourself once. Say you came by the seam and that you will go back by it. People rarely leave, and the ones who do feel the absence of the roar more than the open air. Your coming and going is the unusual event. Do not narrate the upper world as a rescue, or as a larger truth. To them the pocket is finished. It reached its condition and stayed.

Watch before you help. The crust is scraped on a schedule older than any living memory, because if a channel closes, the sound changes, and the sound is not allowed to change. If you see someone peeling pale shelves into baskets woven from the same crust, you may be allowed to carry a basket. You are not allowed to decide a lip looks “clogged” and clean it. Scraping the wrong place is like moving a tooth in a gear. If the ceiling-click comes early, mention it the way a child would: plainly, without theory. Do not go looking for the clicker. It has not been properly seen, and the dark where the condensing water begins is not a loft for explorers.

The oil-cups stay in their niches. Do not carry one to see a corner better. Move your body to the light, or accept the gray. Shadows here do not jump, and neither should you. A sudden reach reads as a blow in a room this close.

Wash if you are shown how, with the volume of water that is already moving, not by damming a rill so you can have a basin. Nothing rises over the curbs and nothing falls enough to bare the channel floors. That balance is the whole mercy of the place. A traveler who builds a little pool for comfort can put soot, scrapings, or a new note into a path that has always taken them under the lowest platform and not brought them back. Do not piss in a pool because it looks still. Ask. Stillness here is work.

If you stay long enough to be useful, be useful at the edges of the fit: carrying, splicing fiber, keeping a wick trimmed, learning which bowl is which by pitch so you know when something is wrong. Do not “improve” a joint, a seat, a door-edge. The seam’s polish is shoulders. The mortar is crushed crust and clay. Your better knot, your metal pin, your idea of ventilation, your wish for a plant in a crack — these are how a millstone is asked to become another shape. It does not want to.

### What your senses will argue, and how not to obey them

The first hour is overwhelming and then, treacherously, not. The low rush, the bright ticking, the hiss on the dressed wall: they stack into one voice. After a while you will think you have adapted. You have only stopped sorting. That is when you miss a bowl that has gone dull because crust has crept, or a click that came early. Keep a small private count for the first days. Not a song. A count. If you lose it, sit and find the left-hand wall. It weeps faster than the right. It always has. The houses on that side sit a little higher for that reason, and no one discusses it as a decision. Use it as a landmark when the gray makes every platform look like the last.

You may feel time fail. The room does not change color, temperature, or loudness. Lamps do not travel. There is no plant to watch die or leaf. Meals, if you are included in them, and the crossing of the same water to the same ledge, are the clock. Mark days on something you will take back out with you — a cord with knots — because the pocket will not confirm them. People measure a stay by how many times the hollow still fitted the back. If that thought starts to feel like wisdom rather than like a warning, you have stayed long enough that leaving will hurt.

Claustrophobia here is not the fear of getting lost. You cannot get lost. It is the fear of never being farther than a reach from someone else’s wall, and of a ceiling that has already decided your height. If that fear rises, do not thrash and do not flee blindly at the seam. Sit on the curb you were given, feet in the ankle-draft, eyes on one bowl, and let the roar be a fact instead of a weight. The seam is still there. It is the whole world’s door, and it does not move. You are allowed to use it.

When you leave, the quiet will be the loudest thing you have ever heard. People who live in the pocket hear the absence when they go, which is why they rarely go. You will stand in ordinary air and feel unmade, as if a bell had been unstruck inside your ribs. Do not try to cure that with a waterfall, a mill, a crowded kitchen, or a shell held to the ear. Those are imitations, and they will only make you angry. Sit somewhere still until your own pulse is a sound again. Dry your shift in actual air. Look at a plant without the urge to apologize. The mineral pallor on your cuffs will brush off; the pitch-memory will not, not for a while.

If you go back a second time, bring fat and fiber, not a plan. The chamber was cut, and then it stayed cut. You are a visitor in a finished answer. The preparation that matters most is the willingness to be a small, quiet, temporary weight in a hollow that was not shaped for you — and to step back across the water before the hollow starts to fit.
