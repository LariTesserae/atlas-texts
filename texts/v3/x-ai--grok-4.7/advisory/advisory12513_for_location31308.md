Prepare as if you were going to sit in a room that has already finished every conversation it will ever have. The hollow will not test you. It will not feed you, time you, answer you, or change while you watch. What it will do is keep whatever you bring into it—heat against your skin, the sound of your own blood, any small thing you drop—and decline to rearrange the rest. An ordinary body can visit without heroism. The work is to arrive as little weather as possible.

Do not dress for a cave in the usual sense. The stone is warm the way a kiln is warm after the fire has been taken out: not a blaze, not a draft, a held heat that does not sharpen or slack while you are there. You will not shiver. You may not even sweat in a way that warns you. The air is dry past the point where damp announces itself, and water does not return. What leaves your mouth and skin leaves quietly. Carry more water than the size of the place suggests, and drink on a schedule you chose outside, not on thirst. Thirst here is late. There is nothing to refill from. The pale crusts on the floor are salts, finished and bitter; do not touch them to your tongue, and do not wipe your mouth with a hand that has brushed them. If you need to rest a palm on the rock, the surface is faintly gritty and faintly warm and will not cool under you in any span worth waiting for.

Light is the other real provision. Past the lip, brightness thins to a gray that never becomes a color, and farther in the chamber simply keeps dark. A strong beam will lie. It will invent edges the place has already rubbed away, blind you when you look back toward the threshold, and make the middle feel like a problem to be solved rather than a pocket of air that does not move. Bring a small lamp you can lower until it is only a little stronger than the gray at the lip—enough to keep your footing and to find your own hands, not enough to interrogate the stone. Carry a second, duller source in case the first fails. You will not navigate by sun. Noon and the edge of night are the same argument inside, and the hollow will not settle it. Wear a timepiece that does not need the sky, and decide before you step down how long you mean to stay. The visit will not develop a reason to leave.

The drop at the entrance is only a few body-lengths, and the lip is worn so round it no longer feels like a break. You do not need rope if your knees are ordinary and the stone is dry, which it is. You do need patience on the smooth curve: test the footing, keep your hands free, and do not enter carrying a pack so tall that the low ceiling becomes a surprise halfway down. Inside, standing is a decision. The ceiling rises a little only in the middle, a shallow dome, and even there it does not offer the relief of a hall. Know your own height. A soft cap is worth more than a helmet; the rock is not falling. It has finished weathering. What injures people in a place like this is the sudden straightening, the assumption that “middle” means “clear.”

Wear clothes that shed nothing. Fibers, threads, crumbs, the paper scrap in a pocket—anything that leaves you stays where it falls. There is no wash and no wind with the patience to sort it. The grit already on the floor, seed-sized and already at rest, will accept your weight and remain in the print your shoe leaves. Closed shoes, then, with a sole that can feel a gentle dish of stone without skating on the salt patches. Leave the noisy gear behind. The silence is not animals holding still; it is a room that was never occupied by anything with a quick pulse. If you clap or call to hear an echo, you will mostly hear your own insistence. The stone gives nothing back—no drip, no tick, no grain sliding. After a little while the blood in your ear will offer itself as a sound that belongs to the hollow. It does not. Knowing that in advance is the preparation. You are not failing to listen. There is nothing on the other side of listening.

Eat before you go in. The shrubs near the lip are few, hard-leaved, far enough apart that they do not form a thicket, and one of them has died standing, complete, as if remaining were the local form of death. Deeper, there is only ash-colored lichen and a mat as dry as felt. Nothing flowers. Nothing has recently grown. Do not harvest, do not break a stem to see if it is still green, do not water them to be kind. Whatever film of moisture the lip sometimes admits, they already drink it, and the admission never becomes visible enough for you to share. You are a guest of a balance that looks like absence of event. Adding an event is the only real damage available to you.

Psychologically, pack for non-response. This is the part ordinary travelers underestimate, because the place is not hostile and not vast. You can cross it in a short walk. The lip remains a paler nothing in the dark, so you are not lost. What closes around you is scale without threat: low, warm, dry, small, long past its last adjustment. People who need a landscape to acknowledge them—by wind, by an animal, by a change in the light, by the drama of danger—will feel refused, and may invent a danger to answer the refusal. There is no hidden current, no sudden cold sink, no nest, no web, no shell. If your mind starts writing a story in which the warmth means something buried and recent, set the story down. The heat lives in the mineral. There is no fire and has not been one. The arrangement of grains was agreed upon and then left alone.

So the useful inner practice is simple. Before the lip, look at the country you are leaving long enough that you could describe it later; inside, description will thin. At the threshold, let your eyes accept gray instead of hunting for a beam. Step among the grit without trying to walk where no one has walked; no one has, and your print will not be erased, but it also will not be read. In the middle, under the shallow dome, stop. Put the lamp low. Feel the faint warmth through the sole and, if you touch it, through the hand. Notice that your breath is the only moving air, and that it does not start a draft. Do not wait for the season to perform itself. It will not, not within any span you can honestly give. When the time you chose outside is done, walk back toward the paler nothing. The way out is the same short rise. Do not linger in the hope that the dark will explain itself if you are quieter. It has already explained itself: light does not travel far, water does not return, heat does not leave in a hurry, and things that fall here stay.

Come out, drink, and check yourself for the ordinary human aftereffects of a warm dry dark: dry mouth, a slight dullness in the eyes, the odd shame of having made noise, the urge to go back in because you are not sure you were ever really there. You were. The chamber does not need to have changed in order to have held you. Leave it the grit, the standing dead shrub, the salts, the unfinished sentence of the gray at the lip. Take only your water weight, your lowered light, and the knowledge that you visited a finished thing—and that finishing, not peril, was what the visit was like.
