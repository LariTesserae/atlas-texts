Pack for a climate that has already finished becoming itself. The air in the basin is cool the way stone is cool after the sun has stood still on it for longer than any afternoon, and damp only where the seeps and the pipe-shadows keep their permanent moisture. You will not need a range of seasons. You will need clothes that do not mind being faintly slick at the cuff and the collar, and that still feel like clothes after many hours of the same temperature. A light wool or dense cotton layer against the skin, something that breathes but does not chill when the condensation transfers; a second layer you can take off and fold without expecting it to dry by any change in the weather, because the weather will not change. Boots with a sole that grips wet grit and the lips of cisterns. A hat with a brim, not against glare — the sun is only a hand’s width above the western edge and never climbs — but against the way the light sits on the face at one unchanging angle until the eyes begin to invent movement that is not there. Bring a cloth for your hands. You will touch walls. The stipple of beads will come away on your fingers and then, if you look back later, will have been restored bead for bead, and the cloth is for you, not for the wall.

Carry your own measure of duration. Nothing in the basin will supply one. The sun does not travel, the shadows do not lengthen, the goats do not finish the grass, the hearths do not consume their wood down to a later state. Your body will still do what bodies do. Hunger will arrive on its own schedule. Thirst. The need to sleep. The slow granular fatigue of attention held too long on a scene that does not develop. A small mechanical watch, wound by you, kept in a pocket against your ribs, is worth more than any sky-mark. So is a simple tally — notches you cut into a stick you do not set down, or knots you add to a cord you wear — because anything you leave in the open will be returned to the arrangement it had, or exactly replaced, and your notch will not survive as evidence. Eat before you feel heroic about local food. The grain and the fruit and the always-ready hearth meals will not run out, and they will not make you sick in any ordinary way, but they also will not mark the passage of a meal inside the place itself. Your stomach is the clock. Trust it, and bring a reserve of something plain and dense from outside, sealed, in case the unchanging readiness of their food begins to feel like a fact you cannot metabolize. Water from the cisterns is cold and still and, by every sign, clean; still, carry a bottle you refill on your own decision rather than slipping into the habit of drinking whenever a pool is simply there.

Do not plan to leave a trace, and do not plan to need one. A mark on a post becomes a lie. A pebble you stack is either back where it was or so perfectly replaced that your stacking has nowhere to lodge. Footprints in the grit blur back into the previous grit. If you must navigate, navigate by the permanent things and by your own sequence of noticing: the seep-line that is always the same length, the civic terrace that holds the whole basin in one look, the particular cistern whose fish hold in the same handspans of water, the compound whose tree carries the same fruit. Draw the map in your head or in a book you keep on your person. Do not expect the map to be confirmed by changes you make. Children run and the terrace is not scuffed afterward; you will scuff nothing that remains scuffed. This is not hostility. It is the equilibrium the place has already reached. Your effort is an eddy. The eddy closes. Prepare, therefore, to remember rather than to record in the stone. Memory is the only erosion the basin permits. Bring a mind that can hold detail without demanding that the detail alter what it describes. A small notebook you write in and then close and keep against your body will serve, if you accept that the notebook is your weather, not the valley’s. Speak at the level of the hush. Raised voices feel wrong because the air does not take them; they fall flat a lane’s width away. Practice, before you go, the volume at which you would speak in a room where someone is sleeping and you do not want to be the one who wakes them. You will need that volume for hours.

Expect your own processes to continue while everything else’s have stopped. You will tire. You will want a change of light and not receive it. The fixed sun can produce a mild, specific strain: not the ache of brightness, but the ache of an angle that never relieves itself, so that after a while the western knife-edge seems to lean, or the coin of reflected sun in a pool seems to tremble, and neither is true. Rest your eyes on the fern mats and the open grit in alternation. Sit when you sit, and choose a place whose shadow already falls where you will put your back, because the shadow will not migrate to accommodate you. Sleep, if you sleep there, will be sleep in perpetual late light or in the shade you have chosen; bring a cloth for your eyes. Dreams may feel unusually sequential, as if your mind, denied external succession, supplies its own. That is useful. Let it. The people of the basin will not be surprised by you if you do not insist on being a change. They take what their hands already know is theirs; the rows refill or are refilled by someone walking the other way with the exact measure. You may be offered the same courtesy. Accept small, do not test the refill with greed or experiment, and do not try to pay in any currency of alteration. A bronze pin you brought from elsewhere, set down, may remain only so long as a hand is near it; their own pins stay in rows because the rows are the fact. Conversation, if it comes, will be quiet and about arrangement — where a thing is, who last stood where you are standing — more than about events. Events do not accumulate here. Do not press for news. There is no news. There is the next noticing.

The unusual danger is not weather or beast or hostility. It is the completeness. The place is a held note, almost too quiet to call a note, and after enough of your own hours the hush can start to seem like a surface you could lean your whole attention on and never need to lift it again. That is pleasant until it is not. Set a personal limit before you descend the pass: a number of windings of the watch, a number of meals, a number of times you will climb to the civic terrace and look at the unmoving sun and then turn around. When the limit arrives, leave while your memory of arriving is still sharper than the basin’s equilibrium. The pass is there. The ridges are new stone that will not become old, edges still edged, and they will look the same when you go as when you came. What you carry out will be what your body did and what you managed to keep in mind — the stipple on a particular wall, the arc a bird redrew, the way a voice crossed one lane and no farther, the fact that a scraped knee in a local child scabs at the rate of noticing and then is as before, while your own small cut, if you get one, will heal on your time and your terms once you are out. Do not expect the valley to confirm that you were in it. Confirmation is your work.

Go in ordinary. Come out with your own sequence intact. The basin does not need you to finish anything, because nothing there is unfinished. Your preparation is the decision to remain a process inside a place that has used up its gradients, and to step back through the pass while you still know the difference.
