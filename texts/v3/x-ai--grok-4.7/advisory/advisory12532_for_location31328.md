Go in expecting cold that does not negotiate, thin air that makes small efforts feel large, and a floor that is alive and already in use. The basin will not meet you as scenery. It meets you as a street system, a set of rooms, and a noise that does not turn off.

**Before you leave the lower world**

Spend real days at height before you ever wet a foot. The tarn sits where the air has already thinned, and the work here is not a viewpoint stroll. You will duck, rise, pole, swim short distances, and haul your own weight onto rafts that shift. If you arrive short of breath on the rim, you will misjudge the water. Cold steals heat faster than altitude steals judgment, and the two together make people do foolish things with their hands: gripping too hard, stepping off the known track, deciding they can “just swim across.”

Learn to be in water that never warms. Not a plunge and a gasp — a half-hour of wrists, calves, and the small of the back staying cold while you still have to think. The middle of the tarn takes the heat out of a wrist in a few breaths even at noon. Your ordinary idea of “I’ll warm up once I’m moving” is wrong here. Moving helps only until you stop, and you will stop: at a lintel, on a sagging causeway, in a room whose floor rocks.

Practice poling, or at least the posture of it. A pole is a third leg and a question you ask the bottom. The willow roads lie just under the skin of the water and do not announce themselves. A jab that is too proud punches through weave; a jab that is too timid finds nothing and you step where you should not. If you cannot swim a short, calm distance in clothes, do not come. The lanes are water. Falling off a raft is not an emergency unless you panic or drift toward the southern notch.

Pack as if everything you bring will be wet within an hour, and as if anything that sheds, dyes, or poisons will be a trespass. Leave scented soap, insect repellent, sunscreen that milks off in a film, batteries that can crack, and any fabric that pills into the current. The mat knits. It does not need your lint as a new species. A waxed pouch, a spare dry shirt sealed until you are inside a column, cord you can afford to lose, and food that does not require a fire are enough. Fire here is a small, rafted, temporary thing fed with dried crowns. Do not arrive planning to improve it.

Wear what can be soaked and still let you climb. Soft soles or bare feet. Nothing with a tread that cuts. The ground is a soaked rug of root, moss, and pale jointed stems; it gives to the ankle and then holds. Spikes, hobnails, and “grip” sandals made for rock are vandalism. A light layer against the wind on the rim, and something you can actually swim in once you are down. Wool that still insulates when wet is worth more than any cloth that feels clever in a shop and turns to ice the moment you leave the water. Cover the head and the neck against the sun. Altitude and a bowl of glass will burn you while your hands are numb.

Bring almost no metal. A needle, if you must, and perhaps a small knife you are willing to keep sheathed and out of the weave. The place already has a few green copper pieces, used as weights and needles, not as a future. A bright buckle, a stove, a coil of wire: these are not tools here so much as arguments. You did not come to start one.

If you wear lenses or need a medicine, waterproof that and no more. Do not bring a scheme for “documenting” the nests on the rim or the swifts pouring off the melt-lip. The birds are part of the volume of the place. A sudden climb into a colony is how you get a face full of wings and a reason to be unwelcome.

**On the rim**

Stop before you descend. The stone above the splash line is dry and lichen-gray; below it, black and permanent. That line has not moved in living memory. Believe it. The sheet coming over the northern lip is not a brook you wade for fun, and the southern notch is a white thread you can hear before you see. Both are mouths. You do not enter or leave through them.

Listen until the noise stops being news. Fall, overflow, poles on reed, the slap of a raft when someone shifts, dippers and wagtails, swifts when the melt changes its note. It holds a volume the way a millrace holds a volume. If that sound makes you irritable on the rim, it will make you stupid in a room. People sleep here. You will have to also.

From above, the town is a scatter of green crowns in glass, not a waterfront you can tour. The open water between columns is square, lane, and empty lot. Shouts carry cleanly. Assume you are heard. Do not treat the bowl as empty because you cannot yet see a face.

Pick your way down only where stone is stone. The mat begins where the bottom stops being gravel and becomes the street. Once you are on it, you are in the town.

**How to move**

The pale tracks are the roads. They are pressed thinner and they do not close. Everything off them is plant grown into use, not waste ground. Walk the pale. If you must cross unmarked mat, do it as a guest crossing a yard, not as a surveyor opening a shortcut. The lesson the floor has learned — or been taught by generations of feet — is old. A new track is a new fact. You are not owed the right to write one.

Your foot sinks to the ankle and finds give. Trust that, then trust it less where the weave looks bruised or freshly mended. Braided willow causeways sag where people have believed in them for a long time. They were mended by tying in new withes, not by throwing out the old. Step where the pole finds resistance. If it finds sponge and a darker green, stop.

Swim the corridors, not the weed. The hair-fine forests stand in clumps with clear ways between, and those ways are where the fish hold. Trout the length of a forearm rest in the shade of the reed-towers and do not flee unless a hand breaks the surface. Do not make a game of that. A school reading as weather is not a resource. Newts the color of wet slate crawl the root-mat in numbers that make the floor seem to move when the light shifts. Set a foot down and look. You will not like the feeling of a body under the arch, and neither will it.

Poles are last year’s reed, cured on the rim until they pale. If someone offers you one, take the offer as instruction, not equipment. Knock before you lean. The sound of pole on reed is already part of the town; a wild thrash is not. Keep your free hand out of the weave when you push. A basket dropped here can be knitted over before the season turns. Your fingers do not get the same courtesy if you drive them through a living tie.

Give the southern notch a wide berth in the water. The overflow is a thread until you are in it, and then it is a decision already made. The northern sheet is the same kind of sentence, only louder. The town lives between those mouths because the water there does not hurry. Stay in the part that does not hurry.

**The rooms**

You enter by ducking under a root lintel and rising into air. Do not stride in upright as if the threshold were a mistake. The pocket smells of wet hay and cold iron, which is honest: leaf, pitch, cold, and the faint sweetness of linings that transpire. The air does not foul. That is a property of the room, not a permission to seal it, smoke it, or wash your clothes in it.

The floor is a raft of bundled stems. It rocks with the tarn’s breathing. Sit before you stand proud. Shift your weight as if another person were already balancing opposite you, because the room is doing that work whether or not you notice. Light arrives high and broken, nets moving on the inner walls. There is no glass to adjust. Your eyes will hunt for a still patch and not find one. Let them.

If you are sheltered, sleep when the basin sleeps, not when your old day says so. The sound does not fade at dusk. People here have had longer than you to stop treating it as an event. A dry shirt, something under the shoulder so the raft’s ribs do not print you, and your ear pointed at the wall rather than at the open lintel will matter more than any ideal of silence. You will wake clear-headed if you have not fought the room. Fighting it — pacing, propping the raft rigid, blocking the transpiration with a tarp — is how a pocket stops being a kindness.

Do not kindle. If fire is wanted, it is a stone sled taken to the middle and fed with what the crowns can spare, and it does not last because it does not need to. Your hunger for hot food is not a local emergency. Eat what keeps. Drink only what you carried or what is plainly offered. The water is the street. Clarity is not the same thing as a promise to your gut, and boiling is not a rite this place performs for visitors.

Touch the leaf-lining the way you would touch plaster in a house that is not yours: not at all, unless someone shows you a mend. It is overlapped, waxy, cobbled with bone awls and cord. Your thumbnail is not an awl. The columns are hollow reed as thick as a waist, lashed and grafted, recrowned when they need it. They are organs of the town, not props. A crown broken for a souvenir is a roof opened to the tarn.

**What the days feel like**

Cool all the way down, even when the sun is simply there — not low, not fierce — and the bowl looks gentle from the rim. Your skin will believe in warmth; your blood will not. Plan every outing as a loop that returns you to air and a dry layer before the shiver becomes an idea. Ordinary human limits apply with no special dispensation for wonder. Wonder is common here. It does not heat you.

The unusual mercy is that the rooms stay sweet and the middle water stays slow. You can stand in the open, pole in hand, and watch fish stitch the shade and birds stitch the air, and the knock of ordinary work going on at a distance. That is the visit. Not a summit, not a ruin, not a wilderness you are the first to read. A hamlet’s worth of ground, a dozen towers, lanes that are also weather.

People will see you. A shout carries. So does the way you place your feet. If you are spoken to, answer at the volume the water already uses; do not import a louder courtesy. If you are not spoken to, do not fill the lane with explanation. The plan of the place settled when the mat closed over the gravel. You are late to it. Late is fine. Late and loud is not.

Baskets may still be half alive when they are used and only later die into stiffness. Do not “finish” one. Do not cut cord because yours is better. Do not leave cord of your own tangled in the gray withes. The old stays. It is slick and still load-bearing. Your improvement is trash the moment it slips the knot.

When you are tired, you will want the rim, dry stone, a horizon that is not a circling wall. Take it, but do not camp on the splash line or under the nests. The rim is a curing-ground for poles and a road for birds. Sleep there only if the town has no room for you, and then sleep high and dry, off the beards of moss that exist only where spray reaches. Descend again in the morning by the same stone, onto the same pale road. The basin is not safer because you have seen it once. The cold is patient, and the notch is always leaving.

**When you go**

Leave with what you brought. The mat will close over a dropped basket; it should not have to close over you. Rinse nothing into the tarn that you would not drink by accident. If you were given a pole, return it to the rim or to the hand that lent it, pale and unsplit if you can manage that.

On the way down-country, do not trust your own warmth. People walk out of cold water convinced the danger stayed in the bowl, then fold on the path when the wind finds the wet layer they kept on “so it would dry.” Change while your hands still obey. Eat. Descend slower than your pride. Thin air and a long chill can arrive as clumsiness hours after the glass has left your eyes.

What you are preparing for is not hardship as a sport. It is a town that happens to be water: ankle-deep give, rooms that breathe, roads that stay open because they are walked, a sound that has been going since the notch cut itself. Arrive able to be cold, quiet, and precise with your feet. The rest is looking, and the place is already good at being looked at — green crowns, moving nets of light, fish holding in shade — without your help, and without your improvements.
