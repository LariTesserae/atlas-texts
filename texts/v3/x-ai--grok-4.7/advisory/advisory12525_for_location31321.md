The chamber will not welcome you, and it will not harm you in any way a warning label could name. It will simply continue. Your task, as an ordinary body that still requires sequence, is to enter without becoming a permanent new term in its equation and to leave while you still remember what leaving means.

Understand the room before you pack. It is not a ruin and not a machine hall in any productive sense. It is a finished equilibrium that happens to contain motion: a flywheel that does not slow, dust veils that do not settle, colonies that flex on the piston-knock and return to the same angles, a chord of industrial sound that has already found its lock and does not decay. Warmth is exact. Dryness is deliberate. Nothing hunts, drinks, breeds, dies, or rounds an edge. Count is a fact. The only gradient the place currently tolerates is whatever has not yet been corrected. You will be that gradient. Preparation is the art of staying small enough, brief enough, and quiet enough that the correction does not finish you into the mosaic.

Travel light in mass and heavy in redundancy of time. Bring three independent chronometers that do not share a power source or a mechanism: a mechanical watch you can hear tick against your wrist, a sealed digital timer, and something stupid and physical—a short sand measure or a beeswax candle marked in notches, even if flame behaves strangely. You are not measuring the chamber. You are measuring whether your own processes still accumulate. Log every check in a small waterproof notebook with a pencil; ink may crust in the mineral air. Decide your exit time before you descend—twenty to forty minutes of your own ticking is enough for a first visit—and obey the notebook, not your sense of how long you have “been.” Subjective duration will lie. The chord does not rise or fall to give you chapters.

Water is the supply that matters most, and you will want more than thirst suggests. The air is warm, close, and aggressively dry; your mouth goes chalky in the first minute, eyes sting, lips tighten, sweat vanishes without cooling you. Carry at least two liters in hard sealed bottles, plus electrolyte powder you can mix without ceremony. Do not count on the mineral rimes. They are not damp, and licking them would be both useless and a disturbance. Food should be dense, ready, and quiet to eat: pressed bars, nothing that crinkles, nothing that requires a new smell to enter the volume. Eat and drink on a schedule tied to the chronometers, not to appetite. Appetite may flatten as your body tries to match the room’s refusal of consequence.

Sound is climate here, and climate does not forget. The chamber returns what it is given at the same amplitude, without decay. A cough, a spoken word, a dropped buckle, the rasp of a zipper becomes another permanent voice in the chord. Practice moving and breathing at a volume you can hold for half an hour: slow nasal breath, no throat-clearing, soft-soled shoes broken in so the soles do not chirp, all straps taped down. Wear hearing protection that you can adjust—muffs you can lift one side of—because you need the chord as an instrument panel (the piston-knock, the bearing’s ring, the wing-rate of the motes) but you will fatigue if it sits undiluted in the skull. The vibration is constant; it will try to entrain your pulse. If your chest starts answering the knock, leave. That is not meditation. That is the room offering you a place in the mosaic.

Clothing should cover skin without trapping heat you cannot shed. Light layers, long sleeves, gloves you can work in, a thin hood. The fauna persist by contact—heat, vibration, mineral film—and they do not scatter. A bare arm brushed against a housing may be treated as a new warm flank; coin-flat animals may edge toward the temperature of your glove and seat themselves. Do not scrape them off. Micro-adjustment is allowed in service of sameness; a flinch that changes their count or their boundary is a larger error. A simple respirator or even a folded cloth over nose and mouth is worth carrying for the first minutes, less because the dust attacks than because the standing veils feel wrong in the lungs and you may gasp. The veils part when you walk and hold their layers afterward. Do not expect them to coat you and then fall. They are weather that has already finished falling.

Light is dim, warm, and assigned. Shadows have shapes and do not lengthen. A bright beam will throw a darkness the room did not budget. If you bring a lamp, make it small, warm-toned, and use it only to read your own notebook or to confirm the lip of the fracture on the way out. Prefer your eyes to adjust. You will be able to read the punched holes. You will not be able to read far, and that limit is structural, not a failure of your gear.

Mark your exit before you need it. A thin cord, brightly colored to your eyes, tied at the lip and paid out behind you, is better than memory. The chamber is not large, but sameness erases landmarks: every plate looks like the plate you already passed, every colony like the one that flexed a moment ago. Do not chalk the floor. A mark that is not part of the equilibrium may either fail to persist or persist forever as a scar the room must then incorporate. The cord you can retrieve. Leave nothing.

What the visit will feel like, if you have prepared, is this. You come down the clean fracture and the boundary closes its attention behind you without a sound of sealing. Warmth sits on the skin at one exact temperature. The chord occupies the whole volume at once; there is no “over there” for the piston. Dust veils stroke the face and reform. The flywheel turns at a speed your blink cannot catch cheating. The jointed bodies ride the knock and reset. You will feel, within minutes, obscenely alive: your breath is a slope, your pulse a stumble, your thoughts a sequence that wants a next sentence. The room does not answer the breath. That indifference is the pressure. Some travelers describe a courtesy in it, as if the equilibrium is willing to let the anomaly stand provided the anomaly does not multiply. Others describe a patience that is worse than hostility, the sense that correction can wait because correction does not experience waiting.

Do not test the machines. The unfinished shape in the jaws, the weld that never failed further, the ladder that stops at three rungs—these are not puzzles left for you. Stopping the flywheel, even for a second, is an attempt to give the room a history it has declined. You do not know what form the refusal would take. Observation is the only safe verb.

Keep your hands off the colonies except to steady yourself, and if you must touch, touch as a temperature, not as a grasp. Watch how little they move to restore the mosaic, then stop. Count nothing twice unless your notes demand it; recounting is a hunger for change. If a thought begins to loop in time with the bearing’s ring, speak it once into the notebook in the smallest pencil whisper and then stop saying it. If the notebook itself starts to feel redundant—every line identical in weight to the last—that is the signal your own sequence is thinning. Climb out.

On the far side of the lip the world, if you still have one, will feel indecently unfinished: air that moves, moisture that beads, sounds that die, edges that have been rounded by actual hours. Your ears may ring with a chord that is no longer present. Drink immediately. Check the chronometers against each other and against a clock that never entered the chamber. Write the discrepancy while it is still news. Do not return the same day. The dryness lingers in the sinuses, and the memory of perfect repetition can make ordinary change feel like noise you are obliged to correct. You are not. You are the kind of creature that requires a later moment. Pack for that fact, obey the notebook, add no permanent sound, and leave the equilibrium exactly as finished as you found it.
