The approach is the last ordinary part, and it is not a warm-up you can skip. Treat the ridge as a real mountain and the pocket as a room that has borrowed a mountain’s weather and refused to let it spread. You prepare for both, and you do not let the kit for one talk you into staying too long in the other.

Go up already acclimatized. The seam sits at a height that makes the blood light, and that lightness does not switch off when the air thickens into a medium. If you arrive breathless on the scree, you will arrive breathless in the room, and in the room breath is a negotiation. Sleep lower for a few nights if the range allows it. Eat. Drink more water than the dry wind makes you want. Do not drink the pocket. It smells of metal and of rain that has not fallen, and it is not a spring.

Learn the outside before you care about the inside. Walk the switchbacks until young scree and a tin-colored sky feel dull. Mark, in your own body, what the ridge sounds like when it is only wind. You will need that memory. Inside, sound stops having a source. People come out insisting the whole range was roaring. It was not. The roar is local, folded into one room, and the ridge a shoulder-width away can be almost quiet. A companion who never enters is worth more than any instrument you can carry through the seam.

Take one companion, not a party. The room is a dozen paces across when the floor consents to be a floor. Two bodies are already a crowd when shelves are being born underfoot. The second person stays on the ridge, out of the metal-smelling breath, with a watch and a simple rule: if you are not back by the agreed mark, they do not come in after you. They wait one interval more, then they go down and tell someone where the seam is. Rescue through that crack is how a second person becomes a second problem. The pocket does not echo a shout. It replaces it.

Agree the mark before you leave camp, and make it short. A cut in the newest face can be a seam by evening, and a seam can be rock again before you finish looking. Nothing you build will keep. The niches already there — white chisel marks, a coil of grass-cord, a missing bowl, flakes from a cobble that does not belong — are not a settlement. They are evidence that someone with early tools tried to make the room hold still and then, quite reasonably, left the tools behind. Do not add yours to the scatter.

What to carry is less than you will want to carry.

On the ridge: the ordinary high-country things. Wind layer, sun layer, food you will not eat inside, water from below, a way to stay warm while you wait for your own nerve. The stone on the path is unfinished and sharp. Ankles fail here in the usual way, before any miracle. Gloves for the approach; you will take them off or change them at the seam, because inside you need to feel heat.

Inside, dress for cold that is not depth and for wet that is not weather. The cool is summit-cool, a spring pulled from height, even when your sleeves have gone heavy. Cotton is a mistake you will pay for on the way down, shivering in a wind that feels suddenly too thin. Wear something that still works when it is soaked and that does not balloon or drag when the air decides to be a medium. Sleeves and hems catch. A snug cuff is worth more than a handsome coat. Boots with a quiet sole, not iron points: the floor tilts as a decision, not as ice, and fresh stone takes a spike and then shrugs. You want friction and the ability to feel a shelf warm for a moment under your palm — that warmth is the rock’s own birth, and it is a warning that the surface is still becoming.

Leave the rope if you cannot bear to watch it become geology. Anchors fail here for a reason that is not rotten rock in the climber’s sense. A conduit opens under the floor, pitch drops out of the world, flakes are taken, and a line you trusted is a line the room has begun to digest. If you use a tether at all, make it a stupid one: short, visible, held by the person outside, paid through the seam and watched, never tied to a feature of the room. The moment it goes taut against a moving wall, you cut it or you drop it. Do not argue with a mountain that has lost patience with the speed of mountains.

Do not bring a chisel, a drill, a stake, a bowl you mean to cache, a flag, a claim. The room unmakes decisions. A niche chopped at dawn is a rumor by dusk.

Light is already there. Noon comes through the pale lid and through the rain-wall at a normal angle, which is why the wrongness is so legible. You can see your hands. A lamp is extra weight and a thing to lose, and it will not help you hear. If you carry one, it is for the seam on the way out, in case the curtain has climbed and the crack is only a darker cold. Do not explore by it. The room is not a cave that rewards the beam.

Sound is the part people under-prepare, because they think of earplugs and waterfalls.

This is not a waterfall. The roar is the sound of arrival: uplift, given one throat. It does not ring in the ears so much as fill them. Speech is a shape your mouth makes. A bird, a foot, your pulse — if they exist, they exist as brief holes in the noise, and the noise closes. Foam plugs will wet, shift, and lie to you. Over-ear shells will fill when the medium thickens and then become cups of pressure. What you can do is this: have your ears looked at before you go, equalize gently on the ridge until you know your own sinuses, and accept that you will not have a conversation. Practice, in some ordinary loud water at home, the few signals that do not need voice — a hand on a wrist, a tug, an open palm that means out. Inside, visibility is the mercy. The day is present. Use eyes, not mouths. Write nothing you need to hear read back. A slate in a pocket is fine for later, on the ridge, if your hands still work. In the room you will not take notes that matter. You will watch the seam.

Breath is the other mercy you do not get to assume.

Sometimes the pocket is air, metallic, and you can sip it. Sometimes it is a medium, and your ribs work, and there is no surface to swim up to — only a thinner place near the lid where daylight lives, and bubbles that do not seek it. They travel sideways and are taken into the stone. Standard diving is the wrong religion here. There is no column with a bottom and a top, no table that knows this pressure, no “depth” that matches the cool in your blood. The elevation never leaves. You are high and under, both exact. A tank and a regulator may feed you for a minute and then become a machine the room has no agreement with, or a weight when the medium thins and you are only wet and shaking. If you are an ordinary traveler, do not enter on a plan that requires you to breathe the pocket for long, and do not enter on a plan that requires apparatus you have not already lived with in ordinary water until it is boring.

The honest method is poorer and safer. You go in on a breath you can hold without drama, already calm, already equalized, with the seam at your back or in your eye, and you leave before the breath becomes a project. If you cannot see the way out, you are not touring. You are lost in a room the size of a chapel that is rearranging itself. Practice calm breath-holds at home, low, rested, never pushing the black edge, never in the thin air of the ridge as a stunt. Altitude and a competitive breath are how ordinary people faint on ordinary mountains. Here fainting is how the room keeps you.

Learn the rain-wall before you give it your shoulder.

It is a curtain with weight. It moves sideways when the pressure changes, climbs, breaks into knuckle-sized beads, reforms without landing. It can occupy the seam. Then the way out is a vertical river, and you do not know, from inside the river, whether the far side is still the ridge. So you do not enter when the curtain is on the crack. You wait on the ridge — cold, bored, companion checking the time — until the seam is only the breath of metal air. You turn sideways. Ten steps. You stop where you can still see the crack as a fact, not a hope. You let your eyes adjust to the fact that the light has not failed, only changed jobs.

Watch the floor the way you would watch a tide you have never met. It tilts. A shelf rises, warm, then cools and is water. Do not correct with a violent step. Lower your weight. Knees soft. One hand free to touch, not to grip a flake that is about to be shed. The newest stone is sharp in a way old mountains have forgotten: cooling planes, a bad glaze where melt met air. It will cut. It will also be gone. Do not stand under a boss that is still arriving. Do not stand over a sound that drops in pitch. That drop is a conduit opening somewhere you cannot see. The flakes tick and are taken. You do not need to see the mouth to leave.

Fauna, if you insist on the word, is a rumor with two sightings: a finger of pale muscle on the rain-wall, a line of bubbles that refused the sky. Do not hunt it, and do not feed it, and do not decide you are alone in a way that makes you careless. If anything here hunts, it hunts by pressure. You are a large, clumsy pressure. Move smoothly. Do not thrash when the medium thickens. Thrashing is a speech the room understands better than your voice. The ecology is almost empty — mineral fur in a crack, one tuft of wire-grass on a shelf that may not last the hour. Let it be almost empty. You are not there to complete it.

Time inside is a physical labor. The roar makes minutes feel like weather. Decide the turn-arounds on the ridge, where sentences still work, and write them on your wrist if you must:

- The seam is no longer a clean breath of air.
- The floor has rewritten your footing once.
- Your sleeves have gone heavy and the sip of air has stopped being a sip.
- You cannot see your companion’s mark, or the crack, or your own hands against the noon-colored wall.
- You want, suddenly, to understand the noise instead of leave it.

That last one is the mind’s failure, and it is common. The pocket does not watch you. Emptiness is real. What watches is your own hunger to make the roar into a message. It is not a message. It is the condition of hearing, for as long as you stand in a mountain that is still being extruded. People stay because the light is ordinary and the room is small and the violence is contained, and contained violence feels like a dare you can finish. You cannot finish it. Creation and destruction are the same gesture here, sped up. You are a visitor at the speed of a human, which is the wrong speed, and the only correct use of that fact is to be brief.

When you leave, leave while the seam is a seam. Sideways, the way you came, counting the steps you counted on the way in. Do not stop in the crack to listen. The crack is the hinge between a quiet ridge and a throat, and it is a bad place to change your mind. If the curtain has climbed onto the exit, you do not push through on hope. You retreat to the last spot where the day is clear, you make yourself small, you spend the breath you saved, and you go only when the wall peels and cold air insults you with how ordinary it is. If that does not happen inside your mark, your companion already has their orders. Your job is not to invent a second exit. There is one. It answers some of the time. “Some of the time” is the whole theology of this place. You planned for the some, not for the rest.

On the ridge, do not talk at once. Your ears are full, not ringing, and they will lie about wind. Sit. Let the companion look at your face, your hands, the cuts you did not feel. Change the wet layer before you admire the view. The shiver will come late, after the roar has stopped being the landscape. Drink the water you carried. Do not describe it yet. The pocket does not echo, and for a while neither will you, not in any voice that still believes a sound belongs to a source. That passes, on the way down, in switchbacks of ordinary scree, if you kept the visit short and the seam in sight and your companion outside in the wind.

If you are tempted to go back for the cord in the niche, or to prove the floor can be stood on longer, or to catch the muscle in the rain-wall so the emptiness will have been a story with an animal in it — that is the moment you are done with this mountain. The room has had minds in it before. It kept none of their reasons. It kept a coil of fiber and a stain on a flake, and it is already unmaking those. An ordinary traveler’s success is uglier and better: you turned sideways, you were under and high, you saw the day still happening inside the drowning, you did not make the room a home, and you were on the ridge again while your name still fit in your mouth.
