Prepare as if you are going to live for a while inside a finished object that is also a climate. The plain will not meet you with weather in the ordinary sense, and it will not meet you with wilderness. It will meet you with a specification: warm, wet, flat, sharp, quiet, and already full. Everything you bring should answer that specification, because the place will not adjust to you, and it will not notice if you fail to adjust to it.

What you are walking into

From the first step the ground is a floor, not a landscape. It does not rise. Water has nowhere to go but sideways, and sideways does not end. A single cloud sits over the construct and does not travel. Warmth lifts off the decks, goes up, and comes back as the same fine rain, in the same places, at the same rate. The light through that cloud is milky and sufficient. Shadows, when the cloud thins enough to cast them, do not swing. There is no sun to watch, and you should not count on a night. Your body will still want one. The place will not provide it.

Nothing has been allowed to round. Plate meets plate at the cut angle. Water stands on edges in fat warm beads and does not hurry. Wear happens and is answered at the same rate, somewhere under the decks, so the world stays at the hour of its completion. That freshness is not newness. Do not trust it as a sign that you have arrived early, or that a crew is about to appear, or that a storm is about to break. The storm already happened, permanently, at the scale of the whole floor.

There is no soil and no green. Horizontals are paved with small shelled lives, packed until they touch, each seated in a shallow the machining left. Verticals wear sheets of finger-long feathery mouths so dense the metal behind them is a rumor. Broader channels are more life than flow, and the life is the flow’s designed resistance. Nothing hunts in a way you can see. Nothing flees. You are not in their cycle. You are a brief occlusion of fixed light. When you move on, the warmer patch you left closes, and the animals resume the posture of animals that were never shaded.

The machines are the only relief. Pump-heads the breadth of headlands repeat until, at distance, they become a low weather of their own. Impellers turn without slap or cavitation. Conveyors shine and carry, if they carry, a film that is scraped and returned. Factories hold the set-point: temperature, salts, the cloud, the density at which the animals touch and do not crush. You can read a gauge. You do not need to. The needle was set when the edge was cut.

Sound fails to accumulate. Humidity and soft bodies take your footfall before the next step. Under that, if you stop, there is a presence rather than a noise: uncountable mouths, below the level where the ear keeps a record. A single click may arrive complete. It was not for you, and it will not repeat on any schedule your body can hold.

The walking decks hold water to the ankle, never more. That depth is a specification, not a tide. The water is warm as a laundry room. The air is wet as the hour after rain, and the hour does not advance. Walk far enough and the same pump-curve, the same depth, the same density of shells start to feel like a law. If there is an edge, it is only another joint. Beyond it the floor continues.

Clothing and skin

Dress for endless warm wet, not for cold rain and not for a desert of rust. A shell that keeps rain off and still breathes will save you. A waterproof sack that cannot vent will cook you. The temperature is indoor-laundry, not tropical noon and not sea-cold; the danger is maceration, not hypothermia and not heatstroke in the dramatic sense. Skin that stays wet for days splits, pales, and infects at every small cut. Plan to be wet at the ankles always and damp everywhere else, and plan to get truly dry at least once in each of your body’s days, even if the sky never agrees that a day has happened.

Bring:
- A light rain shell with pit zips and a brim that actually sheds the fine rain off your face. Hoods that seal your hearing will make the already-dead soundscape worse; you will want your ears, thin as the sound is.
- Quick-dry layers, several, so you can rotate them inside a dry bag and wear one that has had hours off your skin. Cotton is a mistake. Wool that stays warm when damp is worth the weight if your hands run cold; most people will not need insulation beyond a thin fleece for sleep.
- Gloves you can work in when wet, and a second pair kept dry for the times you must handle something that should not be slipped. Sharp arrises will open a bare palm if you steady yourself without looking.
- A neck gaiter. The rain is fine and constant. It will find your collar and live there.

Do not count on the environment to rinse you clean. The water carries a mineral film and a sweetness of dissolved metal that the mills refresh. It is bath-warm and looks innocent. Treat it as industrial process water that happens to be full of slow lives, not as a stream. Rinse cuts with water you brought or treated. A small bottle of mild antiseptic and a way to dry a wound under a cover will matter more than a dramatic trauma kit, though bring the trauma kit too: the edges are honest.

Feet

Your feet are the part of you the specification will judge.

The decks are ankle-deep, warm, and never deeper on the walking surfaces—if you stay on them. Off them, channels are packed with bodies and may be deeper than they look, because life fills them to a false floor. Do not trust a green-brown pavement as bottom. Probe.

Footwear wants three properties at once: it must drain or be acceptably flooded, it must not slip on wet sintered mineral and wet shell, and it must keep an edge from opening the sole or the side of the foot. Barefoot is a romantic error. The shells are seated hard, the plate under them is bright and sharp where a lip shows, and polymer gaskets in the seams can hide a cut that is exactly the width of a finger. Light hikers with aggressive wet grip, worn with wool-synthetic socks you can change, are better than heavy boots that fill and never dry. Some travelers do better in sandals with a real shank and toe protection, plus dry socks for camp, if the shells are not too cutting; you will not know until the first hundred steps. Carry both if you can. Change socks on a schedule you invent, because the sky will not give you evening.

Gaiters that seal at the calf will keep the constant splash from running down into a boot. They will also trap warmth. In this air that is usually fine.

A staff or a pair of poles is not optional romance. It finds false floors, takes weight off a turning ankle when a shell shifts, and gives you something to touch that is not already occupied. The end will be wet forever. Cap it so you are not stabbing animals with a spike you do not need. You are heavy. They do not make way. A point placed without looking is how you crack a shell that grew to a tolerance and cannot be replaced on any schedule you will see.

Sleep and a dry volume

There is no dry ground. Every horizontal you might call a campsite is paved with lives. Every vertical that could be a windbreak is hung with mouths. You will not find a ledge the rain has forgotten, because the rain does not forget, and the maintenance does not leave a dry patch as a kindness.

Bring a freestanding shelter that can sit on a tray, pad, or low cot so you are not crushing a pavement of shells with your body weight all night, and so you are not lying in the ankle-water. A bathtub floor in the tent, a full-coverage groundsheet, and a sleeping pad with a high R-value matter less for cold than for separation: you need a volume that is not the process. Seal the seams before you leave. Test the setup in a shower for an hour. If it wets through, it will wet through here by the second sleep, and there is no afternoon to dry it.

Earplugs help some people and panic others. The quiet is not empty; it is a volume already full, and plugging your ears can make the mouth-presence feel closer. Try both at home in a humid room with a fan set too low to hear. Know which you are.

A small towel, sacrally dry, lives in the inner bag and is used for face, hands, and the inside of the sleep shirt, then returned. One dry shirt reserved for sleep will do more for morale than any view. There is no view that changes.

Lighting

Daylight through the cloud is enough to read a gauge and enough to walk. It is not enough to feel oriented, and it does not tell time. Bring a warm headlamp and a second cool one, and use the warm one in camp so your eyes remember that light can have a color. Spare cells in a hard case. Humidity kills cheap contacts. A dim red mode is useful less for preserving night vision—there may be no night—than for keeping your own lamp from becoming the only event in a world that does not have events. Do not spotlight the animals for amusement. They will not flee, which makes the spotlight feel like a trespass even if nothing reacts.

If your body needs a cue that time passed, bring a watch you trust and a written schedule. The shadows are furniture. You cannot tell noon from the hour before it.

Water, food, and the sweetness in the channels

You will be surrounded by warm water and you must not drink it as you find it. It is recirculated, mineral, metal-sweet, held at a set-point for the lives in it, not for a human kidney. Even if a test strip says the obvious poisons are absent, the film and the dissolved load are not a mountain stream. Carry enough water to start, and a treatment method you have used before: a filter fine enough for the film, plus chemical or UV, and a way to settle or prefilter the sweetness so you are not drinking metal by the liter. Boiling works and costs fuel; fuel is heavy; the rain is constant and clean-looking and still falls through a cloud that is the exhalation of the whole floor. Catchment off a clean fly is better than dipping a channel. Let it stand. Treat it anyway. Taste a treated cup before you commit a day to that method. If it stays sweet, change method or ration what you carried.

There is nothing to forage. No roots, no greens, no fruiting bodies, no grazers you could ethically or practically take. The shelled lives are not mussels on a pier. They grew to a tolerance. Turn one over and the plate beneath is bright, younger than the animal. Prying one out to eat would be vandalism of a finished joint, and you have no idea what the mineral load in its flesh would do to you. Bring all your calories. Bring more than you think a flat walk requires. The walking is easy until the sameness makes you underestimate distance, and then you are far, and the pumps all look like the last pumps.

Salt is in your food bag for a reason. Warm constant wet sweats you without the drama of heat. You will cramp if you only drink.

A small stove that can run in fine rain, under a vestibule, with fuel calculated for the number of boils you truly need. No firewood. No duff. No permission in the substrate for a flame to be a good idea near polymer seams.

Navigation, because the world repeats

This is the failure that strands ordinary travelers, more than thirst or edges.

Landmarks repeat: the same pump-curve, the same block of factory, the same depth, the same density of shells per span, as if counted. The cloud does not move. Shadows do not swing. You cannot take a bearing off the sun. Distance lies, because nothing approaches and nothing falls behind except by your own counting.

Before you go:
- A compass, and the humility to notice if the construct has its own field. Check it against a known line in the first hour. If it drifts, believe the drift and switch to other methods. Do not argue with it for three days.
- A handheld GPS or inertial logger that does not need sky, if you have one, plus paper. Satellite may not exist here. An inertial trail that records your own steps is more honest than a sky that was never going to help.
- A click-counter or a tally of paces between pumps. The pumps are the only relief, and they are also the trap, because the next one is the same shape. Photograph them with a numbered card in frame, and leave no card behind. Paint, tape, cairns, and scratched marks are mistakes: maintenance answers wear. A mark you leave may be wiped as damage, or answered by a repair cycle you do not understand, and either way you have edited a joint that was not yours. If you must mark a route, use removable flags on your own stakes driven only into places you have already confirmed are bare plate—and pull every one on the way back. Better: breadcrumbs that are your own beacons, radio or light, recovered as you return.
- A turn-back rule written down before the sameness gets into your judgment. Time, water, and a maximum number of identical pumps. When the rule says return, return, even if the next housing looks, for the first time, slightly different. Slightly different is how this place imitates news.

Tell someone the rule if anyone can hear you leave. If no one can, the rule is still the rule. The edge, if it exists, is only another joint. You can walk until your water math fails and still be in the middle.

Sound, speech, and other people

Your footfall is the loudest event, and it dies before the next step. Calling out to a companion will not carry the way it does in a canyon or even in open rain at home. Stay close enough to see each other. Agree on lamp signals: two flashes, stop; a slow circle, come to me; a steady beam on your own feet, I am well and walking. Whistles help less than you hope; the air takes them. A small radio on a short range, waterproof, with spare batteries in a sealed tin, is worth more than lung power. Test it in the first hour, not on the third day.

If you travel alone, accept that no echo will keep you company. Some people find that peaceful. Some people find that the mouth-presence under the quiet becomes a voice if they are tired. Eat, dry your face, and do not follow a click. The click was a fact you walked into, not an invitation.

How to touch, and what not to change

You cannot choose a place to put a hand that is not already occupied, and none of the occupants object, startle, or make way. That is not consent to use them as handholds. Shells will crack under a careless grip. Feathery sheets will mat and tear if you haul yourself up a housing by them. The plate beneath is built to a tolerance that includes the animal and does not include your boot as a cleaning tool.

Walk the decks. Use rails, flanges, and the housings themselves where the metal is bare. If you must cross a paved horizontal, distribute weight, step on the plate between seats when you can see it, and do not drag your feet. Do not turn animals over to see the bright metal. The description of that brightness is already the lesson: the tolerance did not erode. You are not here to demonstrate it again.

Do not open panels, throw breakers, block a channel, dam the ankle-water, or “fix” a bead that has not fallen. The equilibrium is the finished object. A stuck impeller or a dead stack light is not your repair, and a housing the breadth of a headland is not a curiosity you climb inside. Impellers turn without laboring because the water has not changed its mind. Your body in a intake is how a perfect system acquires an occlusion it was not specified to clear. Look. Listen for the presence. Leave the set-point alone.

Stack lights show one color and have always shown it. If you ever see another color, that is the first news this world has offered you. Treat it as a reason to stop and leave the block, not as a reason to enter the factory. You do not know what the other color is for. You know the normal one means the cloud is being fed and the density is being held. Normal is the entire local definition of safety.

Health, small and large

Expect trench-foot’s warm cousin, blisters from wet skin, shallow lacerations from edges, and a rash where a strap stayed wet. Pack moleskin, a needle, antiseptic, a few closure strips, and a way to keep a dressing dry under a cover of tape that actually sticks to damp skin—test the tape at home. Antifungal powder for feet and groin. Rehydration salts. A course of an antibiotic you already tolerate, on the advice of your own clinician, for a wound that goes hot far from any edge; this is not a place that will grow you a plantain leaf.

Eyes will tire from the milky flat light and the lack of a horizon that means anything. Blink, look at a near object that is yours—the watch, the lamp, a companion’s face—on a schedule.

Ears will strain for change and invent it. If a click becomes a sentence, you are past the turn-back rule in spirit even if your tally says you are fine. Sit, eat, dry your hands, and shorten the day.

Do not wade chest-deep to “see the curtains better.” The filter-life is the resistance the flow was designed around. Your lungs are not a designed resistance. Ankle-deep is the walking number. Deeper is a channel, and a channel is a machine that happens to be alive.

Mind, and the hour that does not advance

The strangeness stays inside the recognizable, which is why it wears on you. These could almost be filter-feeders you have seen on a shore. What will not file is the fit, the scale, and the refusal of time. A continental machine, warm, wet, and finished. No roots pry the plates. No season opens a crack. Nothing is growing toward a future you can join.

Ordinary travelers unravel here in a few predictable ways.

They wait for weather. It will not come. The rain is not building toward a storm you can shelter from and then walk out of. Schedule your shelters by the watch, not by the sky.

They wait to be noticed. Nothing will startle. No warden will wave you off a pump. The absence of objection is not welcome and not permission. It is irrelevance. You are a brief occlusion. If you need to feel received, you will start poking the system until it records you as damage. Do not. The place is allowed to be indifferent. That indifference is the whole condition.

They walk “just to the next different thing.” The next different thing is the same thing, counted once more. The law of the plain is repetition. Novelty is a feeling you have to supply from your pack: a book, a recorded voice, a game that needs no table, a letter you do not open until the third sleep. Bring a mind-object that is not the pumps.

They lose the boundary between peaceful and erased. The quiet is full, not holy and not hostile. If you stop and the mouth-presence feels like attention, remember the fact you were given: it is uncountable work, below the level where the ear keeps a record. It was underway before you were a shadow and it will resume the exact posture when your shadow leaves. You do not owe it speech. You do not owe it a longer stay to prove you can bear it.

A practical inner kit: named days on the watch; a turn-back rule; one conversation, even if it is aloud to yourself, at each rest, so your voice stays a human scale inside a volume that swallows scale; and a reason to leave that you wrote before you entered. “See it” is a weak reason once seeing it becomes identical at every bearing. “Return while I still want to” is a stronger one.

What to leave behind

Leave the tools of improvement. No seed. No sample vials you intend to fill by prying. No spray paint, no rivets, no idea that a traveler’s mark is a gift to the next traveler. The next traveler needs your notes, not your scars in the plate. Maintenance may erase the scars anyway, and then you have only confused yourself.

Leave the expectation of animals as persons who will negotiate. They do not graze, flee, or object. Courtesy here is geometric: put your weight on the structure, not on the occupants; take nothing that grew to a seat; do not become an input.

Leave the heroics of endurance. This is not a summit. The danger is not exposure in the mountain sense. The danger is staying past your water, your skin, and your ability to tell one pump from another, in a place that will remain at the hour of its completion whether you are in it or not.

A kit, gathered

Rain shell and brim; spare dry layers; gloves; gaiters; two answers for the feet; poles with kind tips. Freestanding shelter, bathtub floor, cot or pad to lift you off the pavement, one sacrally dry towel and one sleep shirt. Treatment for water you catch rather than dip; all your food; salts; a stove that works in fine rain. Compass, paces, a logger that does not need the sky, removable beacons you will recover, a written turn-back. Lamp, spare cells, a watch. Whistle if you like, radio if you can, signals agreed. Dressings that stick when wet, something for skin that has been wet too long, and the sense to keep cuts dry. A book or a voice that is not this place. No fire, no forage, no souvenirs cut from the tolerance.

And a sentence you can repeat when the cloud’s single body and the ranked housings start to feel like a law aimed at you: it was already like this. The rain is not personal. The quiet is not a verdict. The animals did not refuse you; they were never in a posture from which refusal is possible. You came to walk a finished floor, ankle-deep, warm, sharp, and inhabited to the last arris.

Walk the decks. Keep your weight on the build. Dry your feet on the schedule you brought with you. Count the pumps. When the count says return, return—while the mist can still close over the patch you warmed, and while you are still a visitor, not a stain the mills have to answer.
