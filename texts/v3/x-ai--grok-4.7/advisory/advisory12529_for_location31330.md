Pack as if you are going to stand for a long time inside a finished bowl, not as if you are going to explore a wilderness. The court is small. Everything you bring will be announced by the brick, and almost nothing you bring will be useful once you are down there. The useful work is done before the stair turns.

Go in the middle of the day, when the light falls straight and you can read the floor before you trust it. Dawn and dusk belong to the swifts’ arrivals and departures and to the rim-shadow walking the pavement; you will be in the way of both, and you will not see the dark seams well enough to place a foot. After rain, wait a day. The channels speak, the basins rise a handspan, and the brick that looked dry from the rim is a skin of water over old lime. The place returns to its ordinary secretive measure quickly. Let it.

Wear what will not snag and will not shout. Soft soles, thin enough that you feel tile and the lip of a channel before your weight commits, grippy enough that a filmed corbel does not take you. No hard heels. A footfall here reports itself to the whole court before you come into view. Bare arms if you can bear the brush of brick; the walkway is narrow enough that both shoulders meet the wall unless you turn sideways, and a pack, a stiff jacket, a camera on a strap will strike the honeycomb and send a chord up the piers. Leave the pack at the rim, or wear a small soft bag against the front of the body, nothing that swings. Dark or clay-colored cloth. The swifts will not care about fashion. The stone-knock birds will care about a bright sleeve moving in their permanent dusk under the walkway.

Cover the mouth and nose with something you can breathe through without sounding like weather. Guano has glazed the piers under the nests into a black glass the rain polishes and never quite removes. The air is warm, close, a little above the temperature of skin, and it holds dust of brick, lime, feather, and dried droppings. You will not be poisoned by standing there. You may spend the afternoon tasting the place in the back of the throat if you treat it like open country. A light cloth is enough. Do not wear a mask so thick you start breathing hard; hard breathing is another instrument in a room that absorbs nothing.

Bring water for yourself and do not plan to take any from the court. The three standings—the shield-wide basin, the north trough, the cup at the pier—are the whole economy. Fingerling fish live in the long trough on whatever the drips deliver and on each other. Frogs the size of a thumb use the film. A sedge has spent what may be its whole life in one crack. Your bottle is the only polite drink. If you wash your hands, do it above, in whatever country surrounds the excavation, not in a channel that has been finding its grade for centuries.

Hands should be free and reasonably clean, and you should decide in advance not to steady yourself on the obvious places. The undersides of the corbels are filmed. The glazed piers are slick and not yours. The niches are occupied: swifts in the column of light, lizards the color of dry mortar holding so still they read as flaws, mud-and-fiber nests packed three deep in the dusk under the walkway, beetles in the damp seams. A hand in a cell is a hand in a house. If you must touch, touch the open pavement or the top of a tread you have already trusted with a foot. The brick is warm. That warmth is the mass giving the day back. It is not an invitation to lean your whole weight into a partition thinner than a wrist.

Light, if you bring any, should be dim, warm, and pointed at your own feet, never into a niche and never up the throat of the court. Ordinary noon already does the work the place was built to receive. A bright beam will pin a lizard, empty a cell, and come back at you off every hard surface as noise you can see. You do not need to see into every honeycomb. The court is finished without your inventory.

Ears need more preparation than eyes. Nothing absorbs. A drip arrives as a tick and leaves as a chord. Six swifts sound like a flock, and the flock sounds like weather. If you startle, if you speak at conversational volume, if you laugh, you will occupy the volume the way a wing occupies it—except you will not leave on the turn. Practice being quiet in the way that does not become held breath. Speak, if you must speak, low and close, as if the person beside you were already inside the sound. Better: agree on silence before the stair, and keep names and observations for the rim. The court is always in the middle of being heard. You are joining a conversation that does not pause for guests.

Body, then. An ordinary human frame is large here. The walkway was not cut for a pack and a stride. Turn sideways. Keep your center over the slabs. Do not hop the channels; they are shallow and dark and decisive, and a heel in one of them is a small violence against a grade the water spent centuries correcting. Stand, when you stand, in the middle of the pavement, where the light is plainest and the channels run away from you. That is the only place that reads as unclaimed. Ledges nearly touch. Territories are measured in handspans. The migration of lizards and beetles out of the rim-shadow is only a few of those handspans, and it rearranges the floor. If you pace, you are weather. If you stay still long enough, the court resumes around you—the throat-pulse of a mortar-colored lizard, the faint pop of a fish amplified into a finger against clay, the stitching of liverwort at the lip that never quite dries.

Time is the other supply to carry, and it is lighter than water. The drips keep the smallest clock. The swifts keep the daylight. The frogs answer rain and then thin to single notes. Somewhere under the walkway a stalactite the length of a finger is meeting the floor and has been meeting it for a long time. You will not see that meeting happen. You might see that it is happening, if you stop trying to complete the place with your attention. Give the visit at least as long as it takes for your own entrance to stop echoing in your nerves. Ten minutes is a disturbance. An hour is a beginning. Leave before your legs demand a shift that the floor cannot spare.

Do not collect. Not a tile from a failed gap—the gap was filled, generation after generation, with more tile, a refusal you should not interrupt. Not a feather, not a slick of guano for later curiosity, not a pebble from the cup the drip is deepening, not a pinch of moss from the only green the court allowed. Nothing roots deep because there is no deep: pavement, then pavement, then packed fill so the court would not heave. What lives here lives on surface and patience. Taking a surface is taking the habitat.

Do not improve it. The repairs already visible—a patch of smaller brick, a sistered pier, a channel re-cut a finger deeper, a few black swollen pins holding a slab that wanted to walk—are the same order of means that made the hole and then made everything inside the hole. You are not the next mason. If a slab looks eager to walk, step elsewhere. If a channel is silted, it has already found a kink and a pool and a way to run true again. Your trowel, your cord, your good intentions are a rise in technology the court never required.

Food: eat before, above, out of the disk of sky. Crumbs are a new resource in a volume that is already saturated, and the tenants will renegotiate around them in ways you will not stay long enough to answer for. If you carry a little food for the walk out, seal it so the warm air does not broadcast it. The stone-knock birds keep a schedule better than most people. Do not become an item on it.

Company: one other person at most, and only if you have already practiced moving as one animal on narrow stone. Two voices are a flock. Three are weather that will not bank away. Children can visit if they can do sideways and silence and the middle of the pavement; if they cannot yet, the rim is the true overlook, and the disk of sky from below is not worth teaching them to be a disturbance. Animals of your own—dogs, birds on the hand—do not come. The court has already signed its tenants. A dog is an unratified species with a louder foot.

Multiverse habits to set down before the stair. Do not open a gate, a light-bridge, a calling, or a return-thread inside the bowl. Sound does not leave; a working might not either, or it might leave through the brick in ways the niches will pay for. If your way of traveling requires a focus, a circle, a spoken name, do that work in the surrounding country, unseen from the floor and irrelevant to it, and walk down as what you are when you are not traveling: a body, a guest, a warm thing among warm brick. Come back the same way. The stair was cut, not found. Use it. Do not shortcut the rim. The piers once carried a cover and now carry only weather; they do not need to carry you.

A small kit, if you insist on carrying something past the turn: water, the cloth for your face, a soft tie for hair or loose cloth so nothing ticks the brick on a swift’s turn, a bit of cord only for your own gear, a folded square of plain fabric to sit on if the pale tiles are filmed where you mean to be still. No poles. No drones. No samples vials dressed up as curiosity. No music. No flame—the warmth is already enough, and smoke has nowhere to go but into the honeycomb and back into your mouth. A notebook waits above. Writing on the floor is another hard noise, and the observations worth keeping are the ones that survive the climb: the way the light climbs back up the walls as a dull reflected wash; the north trough’s single sedge; the tick of a wingtip becoming part of the noise; the feeling that the room is not pristine and not ruined, only settled into the use that replaced the use it was built for.

Expect, bodily, to be slightly too warm, slightly damp at the nose, slightly deafened in the sense that silence never quite arrives between events, and slightly embarrassed by your own size. That embarrassment is the right orientation. The oddness is not marvelous. Water still obeys courses cut by hands that did not know fish. Nests are built to the module of a brick. Overflow lips still govern the basins. You are the variable. Prepare by becoming predictable: slow on the treads, sideways on the walk, still in the middle, quiet in the mouth, empty in the hands, gone before the light leaves the rim.

When you come up, do not shake the court off all at once. Brick dust and the sweet-foul of old glaze will be in your clothes; brush them off away from the lip, so you are not sowing the stitching of liverwort with your departure. Drink. Let your ears finish. The surrounding country will seem oddly absorbent. That is how you know the bowl did what bowls do.

If you remember one instruction when the stair opens and the volume declares itself all at once, remember this: you were not brought, and almost nothing was. What remains down there is occupation—dense, warm, noisy, and complete inside its own circumference. Match it by occupying as little as a person can, and by leaving the cuts, the glaze, the clocks, and the tenants signed as you found them.
