Prepare as if you will be wet, crowded, and never quite finished drying. The bowl is small enough to walk around in the time a pot takes to boil, and too alive for that walk to stay simple. The town is not a ruin and not a wilderness. It is a working place grown into its own drainage, and you will be the only thing in it that expects dryness, quiet, and a clear floor.

**What the visit actually feels like.** Cool, not cold. Hands redden and keep working. Daylight arrives clean over the rim and then spends itself in leaves, so the lanes at noon are a green dusk broken only where someone has pollarded a stand or kept the market open by cutting. The ground gives underfoot—root-mat over peat over rock—and then surprises you with settled stone, black leaf-water, or a pool the width of a door. Passage is a negotiation: slots between living walls worn shiny at shoulder height, people carrying bundles, children at full speed, mending happening in the lane you meant to use. Sound is structural. The lip-falls run all day and thicken when the seams give hard. Frogs floor the noise. Birds argue in the cane. Goats on the roofs keep a clock that never agrees with itself. When the rain increases—and rain is a change in volume, not an event—the thatch turns to hiss and everyone shouts. When it eases a degree, the town reassembles one sound at a time. Cooking smoke fails to climb and wanders the slots at head height. Under the green smell there is wet wool, guano, fish, milk, and leaves that never dry.

Nothing here is trying to exclude you. Nothing here will rearrange itself to keep you comfortable.

**Clothing.** Wear wool, or something that still insulates when damp, in layers you can peel. Cotton next to skin will stay clammy and chill you by afternoon. Take a second dry layer in a truly waterproof bag and do not open that bag in the lanes. A light oiled or waxed outer shell is enough; a heavy modern rain suit will cook you in the green dusk and snag on cane. Hem everything short of the mud. The lowest band stands in finger-deep water after a hard give, and thresholds are already a step high for that reason. Expect to be wet to the knee for hours and not to have a place to hang things where they finish drying.

**Feet.** This matters more than the coat. The slabs are slick, the root-mat is springy and hides holes, the cane walkways and swollen root-bridges polish under hands and hooves. Bring footwear with real grip, that drains, that you can walk in while soaked, and that does not fall apart when wet for days. Ankle support helps when a slab has settled. Pack spare socks in the dry bag and change into them only when you are actually stopped for the night. If your boots are leather, accept that they will darken and stiffen; oil them before you enter and do not expect to dry them by a fire that cannot climb. Foot skin that stays wet will split. At day’s end, get your feet into dry wool even if the rest of you is still damp, and look at them. The town’s people work in this all their lives. You do not.

**Skin, cuts, and the damp that does not leave.** Metal beads. So will you. Assume any nick from cane, a goat, a duck, a fish-bone, or a slipped slab will stay moist and sulk. Carry a small kit: clean cloth, something to wash a wound, a way to cover it that can survive humidity, and a salve you already trust. Do not treat the clear rills as an infinite faucet for this. Wash hands before you eat; the market band is guano, fish-slime, milk, and animal drink, and the water under the lanes carries the shifting of cattle three streets away. Insects are weather. Midges over black water, flies at the drying lines, a high whine in the shade you only notice when it stops. A head-net is not ridiculous if you are the sort of person midges choose. So is a mild repellent, applied before you drop into the lower band, and reapplied after you have been in the hiss of rain. Tuck cuffs. The living walls brush you constantly.

**Sleep.** Do not plan to sleep on the ground or against a living wall. The floor is peat, seep, and other people’s animals; the wall is someone’s house and will shed insects and water onto you. If you can carry a hammock or a narrow camp cot and a bug net, do. A closed bivy that traps breath will become a private swamp by midnight. Earplugs or soft wax are worth more than a better pillow: frogs, falls, a goat shifting on thatch, a bell, a bird starting before the light. Smoke may find you anyway. If you are offered a corner of a house, take the threshold step seriously, keep your wet gear on the wet side of it, and do not peg, cut, or lash anything into the living poles. Those poles are the wall. Harvesting them is how a house is unmade.

**Water and food.** Some rills are clear enough that people drink them, and the seams have been carrying water longer than the town. You are not those people, and you will not know which thread has passed a byre. Carry water from a source you have watched, or treat it, and keep the vessel sealed. The wet market will feed you—fish, milk, greens trained on the same cane as the houses, things dried on the upper terrace where it is drier by a finger’s depth and no more. Eat what is hot or what has been dried up there. Be cautious with still water, raw milk, and anything that has sat in the lower band’s splash. Your stomach is the piece of equipment most likely to end the visit early. A small metal cup you already own is more useful than a complicated stove; fuel is whatever the place burns, and smoke does not leave the bowl cleanly.

**What to carry, and what to leave.** The slots are a sideways turn wide. A large rigid pack will make you a blockage, and a blockage in this town is a social event. Soft, narrow, and lashed so nothing swings into a living wall or a child’s path. Leave the extra metal. Iron here is scarce, shared around a ward, and already complained about; a pocketknife is plenty, and a bright new blade offered casually can land wrong—too valuable, too much like a claim. If you want to arrive with something useful, dry fiber, good needles, waxed thread, salt, or a little clean oil travel better than hardware. Do not bring anything that must stay dry and also must be consulted often: paper maps dissolve into the air, and devices that hate condensation will fog, corrode, or simply become another wet stone in your bag. If you carry a light, make it one that works when beaded, and do not shine it into houses or into animals’ faces after the rim goes gold and the bowl is already evening.

**Moving through it.** You can navigate by the falls: louder toward the west lanes and the lip, softer where a house-row baffles them. The upper terrace is looms and drying racks. The middle is houses. The lower band is market, animals, and the water’s gathering before it goes over. Learn that, then accept that the braid of rills will still turn you. Yield the slot. Do not step into a pool children are using as a street without looking, and do not assume a goat on a roof is lost—the roof bears it, and the upper leaves are fodder. Ducks will treat your ankles as reed. A small deer may come over the rim at dusk; it is not yours to decide. Do not cut cane to make a shortcut, a walking stick, or a clearer view. Clearance is the town’s maintenance, done by the people who will replant the plot to the water’s plan if a house fails. Your stick is not that plan.

The lip is the one direction that is not a town. The last gardens lean over a drop that keeps its own weather, and the town’s water leaves in threads into trees far below. Go there in daylight, without a heavy pack, and do not trust a glistening path of bean-leaves and bitter green after rain has increased. Behind you the bowl will sound contained. It is. You are the one who can step out of the containment.

**Pace and attention.** Arrive early enough to still have light on the rim, and do not try to “do” the bowl. A slow circuit, stopping when a mallet is splitting a wall-pole in your path, is the visit. Sit where you are not in the braid of traffic. Watch how people keep wool moving on the upper terrace because inside it would never finish. Notice that the oldest bridges have swallowed their stone posts. The balance is old: channels cleared, not redesigned; houses harvested and replanted to the same wet logic. You are a dry animal passing through a place that has not needed to hold still for a very long time.

Before you leave the rim path and drop in, set your dry bag, drink, and put the head-net where your hand can find it without looking. After that the town will be louder than your plans, and that is the correct order.
