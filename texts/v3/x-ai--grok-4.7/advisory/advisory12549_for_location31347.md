The visit is possible, and it is small. That is the first correction to make before you pack. From the sill you can see the far rim. The bowl is the size of a room that remembers being a works. You will not trek it. You will enter a few postures, or you will not enter at all, and most of what is worth knowing can be learned from the only true bank: a low lip of older, harder root, warm, wet, already finished.

Prepare for agreement, not for wilderness. Nothing here hunts. The mass does not reach. It has met its appetite and is holding the meeting. What it still does is simpler and more patient than an animal: it takes weight, it takes heat, it takes a shape left against it long enough, and it answers in the language of the machine it swallowed. A boot prints a well. A cut becomes a flange. A body that lies down is a form the leaves can learn. Your preparation is mostly the refusal of those agreements, carried on an ordinary human body that sweats, slips, tires, and cannot photosynthesize its way out.

Go in daylight, and do not stay for a change in the pump’s pitch. Hours are a visit. A night is a negotiation you will lose slowly. Seasons here are a sweetening or souring of the air and a slight shift in an iron knock. You do not need to sample them. The bowl will be the same when you return, if you return; it does not spread, and it does not miss you. Leave while your outline is still only pressure, not habit.

What the place will do to you

The floor is not ground. It is welded stem and leaf-mat, warm as a body left in the sun, filmed with water that has nowhere to go. It gives, then holds. A well fills under a boot and sinks into the plant to reappear somewhere else. There is no current to read and no pool to step around. Every surface is wet: cuticle, scale, seam, rivet. Daylight arrives already broken into small reflections. Nothing glitters. Everything glistens, and the glisten hides edges.

The air is close, ordinary in brightness, wrong in the mouth. Sap and hot oil. A wet exhalation from a seam near the boiler-plate that condenses before it can rise. Hollow stems breathe slow heated air. In the cavities there is little sky and less exchange. You will overheat before you chill. You will prune your fingers white. You will not notice thirst until you are already thick-headed, because the world is made of water and none of it is yours.

Metal is inside the mass, not arranged as a ruin you can walk. Rivets still proud, ringed with callus. Flywheel teeth packed with fiber, still turning, easier to hear than to see. A rail that enters a trunk and does not exit. Pipes. A gauge glass holding brown water that rises and falls with the pump. The plants nearest the heat grow in plates, a few degrees warmer, having taken the habit of scale. They will not warn you by burning where you expect a burn. Sap in the hollow stems carries a low thrill, not quite a shock, as if old wiring kept a fraction of its work. Treat every wet stem and every proud rivet as live until your hand has decided otherwise, and then do not trust the decision.

Sound is continuous and close: drip on metal, drip on taut leaf, drip on a skin of water that never becomes a lake; an uneven knock, iron on iron padded by something soft that grew into the gap; leaves the thickness of hide flexing like oiled leather. It does not crest. It becomes the silence you measure other sounds against. You will stop hearing your companion. You will stop hearing yourself. A startle is almost absent here, which means the one thing that moves—something small and pale in a pipe-mouth, once, maybe—will arrive without a preface. Do not hunt it. Fauna is nearly not part of this ecology. You are the anomaly with a pulse that is not a pump.

The growth is slow enough that a visit will not close over you, and fast enough that anything you leave will be fitted. A leaf that was a door last year is a door still. A cord, a tool, a glove, a knee held too long in one gallery, becomes a measurement the mass can keep. Do not cut. A wound weeps the same brown water the gauge holds, and the lips thicken into a fitting. You do not want your blood in that conversation, and you do not want to teach the place a new joint.

How to arrive

Approach on the sill and sit before you stand inside anything. Learn the knock. Learn the count of the drip from the broad leaf in the flywheel’s vault, if you can see that vault from the lip. Learn which shoulder of the western wall is boiler-plate gone green-black. These are your only honest landmarks. Green on green, wet on wet, the interior will not hold a map in the eye. The place is smaller than confusion, which is how people get turned around in rooms.

Mark the sill itself, not a path inward. A strip of bright cloth tied to your own pack, a stone you brought from outside set on the harder root, the angle of daylight on the far rim. Do not hammer, do not carve, do not push a stake into the living bank. The bank is the exit. Insulting it is a poor first bargain.

If you go in, go in with a partner who stays on the sill, and switch. The bowl does not need a team. Two bodies are already a crowd in a nave of stems. Speech will fail at a short distance; agree on touch-signals and on a time to be back at the lip that does not depend on hearing the pump “sound different.” The pump will not sound different. Your judgment will.

Clothing and the feet

Dress for a hot wet machine room that is also a marsh that stood up, and that will try to keep the shape of your knees.

Wear light, close cloth that dries faster than cotton and does not bloom into loose skirts, scarves, or strap-ends. Anything that flaps will drink, cling, and catch a rivet or a flywheel tooth. Layers you can open at the throat and the wrists matter more than warmth. You want to vent. A thin hood is better than a hat with a brim; brims shed drip into your eyes and snag the undersides of shutter-sized leaves. Bare skin is a mistake: maceration, unseen metal, the brown film. Cover, but do not armor yourself into bulk. You will kneel. Standing is possible only where the housing left a vault, and there the ceiling is one leaf.

Footwear is the real decision. A narrow boot punches a well and then asks you to walk in it. You want a sole broad enough to spread weight across the mat, with a shallow tread that sheds slime instead of packing it, and an ankle that you can still feel. The romantic expedition boot is wrong. So is a bare foot. Think of something between a marsh shoe and a light workshop shoe: drain, grip on wet iron and wet cuticle, and enough sole that a proud rivet does not come up through you. Carry a second pair in a sealed bag if you own them. You will not dry the first pair here. You will only change into dryness you brought.

Gloves: thin, high-cuffs, one pair that still lets you feel heat and the low thrill in a stem, and a heavier pair if you must steady yourself near plate. Do not grip the boiler shoulder. Do not steady yourself on the flywheel, even if the turn is almost invisible. A tooth packed with fiber is still a tooth, and the motion is real. If a glove comes off, it is lost; the mass is better at holding fabric than you are at fishing in a seam. Wrist loops.

Kneepads, soft ones, if you mean to use the gallery of stems. The floor there is living and warm, and your kneecaps are not. Do not kneel in one place as if at a shrine. Shift. The place is slow, not inert.

What to carry, and what not to feed it

Carry water from outside, more than the distance suggests. The bowl looks like drink and is the machine’s blood-film: brown, shared with the gauge glass, faintly of oil and sap, traveling through the wet the way minerals travel to rime the oldest leaves. Do not taste it to be sure. Do not rinse a wound with it. Do not top up a bottle “just to the line.” Purification tablets are a story you tell yourself about rivers. This is not a river.

Food the same: sealed, eaten on the sill, wrappers taken out in your pocket. A crumb is small. The habit of leaving crumbs is how a works becomes a garden that includes you.

A dry bag inside the pack, and the pack itself with nothing dangling. In it: your outside water; a small sealed cloth for your face; a simple light with a red mode, not because the day is dark but because cavities under the leaf-plaster go to a thinner green, and white light will explode into a thousand wet reflections until you cannot see the rivet. Use light little. Let the eyes take the glistening as the native condition.

A short probe that is not a blade: a stick you brought, blunt, to touch ahead of a hand where a seam may be hot or hollow. No machete, no saw, no specimen shears. There is no understory to clear and no clearing to make. Cutting is how you join the plumbing.

Cord, if you bring it, stays in your hand and leaves with you. A fixed line is a gift of geometry. The leaves already remember diagrams—straight runs, right angles, the pitch of pipes. Do not add yours.

Ear protection, the light kind, in a pocket, not on your head at the start. You need the knock at first; it is the heart, and it orients you when sight fails. Later, if the drip has become the inside of your skull, put one plug in and leave one ear to the room. Full silence in here is how you miss the change in your own breath.

A strip of soap and a way to wash with outside water after, not during. Oils and sap will sit on skin. Warm wet metal-cuts sulk and then infect in ordinary human fashion. Your kit is a small ordinary one: clean dressings, something to irrigate with your own water, a way to cover a puncture, and the understanding that rust here is a patina, not a process you can watch, which does not make old iron kind. Update nothing mystical. Update tetanus before you travel, the way you would for any works, living or dead.

Leave the clever instruments in the bag or at home. Wet, heat, and a low conductive thrill are unkind to circuits. If you record, accept a ruined microphone and a lens that must be wiped every minute, and do not chase a better angle into the vault alone. The photograph will not hold the knock. That is not a failure of nerve. It is the place.

How to move, once you are in it

From the sill, name the three things you will touch with your eyes and not your hands: the boiler shoulder, the flywheel, the gauge if you can find its glint. Then decide whether you need to be inside at all.

If you enter, enter low and slow, weight curious, not committed. Test the mat with the blunt stick, then with a foot, then with a transferred knee. Where it gives and keeps giving, back up. Where it gives and then holds, you may cross, and you do not stamp. Stamping is how you open a well and then stand in the drink.

The gallery of hollow stems is for a hand and a kneeling posture, not for a torso you force. Put a hand in only after the back of the glove has felt the breath coming out—heated, sap-sweet, oil-tinged—and only if you can still see your wrist. A stem that is warmer than the rest, or that thrills, is a wire that learned a new sheath. Withdraw. Do not become the experiment that confirms it.

The vault by the flywheel is the one standing-room, ceiling a single leaf veined like plaster, dripping on a count you could learn. Learn the count from the side, not from under the axle. Do not lie down to look up. Lying down is the posture the mass can use. It would take longer than a visit and faster than a life, and you are not here to measure that interval with your ribs.

Do not chase the pale motion in the pipe-mouth. If it was there, it has already declined to repeat. Do not climb the far rim from inside; the rim is root and stained iron no taller than a shoulder, and the inside face is the wettest wall in a wet bowl. Go back to the sill and walk around outside if the outside exists in your world. If it does not, the rim is a view, not a route.

Steam is a sigh, not a whistle, which makes it easier to lean into. The seam near the boiler-plate exhales and condenses. Your face is not a gauge glass. The plates of tissue nearest the heat are not safer because they are plant. They are how the heat learned to wear green. Radiant warmth on the cheek is the border. Past it, you are sampling the engine with your skin.

When to leave

Leave when the knock has become silence, when your hands look like they have been doing dishes for a year, when you notice you are matching your breath to the pump, when a leaf against your shoulder feels like a decision rather than a drip. Leave when you want to cut one stem “just to see.” That want is the place speaking in your tools. Leave when the light is still the light you came in under. The bowl does not do evening in any way you can trust; it does a slower glisten.

On the sill, sit until your pulse is yours again. Drink your water. Wipe the oil-sap off your face with the cloth you kept dry. Do not scrape your boots clean onto the bank; the mud is not mud, and the bank does not need your sole-pattern. Bag the wet cloth. Walk out with everything that entered, including the curiosity.

After, in whatever ordinary air you return to, expect the drip to continue in the room for an hour, and the smell of hot oil to live in your hair. Wash with water that knows how to leave. Check the small punctures you did not feel. If a fingertip still thrills and will not settle, that is a nerve and a story for a medic, not a souvenir to press against another stem.

You do not need rites. You need shortness, breadth of foot, dryness you imported, hands that refuse to teach the mass a new fitting, and the willingness to let a chapel-sized engine-garden be finished without you. Sit on the warm sill. Hear the knock, the drip, the leather-flex of leaves. See, under ordinary daylight, the whole small interior shining with water that has nowhere else to go. That is the visit. The interior is for a few careful postures, and then it is for leaving while you are still only a guest, and not yet a part.
