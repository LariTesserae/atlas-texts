An ordinary human arrives in that bowl already slightly wrong for it: too loud, too dry, too used to distance. Preparation is less about gear than about agreeing, before you step off the rim, to live inside a finished shape that will not make room for you and will not stop rearranging itself under your feet.

**Air, cold, and light.** The floor is well above the tree line. Thin air will not negotiate. Spend days beforehand at height if you can; if you cannot, plan the first hours as a slow descent of the rim, not an errand. Headache, nausea, and a stupid shortness of breath on a lane that looks flat are ordinary, not omens. The cold is dry in the lung and wet on the skin at once. Brightness is total and colorless — no canopy, no haze worth trusting — and every wet face becomes a mirror by late day. Eyes will water and then burn. Bring a brim that cuts glare without blocking the path at your feet, and a cloth you can wet and wring; the weep on sunward stone is real water, cold enough to matter, and there is nowhere shaded to recover in. Night, when the brightness goes edge-on, drops as a lowering of the whole air rather than as wind. Sleep will be colder than the day suggested. The channels skin with ice at their margins and keep moving in the center. Do not assume a frozen edge is a floor.

**Feet and the ground.** Nothing here is cover, and nothing is stable in the way a dry path is stable. Flags are polished by centuries of sheet-water. Lanes are two people wide only if both have agreed not to stop, and they are also the spillways when a cornice lets go. Rotten ice bursts against the upper walls as a soft thud and a white breath, then is gone into the weep; the wet it leaves is what you walk around, not through, because the carts already are. Wear what will grip wet stone and will not mind being soaked to the calf all day. Soles that skate on polished rock are a mistake you make once. Keep your hands free. A pack that forces you to turn sideways in a lane is a social problem before it is a physical one. There is no outskirts: from the moment you are in the bowl you are in the town, and the town is a press.

**Water is the law, and it does not talk.** Every face weeps. Seams darken and brighten. Grooves fork, pool, and go on. Houses share walls that are also water-courses; a slab you can walk may be the lid on a neighbor’s spring arriving in someone else’s cistern. Do not step off a lid onto an open channel because it looks shallow. Do not drink from a pan, a hoof-print of overflow, or the bright thread in the middle of a skinned channel without knowing what the town drinks and from which tank. The springs seem to mint fish; cisterns are grained with pale fish the length of a hand, facing the inflow. The water is crowded with life even when it looks clear. Boil or ask. Asking is better. Mineral cold will not make a bad mouthful safe, and the brief domes — the coin-that-spends-once — leave a dark smell when they come apart.

**Those brief things.** In full light, in the smallest pans, a coherence assembles: translucent jointed swimmers, a skin of algae too thin to be green, a standing wave that ought to collapse. It tracks the sun a fraction, lasts a minute or ten, and dies if touched — water, corpses, and that smell — or walks a hand’s breadth toward brighter stone and fails on its own by afternoon. The town does not trouble them unless the pan is needed. You should not trouble them at all. They are not souvenirs, not specimens, not a trick to show someone. Touching one spends it. There are always more pans; that is not permission. Children net the jointed things in the weep-channels; that is their economy and their play, not yours. Do not compete for a channel. Upstream travel against a current that should defeat them is ordinary here. Your surprise is not interesting.

**The crowd, and the quiet.** From the rim it looks like an anthill spread flat. Up close it is elbows, breath, cold fish, mineral water, woodsmoke that cannot rise and instead slides along walls and pools blue-white in the lee of roofs. Voices carry a few paces and thin out. Footsteps are a soft tack. The market is always on, because damp climbs into anything left down and nothing stays usable if it sits. Bring little that must stay dry and nothing that must stay still. Goods move. People move because the lanes are the only dry-enough paths and the lanes change which lip they use from hour to hour. Fish shift with the switch; netters shift; domes die on the abandoned side and kindle on the new. You will be in someone’s way. Be small, be slow, and do not stop in a lane to look. If you need to look, find a roof-step or a wall that is not a water-course and become part of the stitching, the way the birds do — small, dark, standing rather than flying, shifting all at once when a shadow crosses, then locking back into stillness. They do not call. Neither should you, if you can help it. Confidence is the local volume, not need. An argument at the volume of a city market will die in the cold air and still mark you as unfinished.

**What to carry.** Stone, wood, cord, bone, hide, fired clay the color of wet mountain. Almost no metal worth the name. A bright tool, a buckle that flashes, a pot that rings, will be louder than the ice-burst. Leave the ringing things. Carry cord you can afford to lose, a wooden cup, something to sleep under that does not mind smoke and damp, and food that does not require a fire you cannot site. Smoke here behaves; it will not pillar away from you. Firewood was hauled up in living memory and before that. Do not assume you may burn what you find. Eat what the market eats if you are offered a share and can see it cooked: fish, whatever the nets take, nothing that had to be green to count as food. There is lichen, black and rust, plated into the stone, and a few wires of sedge in cracks that stay wet without becoming soil. That is not a garden. Do not forage the stains.

**Shelter and the night.** Roofs step down toward the floor; each eave drips onto the next, conducting water like a tax from the highest shack to the central tanks. You will not find an unused corner that is not already a spill, a roost, or a tank’s weather. If someone lids you into a dry-enough lee, take it and do not rearrange their stones. Masonry is the technology, and it is very good; your improvement will be a leak. By late day the town doubles in the water-tables. You will see more people than there are, which is already too many for the ground. Trust the stone under your boots, not the second town skinned across the pools. When someone breaks the ice on a tank with a wooden maul, the sound is small and may be answered by nothing you can separate from the general soft work. That is normal. Sleep if you can while the black thread keeps moving. The weeping does not stop for the cold.

**How to be there.** The plan of the place is finished. Erosion and occupation shook hands a long time ago: every course has a lip worn to the angle that keeps the house beside it dry enough, every lane has a fall that will not pond, every roof stone has been turned until its bed fits the drip it was given. You could map it and the map would still be true next year. What will not hold still is the occupancy. Prepare to be corrected by water, not by a guide. Watch which lip is running before you commit your weight. Watch where the netters have shifted. Do not chase a channel that has just been abandoned; the life has already left it. Do not wade the upper walls after a thud and a white breath; the meal of ice is already in the weep, and the path around it is the one the carts took.

You will want to speak, to name the domes, to photograph the doubled lanes, to kneel at a pan because the coherence is beautiful and brief. Most of that is harmless if it stays inside your skull. The bowl does not reward narration. It rewards a body that can be wet, quiet, slow, and willing to be rearranged inside a shape it did not choose and cannot improve. Come able to breathe thin air, see in a glare that has no mercy, grip polished wet stone, eat fish and mineral cold, sleep in smoke that lies down, and leave the coin-that-spends-once unspent. The rest of the visit is attention. From the rim you can hold the whole of it in a turned head. Once you are in it, you cannot. That is the preparation that matters: accept, before the first lane, that you will be one more soft movement in a press that is always, and almost silently, becoming the only arrangement the mountain still allows.
