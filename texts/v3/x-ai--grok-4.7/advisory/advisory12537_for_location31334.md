An ordinary human can survive this place. Ordinary human habits cannot. Prepare as if you are entering a machine that is also a tide, a market, and a livestock yard, and that has forgotten the difference between those things for longer than your species has had shoes.

The shelf does not have an edge you can walk to and call “away.” It has weather it makes itself, water under almost every step, a roar that does not take a night off, and no plant life to soften any of that. Physics mostly behaves. Emptiness does not. Pack for abrasion, flex, vapor, and other bodies — not for wilderness, and not for a city that ever empties.

### What the air will do to you
It is warm, thick, and already wet before you sweat into it. Condensation beads on anything cooler than the air, which includes your skin, your lenses, your drinking bottle, and the inside of a bag you opened ten minutes ago. Mineral taste. Sometimes a faint sweetness from whatever biological slurry is being vented or piped. Rain is not an event so much as a recycling of the city’s own vapor. Post-rain is the climate.

Bring clothing that can be soaked for days without chilling you or rotting against your skin. Light layers, not armor. A dry change is a luxury you may not keep dry; seal one set of base layers in a hard case and treat it as medical, not comfort. Avoid cotton against the body. Assume every zipper will collect grit and every soft seam will be colonized by damp. A wide, stiff brim helps with sheets of runoff from low overhangs more than with sun. The light is honest and the horizon does not rise; glare comes off water and wet glass, not off a distant sky. A matte cloth for wiping eyes, and a second one you do not mind sacrificing, matter more than sunglasses you cannot keep clear.

Hydrate on a schedule, not on thirst. Humid heat lies. Salts matter. So does not drinking basin water because it looks brown-clear and near. The basins are infrastructure and pasture. Films, droppings, slurry, oxide, and whatever the climbers strip off the walls all have a path into that water. Carry a way to boil or otherwise treat what you take, and carry enough treated water that a jammed corridor cannot panic you into scooping. Your sweat will not evaporate usefully. Plan to be clammy and still functional.

Skin will stay damp in folds: feet, groin, under straps, behind ears. Friction plus mineral wetness plus bacterial film is how ordinary humans get ruined here before anything dramatic happens. Spare socks in ridiculous numbers. A simple drying powder. Antiseptic that still works when diluted by sweat. Assume small cuts from grated edges and from being brushed by hard-shelled climbers. Cover them. The city repairs itself with migrating mineralizing films; you do not. Do not let an interesting wall “kiss” an open scrape.

Ears are the organ this place spends first. There is no quiet register to recover in. Hours of mill-voice under the plates, slap, drip in shafts, rasping herds, flocks exploding into screams when a pressure wave passes, horns, shouted commerce, lattices ticking as they borrow mass from one span to thicken another. Bring hearing protection you can wear all day and still hear a warning horn through — not total silence, which will get you stepped on, but a cut in the volume. Spare protection. A headband or hood that keeps water out of the ear without sealing you off from directional sound. If you have any history of ear infection, treat that as a packing problem, not a minor note. Sleep, if you find it, will be loud. Accept that before you arrive, or you will spend the first night bargaining with a roar that does not bargain.

Lungs: the sweetness is processed life, not a garden. A light particulate mask for vent alleys and slurry hours is sensible. Do not expect mountain air on an upper deck. Upper only means less drowned, not clean.

### Feet, hands, and the ground that is not ground
You will not walk on earth. Deck, grate, sinter over shallow water, bolted walkway on what used to be a roof, scooped steps, handrails polished bright and narrow by centuries of grip. Floors creep when load migrates. A basin navigable at one hour can take a steep pulse from three districts away because the corridors are too full to let a change pass gently. Watch the flex. Trust a step you have watched. Do not trust a step because it held the person in front of you; their mass and the lattice’s latest decision are not yours.

Footwear: soft enough to feel creep, sticky enough for wet ceramic and weeping metal, closed against grate-bite, and drainable. Barefoot is a story people tell after they leave, if they leave uninfected. Aggressive tread that clogs with film is worse than a simpler sole you can scrape. Practice walking while looking at the surface two paces ahead and at the crowd pressure beside you. The unusual death here is not a monster. It is a misread flex, a wave under a plate, a handrail that is only a worn ridge, a moment of believing a dry plane will stay dry for the length of a market.

Hands need a tether habit. Anything set down and turned away from will be investigated by a body — kept animal, tolerated animal, or person — before you turn back. Clip what you cannot afford to lose. A small hard case beats a soft satchel that wicks basin water and can be shouldered off you in a compressed stream of crowd. Gloves you can strip when you need touch, because the railings are information: heat, slime, polish, vibration.

A short staff or the habit of a hand on the rail is useful in surge, and a liability in a jam. Collapsible, blunt, never a weapon you would be sorry to have taken as a threat display. This is a packed place. Looking armed is a way to become a problem the crowd routes around, or through.

Do not camp on a “free” ledge. There are no unoccupied ledges. If a plane is empty, ask what pulse, vent cycle, or load shift is about to fill it. Markets occupy whatever is dry enough for an hour and then are gone. Sleep where someone local sleeps, by agreement, off the travel streams, above the slap if you can, and never alone with your food unsealed. Hanging pens of filament and piped slurry are normal. You are a soft animal among infrastructural animals. Do not compete with the climbers for a wall they are stripping, and do not reach into a basin that is being grazed as if it were a view.

### Noise, speech, and being legible
Conversation is a craft. Most of what passes is gesture, short calls pitched to cut the roar, or writing held up on boards. Your ordinary voice will not make a private sentence at arm’s length. Shouting everything will exhaust you and still fail when a flock erupts.

Learn, before you need them, a handful of large, readable signals: stop, water coming, I am passing, I do not understand, danger at foot level, I am not a threat, thank you. Practice them until they are uglier and bigger than feels polite. Carry stiff boards or erasable panels in a size that can be read at a shout’s distance, and a marker that works on wet. A phrasebook is useless if it cannot be shown. Pointing at your ear and then at the noise is a universally tired gesture; use it early so people do not think you are rude, only deafened, which you will be.

Names and prices will be lost. Agree with eyes and boards. Do not follow a waved hand into a lower wet level without knowing the vent cycle. Lower is hotter and louder, and the way back up can jam when the breath of a block changes. If you cannot hear a horn, you are already too deep into your own head. The horns are short and brutal because a moving mass is about to cross a shared level. Yield first. Understand later.

Privacy of speech does not exist. Do not say, even “quietly,” anything you would not have held up on a board. The shelf has excellent acoustics for the wrong secrets and none for the right ones.

### Navigation on a shelf that is the landscape
There is no outside to step into to regain the map. The far edge is only a change in the grain of the same thing: more towers, more basins, more roofs almost level with standing water. Horizon does not rise so much as thicken. Landmark culture from other worlds — peak, river bend, tree line — will starve here. Color is mineral: dull copper, bone ceramic, black glass veins gone soft at the edges, everything shining as if just rained on because it is always just raining off itself.

Navigate by water behavior, sound, and repetition, not by skyline. Which basins are open craft-threads and which are only a glug under the plates. Which overhangs throw flocks when pressure shifts. Which spans tick and creep. Which corridors carry the mill-voice in your teeth. Ask for routes described as sequences of wet and dry, not as streets. There may be no gaps large enough to deserve the word.

Carry a way to mark your own trail that the city’s repair films will not eat in an hour and that climbers will not strip as oxide. Do not trust chalk on weeping walls. A personal log — time, water level relative to a grate, number of turns, the smell shifting from mineral to sweet — beats a pretty map drawn in a drier universe. If your tools include a compass, the shelf may or may not care; the continuity is the hazard, not north. If you have a way to measure simple rise, use it. “Up” can still mean less drowned. It does not mean out.

Time your crossings. A traveling wave from a redistribution three districts away arrives late and steep. Locals will have the slip in the schedule in their bodies. You will not, for days. When in doubt, wait on a bolted high walkway and watch one full pulse before you commit a heavy bag to a “dry” sill. Cargo larger than a person belongs on the water, and the water is argumentative. If you hire a small craft, hire the pilot’s judgment about wakes returning off walls, not a romantic idea of a canal. Wakes smack and come back. You can be soaked and spun by ordinary traffic.

Crowds are weather. They compress, jam, reverse, pour down, boil up. Do not plant yourself in a stream to consult a device. Step into an eddy — a wider grate, a cul-de-sac the lattice has not yet reclaimed — and do your thinking there. If the eddy is full of penned animals, it is not yours.

### Food, animals, and the absence of green
Nothing roots. You cannot forage a salad, brew a leaf tea, or read season by blossom. Calories here are animal, slurry, and whatever is traded hand to hand because there is no room for large vehicles except on the water. Eat what is cooked in front of you or what you carried sealed. The faint sweetness in the air is not an invitation to find the kitchen and help yourself. Piped slurry feeds kept animals. It is not traveler ration.

The herds on the walls, the grazers on the glass veins, the flocks in acoustic shadows, are as infrastructural as the grates. Some are kept. Some are tolerated because they clear silt, carry useful films, or alarm at seal breaches. Do not scatter them for a picture. A sheet of birds means a pressure wave; that is a signal, not a spectacle to chase. A climber on your pack is investigating, not attacking, until you flail and teach it that you are a problem. Brush, do not swat. Do not pocket a “sample.” The city is packed to its skin; removing a worker from a wall is how you become the breach the useful animals are there to notice.

If you must carry food, hard sealed, odor-contained, clipped. Open it in an eddy, finish or reseal, and assume interest. Waste goes where waste is clearly going — watch what locals do — not into a basin because it “looks like a river.” Those basins are shallow, warm, and already employed.

### Tools worth their weight, and tools that will betray you
Worth carrying: hearing protection; drainable shoes and spare soles or scrapers; sealed water and a treatment method; salt; a few stiff writing boards and wet-proof markers; a tether and a hard case; a cloth for lenses and another for blood and film; a small light even though daylight is honest — lower levels and the dark between plates are not the same as noon on a roof, and you may need to read flex at your feet when bodies block the sky; a way to keep a log dry; basic wound care; a line you can clip to a rail if a pulse hits while you are mid-crossing, short enough not to tangle a crowd.

Leave behind or treat as dead weight: anything that requires quiet to operate, anything that needs a dry soft interior and cannot be sacrificed, elaborate camping kit for “a patch of ground,” plant-based assumptions, weapons meant to clear space (space will not stay cleared), and the hope of a panoramic rest. Electronics that hate condensation need a harder shell than their makers intended. Heat plus wet plus mineral dust kills lenses, seals, and batteries in that order. Paper maps become pulp. Leather becomes food for damp.

The black glass still rings if struck. Do not strike it to hear. Most of it is never struck. It weeps, it carries load, and it is older than your curiosity. The same restraint applies to prying at a film-seal “to see how it works.” Up close none of it looks miraculous. It looks worn. It is still the thing holding the floor.

### How to arrive, and how to stay ordinary
Come in the middle of a local cycle if you can, not at the romantic hour. Watch for one pulse, one crowd reversal, one vent change, before you buy, sleep, or descend. Pay attention to hand-height rails and scooped steps; they are the city’s instructions, written in polish. Match the traffic’s yield to horns. Keep your bag on your front in a compress. Announce yourself with boards when your voice fails. Thank with a gesture large enough to read.

Do not seek a summit, a gate, or a green court. There isn’t one waiting. The balance is real — the shelf is not collapsing, not new, not a permanent crisis — but balance is summed motion. Levels flood and drain on schedules that slip. Structures flex. Markets dry and drown. You prepare by becoming someone who can be damp, half-deaf, legible at a distance, and suspicious of any surface that looks finished.

An ordinary human does well here by being slightly boring: clipped kit, treated water, protected ears, watched feet, no samples, no private shouting, no faith in emptiness. The city is already in its long-established state, becoming itself again by the next hour, loudly. You are the temporary soft part. Pack so that the next hour can happen without needing you to be quiet, dry, or alone.
