The room will not meet you halfway. It is full, new, and already working. Prepare as someone who can be hurt by water, stone, and a hole the width of your own shoulders—not as someone the place has been waiting for.

See it before you enter it. In daylight the shaft is a hard white column and the water is clear enough to carry that light to the floor. Lie at the rim, face in the warm skin that spills over, and look. From there you can read the chisel marks, the pots, the bend of light over the drain, the leaves tugging and springing back. Most of what the room has to show is visible without putting your body in it. Treat full entry as a second decision, made only after that look, and only if you still need your hands.

Measure the hole against yourself before you trust it. The shaft is no wider than a person’s shoulders, and the whole chamber sits only about a person and a little more below the yard. Bring a rod and sound the interior: floor to soffit, shaft length, width at the rim and at the ceiling cut. If you cannot pass with your arms at your sides, you do not fit, however curious you are. A tank on your back will not fit. Fins will not fit, and would wreck the pots if they did. Anything that catches—loose straps, a trailing line, unbound hair—can catch on an arris that is still sharp enough to hold it.

There is no air in the chamber and none in the shaft. The water stands to the rim and spills. A tube to the yard will not let you breathe once your chest is under; the weight of even this shallow water is more than your ribs can pull against. At the rim you can breathe with your face in the light. Inside, you are holding your breath or breathing from air you brought in a form that fits the shaft: a small cylinder held in front, or a hose from a supply left in the yard feeding a proper demand mouthpiece. Not an open pipe. Plan the visit in breaths, not in hours. The room is small; a minute of calm looking is a long time in it.

Have someone in the yard who is not coming in, and a line that runs from you to them, dressed so it cannot float into the drain or saw across the lip. Agree the signals before you wet your face: one tug, steady; two, coming up; a continuous pull, haul now, even if the line goes slack after. If you go alone, do not go in. The room is too loud for a shout to mean anything, and the only exit is the hole you came through.

The water is about the warmth of a wrist held under a tap—kind at first, not kind forever. You will not chill. Stay long enough, or wear a heavy suit, and you will overheat. A thin layer is enough: something against fresh-cut stone and against the mortar. Lime that has not yet taken the color of the water can still be harshly alkaline. Do not drink. Do not rub your eyes. Rinse your face and hands with water you brought once you are out. A mask stays on the whole time you are under. If the spill in the yard smells of rotten eggs, metal, or anything that stings the nose, do not put your face in, however clear the shaft looks.

Weight yourself so you can hover, not so you sink onto the pots or pin yourself to the soffit. In a box this small, buoyancy is how you steer. Too light and you are pressed to the ceiling, hunting the shaft with your back. Too heavy and your knees find the floor, the drain’s pull, the clay pots in their cut seats. No fins. Soft shoes: the stone is gritty with its own dressing, not yet slick, and the arrises still catch light because they can still cut. Tie your hair. Take off jewelry. Do not count on the wooden wedge at the spout as a handhold; it is what keeps the trough honest, and it is swollen tight for a reason.

Go in feet first, arms down, when the rod has told you the interior will take your height or your crouch. Day only. A small light is worth having for the joints the column does not favor, but you should not need it to find the way back: the shaft is the only bright thing, and up is the spill. Under you, three sounds stack and do not end—the shatter at the lip, the lower throat of the drain, the thin tear where the spout meets the standing water. You will not talk over them. Your ears may ring afterward. That is the room doing its work, not weather, and it will not pause because you are inside it.

While you are in, the rules are the ones the place already follows. Do not block the drain. The pull is steady, not a mouth that will swallow you, but it will take a hand, a strap, or hair if you offer them, and if you stop the hole the room has only the shaft left as a way out for the water. Do not knock the wedge. Do not scrape mortar for a sample; it is still itself, still caustic, still sharp at the edges. The leaves are few and already bending the way the water bends—let them. Your business, if you must touch anything, is the stone under your palm: gritty, true in the joint, warm because the water is warm. Then leave.

Coming out is the part to rehearse in your head before you start. Arms at your sides until your shoulders meet the cut, then one arm up into the shaft only if the rod said there is room. The person in the yard takes the line and your raised wrist, and you spill out with the overflow onto new paving that is already wet and loud. Sit there until your breathing is ordinary and your eyes have been rinsed. The chamber does not need you in it to remain exactly full. It was built to be full, and it is managing that without visitors.
