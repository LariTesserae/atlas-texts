Pack for a place that will not meet you halfway. The valley does not hurry, and it will not hurry for you. Most of what an ordinary traveler usually prepares for—storms, predators, crowds, cold at height, a river that can rise—is simply not on offer. What is on offer is saturation, stillness, young stone, and a quiet so complete it starts to feel like a substance. Prepare for that, or the first day will feel like a malfunction.

**Body, before you go.** The land is high and warm at once. Your lungs will notice the height even if your skin does not. Do the slow acclimatization you would do for any upland: arrive staged if you can, sleep lower for a night or two, walk before you climb, and do not treat the gentle air as proof that the air is thick. It is thick with water, not with oxygen. Headache, dull nausea, and a strange inability to catch a satisfying breath can show up in the first hours and then pretend to be mood. Give yourself the margin. At the same time, this is not a cold camp. You will sweat. You will not dry. Cotton is a mistake; it will plaster and stay. Take light, loose layers that still work when wet—linen or a thin synthetic that does not cling—and one warmer layer for the rooms, because stone holds a cool the air does not, and night, if night ever quite arrives as a change, will not be a relief so much as the same climate with the light turned down. A brim matters more than a coat. The daylight is even and pale and does not announce itself as harsh, and you can burn under it while feeling only damp.

Bring more water than the sight of water tempts you to skip, and a way to make still water trustworthy. The channels and tanks are full, level, and clear, and none of that is the same as safe. Nothing moves in them. No fish, no insects, no current to turn a seep over. The wet has been sitting at one height for longer than your habits can measure. Boil, filter, or treat, and do it as ritual, not as emergency. A leaf set on the surface will not drift away and prove the system clean. Taste will tell you mineral and warm and very little else. The town drinks it; you do not yet know how the town’s bodies have made peace with what yours has not. Carry a cup with a mark on it so you notice whether you are drinking. In air this wet, thirst lies. You can be drying out from the inside while your sleeves never dry.

Salt, and food that does not depend on a hunt. There are no animals. That is not a season and not a shy hour. Nothing walks, nothing calls, nothing flickers in the tanks. You will not supplement your stores with anything that moves, and the plants are not a pantry you understand. Lush here means saturated stems, separated clumps, runners on wet stone, small flowers that do not ask to be found. Some of that green may be food to the people of the terraces. You do not know which, and the ecology has no browser, no bird, no insect to have tested it in a way you can read. Eat what you brought. Pack dense, quiet food—things that do not need a draft to cook. Fire is possible and awkward. The air is stable from basin to rim. There is no slope-breeze to feed a flame or to carry smoke aside. A small stove with its own air, used carefully, is kinder than an open fire that sits and smokes straight up into a haze that will not move it. If you cook, cook little. The smell of your food will be the loudest announcement you make all day.

**Feet, hands, skin.** The rock is sharp in a way old mountains are not. Edges are crisp, jointing fresh, grit angular. This is not a place that has worn itself into kindness. Sturdy soles with real tread, and gaiters or high cuffs, because the damp films every face and the shallow soil is a mash that slicks without looking slick. You will slip on a sheen, not on mud. Gloves are worth it: the stone will cut a steadying hand, and the cuts will stay wet, which means they will not close on your schedule. A small wound kit, and the habit of drying a cut even when nothing else will dry—alcohol, a clean wrap, a moment of attention. Fungus lives in the shady cuts of the rock and will be interested in your skin if you give it a warm fold and a day of damp. Change what you can against your body when you stop. Powder, a second shirt, spare socks in a dry bag even if “dry” is a relative word. Between your toes and under a strap are where this climate will try to become an infection.

Eyes and ears need their own packing. A cloth for lenses and for your own face: moisture beads and stays, a lens that never quite falls. The light is honest and shadow-soft, and after a few hours the sameness exhausts the eye the way glare does, only quieter. Earplugs seem absurd until the quiet stops being peaceful and starts being pressure—the faint tick of condensation below the rate you can quite hear, the idea of channels that do not flow. Some travelers sleep better with a little sound they brought, a small thing that does not need the wind. Others find any added sound obscene here and should pack nothing that beeps, buzzes, or ticks. Know which you are before you need to know it at dusk, if dusk comes as a change at all. Watches will bother you. The day holds one register. Shadows do not seem employed in lengthening. Eat and rest by decision, not by the sky.

**What to carry through the town.** The settlement is a town by intention, not by crowding. Real distance between buildings, courtyards with a single tree or a single tank, streets of packed grit that respect the ridges. You can be in a city and still be alone for a long walk. Do not arrive as weather. A heavy pack, a bright shell, a voice used at the volume of a road, will be the strangest thing on the terrace. Take a smaller bag for moving between roofs: a notebook, the cup, the filter, a cloth, something to sit on. Stone steps and sedge mats are not furniture arranged for a guest in a hurry. Metal is scarce—hinges, bowls, a few copper sheets gone dull green. Do not count on buying what you forgot, or on trading for tools. A simple repair kit for your own kit is worth more than money you may not be able to spend. If you bring gifts, bring small, dry, and quiet: salt, a needle, a packet of seed you are willing to never see again, a page of paper. Not a bell. Not a flute, unless you are prepared for it to be the only music in a day’s radius and to learn, quickly, that the place does not reward it.

Learn, before you need them, a few gestures that do not require sound. The people, if you meet them, fit the rule the water fits. A figure in a doorway will not hurry your eye. Work, if you see it, may be a hand smoothing a stone already smooth, a mat shifted on a roof that does not leak, a cup lifted and later returned. They may not be in the habit of voices. Sound dies against damp stone and does not return, so speech is expensive here in a way it is not at home. Stand where you can be seen. Do not fill the quiet with explanation. Accept that you may be looked at the way a tool set down beside an unfinished course of wall is looked at: present, not urgent, possibly meant to stay. If you are offered water, you may accept the gesture and still treat the water. If you are not offered anything, do not read it as refusal of a kind you know. The town may be maintained and unfinished in the same glance, and your arrival does not obligate it to finish a sentence.

**How to move.** The scale is a valley you can see across and still not be done with. From one rim: terraces, roofs, the silver geometry of still channels, then the same ridges repeating until haze closes them. It is one region, one climate, one quiet. That sameness is the navigation hazard. Sharp lines look like landmarks and then repeat. A channel looks like a path and is a basin of unmoving water; a leaf will not show you downstream because there is no downstream you can use. The gradient is so slight that water occupies the channels rather than travels them. Do not follow water expecting it to lead out. Mark your own route in a way the damp will not eat by evening—notes, a counted sequence of turns, a photograph if your device can keep its lens clear, a stack of small stones you are willing to unstack so you do not invent cairns the town must live with. The upper rim is the barest and the least kind to hands: fresh faces, each weeping a little, mineral just beginning to crust. Under the town’s level the cliffs stop offering paths. The settlement sits where water can stand. If you came to descend, you may not. Plan the walk you can reverse before the light, such as it is, stops being a guide.

Go slower than your legs want. The footing films. The air does not cool you when you push. There is no animal trail to trust and no birds to warn you that a terrace ends. Plants manage by runners and by seed on a wind that is not really there; you will walk among separated clumps, bare young stone, a skin of mineral damp, moss in the joints. Do not grab a stem to steady yourself. You do not know what it stores, and the interval of green is the whole living system—you are the appetite that moves, the first one, and you should not act like a catastrophe with boots. Stay on grit and on stone. If a courtyard holds one tree, leave it its distance.

**What the stillness will do to you.** This is the preparation people skip, and it is the one that matters most. Nothing is in the act of becoming. Pools sit at a line that might as well be carved. A frond will not unfurl while you watch. Tools do not rust at a speed you can see. The weirdness is only ordinary facts that rarely coincide: high and warm, wet and still, young and already built upon, alive and without animals, urban and uncrowded, quiet in a way that is full rather than empty. After a few hours the mind starts hunting for the missing half of every sense. You will invent wind. You will think you hear the channels. You do not. You will wait for a drop on a fern-tip to fall, and when it finally does it rejoins a pool that does not rise, and the sound is too small to keep, and something in you that expected a consequence will feel cheated. That feeling can turn into irritation, then into a low dread, then into a conviction that you have gone wrong—that the path was missed, that the town is abandoned, that time has stopped in a way that means you cannot leave.

It has not stopped. It has agreed with itself. Eat on a schedule. Speak aloud once or twice a day even if only to yourself, so your voice does not become a surprise. Sleep even if the light does not tuck you in. Write down one thing that changed, even if the change is only that you moved. If you cannot find one, write that. The equilibrium is not a pause between weathers. It is the weather, and the geology’s current age, and the town’s match for both. You are the change. That is allowed. It is also loud. Do not punish the valley for not narrating your visit back to you.

If the quiet tips from strange into unbearable—if your ears ring with pressure, if you start walking faster only to hear your own steps, if the absence of animals starts to feel like a verdict—go back to a room, a mat, a tank you have already seen, and stay until the urge to make something happen passes. There is no emergency in the stillness. The emergency would be yours: a cut you did not dry, a climb down a cliff that was never a path, a fire you asked to burn in air that will not feed it, a night walk among sharp joints because you could not stand another hour of the day holding still.

**What to leave behind.** Anything that needs weather to work. Kites, sails, a plan that depends on a breeze to clear smoke or dry a wash. Anything that needs animals. Hopes of fishing, of birdsong as a clock, of dogs, of pests you can swat and thereby feel the food web is normal. Anything that needs a slope in the water. Boats are a joke; a leaf is a sufficient demonstration. Heavy cold-weather kit, beyond the one layer. Bright caravans of gear that assume a trailhead and a way out by evening. The ambition to see the place change while you watch. It will not perform becoming for you. A drop may fall. The pool will not rise. That is the whole spectacle, and it is enough if you let it be small.

Come able to be warm and wet and a little breathless, to drink carefully, to cut yourself on beauty that has not been rounded, to eat only what you carried, to speak rarely, to be the only animal on the terrace and to act as if that were a responsibility rather than a freedom. The day will hold. The water will hold. The sharp land will hold its edges. You are the one who has to decide, on purpose, to arrive, to stay a while at the speed of a seep, and then—because you are not from equilibrium—to leave while you still remember that leaving is something a body can do.
