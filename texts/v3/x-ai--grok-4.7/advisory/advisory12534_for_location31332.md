Go as if you are walking into a room that has not finished being built, and that will keep revising the floor while you are on it. The place is warm, dry, dark, and large, with almost no sound and no living thing to read. Preparation is less about wilderness craft than about heat, footing, light, water you must carry entirely, and the habit of not trusting any surface for long.

**What the place will do to a body**

The air is warm and moves in slow bodies rather than wind. It will not cool you. Sweat will not help much, because there is nothing wet in the air to suggest a climate that knows evaporation as relief; you will dry out from the inside while the heat sits on you. The dryness is absolute in the way the description gives it: not a desert that remembers rain, a place that has not been given water. Lips, eyes, sinuses, and the skin of the hands will complain early. You will not find a seep, a shade-tree, or a night that resets the temperature. If there is a ceiling, it is close and black, and sometimes it lowers until a ridge nearly meets it. Treat “open country” as a lie the dark tells. You can walk a long time between events, and those events are geological, not weather you can wait out.

There is no plant or animal life, and nothing has died there. That means no food, no fiber, no scavenged water, no warning calls, no tracks that mean anything, and no ecological season to time your visit against. The only schedule is the land’s: cooling, fracturing, welding, a terrace giving somewhere out in the dark, a slab you are standing on deciding whether it means to remain a floor. The veins of heat under the crust do not light the way. They only prove the dark is busy.

Sound will not save you. The loudest thing will be your own steps, and they will sound wrong — crisp on glass, muffled on mealy mineral. A collapse arrives late and small. Do not expect to hear the next revision coming.

**How long to stay, and how to leave**

An ordinary traveler should plan a short visit with a hard turnaround, not an exploration. Hours, not days, unless you have support you can actually trust to extract you — and even then, the ground you marked on the way in may not be the ground on the way out. Carry your exit with you: a direction, a timer, a physical line if the scale allows it, and the discipline to leave while you still feel fine. Heat and dry air make “fine” a poor signal. Thirst lags. Judgment thins before the legs do.

Do not cache supplies on the ground and expect to find them. A pale fan of fresh mineral, a trough that split and welded wrong, a low wall of uplift that was not there when you started — any of those can bury, shift, or simply cease to be the landmark you used. If you must leave something, leave it on your person.

**Water, salt, and food**

Bring all the water. There is nowhere wet for warmth to go, and nowhere wet for you to drink. Double what you would carry for a hot, still day of the same length, and treat the surplus as non-negotiable rather than comfort. Electrolytes matter more than flavor. Dry heat and continuous low-grade exertion will pull salt even when you do not feel yourself sweating in the familiar way. Food should be dense, already hydrated if you can stand the weight, and not dependent on cooking. There is nothing to burn that is alive, and fresh mineral faces are the wrong kind of heat.

A closed container for urine is worth the indignity if the visit is long enough that you would otherwise pour water onto ground that will not give it back, and that may not even remain the ground. Do not count on the body to recycle cleverly. Count on carrying mass.

**Light**

The dark is the operating condition, not a nighttime you wait through. The ceiling may be rock that never quite arrived. Veins brighten and dim too faintly to navigate by. Bring light that does not depend on a single point of failure: a strong beam for the next few paces, a lower flood so you can see the character of the ground (glass, grit, mealy, still plastic with heat), and a small marker light you can see without turning your head. Redundant cells, and a way to change them without setting anything down on a skin of crust.

Light will not show you the whole region. It will show you whether the slab under you is a floor or a suggestion. Use it that way. Sweep low and often. A ridge with the profile of a spill frozen mid-pour can slump along a seam; the new face may steam and then cool into different stone. You want to see edges, seams, and places where the ground is only a skin.

Do not rely on the sky. There may be no sky you can separate from the high dark. A warmer shelf of air can make the black seem to lift; that is not dawn.

**Footing and the habit of weight**

The ground is new. Sheets of glass, still faintly plastic where the last heat left them, overlap plates of grit that have not learned to be sand. Edges are raw. Erosion has barely begun, which means nothing has been rounded into a path. Boots should take heat, abrasion, and a surface that changes from slick glass to deep meal in one step. Thick soles, gaiters against grit, and a willingness to test before you commit weight. Probe. A dull press and a soft internal click may be the only notice that something under the skin is still sorting itself.

Spread your load. A heavy pack on a thin crust is a bad argument. If you carry a staff or pole, use it as a question you ask the ground, not as a walking stick you lean your life on — it may punch through.

Avoid the warmest seams and the fresh faces. A trough that is still warmer than the rest, a ridge steaming after a slump, glass that yields like cooling candy: these are the land still deciding. The firmest ground is where the few alignments stand, three slabs on edge, bases already being swallowed by the next upwelling. Firm is relative. Even there, the next spill can overgrow what looked permanent.

Rings of darker clasts, too even to be falls, with the interior kept clear of the newest spill, hold heat differently. The air against the shins inside one may be a degree warmer and very still. They are not shelter. They are a mineral habit, or a shape the land practiced and then lost interest in. Do not camp in them. Do not treat them as rooms. If you enter one, do it as a brief measurement, and step out before the stillness convinces you the place has paused for you.

**Heat, skin, and lungs**

Clothing should be light, covering, and not clever in a technical way that assumes wind or evaporative cooling. You want a layer between skin and air that is warm-moving, and between skin and mineral that may be hotter than it looks. Gloves. The glass is young; raw edges cut, and a fresh face can still be hot enough to brand. A brim or hood helps with the ceiling of dark and with grit, not with sun — there is no sun to bargain with.

A particulate mask is reasonable. New mineral, mealy and uncompacted, will rise when you step. You do not know the chemistry of every band that shows dull rose when a face splits. You do not need to know it to avoid breathing a cloud of it. If a shelf of warmer air passes and the gap between ceiling and ridge ticks with small static, expect a faint mineral smell and dry air that may irritate. Cover the nose and mouth. Rinse eyes with water you brought; do not rub glass-dust in with a glove.

Heat illness here will not announce itself with a dramatic climate. It will be a slow dulling: headache, impatience, a decision to “just see the next uplift,” a belief that the quiet means safety. Set a turnaround on a timer you cannot argue with. Drink on the timer, not on thirst.

**Navigation when landmarks are events**

There is no lower country in view. Scale is regional and open, and the dark cheats the eye. A standing stone reads as architecture because the emptiness is large, not because anyone built a city. The alignments — slabs set on edge, rings placed rather than fallen — are the only marks that look deliberate. They are also being swallowed. A slab half-overgrown by the next upwelling is a poor benchmark. It may be more buried, or gone as a profile, when you look back.

Carry a direction that does not depend on sight of a horizon. A compass may or may not mean anything in a place that is still sorting its minerals and ticking with static; do not bet the walk on it alone. A traveled line — physical cord on a short loop, if the ground will allow you to recover it, or a recorded pace-count and bearing you check constantly — matters more than a map of features. Features here have the lifespan of spills.

Mark time, not scenery. “I have been out for this long, I have this much water, the floor under me has revised once” is a better log than “the pale fan was on my left.” The pale fan may be a different stone by the time you return.

If the ceiling lowers until the tallest new ridge nearly meets it, that gap is not a pass to squeeze through. Static, close rock, and a land still uplifting are a reason to retreat to where the dark has more room, not a route.

**What to do when the floor changes**

You will hear, or almost hear, the revision: a far slab detaching with a noise like cloth, a pressure change that pops the ears, the quiet crawl of cooling crust, then a terrace giving, the report late and already finished. When the ground under you shifts, do not run. Running is weight arriving in the wrong rhythm on a skin. Stop, lower your center, widen your stance on what still holds, and wait one breath to see whether this is a slump moving away or a seam opening under the boots. Then move off the newest face, diagonally, toward older-feeling plates — firmer, less steaming, less mealy — even if that is not your planned bearing. Re-find the exit after you have a floor.

Do not investigate a fresh split for curiosity. The interior may show a dull rose for a moment before the dark takes it. That is the place practicing chemistry, not a view worth a closer step. Bands can continue to move after the rock has seemed solid, heat passed along a vein. Standing over a vein to watch it is how an ordinary traveler becomes a sample.

Ears popping is a pressure change, not a summons. Note it, check the ceiling and the nearest ridge, and do not assume a cave-in you can outrun. The events are sparse. Between them the quiet is thick. The danger is that sparsity feels like permission.

**Company, tools, and what not to bring**

Go with another person if you can, close enough to see each other’s lights, far enough that one crust-failure does not take both. Agree on the turnaround before you enter the dark. Talking will feel loud and useless; do it anyway, on a schedule, because the place offers no other voices and your own steps already sound wrong.

Tools worth their weight: light, water, a probe, gloves, a mask, a timer, a way to record bearing and time, a small shelter from radiant mineral heat if you must stop (a reflective sheet to sit on, not a tent you stake into a living crust), and a means of calling whatever brought you — if such a means exists in the way you travel. Tools not worth their weight: digging gear, climbing hardware for a summit that does not announce itself, anything that assumes timber or ice or soil, weapons against animals that are not there, books of regional botany. This is not a mountain and not a desert and not a cave in the usual sense. It is a floor that has not finished deciding, under a ceiling that might be rock.

Leave the alignments alone. They are not tools, not buildings, and not yours to sample. Moving a slab, or clearing a ring that the ground has kept clear, is an argument with a process that is already revising itself without your help. An ordinary traveler’s job is to cross and return, not to finish the land’s sentence.

**The manner that keeps you ordinary**

Walk as if each pace is a question. Keep the beam low. Drink before you are thirsty. Speak the time aloud. Do not romanticize the quiet; it is only warm air carrying little, and the land between collapses. Do not read the rings and the three slabs as a welcome. No hand is there. Whatever arranged them has gone, or was never an animal, or was the ground trying a shape and losing interest. You are not expected. You are not forbidden in any voice you can hear. You are simply heavy, wet, and temporary on a mineral ecology that has not tried life and does not need you to begin.

The deepest preparation is the decision, made before you step onto the dark floor, that you will leave while the slab you are standing on is still willing to be a floor. The place will not announce the summit, because there is no summit. It will not announce the end of the visit either. That part is yours.
