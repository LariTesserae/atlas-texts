Prepare as if the plateau will not notice you, and as if every system you rely on will be slightly wrong for the entire stay. The shelf is not hostile. It is finished. Its weather, its water, its animals, and its mills have already occupied every volume of air and every joint of stone. You are the only unfinished thing that will arrive.

The height is the first correction. The plateau does not look high, and the light is ordinary daylight through a high wet haze, but the air is thin enough that a walk up a stacked stair, or a sudden fright, or even steady talking in the lower noise, will leave you short. Spend your last days at home walking with a light pack and pausing whenever your breath shortens, so the sensation is familiar rather than alarming. Do not expect dramatic mountain cold. Expect a warm that does not fall at night, held in wet stone and in the mass of the works. The failure of the air is oxygen and clarity, not temperature.

Dress for damp that never dries, not for rain that comes and goes. The stone sweats between showers. Water stands in every low joint. Cloth left in open air mildews; the drying sheds exist because the air itself will not finish the job. Bring fewer garments than you think you need, all of them quick to wring and slow to rot: close-woven plant fiber or treated wool that can be turned and aired, not cotton that stays cold against the skin once wet. A light oilskin or waxed layer for the races and the lower streets, where water runs ankle-deep along one edge of the paving and the dog-sized animals move in bands through it. Footwear with a sole that grips wet mineral film; smooth leather will skate on the glazed races and on the polished courses where generations of shoulders have worn the walls concave. Carry a spare pair in a waxed wrap. Change them when you come in, every time. Sleeping dry is the difference between a tolerable week and a fever.

The film on the stone is not dirt and not quite food, though people scrape it and animals eat it. It is a brown-gold mineral skin, darker where water pools longest, lifting in flakes when levels drop. Do not assume it is harmless to an outside body. It smells of warm metal and dust. Wash your hands before you eat, and do not let standing pool-water into a cut. The sour fermented paste made from the darker film is the local staple; it smells like the streets because the streets smell like it. Taste a small amount early, with something you already trust in your stomach, and wait. Many visitors cannot keep it down the first day. Your own food will be a problem: there is no green thing. No salad, no fruit, no grain that was not already ground in a mill and stored against damp. Meat, eggs in impossible quantity, rendered fat, mineral salts, and that paste. If your gut requires plants, bring dried stores sealed against moisture and accept that you will be eating them cold or barely warmed, because open flame and stored fuel are managed carefully around the old timber and the stacked housing. A multivitamin is not romance; it is the difference between clarity and a slow fog after ten days without leaves.

The animals are not a hazard in the way of predators. They are a density. Cornice birds the color of wet stone, moths and flies in the light, beetles in the warm gaps behind pipe, low dark bands in the flooded gutters, heavy grazers on the flats between districts that bed against the outer walls at night. They do not startle usefully; there are too many of them. Do not wave arms or shout to clear a path. Step where they are not, and wait when a flock’s long soft roar fills the air above the roofs—it rises, it does not peak, and it thins. It will be louder than the mills for a while. Speak into that roar only if you must, and then close to an ear. People here already do. Ear plugs will make you miss the water ticks and the belt-stitching that tell you whether a race is running true; better to let the sound become weather. After two days you will stop separating the mill-throb from the pour in the channels. That is adaptation, not defeat.

Water law is the only law that will matter to you as a stranger. You do not cut, dam, divert, or even prop a loose board in a way that changes a centimeter of grade. Districts keep water courts for arguments that last months over a thousand meters of causeway. A street with a finger of standing water along its lower edge is in working order. A street someone has swept dry is a failure waiting to reappear inside a wall. If you are housed, ask where the spill marks are and do not store your pack below them. If you walk the causeways, do not relieve yourself into a race or a cistern lip; the great rectangular cuts are older than the districts that drink from them, and the tea-colored pools are warm and alive. Carry a bottle you can fill from a tapped channel someone points out, not from the film-dark pools the grazers drink.

Navigation is by stack, by sound, and by the age of the masonry, not by a clean map. Older districts have worn their corners off; newer ones, newer only by a century, still show saw-cuts in sheltered joints and smell more sharply of hot oil under the wet. Visibility is short. Flocks, haze, and the stacking of walkways over walkways turn distance into a change in grain. A small lamp for the lower levels is worth more than a compass; day reaches them, but softly, and the afternoons thicken. Do not expect to see the edge of the city. There isn’t one that matters. The wet is the shelf.

What the visit is like, once the first shortness of breath has settled: a warm, metallic, feathered constancy. You will be slightly damp at the collar by noon and slightly deaf by evening, and neither will feel like an emergency. The light stays white-yellow behind the haze. Iron rails warm enough to be uncomfortable. Somewhere a board knocks in a cistern. A herd shifts against a wall and the masonry, already concave, takes the weight without comment. People call under awnings in a brisk market voice that does not dominate the water or the wings. If you stay long enough to stop looking for green, you will start to read the film the way they do—glossy or merely damp, thick enough to scrape or still returning—and you will understand that production here is not urgency. It is climate. The mills do not stop for you. The flocks do not finish. Prepare to be a small dry intention moving through a place that has already decided, for centuries, how wet it intends to remain.
