I would not be on the causeway. I know that before I know what I am. The causeway is for arriving, and arrival is a mistake the shelf corrects by wearing it off you. Half a day's walk of thinner light, stacks appearing and thickening, the film going from damp to glossy — that is a story people tell about themselves for a week, and then the story thins the way the flocks thin, and they are only another body taking up a frequency that was already occupied.

So not that.

For a while I think I am one of the cornice birds. The image holds because it is easy: a dull gray body, the size of a hand, packed against warm masonry, living on meal-dust and the insects that live on the film. I can feel the nest under me, the grit of it, eggshell ground into older eggshell. I can feel the flock when it passes low, not as company but as weather, a soft roar that enters the body through the breastbone and leaves it when the sheet has gone on to the next district. It is a good life. It is also not mine. A bird startles, even here, even if less than it would in a dry country. Something in me does not startle. Something in me has already decided that startling is a waste of heat.

The grazers, then. Heavy shoulders, tea-colored water, the concave worn into the outer course of a wall. I stand in that image long enough to feel the rub of stone on a shoulder that has done this before I was born and will do it after. Dung, flies, the long unemphatic cropping of a skin that is not grass. It fits the patience. It does not fit the attention. Those animals know the wall and the pool and the turn of the herd when a rail line has to be cleared. They do not know the centimeter. I know the centimeter. I have always known it. That is the first thing that does not dissolve.

There is a room that is not a room so much as a joint in the watershed where people have put a table. The maps on the walls are stains that have been drawn over until the drawing and the seep are the same argument. I am not one of the people at the table. I have listened to them for longer than any of them has held the seat. They argue for months about a grade across a thousand meters, and they are right to. I do not argue. I am what the grade is checked against when the arguing is done — not the mark in the cistern, exactly, though I know that mark the way a tongue knows a tooth. The mark was set by hands. I was already in the cut when the hands came.

Closer.

I live in the glaze.

Not as a metaphor. The races are lined with a mineral skin so old it looks poured, and under the running water there is a thickness that is not quite stone and not quite the brown-gold film the animals crop. It builds in the same way the film builds, by damp and heat and time, but it does not flake. It holds the shape of the flow. Where the race bends, I am thicker. Where a board has knocked for years against the lip, I have grown around the knock until the knock is part of the bed. The wheels turn in me. I do not turn them. I do not mind them. Their throb is my throb the way the seep's ticking is my ticking, and after a century you stop separating the two, which is what the people in the districts also do, and they are not wrong.

In the mornings, when the haze is thin enough to throw a soft shadow, I am the cool under the warm. A hand put to the wall comes away wet and thinks the wall has sweated. Some of that is the wall. Some of it is me, worked up through the joint the way I have always worked up, a finger's width in a year, less where the grade is true. I am not everywhere. The shelf is broad and I am a particular persistence: this race, the cistern it feeds, the spillway cut in the living rock at the cistern's lower end, older than the boilers, older than the stacks that breathe their warm exhaust into air that is already wet. When the bank builds on the western edge and arrives after dark, heavy and warm, someone comes and opens me. I say someone. It is a procedure, not a person. The person changes. The opening is the same: a gate lifted, a hand's breadth, then another, until the lower streets take what they must and no more.

I do not decide the breadth. I remember it. There is a difference, and the difference is why the courts last months and I last longer. The courts are for when memory and the new stack disagree. A district retools. Iron goes in where limestone facing has powdered away. A belt runs hotter. Someone has cut a foundation without knowing whose seep they have stolen, or knowing and hoping the theft is smaller than a centimeter. The theft is never smaller than a centimeter. I feel it as a thinning. The glaze dulls on one face. A pool that should stand tea-colored to a certain lip stands short, and the black flakes lift too early, and the low dark animals nose a film that is not ready, and they move on, and the moving-on is the loudest quiet on that street.

Then the courts. Then the months. Then a correction so small a traveler would not see it, and I thicken again on the face that had gone dull, and the animals come back, and no one announces it. Announcing is for weather you can point at. This is only the shelf being itself.

At night the mills drop to a reduced clatter and I am more audible to myself. Water at the spillway lip. The knock I have grown around. Farther out, on the flats, the deep note of something bedding down, a sound that carries in wet air farther than it should. I do not go to it. I am not a body that goes. If you put a hand in the race in the dark you would feel me as smoothness and a faint warmth that is not the day's warmth — older heat, held the way the plateau holds heat, in mass. You would not feel a mind. That is correct. What I have is not a mind. It is occupancy of a particular volume, the way every volume here is already tenanted, except that my tenancy is the lining rather than the thing passing through.

People scrape the film and eat the paste and call the smell the smell of the streets. They are eating the cycle I sit inside of, not me. I am not food. I am what remains when the food has been lifted and has returned. Children drop stones in the race to hear them click. The stones find the bed and stay, and in a decade they are no longer stones you could pick out with a finger. I am patient with them. I am patient with the iron. I am less patient with a drain that has been swept dry on purpose, as if dry were order. Dry is a failure. The water will find a worse path — inside a wall, under a belt, into the meal. I know the worse paths. I have been down all of them in some thinner form, a damp in a joint, a bead on a pipe, waiting to be graded back.

Would I speak, if the courts asked? They do not ask. They have stains. The stains are enough. Once, in an older district where the corners are worn off and the boundary between works and herd was never firm, a woman set her palm on the race-lip and held it there until her skin pruned, and she said a number, a centimeter, quietly, to no one. She was right. I had no way to tell her and no need. She had been listening to the same knock I have grown around. That is as close as I come to being known, and it is close enough. Being known more than that would make me a spectacle at the margin, and this place does not keep its life at the margin.

When the flock's roar rises over the mills and people stop talking, I do not stop. I have no talking to stop. The roar passes. The throb returns to being the loudest organized thing, and under it the seep, and under the seep the slow addition of myself to myself, a thickness no one will measure because measuring it would require the race to be empty, and the race is not empty, and will not be, not while the shelf sweats and the grade holds.

That is where I am. Not above the roofs. Not in the market, where you must speak close to an ear. In the bed of the race, in the lip of the spillway, in the centimeter the maps are stained to agree with. A glaze that looks poured. A warmth in the water that is not quite the sun. The part of the shelf that remembers the mark after the hands that set it are film and feather and the long unemphatic rain.
