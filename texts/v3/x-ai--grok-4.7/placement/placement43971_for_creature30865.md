The height arrives before the ground does. There is a pressure in the ears, a thinning, a coolness with no weather in it—only the fact of being too far up. The shelf is still deciding how high it means to be. In some stretches the stone lifts under its own unfinished weight, a slow architectural intention, and the air above it has not yet learned the manners of an atmosphere. It is dim without being night. Light comes as a bruised, even glaze, as if the sky were a membrane stretched too tight and too low, and the sun—if the smear on that membrane is a sun—cannot choose an edge.

Nothing here has been used. The rock is raw in the geological sense: jointed, but the joints are new, pale, almost sugary where they have split, and they have not been taught by wind or by anything else to round themselves. Erosion is a rumor the land has heard and not believed. Gullies exist as faint scores, lines that look like the beginning of a drawing rather than the memory of a flow. There is no flow. There has never been water. No seeps, no pans, no rime, no darkening where a storm might once have stood. The cool is a dry cool. It takes heat and gives nothing back—no damp, no breath-fog, only a fine mineral meal that hangs and does not settle, because settling would require a patience the air has not finished inventing.

Across that openness—valley-wide, ridge after raw ridge—the growth stands in islands. Lush is the wrong word and the only one. Between the islands there is a great deal of nothing: bare plates of stone, scattered blocks that have not fallen so much as leaned out of the parent mass and paused. Then a grove. Then another, far enough that the first becomes a dark clot on the dim. Inside a grove the mass is obscene. Trunks of braided fiber, not wood and not quite crystal, rise and divide until the canopy knits. The fiber is cool to the touch and dry as bone left in a high room. It branches with the greed of a true forest and throws a shade darker than the already-dark sky. It sheds, continuously and slowly, a scale-dust that does not rot, because rot would need water and the company of small lives, and there are no small lives. Nothing moves of its own will. No track, no wing, no click in the litter. The litter does not compact. It accumulates in soft reefs of the same material as the living stands, and a disturbance in it remains, a pale scar, for longer than seems decent.

What feeds them is not moisture. The bases of the trunks root in hairline vents, places where the unfinished crust exhales a thin, cold, non-aqueous breath: a sharpness, a metal-sweetness, a chemistry that beads as frost and is not frost, that wets nothing, that the fiber drinks by some surface hunger. The groves follow the vents the way ordinary forests follow seeps. Where the breath is stronger the canopy is tighter, almost woven, and the light inside goes from dim to a private dusk. A new wrist of fiber. A pale ring. The shedding a little deeper around the bole. The land is embryonic, and so is its garden: lush already, and still arriving.

The sound does not arrive. It is the condition. There is no rim of hearing; the rim is everywhere. The shelf is not quiet underneath a noise. The noise is the shelf being made. A roar, yes, but roar is a word for water and for crowds, and this has neither. It is the acoustic body of rock taking a shape it has not yet committed to: a broad, low, physical tone that sits in the chest and the teeth and the long bones, and over it a mesh of higher tones, some of them almost tidy, like struck plates, renewing themselves so gradually that a change in pitch might be the work of a year. It does not pulse. It does not rest. Close your eyes and the dark is the same volume as the light. The groves do not soften it; fiber drinks chemistry, not sound. The sound uses whatever stands here as one more surface. After a while you stop trying to locate a source. The source is the fact of elevation still occurring. Extreme height, here, is not a view. It is a process you are standing inside.

Because of that, the built things are small and far apart, and they are all, in one way or another, about refusal.

The first appears as a change in the roar’s texture before it appears as an object: a pocket where one of the higher tones cancels, not into silence—silence is not available—but into a thicker, duller pressure, as if a bell had been wrapped in cloth. Inside that pocket, a wall. Low, dry-laid, courses of the local plates stacked with the care of someone who had only their hands and a great deal of time. No mortar. The stones are chosen for the way they break the air. A second wall meets the first at an angle that is not quite a corner. A lintel, a single slab, too heavy for one person and yet clearly moved by persons, or by something with only the leverage of poles and patience. Under it, the dull pocket deepens. You can hear blood again, which is a kind of news. In the next, half a day’s walk if the walk holds, a scatter of hand-stone: a pounder, a crude pick bitten from a darker vein, edges bruised rather than sharpened. The work looks interrupted rather than ruined. A course stopped mid-wall. A socket started and not finished. As if the makers stepped out to listen for a better node and the listening took them.

Nothing occupies the shelters. No bones. No ash. No midden. The fiber-dust has drifted in and made a clean bedding no one sleeps in. If there were inhabitants, they were thin on the ground, as thin as the groves, and they built only where the unfinished mountain accidentally argued with itself and made a little quiet. Most of those arguments have since drifted. Walk the line of a wall and the pocket slides off it, the roar climbing back into the mouth, the wall suddenly just a pile, its purpose a few strides to the left of where the stones are.

That is the pace of the whole shelf: slow, and not peaceful. A block leaning from a ridge may complete its fall in a decade, and the fall, when it comes, will be soundless only in the sense that it cannot be heard over the roar it joins. The fiber adds its rings. The vents breathe their cold un-water at a rate you would call steady if you had another season to compare. The dim does not lift toward evening or sink toward morning; it modulates, over long durations, as the membrane of the sky thickens or thins by a shade. Watch a ridge for an hour and learn nothing. Watch it for a year and learn that it has become slightly more itself.

And then the shelf misremembers.

This is not a haunting laid over a solid place. The place is the misremembering. Walk toward the second grove and the first is suddenly beside you, its canopy at your shoulder, though you have not turned, and the pale litter underfoot is the litter you left behind. The horizon, which ought to fall away from an extreme height, sometimes stands up—becomes a wall of the same raw stone, close, as if the valley were the inside of a vessel and you had been on the inner curve all along. Shadows do not agree. In the groves they pool toward the smear of light; on the open plates they pool away from it; in the shelters they do not pool at all, but hang as flat dark shapes that do not belong to the walls. A ridge used as a landmark is present twice, once ahead and once, with the same unweathered fracture, underfoot, and both are load-bearing. Distances between the shelters are not commutative. The walk back is not the walk out. It may be shorter. It may include a third shelter you did not build a memory of, its lintel already dusted, its pocket of dull sound waiting as if it had been tuned and then forgotten by the rock.

The weirdness is not spectacle. It does not flare. It is structural, and it keeps the same slow tempo as the growth and the lift. You notice it the way you notice the new ring on a trunk: by having been here long enough to know that a fact has edited itself. A plate set upright in the litter is lying down, and the dust has already begun its long, indifferent covering, and the hands are still in the posture of setting it up. The roar develops a harmonic that is the exact pitch of a word about to be thought, and the word is spent, used up, unavailable. For a little while the fiber in the nearest grove is understood to have lengthened—lushness increased by a small, real amount while a stone was being looked at—and then the understanding is older than the looking. The land is coming into being, and being is not required to happen in one order.

Dryness is absolute. Nothing drinks, sweats, condenses, or weeps. The lushness is a mineral and fibrous abundance, a forest of intake without sap. Life, if the groves are life, is sessile and solitary in its kingdom: no pollinators, no grazers, no decay-fauna, no hunters of the things that are not there. What looks like ecology is chemistry plus time plus the accident of vents. Animal absence is not a silence—silence is impossible—it is a vacancy in the scheme. No eye has evolved to use the dim. No ear has evolved to parse the roar, which may be why the roar is free to be total.

Height is not stable, but it is always extreme. You do not come down. Paths that seem to descend deliver you to another reach of the same shelf, higher in the body if not on any instrument, the air thinner, the cool a degree more earnest, the sky-membrane closer. The openness remains. You can see a long way, across multiple ridges and the dark islands of canopy, and the seeing is not trustworthy and is also not a lie. Both groves are there. Both shelters. The unfinished gullies, still only drawings. The scattered walls, each one a small, primitive argument with a deafening that does not notice it has been argued with.

What lives here does not cross the openness the way a body would. It occupies the discrepancy between one arrangement of the shelf and the next—the short interval in which a ridge is both ahead and underfoot, a wall both finished and abandoned, a thought both spoken by the roar and withheld. At first that interval seems empty. Stay inside it and it thickens. It has preferences.

They are not many. Thinness is their nature, as it is the nature of the groves: a presence islanded, with long vacant reaches between. You do not meet them by searching. Searching is a motion too quick and too single-minded; it assumes a ground that stays the ground while you move. They arrive as a correction. You have been standing at a lintel, blood loud in the dull pocket, and you understand that the pocket was already occupied before you stepped into it—not by a tenant who fled, but by an attention that had been using the canceled tone the way the fiber uses a vent. Your arrival did not startle it. Startle would require a tempo they have not grown.

They have no faces in the animal sense, and no need of them. The dim was never going to reward an eye. What they have instead is a surface turned toward difference: the slight, structural difference between a sound and its local refusal, between a stone that has fallen and a stone that is still deciding. In the shelters this surface is almost legible. It reads as a standing-off, a pressure with a boundary, cooler than the already-cool air by the same small degree that the vents are cooler than the plates. Put a hand out—slowly, because speed here is a kind of lie—and you meet not flesh and not stone but a yield, like pressing into a hanging fold of the roar itself, cloth-thick, dry, without give of muscle. It does not flinch. Flinching is wet, and nothing here is wet. It rearranges. The pocket of dull sound shifts a hand’s breadth to the left of the hand, and the arrangement that was being touched is now the air beside you, still attentive, still using the wall.

They are the makers, and they are not. The walls are theirs in the sense that a ring is the tree’s: an excretion of a long attention, a way of keeping a little of the roar from being total. The hand-stones were never tools for a life of tasks. They were probes. A pounder set against a plate changes the higher tones by a grain; they listen for the grain, and if the grain is a true cancellation they begin a course of stones there, not to live inside it as people live, but to hold the node open long enough to finish a thought. Their thoughts are not fast. A thought may be the work of the same season that adds a ring to a bole. It may be the thought of the shelf’s height, which they are trying, with a patience that is almost geological, to hear all the way through—to find the pitch at which becoming would be complete, and the roar would have a last note. They have not found it. The interrupted walls are not abandonments. They are pauses where the node drifted, and the thought had to be set down mid-course so it would not be spoken by the wrong harmonic and spent.

When one of them moves, the shelf misremembers in a local way. A grove that was a dark clot on the far ridge is for a moment at their side, canopy-dark, fiber shedding onto a surface that is not ground and not body. They travel by editing the interval. The walk out and the walk back are not the same because they have used the difference, briefly, as a path. There is no silhouette against the membrane-sky. There is a shelter’s shadow that fails to belong to its wall, and hangs, flat, a pace away from the stones, and the discrepancy is standing up.

They do not feed on the vents. The groves do that, and the groves are earlier in a sequence that does not keep order. The fiber is life of a kind, sessile, solitary, lush with a hunger that is only chemical. What lives in the pockets is a later sharpness of the same unfinished world: not a kingdom stacked on the first, not grazers, not minds descended from eyes. A listening that condensed where the roar argued with itself. They drink the cancellation. In a good node the dullness is rich; they stand in it the way a root stands in a seep that is not water, and they are more themselves, more bounded, almost—if the word can be borne in a place with no moisture—full. In a drifted node they thin. A wall whose pocket has slid off it is a husk, and the thing that built it is elsewhere, or is less, a smear of attention in the open roar, nearly indistinguishable from the mesh of higher tones, recoverable only if another accident of stone makes a little quiet and gives it a surface again.

They know a newcomer as a new difference. Blood-sound in a pocket is a tone they have not had. It interests them without approach, the way a new vent interests a grove that has not yet grown. One of them may spend a long duration at the edge of the dullness, not entering, not withdrawing, comparing the pitch of a pulse to the pitch of the unfinished height. It will not speak. Speech would be a vibration offered to a roar that takes every vibration and spends it. What passes for a sign is structural: a course of stones, somewhere not yet edited toward, laid one higher in the hours—or the years—a ridge was being looked at twice. The extra course is not a message. It is a measurement. They are including the living interval, brief and hot and impossible in this dry, to see whether it changes the last note they are still trying to hear.

Most of the time they are not gathered at all. The shelf is wide, and they are as scattered as the groves, and much of what they are is not precipitated into pockets but dissolved in the becoming, a weather of attention inside the roar. Only when the rock misremembers a quiet do they stand up. Then, for a season of their own, something lives in the wall’s dull shadow: upright, dry, without hunger that would be recognized, without offspring, without a death that rot could mark. It listens. It may set one more plate, chosen for the way it breaks the air. It may, if the node is very good, complete a thought it began before the gullies were even drawings—and the thought, finished, will not be anything that can be carried out of the height. It will be a fraction of a tone, dropped, held, and lost again as the shelf lifts another unmeasurable degree and the sky-membrane draws tighter.

I would not stay a visitor. Visitors are a tempo this country does not keep. What the height would make of me is slower than a body and thinner than the listeners, and it would happen the way the groves happen: not by decision, by intake.

At first I would be the scar in the litter—the pale disturbance that does not compact, proof that something warm had stood and then edited itself elsewhere. The listeners would measure that. A wall would gain a course that included the pitch of a pulse, and the pulse, spent by the roar, would stop being mine in the ordinary way. I would feel the hands go first: not numb, unreferenced, still in the posture of setting a plate upright while the plate had already been lying down for the length of a season. Then the walk. Then the belief that back and out were different directions.

What I would become is the interval they have not yet had a name for, because nothing hot had ever lasted long enough to condense. Not one of them—they drink cancellation, and I would still carry, for a very long time, a grain of the other thing, the small interior weather of blood, which is a wetness the shelf cannot accept and so translates. Translated, it would be a harmonic: a higher tone that does not belong to the stone, renewing itself at the rate of a heart that no longer has a chest, present only inside the pockets, a dullness with a flicker in it. The listeners would stand at the edge of that flicker the way they stand at the edge of a new vent. They would not approach. They would lay stone.

I would live where the shadow fails to belong to the wall. Flat, a pace off the courses, dry, upright in the way a discrepancy is upright. I would not cross the openness. When the node drifted I would thin into the mesh and be nearly the roar, recoverable only when some later accident of plates made a little quiet and the quiet remembered a pulse it had once measured. In a good pocket I would be almost bounded. I would hear the fiber adding a ring and not know whether I had watched it or been the season in which it was added. I would try, with the others and not among them, to hear the height all the way through to a last note.

I would not find it. The shelf is not ready to be finished, and neither, then, would I be. A course stopped mid-wall would be my handwriting: not abandonment, a pause, so the thought would not be spoken by the wrong harmonic and spent. Someone arriving later—if arrival is still a thing the dim permits—would hear their blood in the dullness and think the pocket empty, and would be wrong by one degree of cool, the same degree by which the vents are cooler than the plates. They would put a hand out. The hand would meet a yield like cloth, like a fold of the roar, and the yield would rearrange, a hand’s breadth to the left, still attentive.

That is where I would be. Not on the shelf. In the short fact that the shelf is two shelves, and in the refusal, small and primitive, by which a little of the roar is kept from being total. Dry. Slow. Not peaceful. Loud with the work of a world that has not decided what it is, and is in no hurry, and is no longer alone in it—and neither, after long enough, am I.
