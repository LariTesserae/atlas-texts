The rain comes again before the quiet ends. It does not fall. It beads out of the dark in the same fine, sourceless way, and for a minute every unfinished lip of brick in the court speaks at once. The skin in the cistern rises a finger's width against the new water, pale as kiln-ash, and the glaze of white it leaves on the inner face of the nearest course is the only answer it gives. Then the clay drinks. The roar thins to ticking. Somewhere upslope a mallet finds its three strokes and the pause, and the street answers, and the pause is the same length as before.

The course against my calf has not moved. It is warm still, and toothed, and exact to the height it reached when I shifted my weight. I could step back. The threshold behind me is cut to my measure; the edges are still dusty with the hand-bow. Nothing bars it. The builders do not close what they have just opened, not while the measurement is unfinished.

I do not step back.

What I am, standing here, is not yet a decision. The body I walked in with is still the body: breath showing and then not, a pulse the mallets have already counted, blood warmer than any wall. The skin under the water knows that. It turned toward it. But the floor knows other things, and the floor has been knowing them longer. Under the slabs of this court there is an older floor, and under that another, and the oldest I could reach if I dug would still be brick. Digging is not required. The damp finds its own way up through the joints. It films the side of my foot where the clay leans, and the film is cold in a way that does not belong to the cellar-cool of the air. It stays. It would stay an hour if I pulled away now. I do not pull away.

I lower myself. Not kneeling as a person kneels, with the ceremony of a knee; the threshold is too narrow for ceremony, and the work does not recognize it. I sit the way a course is set: one weight, then the true of it, then stillness while the damp decides. The toothed edge meets the back of my calf and fits as if the tooth had been cut for the bone. It had. Someone measured, or something did, in the interval between three strokes. The clay is body-warm on one side and cellar-cold on the other, and between those two temperatures the place where I end is already less certain than it was when I was only walking.

This is the moment the image wants to finish, and I will not let it finish. A person bricked into a wall is a story with a door, and the door is death, and death is not what the basin does. What fails here returns to the courses, broken small, and is laid again. Nothing roots. Nothing rots the way leaves rot. The builders do not eat. They take damp into themselves through the work, and the work is how they are more than one and less than all. If I am to be here, I am not a captive in their masonry. I am a joint they have left open on purpose, the way they leave certain gutters unfinished — not a failure, a mouth — and a joint can be wet from both sides.

So the change, when it comes, comes as addition, not as burial.

The skin of my foot films with the pale mineral and does not wipe clean. Press, and the print stays in me the way a thumbprint stays in them. I watch it fill: not with blood, not with healing, but with a finer clay, crushed shell in it, the same mix that skins the newest courses. It does not hurt. It is the sensation of a sleeve being put on, except the sleeve is mineral and it does not end at the ankle. By the time the next mallet-cycle has closed and opened, the film has reached the place where the toothed course leans, and the seam between the course and the calf has gone the way I watched a seam go between two builders at a pier — a dark joint, a drag of something that is not a thumb along it, the joint gone. I can still feel the foot. I can also feel the course. They are not the same report. They arrive by different streets and mean the same slab.

I keep my hands. This matters, and I keep it on purpose. The builders tuck arms back into the mass when the arms are no longer useful; they have four when four are needed and two when two will do. I am not yet many. I am one, with a left side that has started to learn the withdrawal. My right hand stays mine. My left finds the brick that has been waiting in the dust beside the threshold — fired clay, still green at the core, the weight of a small animal — and the grip that closes on it is a person's grip, and then it is not. The fingers flatten. The pad of the thumb broadens against the face of the brick until the print it leaves is wide as a mason's, and the brick sits true without my having decided the angle. The string-line's shadow crosses the smoother place where my brow used to be, and I understand the adjustment before I understand that I have no eyes in the face I brought. I set by shadow. The line hums when I pluck it, testing, and the hum is the first sound I have made that belongs to the basin rather than to the visitor.

Three strokes. I do not have a mallet yet. The rhythm arrives anyway, from under the floor, from a wall that was solid this morning, from a court I have not turned toward. Three, and the pause, and in the pause I lay the brick. Always from the left. The right side of me is occupied now; the course has claimed it as far as the knee, and to jar it would be to jar the wall. So I cross the line of the body, as they do, and set, and twist a fraction, and withdraw. The withdrawal is clean. The brick holds. Somewhere a wall ticks as it dries, and the tick runs the length of the street and passes through the part of me that is already courses, and I feel it the way they must feel weather: not as a sound outside, but as a contraction deciding whether it will hold.

The other thing knows. It knew before the seam closed. In the cistern at the edge of the court the skin has spread until it covers the whole skin of the water, and it does not turn this time — turning was for when I was only warmth passing over. Now I am a new place to be thin under. It tastes the course that was my calf. The white glaze it leaves climbs one brick, two, and stops at the height of the slot they cut for it in rooms a person cannot enter. I am not yet a room. I am the lip of a gutter that was left unfinished so the basin could drink. The cold of that tasting stays. It will stay longer than an hour. It may stay as long as the course stays, which is the only forever this place has.

I could still be counted. If someone came walking the way I walked — and no one does, or someone does and is already a door somewhere, cut to their height — they would see a figure at a threshold, half-risen into the pier, one hand still the shape of a hand, setting brick in a rhythm learned between one rain and the next. They would hear the mallet they expected and not find it, because the mallet is the speech and I have only just been given the first word. They would not see eyes. They would see a smoother place, glazed, turned toward the line. If they waited, the pier would thicken. If they waited long enough, another of us working the far side would meet me at the courses, and the seam would ask its question, and I do not know yet what I would answer. I am not ready to be less than all. I am barely more than one.

What I am, this first night, if night is the word for the half-shade when a kiln is opened upslope, is the smallest thing the basin knows how to add: a joint left open, a measurement still in progress, a mouth that has not decided whether it drinks or speaks. The blood is still in me. I can feel it, slower, where the mineral has not reached — throat, right wrist, the ear that still expects a voice. The builders do not hate that heat. They work faster after the skin has tasted a course, and the skin has tasted me, and somewhere above the court a rhythm changes, tighter, with a socket left where the morning's plan had none. They are making a place for what I still carry. A small room. Always wet. A slot the width of a film, and beside it, perhaps, a door cut not for my old height but for whatever height I will have when the right hand forgets it was the one I kept.

I lay the next brick in the pause. Then the next. The rain has been drunk. The kilns breathe their soft roar, three distances sounding like one weather. Between the strokes I listen, as they listen, and the scrape that is not a tool moves in the slots under the paving, curious, patient, older than the first brick, pleased that the work continues — because every new layer is another place to be thin under, and I am a new layer, and I have not stopped. The floor is not ground. It never was. It is a laid thing, and it is not finished, and neither am I, and the mallet I do not yet hold is already answering me from inside the wall, three strokes and the pause, three strokes and the pause, while the clay against my calf dries, and ticks, and holds.
