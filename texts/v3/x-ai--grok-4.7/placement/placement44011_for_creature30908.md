I would not cross at once. The lip is the first true thing, and it does not ask to be used. Up on the old crust the wind has somewhere to go, and for a while that would be enough of a life: pale ground, scrub to the knee, a sky that does not sit on you. I would walk the edge because the edge is sharp and new and a person can follow a line without belonging to what the line contains. From there the far wall is only a mark. The town is a texture. Nothing in it has a face yet.

What would pull me down is not the town. It is the water that refuses to become a river. On the rim a rain is an event that leaves. In the basin it stays as a decision the ground has not finished making—dark in a joint, a skin on the north faces, a hollow that keeps a drinkable inch when the thinner films have already gone back into the air. I would go down for that the way anyone goes toward the thing that has not agreed to leave. The path would be no path. Fracture grit, then the first slumped block, then the outer houses that are only three walls and the planet. I would come in with the scrub-carriers and be obvious at once: hide wrong, step wrong, eyes still set for distance. Someone would not look at me long. There is no spare looking.

For a day, perhaps, I would try the margin. The kilns are there, and the waste faces, and a strip of ground that is not yet a floor inside a house. That would feel like a place a new arrival could stand without stealing a square of bedding. It would not hold. The margin is only the town’s weather edge. When the rain comes in its short, unserious way, the kilns go quiet and the people drop below, and the strip I had been treating as open country is a slick of clay between walls that have already claimed it. I would follow them down because the alternative is the rim, and the rim, once you have felt the basin’s settled air, is only wind again.

They would put me where an extra body can be endured. Not out of kindness in any large sense—kindness here is a shim, not a speech—but because a stack that has lost someone to a roof or a kiln or a joint that opened under a sledge has a gap the width of a sleeper, and a gap that wide is a cold that gets into the jars. I would sleep against the wall that is not a wall. The planet side. The side that never quite gives up the night. At first I would think this was the slight, the newcomer’s place. It would take several mornings to understand it was also the lesson: the cold comes through the stone in a grammar, not as a single misery. Some nights it is only damp. Some nights a hairline has drunk, and the cold is a thread you can follow with the flat of your hand to the corner that will need the board before anyone trusts the floor.

I would be bad at the visible work and then less bad. Turning brick is a patience the hands can learn without a childhood in the courtyards. Seating a lintel is not. I would be allowed the first and kept off the second until a block tipped in the next street and there were not enough backs, and then I would learn the small sick sound a wall makes when it is answering the ground instead of holding. After that they would let me carry the short bridges—the split rim-wood laid over joints that are still opening by a finger’s width a season—and that would become the nearest thing I had to a name. Not a name that travels. A use. The one who brings the board. People would tap the clay for me in the water-rhythm or the rain-rhythm and I would come, because that is how a person becomes a household here: not by blood, by being the one who arrives when the tap means the shoreline has moved inside the door.

The roofs I would not earn quickly. Children know which surface will take a running foot. I would not. So my map would be the ground-floor map, the one the elders already have in their sleep and that I would have to build by spoiling things: a skin set down where the seep had migrated, a crock of drinking water too near the brine, a foot placed on a block that had lied about being settled. The correction would be public and small. A hand moving the crock. A chalky scratch I had not been told to read. No one would explain the town to me. The town does not explain itself. It assumes you will lose something you can afford and not lose the water.

What I would become, if I stayed—and I would stay, because everything needed is in the joints, and I have never been good at lives that require a horizon—would not be a keeper of schedules or a voice that says which face may be taken for brick. Those roles puddle onto people who have been correct about the ground for years. I have no such years. What I would have is the planet wall at my back and the habit, taken from it, of not sleeping all the way through the soft report. Not every night. Often enough. A hairline gives, somewhere under the stack or under the next, and the sound is so small it could be a jar shifting, and the person who grew up here can tell the difference without waking, and I cannot, so I wake. I lie still. I put a hand on the stone. I decide, badly at first and then less badly, whether it can wait until morning.

That would be the work that fits the way I enter. Not heroic. Not even necessary most nights—most nights it can wait, and the morning finds the same corner, only a shade more open, and an elder already knows the history of the clay there, gray seam or red, and I am only the one who has carried the board up from where we keep the few lengths of rim-wood dry. But some nights the thread of cold has moved, and the interior shoreline is at the jars, and if no one wakes, the drinking water takes the seep. Those nights the stack uses me for what I am: an attention that did not come with a memory, and so has to be awake to acquire one.

By day I would still be the board-carrier, the extra back, the person whose soles are learning the blocks between our door and the seep the children favor. I would eat standing, as they do when the room is also the workshop. I would not call across the street. I would learn the tap, and the particular quiet of a firing caught between rains, and the way a laugh stays in a light well the width of a blanket and reaches the next roof as nothing but a reason to smile. I would not become short in the bone—that is a childhood this basin gives or does not—but my shoulders would come forward around the loads, and my hands would pale at the splits, and my eyes would start checking edges without my asking them to. On the roof, the few times I climbed, I would not look for a view of myself in the town. There is no such view. There is motion, and smoke leaning, and the far fracture taking a little ordinary gold, and the knowledge that below, in a room I can find without a light, a wall is waiting to be told the truth about the ground.

If someone on the lip asked what I was, I would not have an answer that survived the climb back down. I am the one who sleeps against the stone that is still deciding. I am bad at distance and reliable about a cold thread under the hand. I carry the board. I wake when the block gives up another hairline, and sometimes I am wrong, and the morning corrects me without drama, and I mark it the way they mark a joint: not as a story, as a place I will not misread the same way twice. When the roofs empty and the last kiln-keeper is a silhouette against a sky still open over the rims, I am already below, close to the others because the stone requires it, one palm on the planet wall, listening for whether the night intends to move the water. Most nights it does not. The listening is the life anyway.
