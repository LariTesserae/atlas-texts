You would not arrive by the hoist. The hoist is for billets and drums, and the cables complain in a register the thin air only half-carries; anyone watching the upper walk already knows a load is coming by the way the brackets tick. You come the other way, on the rope that finishes the broken stair, hand over hand, the new fibers still sharp enough to print a red map across your palms before the dry cool closes the lines into pale seams. By the time your boots find plate, the map is already becoming callus. That is the first decision the cleft makes for you.

The second is quieter. A child—no, one of the small furred ones, harnessed, tray of seed-fasteners balanced like a second sternum—threads between your ankles and does not look up. You step wrong anyway. The tray tips a finger’s width. Three fasteners kiss the plate and skitter toward the gap where the middle walk does not quite meet the shear. Before you can spend the breath to apologize, a hand you do not know has already cupped them back into the tray. The hand is old in the way all their hands are old: the first finger gone to shine, the pad mapped to something narrower than a lathe. The owner does not meet your eyes. Two fingers touch your wrist, not to count, but to still it. Then the child-animal is gone into a yellow slot, and the hand is gone with it, and you are left holding air that does not want to be held.

They put you on the drum-count because your chest is still learning the ration and the count does not require you to stand straight. The official pourer—the one with cleaner hands—does not speak your name. There is no name yet that would survive the walk. You are the new measure. You learn the volumes by the weight against your sternum when you lift, by the way a seal either sighs or does not. Water is never called water. It is the next, or the last, or the one that must wait until the presses cycle down. You pass the cupped measures and watch mouths consider them the way a press considers a billet: fully, once, without performance. When someone coughs into a shoulder instead of drinking, you make a mark on the slate with a nail that has already begun to map itself to your thumb. The mark is not mercy. It is so the next shift does not pour for a body that has been sent down.

At first the small animals avoid your boots. Your step is still the step of thicker air—too much lift, too much leftover intention. They correct you by absence. Routes that ran across your path simply stop running there. You notice the correction the way you notice a missing tooth: with the tongue, constantly, unable to leave it alone. So you shorten the step. You learn the exact distance at which another chest can rise without stealing. It is not kindness. It is the grammar that keeps the tray level. After six shifts the animals begin to use you as furniture again. One sleeps against your ribs between counts, harness unbuckled and hung on the peg with the slate. Its breath is faster than yours and warmer. You do not remark on it. Remarking would make it a choice.

The larger pens are not your work, but the sound of them reaches the drum-bay late and incomplete, the way all sound does. A hoof, a click of horn, the single clean tick of fresh timber. You do not go down. The tenders wash in a basin that is also on the ledger, and their forearms are the color the rock wants everyone to be. When one of them passes the middle walk with a wrapped portion—dense, dry, nothing left that could be called waste—they do not look at the measures in your hands. You do not look at what they carry. The glance that is not exchanged is the vote that keeps both of you in your bays.

There is a night, or the hour the clack calls night, when the shear opens another finger’s width and new dust films the brackets you have only just stopped seeing as new. You are awake because the animal against your ribs has gone rigid, listening to a frequency the presses do not make. In the bunk-slot above, someone lays a palm to the fresh face of rock—not worship, acknowledgment—and the sound of skin on stone arrives a moment after the motion. You feel the ledge under your own palm answer, faintly, as if the mountain is still deciding whether the housing it has become is acceptable. The animal settles. Your knees are already drawn. The blanket has not cooled. Somewhere a motor is walked, six inches at a time, into the space a family has already agreed to vacate without having woken fully enough to call it agreement.

You stay because the route has accepted the length of your step, and because the shine has started on the thumb that holds the nail, and because when the pourer is off-shift the others arrange themselves around the slate as if the volumes might be shy. No one asks if your blood has finished coughing out the lowlands. The question would be a sentence. Instead they leave the cupped measure a little longer in your hand before they take it back, which is either extra or exact, and you have not yet earned the breath to know which. The mineral film continues its slow argument with every new face. You are still the new measure. The cleft has not finished editing the slot where you sleep, but it has stopped ending the stair in midair above your head, and the rope, when you touch it, no longer prints red. That is as close as the place comes to saying you may keep the hands it is making.
