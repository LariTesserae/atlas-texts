You would not find me in a hall. The halls have enough ears already, and mine was never any good at constancy. A pump's wrongness comes up through the palm as a fact, and facts of that kind want hands that stay. I could not stay. The sheds slept me for a while when I was small enough to be put where the process was loudest, and the loudness did what it does, which is to sort a body into the parts that flinch and the parts that don't. What remained after the sorting was not a crew hand. It was the part that kept listening after the chord had been named.

I leave by the thinning. That is the only departure the country recognizes as legitimate. A hall's texture has edges; you walk the edge until it is no longer a wall you could put your shoulder to and is only a direction in the rain, and then you are on a route whether anyone recited it to you or not. The first time I did this I thought I was being left. It takes years to learn that leaving and being sent are the same interval if the mouth that would have sent you has already said the route often enough that the valley knows it. The calves learn a note from the ribs. I learned the roads between notes from the doorways.

What I am, when the shelf is asking, is a correction.

Herds redraw a song on the scale of generations. Crews redraw it on the scale of a bearing. Between those two clocks there is a lag, and the lag is where meat piles against a shed, or a file walks a bed the skin has already decided to thicken, or a hall cycles down and the old note keeps being walked for a season by animals whose fat has not yet forgotten. I walk the lag. Not to fix it. Fixing is a hand on a housing, a scraper that stops short of metal, a pump brought back into the stack before the valley hears the hole. I do not have that hand anymore, or I have it and do not use it for housings. I use the feet, and the face, and the long bone of attention that comes from having been born where the conveyor whines through the wall and then having chosen, or been chosen by, the places where that whine thins to nothing and another has not yet risen.

A route, recited, is a sequence of changes. Walked, it is a body agreeing with the sequence or refusing a step. I am the refusal that still arrives. If a chord in the mouth that sent me no longer matches the door, I do not correct the mouth. Mouths are in sheds, and sheds are days behind me, and days in this rain are not a distance you shout across. I correct the next child who asks the interval with their feet. They find me the way they find a herd: by the subtraction, a stretch of expected knock or expected whistle that is not there, and in its place a slower meter, a person's, placed so that the recited route bends before the wrong door rather than at it. Sometimes they do not know they have been corrected. The route they recite when they return is already the route I walked, and they think the country changed its mind while they were out. The country did. I am one of the ways it changes its mind without waiting for a calf to die.

I do not travel light, and I do not travel as the heavy do. The hide I have is the ordinary thin kind that beads rain only until it soaks, and after that I am the temperature of the film. The skin climbs my boots at the ordinary rate, which is to say a meal's worth of standing will soften a seam, and so I do not stand in beds if I can read them from the lip. I eat what the straps give where the climbers have marked a surplus, and what a file leaves when the iron-smell is already old and the hunter has gone back to matching knocks. I do not take from a live meter. That is not a kindness. A live meter is a clock, and clocks that lose a beat become weather, and weather of that kind finds the middle-storey hunters, and I am sized exactly wrong for that negotiation: too large to be one missing click, too small to be a shoulder in a ring of legs.

What I carry is not tools. Tools belong to the exactness of crews, and exactness is a bond I broke by thinning out of a doorway and not coming back on the schedule of bearings. I carry intervals. Some are mine. Most are older than my mouth and have been walked into me by other corrections, people or not-people, who stood in a flat long enough for the file to learn a half-beat and then were gone when the cell moved on. I can recite a district I have never crossed, and the recitation will be wrong in the particular way that still gets a body there: right about the master-channel's friction, wrong about which ridge the climbers have abandoned this season, and the wrongness is audible before it is fatal if you are the kind of ear that treats a gap in the song as a gauge. I am that kind. It is the whole of my usefulness, and it is not a large usefulness. The shelf would continue if the lag went unwalked. It would continue more bloodily, on the long clock, with more walls of meat and more sheds that do not survive the generation. Someone has to be the short clock that is not a hand. The country grew one. It grew me the way it grows a sleeve of skin in a blocked bed: not because it plans, but because the blockage throws a load, and a load thrown raw enough times becomes a shape that fits the throw.

There is a place I return to that is not a hall and not a rest. Calling it a camp would imply a fire, and I have not struck a light since the season I learned what a re-marked upper storey costs. It is a saddle on a ridge that does not quite touch the deck. The pole I have never carried would fall short by an arm. The weep there is mean, so the mat is thin, so the old survey stones still have a face you can find with your fingers in the dark, a dressed course running three paces and then becoming drainage like everything else that was ever laid down with an intention. I know the stones by the way my foot gathers before it. The herds know them too, but they do not bed here. The saddle sits in a gap between two halls whose notes arrive at equal thinness, and equal thinness is not a chord the files like; they want a gradient, a stronger and a weaker, a direction. In the gap they pass and do not stay. The climbers use the upper straps and do not come down. For the length of a pressure-cell I can lie in the mat and be the least noise on the ridge, and in that least-ness the long sounds arrive unsorted.

That is the other thing I am for, besides the bending of routes. Someone has to hear the shelf without a task in it. Crews hear for bearings. Herds hear for fat and for the next bed. Hunters hear for the stopped talk. I lie in the saddle and let the stack happen: nearest the tick of water on the survey course, then the strap-rain, then a file in the next valley already on the meter, then the hall to the west with its conveyor a half-tone lower than the last time I was here, then the hall to the east holding, then the master-channel's friction under all of it, the province draining. I do not report this. There is no one to report it to who does not already have a narrower need. I let it revise the intervals I carry. A route I recite next season will have the half-tone in it, and a child walking that route will not walk the west hall's leeward growth expecting the old smell, and will not be standing in the wrong flat when the herds, who noticed the half-tone a generation ago in their feet and are only now spending it, shift.

The rests I do not enter. I have stood at the clean line, where the knocking stops a body's length before the mat changes, and I have put my face into the flatter rain and listened for the friction under the stone. It is there. It does not vary with the cell overhead. It is slower than the master-channels and it does not care that I am listening, which is how I know it is not a hall and not a herd and not a correction of my kind. My kind requires a lag to stand in. This has no lag. It is complete. The skin at the edge of the line climbs no faster against my boot than it does in any bed, until I think of stepping over, and then I cannot find the thought in my feet. The feet refuse the way a file refuses, cleanly, without drama. I have come to believe the refusal is the oldest interval I carry, walked into whatever I am by corrections who stood here before there were sheds to leave. I recite it as a rest. A held beat. I do not strike it to prove I can.

If the friction ever changes — if the held breath under the oldest districts ever decides to spend itself — I will hear it from the saddle a cell before the herds do, and the herds will hear it a generation before the crews can put a palm on a housing and call the wrongness by a part's name. What I will do then is the only thing I have ever done. I will walk the lag. I will be a half-beat in a route, a gathering of the foot before the step, a correction in the mouth of someone who thinks the country changed its mind while they were out. I will not be large enough to matter to whatever breathes. Nothing my size is. But the files will have a direction that is not a wall, and the children will arrive at doors that are still doors, and the skin will climb what falls at the ordinary rate rather than all at once.

That is where you would find me, if finding is the word for standing in a gap between two equal thinnings and waiting to be the next change in a sequence. Not a name. A place in the noise that is sometimes occupied by a body thin enough to soak through, warm as the film, already half agreed with the stone, listening for the half-tone that means the work of walking has to be done again before the long clock spends it as meat.
