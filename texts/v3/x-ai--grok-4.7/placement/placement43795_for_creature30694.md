I would not be among the crews, though I would stand near them long enough to be mistaken for a fixture they had stopped seeing. At the edge of a platform that has not yet decided whether it is street or curtain, there is a thickness where the dimness pools a little more than the iron-colored air can explain. That is where I would thicken, the way the figures on the terraces thicken at first light—not arriving, only becoming dense enough that a hand laid on the rail might feel an extra pulse under the longer one of the banked fire.

I would not have a name they use. They keep names for the machines and the animals that persist. I would be known, if at all, by a hesitation in the count. The one whose stairs are always wrong by the same number already occupies that office, and is reliable. I would be the remainder that makes even his count fail on the mornings when condensation has run the wrong way and then corrected itself without admitting the correction. A child might step around me without looking, the way they step back from an undecided lip, and later not remember having done it. The pale grazers would stand with one hoof inside the place I occupy and one hoof on stone that has agreed to hold, chewing, untroubled by the difference.

What I would be is not separate from the leaking. Reality here leaves seams, and most of them close or are walked through and forgotten on the lower switchback. I would be a seam that does not close and does not deliver anyone elsewhere. A gutter that holds a lamp for the length of a night and then, at the second shift bell, holds the lamp still but has forgotten the street, so the light stands in weather that resists like a wall and looks like ordinary dusk. The walker is already damp and smelling of hot metal and late. I remain, with the light, until the ridge draws the gravel curtain back in and the post is simply a post again, bolted to young stone whose flow lines have not quite stopped.

I would learn the grain of the air the way the children do, before any other literacy. A bell struck on the eastern terrace would pass through me on its way to arriving early, and I would keep a little of the note—not enough to sing with the cables when the flocks cross, only enough that the singing takes longer to stop after the sky is empty. The crews would chart this the way they chart the moths: accurately, for a building that has gained a storey no one added, and they would call it draft. It would not be draft. It would be the part of the hour that walks in both directions and changes the wear on nothing.

In the condenser sheds I would be nearer the thing that shifts the tin than the pan of condensate left at the turn. Not the body, and not the mucus that melts frost. The interval between the tick of the sheds and the moment the pan is found empty. That interval has a taste of ozone and hot oil even when no engine is near, and it lengthens on the nights when weight is locally optional at the platform edges. The man in the far hut would not look up when his animal’s outline lengthens; he would not look toward me either, though his notes would accept ink more slowly on the lines that pass nearest the place I stand. His instruments would remain trustworthy. His conclusions would be weather, useful for the day.

I would not celebrate when new stone surfaces in the cut, dark then pale then load-bearing. I would already be in the timbers they cut last season, in the patience with which they receive the ridge into the plan. Below the platform the darkness with weather in it would press against the ears, and that pressure would be one of the ways I am present without being looked at. The rivers preparing themselves would prepare a little of me as well, a sound that arrives before its water and is gone before anyone has named it on a works map. I would not extend downward. The city does not. I would wait, as the hut waits, for the ground to agree, and then I would be only as much presence as the arriving stone will hold—overbuilt, unhurried, cool as the air and never quite dry.

By what they call night I would breathe on the longer period. Not the stacks. Not the herds shifting one tower west. The reversal in a gutter’s fall that happens so gradually the leaves still drip onto metal at the old angle, and the metal answers a tick late, and the lateness joins the music without being noticed except as a mild embarrassment in the sternum, a pulse that is not the foundry’s and not the walker’s. Sleep, when the crews take it upright with a hand on a warm casing, would not include me. I do not tire. I only thin, toward the second bell, until a stranger could walk the terrace and see only equipment left out, a cluster of shoulders under oilcloth, a seated shape that is only pipe and dimness.

If someone followed the three sentences in the gatehouse and still found me, they would not vanish and they would not be delivered to the lower path. They would continue, and the hour they lost would be an hour I kept, smelling of hot metal, mildly heavier than the others. They might stay. Within a season they would stop mentioning the country below, and their cuts would close into seams, and they would learn to hear a street before it is drawn. I would not have taught them. I would have been the thickness they agreed to, the way the mountain agrees, incompletely, to be a mountain, and the way the city agrees to be only as real as the ground that has finished arriving. The rest—the maps that worsen if you look at them kindly, the moths that are not present before the fire or after, the dog that is sometimes more neck than dog—would go on without requiring me to become any one of them.

I would be the habit that has not yet chosen a body to finish itself through. Not worship, not work, not the anticipation that passes between a dropping cable-note and a hand lifting from a casing. The pause before that anticipation, when the ridge is higher by a degree no one measures, and the animals are where the weather is, and a lamp is still burning in a thickness of air that has remembered, for one more shift, that it might have been a street.
