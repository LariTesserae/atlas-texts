I come down from the rim wrong, and the wrongness does not correct itself by the time the first terrace loses the wind.

Up there the light had a direction. It found the cutting edge of the young stone and made a pale line I could follow with a finger, and the insects said their names without being asked. I had meant only to look, the way the few who walk the high walls look, and come back with a negative report: the mist thins, the plants grow ordinary, the eyes hurt. I did not come back. Or I came back and the town’s manner would not take me, which is the same failure told from the other side of the sill.

They know me by the way I drink. The first time someone set the cool cup in my hands—stone and green, a fallen bead shared thinner than a fingernail—I finished it. Thirst from the ridge. The habit of sources. The person who had offered it did not frown. They only took the empty reed back and did not refill it, and the household drank channel-water that evening as if a tapping had been attempted, though I had not touched the braid. Shame arrived before anyone taught me its shape. I have since learned the sip. My mouth still wants the rest. That wanting is the ridge still living in the throat, a small bright directional thing that does not know it is rude.

I sleep in a room that was not built for me, against a shoulder of block where two houses meet and the joint must be swept. They did not offer it as kindness so much as routing. A file of grazers had been standing all day at a bend I had made without meaning to—my pack set down on their line, then myself, then a long sitting while I tried to remember how argument is supposed to sound. By dusk they had invented a shallower bend around me and rejoined, and the people read this the way they read a cistern emptying after a splash. I was an obstacle not yet understood. The room is the understanding. I am to stay where the line can include me without tearing.

My body does not match theirs and does not match the rock. Shoulders too wide for the deep-set doors, so I turn sideways, and the turn is exactly the wasted motion they have forgotten how to make. Hands too short, pads too smooth; the fiber slips when I try to braid, and the braid remembers every slip as a thick place. Skin that dries if I stand near the lamp too long, then tightens, then hurts in a small selfish way the cup has no use for. I wipe the bead of damp from the hollow of my throat and they notice, not with disgust, with the attention they give a frond taken past the tip. I have stopped wiping. The gray-green does not come into the folds. I remain a color that belongs to the terrace where children dare each other and stop.

What I can do is wait, and count, and be still with my hand in water.

The counting they already do, at the end of the day, before the lamp is pinched: files, flex, bead-travel, the texture of a wall. I was taught it by being stood where a person stands, in a doorway not mine, and left until I understood the count was occupancy and not omen. My version runs long. I count the grazers and then I count the pauses between them, and the pauses are uneven, and the unevenness keeps me in the doorway past the hour the household has lain down in its stacked heat. They do not tell me to come in. A quiet night with too little life is the emergency they recognize; a quiet night with too much counting is only a guest still learning which fullness to leave untouched. Sometimes I am right. Sometimes the channel has not resumed because the cisterns are balanced, and I have mistaken peace for absence, and I go in at last with my face hot from a vigilance nobody requested. Sometimes I am right the other way: a joint unswept, a drift too thin, a wall that breathes at the wrong interval. Then someone rises without being waked—the cup passing is unnecessary when the body already knows—and we walk, and the walking fixes it, and no one thanks me. Thanks would be noise.

The hand in the water is simpler. Children are allowed to watch the segmented things and not to touch; a still hand is acceptable; a splash is a dull hour downstream and a known guilt. My hand, held still, is accepted more readily than my voice. The lenses gather at the fingers as they gather at a true-cut lip. I do not know whether they are reading the pulse or the heat or the ridge-salt still leaving the skin. I do not move. Clarity trades itself against whatever I carried in, and the downstream runs a little clearer than my presence deserves, and for that hour I am almost a colleague. When I withdraw, the water closes without a sound. I have started to think of this as the only speech I am permitted that does not mark me as unfinished: saying nothing, while something that is not a face decides I can be filtered and survived.

I am not of the tree. I learned that fast, and the learning was a mercy. Once, before the shame had finished teaching my mouth, I stood under the braid at the hour the beads travel fast and I lifted my face, not to tap—only to let one break on the brow, the way rain used to, when rain was a source you could point at. A leaf sealed. Not the leaf I had meant; a neighboring one, as if the canopy kept its own ledger of intention. The household that had routed me into the joint-room drank channel-water for a month, and so did I, and the sharing of that dullness was the nearest they have come to calling me theirs. I have not stood under the canopy since except in the crowd when the roost unburdens, and even then I stand where the drip cannot find me, and I do not cheer, and I do not sip unless the reed is put in my hand and taken back.

The rim still calls, not as desire, as a direction my eyes remember. On the days the mist thins to a band and the upper terraces hold a little more of the gray-green, I feel the hurt behind the lids that means a source is available if I climb. I do not climb. The ones who walk and return sleep poorly; I never returned, so I sleep poorly by a different contract. I dream the pale line on the cutting edge, and the insects saying their names, and I wake to the thread of a channel starting and stopping, and the dream is the louder of the two, which is how I know I am still a guest. Guests dream in the accents of the last house. The cup does not enter the dream. It waits, occupied, until I am awake enough to count it.

If they have a use for me beyond the long count and the still hand, it is this: I am practicing the thinness they already embody, and my failures make the line visible. A person born here does not waste a turn; the wasted turn is invisible to them, corrected before it completes. I complete it. I finish the cup. I wipe the throat. I look up. Each completion is a mark on the rock the way a bad cut is a mark, and they hate a cut that wanders, and so they keep me where the wandering can be seen and routed around, a living sill, raised a hand’s breadth, teaching children by negative example where not to step. I do not resent the use. Resentment is a contest of noise. I set no cup between myself and them because I am the one who would drink it.

Toward evening the mist lowers and the cup becomes a room and I take my place in the doorway of the joint. The files pass, paler on pale. The wall subsides into texture. Somewhere a channel balances and the small sound stops, and I do not go looking, because I have finally learned that some stops are the system resting and not the system gone. My hand is still damp from the cistern. My throat beads and I leave it. I count what moves, and then I count what I will not touch, and the second count is longer, and it is the one that lets me pinch the idea of a lamp I do not own and lie down against the shoulder of block, too wide, too dry, too full of a ridge that is slowly, unwillingly, forgetting its own light.

I do not know what I am becoming. Not one of them—the pads will not ridge, the blink will not slow enough, the eyes still flinch toward sources that are not here. Not one of the files, the filters, the drifts; they have errands, and I have only the correction of my own. Perhaps a bend. Perhaps a joint that must be swept a little oftener than the others, because something clumsy lives in it and is trying, sip by sip, not to. The town’s map is a genealogy of the useful dead; I am not dead, and not yet useful, and the vines have not voted on me. Until they do I remain what the doorway holds at the edge of sleep: a count that runs long, a hand that has learned stillness, a thirst that stops halfway, and the quiet, enormous relief of a place that does not require me to announce myself in order to be allowed to stay.
