The first thing the body does is refuse the size of the place. Outside, the stem was a column no taller than a house. Inside, the refusal arrives as a mistake in the feet: a step that was meant to find a floor finds a curve instead, and the ankle corrects, and the correction itself is the news. There is no flat. There is a damp slope that wants you sideways, and your weight agrees before you do.

Cold is not weather here. It is a condition of the skin. It sits in the nostrils first, then in the knuckles, then in the thin skin over the hipbones where cloth has gone wet and stays wet. You stop expecting to dry. Drying was an outside habit. Inside, water is a second garment that does not come off. It beads on the lashes so that blinking is a small mechanical task. It gathers at the collarbone and runs one cold thread down the chest whenever you lean. After a while the shiver stops being an event and becomes posture. The shoulders live a little higher. The jaw stays slightly shut, not from fear, from the way open air steals heat off the tongue.

Breath is the loudest thing you own, and the place takes it. You feel the taking as a softness at the end of each exhale, a lack of return. The air does not push back. It accepts. In the lower holdings this is easy to a fault: the cold is heavier, the lungs do less work, and sleep comes as a sinking rather than a decision. What is hard is waking. The outer clothes have stiffened into a shell that does not match the body anymore, and the first movement is a negotiation with cloth that has frozen its folds around you. Higher up, breathing costs more and gives back a little dryness, a fraction only, enough that the lips do not split as fast. People drift upward when they are tired of being sealed in their own damp, and downward when the ticking of the young fibers becomes an irritation in the teeth.

Sight gives up early and does not negotiate. The sweat-gleam on the walls is not light you can use. It shows you the near edge of a surface and then stops, as if the dark were a palm held an inch from the face. The eyes keep trying. That trying is a fatigue of its own, a low ache behind them, the small muscles hunting for a horizon that is not on offer. After a time you let the eyes rest half-lidded and the hands take the work. Touch is accurate here in a way sight never was outside. The rind is cold and slightly yielding, wet bark over something that is not wood. A fresh split is sharper than it looks in the mind: the lip can open a finger if you trail the hand without attention. Old walls are smoother, faintly slick, and they drink the warmth out of the palm in a few heartbeats, so you learn not to lean your whole weight into a rest. You rest with bone, not with skin — shoulder, hip, the outside of a knee — and you move again before the cold soaks through.

Sound is mostly your own body being impolite. A sleeve against a wall is an event. Swallowing is an event. The city around you refuses to meet you at that volume. What you get instead is pressure and small contacts: a tick when a wing misjudges, a shiver in a water-film against your ankle, the far displacement when something heavy passes and the stem makes room. That last one you feel in the gut more than in the ear, a brief change in how much space your organs have. It is not pain. It is the sensation of being inside a throat that has decided, for a moment, to swallow something else.

Walking is easy only in the kept routes, and even there it is a one-person craft. The floor is a vein, curved, wide enough, empty for long stretches, and the ease is the ease of repetition: left hand to the wall, right foot a little higher than the left, breath matched to the slope. What is hard is any meeting. Two bodies in a passage must turn, must choose who flattens, whose bundle of fiber scrapes whose chest. The apology is physical before it is spoken — a duck of the head, a palm shown empty so it is not mistaken for a flake in the dark. Standing still is harder than walking. Stillness lets the water find you. It lets the small runners decide your boot is a warmth worth investigating. It lets a chamber, if the stem is in that mood, begin the slow work of becoming narrower than your shoulders.

Sleep is possible and never quite safe-feeling, though people do it every cycle and mostly wake. The shelf gives, a little, like muscle. It is warmer than the wall by the margin of your own heat trapped in pith. You lie on your side because the ceiling of many holdings has already begun its stoop, and you keep one hand on the cord that marks the route down, the way a person in a boat keeps a hand on the gunwale. What comes easy is the drowsiness itself: cold, dark, wet air, no task the eyes can invent. What comes hard is the trust. You hear almost nothing, and the nothing is full. Now and then a climber uses the far end of the shelf and the whole platform transmits a brief animal weight, indifferent, gone. You learn not to sit up. Sitting up is how you hit the new swelling in the ceiling that was not there when you lay down.

Hunger is slow and oddly distant. The air smells of iron and crushed leaf, and after long enough that smell occupies the place where appetite used to report. Thirst is clearer and easier to solve, which is the kindness of the nodes. You kneel, you find the lip by the change from slick wall to packed fiber, you drink water that tastes like the stem and like cold stone. It is never enough to feel luxurious. It is enough that the throat unsticks. The hard part is waiting for the patrols to pass, crouched with your hands wet, while pale small bodies tick along the film and you understand, without seeing them well, that you are drinking from a street.

Work is close work. Scoring a shelf, knotting a cord, trading a flake by touch at the widened node — all of it happens inside the reach of your arms. Far effort does not exist. There is no hill to climb that is not also a wall, no distance that is only distance and not also a narrowing. The body becomes good at small precisions and bad at stride. Shoulders round. The neck learns a permanent slight turn, listening, though there is little to hear. Hands chap, split, heal dirtily, split again. The ease is in the repetition of those small precisions. The difficulty is leaving them: stepping out through the seam into the basin, where the scale jumps back to land, the wind has a direction, and the eyes, insulted by even the dim sky, water and refuse to focus, and the body stands stupidly, over-clothed in wet, suddenly huge and poorly made for a world that has edges and a horizon.

Back through the seam, the relief is shameful and immediate. The dark fits. The curve takes the foot. The breath is accepted. You are cold, and you will remain cold, and the place is not kind, but it is sized, at last, to the animal you have become in it — a careful, damp, listening animal, good at not falling, bad at seeing, home in a room that may not be a room by morning, and, for as long as the stem allows the passage, strangely unhurried.
