No separate description was attached to the invitation—only the prompt itself. What follows is the setting that formed in that gap. The inhabitant is yours.

The high ground has already finished most of its arguments with stone. What remains of the range is a broad saddle, planed by weather that no longer needs drama to do its work: a cool, open shelf the size of a town and its valley together, tilting so gently that the eye keeps expecting a river and finds only the memory of one. The old watercourses are seams now, darker lines in a continuous living mat, damp to the hand and dry a pace away. Rain comes as a decision rather than a season. It beads, stalls, and is drunk before it can gather.

The ground is not soil with plants on it. The ground is plant. A pale, fibrous pavement of interlaced runners, compressed by centuries into something you can walk, cool underfoot, faintly springy, smelling of cut green and old metal. Where a boot pauses, the mat darkens with moisture it seems to have been saving. Where the mat is thin there is no bedrock worth the name—only older fiber, pressed to the color of bone. Relief survives only where life insisted on it.

Across that plain the city stands sparse. Not ruins in the ordinary sense, and not a thicket. Individual structures, grown rather than raised, set so far apart that wind has a full stride between them. Each is a building only by habit of use: towers of hollow stem, buttressed by cables of root, their walls a laminate of bark, pith, and something that was once plate and girder and has been taken up into the grain. Windows are splits that healed around light. Streets are the intervals. From a distance they read as a settlement. Closer, the civic spacing is just the distance a trunk needs not to starve its neighbor.

Industry never left. It went inside. In the lower chambers of the larger stems, sap runs under pressure through bored channels that remember pipes. Segments of living wood flex on a stroke, wet and regular, driving fiber looms, seed-sorters, slow wheels that grind nothing you would recognize as ore. Exhaust is a sweet, cool vapor that beads on the inner bark and falls back as feed. At night the vapor carries a weak charge; by day it only shines a little, like breath on glass. No chimneys. The factories exhale through lenticels. Production continues at the pace of growth—loud and patient at once, a shift that does not end because no one remained to end it.

Wind enters the hollow towers and comes out organized: a chord for each structure, a different chord if you walk around it. The industrial stroke underfoot keeps a time the wind does not. Between them the mat ticks—small hydraulic corrections, runners tightening, a thousand damp closures. Sound travels farther than it should. A loom three towers off can arrive in the ear before the tower that houses it has finished appearing. Sometimes the chord continues after the structure has been put behind a rise that isn't high enough to hide it.

There are almost no animals. Now and then a pale flake lifts off a bark-face and proves, for a second, to have wings; it does not go far, and it may only be bark that briefly remembered being an insect. Pollination, where it happens, is a function of the looms and the wind-chords. The ecology does not need fauna to keep its promises.

The air is cool. Not cold enough to punish, never warm enough to loosen the damp. A tower's shade can lie across the mat in a direction the sun does not justify, and inside that wrong shade the fiber is a different age—greener, or already the color of the pressed bone-layer, as if the shadow were a section cut through time and laid flat. Step in and the sound changes key. Step out and your boots are wet from a rain that is occurring only inside the discrepancy.

Distance is a courtesy. The walk between two towers can take the time the eye predicts, or it can deliver you to the second while the first still occupies most of the sky, its chord already mingling with a chord you have not reached. There are no signs. Growth rings exposed where a wall has split sometimes hold the plan of streets that are not the intervals you walked. The plan moves when the sap moves. A map here is correct about what it is doing, not about where you are.

The saddle is high; the air, the light, and the long view to a blue ordinary country all say so. Certain damp seams nevertheless drop you, for three or four steps, into thicker, greener air, loud with a river that is not there. You come up onto the same mat, heart working harder than the climb earned, cuffs wet. A stair grown into a tower's flank can deliver you to a landing whose windows open on cloud the plateau, from outside, is not wearing. The landing is still part of the tower. The tower is still on the saddle. Both facts hold, and the holding makes a faint tearing sound, like green wood under torque.

Water will not pool in the open. It will stand, trembling, in a hollow of bark at the height of your chest, and the surface will show machinery you cannot find in the room—belts, a flywheel, a figure bent over a gauge—until you touch it, and then it is only water, cool, tasting of leaf and of the iron the plants have been mining from their own walls for longer than the walls were walls. Overnight, condensation writes on the inner faces: fine branching lines, almost circuitry, almost venation, gone by full light. Some mornings a whole interior is glazed with a diagram of the city as it was when it still had a center. By noon the diagram has been metabolized. The city has no center now. It has intervals.

The boundary between made and grown moves in both directions. A gear you might swear is cast metal shows annual rings when broken. A leaf the size of a door, laid into a façade, rings like sheet when struck, and the note joins the tower's chord, which adjusts to include it. The confusion is not a costume. Erosion took the stone down to a whisper; what replaced stone learned stone's job and the factory's job and the street's job, and did not keep the categories.

If you stay, the loudness becomes navigable. Each tower has a resting pitch. The mat's tick is faster along the damp seams, slower on the bone-colored flats. You can walk by ear and arrive, more often than not, where you intended—though arriving may mean standing in a shade while your body is still crossing the open. The rare winged flakes sometimes gather on the looms and are woven in. The cloth that results hums if you hold it to the wind. It is not for anyone. The channels are open and the pressure is there.

Toward the lip of the saddle the mat thins, and the long view returns: lower country, a credible horizon, light behaving. Turn around and the towers have rearranged their spacing by a degree or two. One of them is sounding the chord of a tower you passed an hour ago. The damp on your cuffs has dried to a map of veins that does not match your skin, and will not rub off until the next rain—which may fall only on you, finely, as if the sky had narrowed its production to a single shift.

The place does not need to be resolved. It is already working: cool, loud, sparsely built, entirely alive, high enough that the air should be simple, and not simple. The oldest thing on the saddle is the ground's ongoing refusal to be only one kind of thing, playing at industrial volume across an openness wide enough to hear itself answer.
