You would not find me on the belts, and you would not find me by asking for a name.

I am in the water rooms, which are not rooms so much as the insides of arguments that have already been won and have not yet been forgotten. A stained map. A bench worn to the shape of people who sit for days. A slit of a window that does not show a district so much as the color of the air above a causeway, tea-brown or paler, and that difference is the news. I sleep against a throb that is one street off from the household that claims me, close enough that I know when the line drops to the reduced clatter and far enough that I am not required for it. That gap is the whole of my arrangement with the district. They fetch me when a stain must be watched. They do not fetch me for meat, or for a sole, or for the knock-signals, which I can hear and do not send.

What I am is a patience with a body around it.

The body is not remarkable on the shelf. Short, wide in the shoulder, stained at the knuckles and along the side of the neck where a wet collar has sat so long the film has stopped being a visitor. Glossy in the way masonry is glossy. Boots pegged properly, because I walk the causeways in rain more than most, and a bad sole would make the watching useless. I eat the paste. I ate it before the window closed, and I do not remember refusing. The sourness sits in the mouth the way the races sit in the ear: a fact you stop sorting, until you need it. I can tell the darker film from the gold. That is not a gift. It is how long I have had my face near standing water.

The work, when it is work, is this: someone has cut, or means to cut, or has let a drain fail in a way that amounts to a cut. A centimeter is in dispute. The map-room has listened. They send me out with no instrument worth the name, because instruments rust and also because the shelf has already built the only one that matters. I go to the place. I learn the seep as it is, not as the two households describe it. I mark a stone with nothing more permanent than a thumb-width of paste, which the rain will take, and I watch where the water sits when the cloud thickens and where it sits when the cloud thins. Watching takes days. That is the point of me. A person who still counts the belts in their sleep will invent an ending before the stain has dried. I have aged out of that invention. The reduced clatter is my night, and the full throb is weather I do not work inside.

People think the watching is empty. It is not. On the first day I still hear myself: the desire to be the one who calls it, the small vanity of a correct grade, the memory of children being told about someone who stayed awake on the western rim. By the second day that voice tires, the way a flock-roar tires, and what is left is the water deciding. A film of tea-colored wet advances along a joint and stops. It does not stop where either household said it would. It stops where the stone is a finger lower than the maps, which are mostly stains themselves, and which were right when they were made and are righter as stains than as lines. I do not correct the maps. I come back and say where the water sat. If they ask what it means, I tell them to send someone else to watch the same stain dry. Two watchings are a fact. One is a traveler's impression, and I am not a traveler, even though I walk.

Between disputes I am harder to find, which is how I prefer it, and how the shelf allows it. I go out onto the flats when the herds are bedding and the light is the ordinary white-yellow that does not reach the next stack. I am not good at turning them. I do not try. I stand where the water-smell is already wrong for their path, and they revise around me without ceremony, heavy shoulders, warm bodies, the wall of them smelling of the film they have cropped all day. I am a grade they have accepted. That is as close as I come to the celebration of being fetched.

I know the bands in the lower gutters of three districts and not of a fourth, and the fourth is a hole I keep rather than a failure. Their short names I do not say aloud. Saying a name in the market is a claim, and I do not claim them. I know which one will nose a hand held low, palm open, not offering food, and which will not, and I do not test the second kind to be sure I still remember. Certainty is a thing you spend. The shelf is full of people who have spent it on louder questions.

Sometimes, in the thin hour before the afternoon cloud thickens, I climb a stair that is not mine and stand where a cornice birds' ledge has stained the facing paler than the country rock. I do not take eggs. The schedule is not mine to keep, and a person who watches water should not also be a person who empties a ledge, or the district will stop trusting the watching. From there the shelf is what it always is: no green, no far, stacks breathing, a flock drifting rather than wheeling, the long soft roar arriving as weather. I do not wait for it to finish. It will thin. I have never seen it finish. The desire to see it finish is the dry-country desire, the one the people who stay down are said to catch, like a cold night. I let it go the way the races let a high mark go, over a spillway, without mourning the water.

If you asked me where I am from I would not give you a seep. That is the intimate fact, and it is not for the first asking, and it is not for a stranger who has not yet tried the paste and failed or stayed. I would give you a level, and a throb, and the side of the street that holds the water in working order. If you stayed, and if you learned to speak at the distance of an ear, I might tell you that the seep I drink from was stolen once, two generations back, in a cut that the map-room took a season to name as theft, and that the household I am spliced into still does not cut new foundation on that side. The restraint is the inheritance. I am what the restraint looks like when it has a wide hand and a good sole and time.

At night I lie in the splice and count layers. Water ticking. Belts stitching and then, in the reduction, only the throb. A mutter in a gutter that means the band has moved and not that the drains have failed. If the races are running hard, I do not get up. Someone is at the spillway. That someone is almost never me. I am for the slow argument, not the swollen board. Knowing the difference is how I sleep. The stone is warm. Another body may or may not be on the mat; the household is a vertical fact, and some nights the mat is a cistern-share more than it is a marriage. Warmth is not a metaphor here. It is the condition under which the film returns, and the condition under which I will return to a stain in the morning and find it has crept a thumb's width toward the truth.

When I die they will not carry me far. I have already said, in the way such things are said — not solemn, over paste, in the map-room, where it can be overheard by the right people — that I want the shaft above a race, the clean stone, not the furnace and not the flats. A watcher should go where the water can be heard changing its mind. They may not do it. Household ways outrank a preference, and I am only spliced in, not the seep itself. If they give me to the flats, the film will take what it takes, and the flies, and the herds will bed against some other wall. The name I use will stay in use. That was never the part I was keeping.

What I am, then, after the first image wears off and the shelf has had time to correct me: not a priest of the water, not a hero of the rim, not the person who opens the spillway with their hands. A wide, stained, close-spoken animal of the middle levels, fetched for centimeters, trusted because I can be bored for three days without inventing an ending. I will feed you if you are in the room when the ceramic comes around. I will watch your face on the paste, not from cruelty, from the practical question the street always asks. If you stay, come to the causeway the next time the cloud sits low and the light cannot tell a stack from the haze. Do not talk. The water will sit where it means to sit. I will be there long enough to see it. That is the whole of my use, and on this shelf a use that lasts is a better name than the one my household already recycled from the dead.
