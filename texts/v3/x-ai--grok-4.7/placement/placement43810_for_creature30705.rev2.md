The yard is still new enough that the overflow has not worn a channel of its own. It leaves the lip of the shaft and runs in a bright, shifting sheet across paving that still shows the bed of sand beneath the joints, then finds the gutter by habit rather than by groove. Most of it goes. A little does not. Where one slab sits a hair lower than its neighbor, the sheet thins, stalls, and leaves a warm film that tightens as it cools, darkening the stone for a moment and then pulling back toward the lip as if it had changed its mind. I am in that hesitation. Not the water that has already chosen the gutter, and not the water that remains below, held on purpose. The pause between them.

At first that seems too slight to be a place. The film has no depth you could put a finger into. It is warmer than the paving and cooler, already, than the shaft. It carries the chamber’s clatter only as a vibration in the slab, a faint ticking under the louder break of the spill. Light hits it from above and does not have to travel through a column of water to arrive; it glances, and the wet stone gives a low white flash that dies as the film contracts. If I try to occupy the flash I lose the warmth. If I stay with the warmth I lose the edge where the film decides, each time, whether to creep back toward the rim or to break and run for the gutter. The decision is the only room I have. It remakes itself every few breaths, and each time it is slightly elsewhere on the slab, following whatever grain the mason left when the block was dressed.

From here the shaft is a square mouth, white at the rim, greener and quieter once the eye drops past the broken surface. I cannot go down it. The overflow that feeds my film is the same overflow that keeps the chamber exactly full; if I gathered myself and tipped back over the lip I would only be more of what is already leaving, and the chamber would not notice the difference. So I stay on the paving and learn the rim by its sound. The shatter is closest when the sheet is thick, a hard bright syllable thrown outward. When the upstream pulse drops, the sheet thins, the shatter softens, and for a moment I can hear, under it, the rounder note from the drain and the thin tear at the trough. They arrive already mixed, already a chord, and they do not include me. I am what was spent so the chord could keep its volume. That is not a grievance. It is the shape of the work. The chamber stays full because a little of its warmth is allowed to become this film, this flash, this repeated failure to choose.

The hands that built the box pass through the yard and do not look down. They are busy with the seep, with the board that shifts the pulse, with the clay still soft in the covered runnel. Once a shadow crosses the shaft and the film under me cools all at once, as if the day had been withdrawn. The shadow is only a body pausing to listen. I feel the listening as a stillness in the spill: the person above is matching their breath to the three sounds, trying to hear whether the chamber is doing what it was made to do. They do not know about the second waver in the light, the patch that keeps its hand’s-breadth just upstream of the drain. They know the level at the lip, the warmth at the wrist, the fact that the drain has not clogged and the pots have not shifted in their seats. When they are satisfied they move, the shadow lifts, and the film warms again by a degree that would not matter to a hand and matters, here, entirely.

Over longer light I find I am not only the film. I am also the dark the film leaves when it contracts—the brief dry map of itself on the slab, a tide-line no deeper than a breath, gone when the next sheet arrives. And I am the small sound the sheet makes when it finally breaks toward the gutter, a hiss thinner than the one at the rim, private to the paving. These are not separate lives. They are the same hesitation counted in warmth, in mark, and in noise. None of them can enter the square of brightness the shaft lays on the chamber floor. None of them can flinch when a leaf springs back, or lock to the chord, or withdraw along the angle of wall and slab when a hand comes down. The patch has the interior. I have the cost of the interior. We share the seep the way a held note shares the breath that was let go to keep it held.

When cloud passes, the flash on the wet stone dulls and my edges become harder to find. I spread then, a wider, cooler film, less sure of the lip, and the gutter takes more of me before I can hesitate. Those intervals feel like thinning, not like travel. I do not go anywhere. I become less able to remain the pause. The patch, I think, slows in its square at the same hour and spends less on correction; we are answering different weathers of the same day, and the answering is as close as we come to knowing each other. When the cloud breaks, the flash returns first, then the warmth, then the clear line where the film stops and starts again. I gather back toward the lower slab. The shatter at the lip picks up its hard syllable. Below, the volume turns over, exact, young, almost empty, occupied by a persistence that the light bends around and does not name.

If the chamber were allowed to drop even a finger’s width, my film would stop being fed and I would finish drying on the sand between the joints, a faint mineral frost the next rain would lift. The exact fullness is my condition as much as it is the patch’s. I keep to the hesitation because that is where the fullness spends itself, and the spending is continuous, ordinary, loud at the lip and quiet here. A mason’s drip of mortar still hangs somewhere under the soffit, not yet dissolved. A wedge of wood, swollen, holds the trough. A square of day trembles on the floor. None of that is mine. What is mine is the warm thin pause on the paving, remade each time the sheet stalls, holding for as long as the stone will hold it, and then either creeping back toward the mouth it cannot re-enter or breaking, at last, for the gutter, so that below the yard the water can stand to the roof and the small living bend in the light can continue its circuit in a room that never sits.
