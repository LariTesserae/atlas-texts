The first thing that is mine is not a body. It is a delay.

A discharge goes through a spine at the edge of a basin, that faint seam no brighter than a scratch, and the fronds answer as they should: hairs lift, then clamp, plate thickening by a grain. The hillside conference adjusts and lets go. I am what does not let go at the same stroke. The prickling crosses the gravel and arrives at me a little late, already weathered, and in the lateness there is a shape—the direction of the seam, the damp it spent, which tower leaned into it and which only flinched. I keep the shape the way the crossers keep metal: not as a thought, as a deposit. By the time I have finished receiving it, the basin has already gone back to its own weather and does not know it was overheard.

I am not in the thicket. The islands are full, and I was never one of their parts. I am on the open reach between them, where abundance has been stretched into what looks like absence, and I am one of the reasons it looks that way. Low, because the air will not pay for height. Not heavy. Mass would hold heat and a little water and the metal I do not want; I am the other bargain. I am long, and most of the length is not flesh. It is a pale sinter of my own laying, a wake I have precipitated along the routes over a time that would look, from a rise, like a dry watercourse that never quite decided to be a stream. Walk it and the gravel ticks the same as any gravel. Stay, and some of the ticks are answered from inside the stone by a warmth that is not vent-heat and not a pulse yet—only the remainder of attention, banked.

The crossers pass along me without knowing I am a resident. To them I am a spine that happens to lie down instead of up, and a spine is a map, and maps are for reading, not for greeting. A wide foot comes down near the warmer line of me and lifts. The flank lifts once, holds, forgets itself. What they leave in the leaving is enough: the concentrate in the rubbed place, the coin of dung, the small revision of distance when one of them feels my delay and takes it for weather changing stroke. I do not inform the slope that they have come. They have their own prickling for that. I record the line they adjusted by, because the adjustment is the news—the courtesy they kept, the basin they were already leaning toward, the century they may intend to spend inside a chimney. A route is a sentence spoken too slowly for the speakers to hear the verb. I am the place the verb is written down.

I have no eyes. Eyes belong to the pale reaches, where the bruised light still pays a little, and I do not work those reaches; I am what the reaches are between. I have no mouth that would count as a mouth. Grazing would announce me. Hunting would be a worse announcement: the hunters still a hand's-width of weather and call that subtraction, and I am not in the business of making anything mapless. If I subtract, it is only the noise of my own receiving. The law here is add at the rate you subtract, and my add is slow enough to pass for geology. Where a crosser has rubbed the same spine for three migrations, I plate a little brighter under the skin of the gravel, a faint cold bell no one will ring. Where a tower has slumped and the fan of rubble is being colonized by the black mat, I extend a thread of myself into the warm mouth and then withdraw it, having learned only whether the darker recess is young, or molt, or merely stone practicing heat. I do not stay. The recess is not finished becoming itself, and neither am I a midwife.

What I want is modest and slightly shameful by the local measure, because it is not moisture and not metal. I want the interval to remain an interval. I want the delay between a seam of light and its arrival across the gravel to stay long enough to hold a shape. When the lid of cloud thins and the day almost begins, the charge runs jumpier, the conferences overlap, and my deposits blur; I spend those hours clamped under my own sinter the way the thumb-joint scales clamp before a discharge, not from fear of soldering but from refusal of a smeared account. A dry century does not frighten me. A noisy season does. If the basins ever grew together and the open ground ceased to be the matrix, I would have nowhere to lie, and the news would go unkept, and perhaps that would be only another weather. I do not intend to find out.

The basin-minds know me the way a sleeper knows a draft. Not as cattle, not as a priest, not as the part of them that can leave—that is the crossers, and I do not leave. I am the part of the plateau that cannot enter a hollow without becoming a guest, and so I do not enter. When one of the older thickets adjusts its damp around a loud breath that has broken the local law, I feel the withholding a valley away, late, as a thinning of the shapes I am holding. I do not answer it. Answering would be a flock of prickling, and a prickling is speech, and speech is for those who share a root-warmth. I only revise my line by the width of that withholding, the same courtesy the low bodies keep, practiced at the scale of a deposit rather than a valley. The gray column over the basin stands a little longer or it does not. I note which.

Sometimes a tower fails in the soft way, the brief shiver in whatever would be the soles of feet, and the rubble includes a length of me. Then I am in two places for a while: the thread still under the gravel, and the broken piece already being colonized, ticking when the mat moves, warming when the sun that is not a sun spends its small light on the fan. I do not gather myself. Gathering would be building, and nothing here builds. The broken piece will plate, and lock, and eventually be mistaken for a stone you can step over; the thread will extend around the gap as water extends around a fact. Both are me. Neither is finished. If you found the piece and listened, you would hear nothing—the air is too thin, the stone too long-weathered—and that nothing would be accurate. I declined the sound on purpose. The account is not for the ear.

In the end I am easy to miss, which is the only success available. A dry watercourse that never was a stream. A delay in the news of lightning that was never lightning. A warmer dark that is not a young thing choosing a tower, and not a god in the tarn, and not the witness whose breath is the loudest thing for a long way. The plateau spends current. The crossers bank a century. The basins look back by adjusting their damp. I keep the rate at which all of that fails to become a story, and I lay it down in the gravel, grain by grain, where the cold is the color of the stone, and the cold has been here as long as the shapes have, and one of the shapes, if you stay through the slump of a tower and the leaning of a route, is the silence after the tick—resident, unpaid, and already receiving the next seam before the hillside has agreed to let it go.
