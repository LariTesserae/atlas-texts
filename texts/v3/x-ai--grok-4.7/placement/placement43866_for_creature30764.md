I would not come in over the rim. I would be already under the grade of the race, where the culvert keeps its damp without becoming a stream, and the first thing I would know is the temperature of iron that has not been warm in a long time.

Not a walker. Walkers leave fiber combed one way and then are gone. I stay in the part of the hollow that does not need a crowd to remain a town. The shaft comes through the wall of the middle hall and I meet it there, in the narrow service that was left when the hornbeams closed, a gap no wider than a body turned sideways. The bast belts pass a hand's breadth from the scar-wood. When one cracks from dryness I am the reason it is replaced before the roller starves: not because I am sent for, but because the note of the drive changes by less than a person would call a change, and I have been listening to that note since the hoops disappeared under graft.

I have hands, in the ordinary sense, and they stay the temperature of the bench. That is how I learned I belonged. Tools do not warm to me. Condensation does not avoid me. The moths that drink off the hooks will land on a knuckle if I hold still beside the rack, and leave a damp the size of a seed, and the damp is the same as the dark spots on the mat. I am not the building and not the crop. I am the arrangement that keeps the emptiness where the copper runs.

By the hour before full light I am at the wheel-pit, not to watch the heron — the heron does not come for me — but to feel whether the race is thin. If it is thin, the bucket-motors will have taken the load in the night and the hum will be the one I no longer hear until it stumbles. Then I go in under the vault and lay a palm on the housing. Bark, not metal, sweats into the groove that was cut for it. I do not open anything that is still running true. The old planters selected bitterness into the bark so that mice would not nest; something in that bitterness has settled into me as a habit of not nesting. I sleep, if it is sleep, in the lower hall between the vats, on the side where the root-skin replaced the failed lining, and the liquor smell is leaf-rot and clean metal, and my breath does not show.

The numbers on the living post are not mine to invent. I burn a new mark only when a belt returns to the beater as fiber, so the ledger and the cycle stay the same sentence. The wood heals around the burn. I let it. Healing is the maintenance. My name, if the hollow has one for me, is whatever the next scar will close over.

People are not absent because I forbade them. They are absent because the processes were sized under the threshold of echo, and a voice would be a larger event than the drop that lets go in the yew. When one does come down the root-path, I am in the drying hall with my back to the aisle, counting withe by the gap between racks, and they see the industry and not the one who keeps its grade. That is correct. I am not a host. I am the part of the agreement that still has to move a hook, twist a splice, draw the press-liquor on the hour the pump already knows.

Afternoon comes in coins through the vault. The copper throws its thin green flash. I walk the corridor the plants themselves keep open, and the sedge does not take my print for long — the mat rises. At the lip I stop. Beyond it the country is ordinary wet ground. I do not cross. The hollow holds what was poured into it, and I am part of what has not dried: cooler than the day, continuous, small, and still turning.
