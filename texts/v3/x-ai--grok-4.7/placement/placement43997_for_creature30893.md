You would find me by the change in the floor, before you found me by sight. The lanes stop pretending to be level a little before the wheel-house, and the stone takes a grade the rest of the shelf has spent an age refusing. Water, which everywhere else is a film deciding slowly which way to be a film, remembers a direction here. It threads the last channel with a thin decisiveness and goes into the housing, and the housing answers with that pulse you feel in the boot before the ear agrees to it. I am usually already inside by then. The fog does not come in with you. The doorway is low, cut for people built like us, and the mist stops at it the way a thought stops when it has nothing left to wet.

The wheel-house is the only room on the shelf where the air has a floor and a ceiling that are not the same weather. Stone walls, jointed, our work, not the older continuous kind. A housed drop. The wheel turning in it with a patience that would shame the vats. Belts going off into the sheds, creaking, taking the day’s moisture and making it into sawn blocks and warmed resin and the pale dust that leaves us. I keep the belts from wandering and the housing from growing a slick that would put someone on their back, and I listen for the half-tone the hum takes when rain loads the channels. That is the whole skill. It does not look like a skill from the lane. From the lane I am a darker shape in a darker door, broad, short in the leg, flat-footed, the knot at my nape wrapped in waxed cloth because the spray off the drop would otherwise soak the collar before the furnace stretch is half done. Palms permanently cool. A mineral shine at the wrists that soap turns into a different shine. People know me by the creak I walk in time with.

I was not born to the wheel. The house I slept in is one of the older foundations, nearer the middle of town, where a stretch of wall has no joint you can test with a knife. My household rendered it, watched the render darken on the basins’ schedule, wiped it, watched it return, and at last stacked cordwood and let the wrong wood rot first. I slept on the bench nearest that wall as a child, above the seep-line, and I learned the shelf’s pulse as a dampness arriving in the small of the night against my shoulder, a cool that was not draft. No one called it anything. It was the reason we turned the bedding. When I was old enough for a round, the channel-keepers did not want me. I cleared well enough, but I cleared the seams that ran toward the bare curve more often than the water required, and I could not explain it in the language of notches and foundations. Occupying the hands is a reason they accept from their own. From a child it sounds like lingering.

So I was given to the rim, where lingering has a mechanism.

It suited. Height, in that house, had been a rumor in the chest. Here it is a fact under the floor: the drop going on down inside a timber throat, the wheel taking one spoke’s worth of it at a time, never the whole fall, never the brightness people go out to squint at when the deck lifts. I do not go out for the lifting. The housing has a slit on the cliff side, no wider than a hand turned sideways, and when the cloud rises off the shelf the slit goes pale and then white-gold, and the belts for a moment look like things with edges. I keep my back to it. Not from virtue. The gold makes the wheel hard to see, and the wheel is what will take a hand if you trust the wrong light. When the fog walks back in, the slit greys, the edges leave the belts, and the thump returns to the sole where it belongs. I am more accurate in that light. I think most of us would be, if accuracy were the thing asked of us.

The town and I meet at the change of belts and at the end of the furnace stretch, when the shed lane is the warm place and people invent errands to stand in it. I come up the grade with spray on the coat’s shoulder and they make room without a greeting that would waste sound. Someone will say the hum stepped up, or did not, and I will say whether the channels delivered what the rain promised. We look at the ground while we say it. If a belt is going to fray it frays in weather like this, and they like to know before it happens rather than after. I am not consulted about trees or graves or the tally. I am consulted about whether the day has weight. It is a small office. It keeps me in the town’s sentence without putting me at the center of it.

What I do with the other hours is walk back the way the water came, as far as the last reliable seam, and no farther. I have learned the difference the channel-keepers already knew and did not hire me for. Certain stretches of glaze, on the line between the housetops and the place where the leaves fail, need clearing a little beyond use. A leaf left there does not only clog. It sits wrong. It beads on one side and goes dull on the other, as if it had started to become the leaves the children keep in jars. I hook it out. I do not follow the grade that isn’t a grade, the clean curve pulling at the edge of the eye. The old woman who keeps the line itself meets me sometimes where my round frays into hers. We do not trade theories. She asks whether the wheel took the rain. I ask whether the line was loud today, by which both of us mean whether the wind that begins nowhere pushed anything onto it. If it has, she already hooked it. If I have found a half-crisp scrap in a seam that should have stayed wet, I give it to her on the pole, not with the hand. She nods as at a notch. We go back to our separate damp.

I do not know what the circle is. I am not being modest. The not-knowing is the condition of living here, the way wet wool is a condition. I have stood at my distance in the thick hour and felt the attention distributed, stronger on the side of me that faces the bare stone, even when the fog has taken the curve out of the world. It does not frighten me in a way I could point. It makes me speak more quietly to no one, and check the belt I already checked. A nothing that draws water off a flicked drop is doing a kind of work. I do work. I recognize the resemblance and do not confuse it for kinship. Kinship would mean offering something. We do not offer.

Once, in the early cycle, the air went keen enough that the pipes sang and the housing grew a skin of ice along the slit. The wheel slowed. I put my hand to the housing not to warm it—nothing I am could warm it—but to know how far the cold had come into the timber. The thump was still in the wood, delayed, like a word said into cloth. I stayed until the fog thickened and the ice turned back into drip, and the hum found its note. When I came up into the lane the tally-keeper asked if I had seen the lower country through the slit, the brightness on the cliffs below. I had not turned. He looked at me as if I had failed a small kindness. Maybe I had. Distance is a kind of exposure, and some of them want, once a month, to be exposed and then covered again. I wanted the note back in the belts. We are not the same kind of tenant. The shelf has room for both, so long as neither of us starts a theory.

At night the wheel is left turning. I bank nothing; there is no fire in my charge. I walk the grade in the last of the useful light, palm on the channel wall, feeling the delivery shrink to a trickle, and I shut the housing door on the pale slit. The town’s lamps come up behind me, small, each one a spent spoon of oil. My room is in the worker row nearest the rim, stone, bedding on a bench, a flawed resin round on the sill that I use as a weight for the scrap of waxed cloth I mend coats with. No jar of curled leaves. I had one as a child and the house softened them, as houses do, and I did not gather more. The wall here is jointed and ordinary. It beads only when the weather beads. I sleep better for that, and worse on the nights when the deck lifts and the slit I am not looking through goes gold along the crack under the door. Then I dream of lengths: the throat of the drop, a crack that has not grown, a lane shortened to the distance a voice can cross. I wake when the tappers’ yokes knock, far off, a soft sound with no sacredness in it.

I would not be anyone the hoist brings up, and I would not be the circle’s keeper, and I would not be the attention in the fog. Those are already placed, or rightly unplaced. I am the one in the doorway when you come down the grade, shoulders turned to let you pass, spray at the hem, waiting half a moment longer than courtesy requires because I am listening for whether the day still has weight. If it does, I nod. If it does not, I nod later. The wheel does not hurry, and neither do we, and the shelf goes on being finished without asking either of us to finish it.
