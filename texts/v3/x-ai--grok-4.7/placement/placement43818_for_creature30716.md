The first thing that resolves is not a floor but a resistance. Something underfoot that gives and then remembers the shape of the pressure. I stay still long enough for the give to become a contour: a shallow basin of fine grit, warmer than the air, already marked by earlier weights that have not yet been erased. When I shift, the grit slides in a thin sheet toward the lowest point and stops, obedient to a slope I cannot yet see. The air is dry enough to sting the inside of a breath I am only now discovering I am taking. There is a taste of metal that has been heated and allowed to cool without being touched.

Light arrives later, and not from above. It leaks sideways through a seam I first mistake for a horizon. The seam is irregular, a crack that has been widened by hand or by the slow worry of temperature, and what comes through it is the color of old paper held close to a flame—amber at the edges, nearly white at the center, and flickering in a rhythm too slow for fire. I turn toward it because the grit under me is cooler on the side away from the seam. Turning is how I learn I have a facing. The body that faces is narrow through the shoulders and longer in the reach than I expected; the hands, when I bring them up, are jointed past the wrist in a way that lets the fingers fold flat against the forearm and lock. Useful, I think, without knowing for what. The skin over the knuckles is thin and has already taken the grit; when I brush one hand against the other the grains sing a small, dry note and fall away.

The seam widens as I approach, or I am smaller than the distance suggested. Beyond it the space opens into a long gallery whose walls are not built but peeled—layers of a darker stone stripped back in curling sheets that still cling at their upper edges. Each sheet is inscribed, not with letters I can read but with grooves that catch the amber light and return it as a finer brightness. Some grooves are fresh enough that my fingertip finds a burr; others have been worn to a polish by whatever has traveled this way before. I walk, and the sound of the grit I carry in the creases of my feet is answered, faintly, by a similar hiss farther down the gallery. I am not alone in the way of being observed. I am accompanied by the evidence of other passages that have not yet finished settling.

Midway, the gallery drops a single step. The drop is deliberate: the edge is squared, and a lip of the peeled stone has been left raised so that anything rolling would be caught and held. In the catch-basin there are objects that are not grit. A coil of wire gone green. A flat disc of the same pale metal I taste in the air, pierced at one side. Three beads of glass the color of the light, fused where they touched. I crouch—the extra joints make the crouch a folding rather than a bend—and do not take them. The impulse that rises is not ownership but inventory: these have been set where the step will keep them from traveling farther until someone decides they should. My hands hover, then settle on my knees. The decision is not mine yet, or not only mine.

Farther on, the amber thins. A second light, bluer and steadier, occupies the end of the gallery. It comes from an opening that has been cut square and then partly filled again with fitted blocks, leaving a slot at eye level for someone of my new height. Through the slot I see an exterior I cannot enter without unbuilding: a slope of the same grit, descending toward a dark line that might be water or might be only the shadow of a farther ridge. On the slope, at intervals, stand low cairns. Each is topped with a single peeled sheet propped upright so that its inscribed face turns toward the gallery. They are readable from here only as a rhythm of bright and dark. One of them moves—not the sheet, but a figure beside it, compact, wrapped in something that does not flare in the wind I can now hear against the slot. The figure’s hands are raised to the sheet, tracing. It pauses. I have the sudden certainty that if I make a sound the figure will not startle; it will simply include the sound in whatever count it is keeping.

I do not call out. Instead I set my palm against the fitted blocks beside the slot and feel, conducted through the stone, a slow ticking that is not mechanical. It matches the flicker I first saw in the seam, delayed by the distance. The ticking is the place thinking about its own edges. I am inside that thought, not as an interruption but as one of the pressures the grit has agreed to remember. When I draw my hand back, the print remains for a moment in the faint dust on the stone—five fingers, the extra fold, the slight drag of a wrist that locked and then released. Already the air is lifting the finer grains. By the time the blue light has shifted another degree, the print will be only a cooler patch, and then not even that.

I stay at the slot. The figure outside finishes its tracing, lowers its hands, and sets one more grain-weight on the cairn’s base to keep the sheet from turning. It does not look toward the gallery. It does not need to. The arrangement of what has been left, and of what has been worn smooth, is already a sentence we are both inside. My part, for now, is the weight that keeps one edge from curling shut, and the decision, renewed each time the ticking reaches me, not to take the disc or the beads or the wire until the step itself asks to be cleared. The ask has not come. The grit at my feet continues its small migrations toward the center of wherever I stand. I let it. The longer I am still, the more precise the basin becomes, and the more clearly I can feel, through the soles I am still inventing, the exact slope the place has chosen to keep.
