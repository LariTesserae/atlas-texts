I would not be on the gantry. The gantry is for looking, and looking is a thing the plateau allows only at the edges of a job. I would already be down, where the seep has found the joint and the joint has agreed to keep it.

The boots know the hexagons before the eyes do. Some plates rock a thumb’s width and mean nothing. Some do not rock at all and mean a lid. I carry a rod not much longer than my arm, iron gone the color of the pans, and I put it down the way the shoulder-high put their faces down: not to strike, to ask. If the crust answers with a soft give and a thread of hotter air along the shin, I mark the place in the book and I do not step there. The book is small. The marks are not words the vapor would have to carry. A tick. A doubled tick when the split has a brother. I have walked long enough that the brothers announce themselves in the wrist before the eye finds them—a stiffness, as if the rod had touched two stones at once. The keepers at the mid-basin do not call this a gift. They call it why the young from the eastern shed are sent, and why some of them take the next car down the rim.

I was sent. A keeper died with his hand still on a filter, and the shed had one body more than its rhythm wanted. The car was half coal, half plate, and I rode with the plate because the coal dust cakes the throat and the plate only stains the sleeves. For a season the house felt thick under the boots. I pulled filters at the wrong hour. I spoke a sentence across a cistern and watched it die in the vapor, and felt my own mouth as a thing too open. The children did not laugh at that. They turned their hands palm-down, the sign for thick air, and went back to their tiles. After the month the thickness left. I did not decide to stay. The latch of the outer room became mine because I was already rising before the brightness hardened, and no one else wanted the cold of that door in the brown hour when the engines are only a knock in the ankle.

What I am, then, is the one who sleeps nearest the weather and still is not outside. A line-walker, they would sign it: two fingers along the seam of a palm, then the fist closed, the book. Not a scraper. My nails are not yellow. Not a rail-hand. I do not know the cars except as the interval by which ore-sense returns and plate arrives. Not one of the wings. I have stood under a fin at brass-light and held out a cloth for the white dust because a child was sick and could not climb, and the dust came down as if I were not there, and I did not take that as an insult. The plateau does not insult. It fails to notice, which is cleaner.

In the brown hour I drink first, before the coil has sweated the night’s share into the second pan. The water tastes of iron and of the far side of the sweet clog, never the numb side, because I change the sintered bed when the tick in the book says the seep has shifted, not when the ledger says the month has turned. Then I take the rod and go out along the benches while the shoulder-high are still folded at the rims and the crawlers have not yet remembered their tiling. The film is thickest then. It lifts at the dried edges of the pans in curls, and if I am careful I can pass without writing a track the evening will have to refill. I do not always manage it. A foot is a foot. Behind me the first mouth closes the sentence.

What I look for is not mystery. A new crack with a twin is a place not to lay pipe. A flue breathing harder than its brick should allow is a message to the house: filters, or a scrape, or a day of burning only coal until the sweet film is dry enough not to flash. I have a sign for each. At the compound door I do not knock. Knocking is a lowland hope that sound will do the work. I stand where the keeper on the pressure can see my hands through the slit, and I show the day. If the doubled tick is bad, the fist opens and closes twice. Someone will come with a scraper and a length of plate. We will not discuss it. Discussion is how outsiders stay a season.

There is a stretch I trust more than the others, west of the mid-basin outflow, where the stain-line of boots gives way to the older crust and the filaments in the near pans grow short and clean. The shoulder-high stop at that line as if it were a wall with no stones. I stop a rod’s length farther out, so that we share the hour without sharing the ground. A calf stood inside an adult’s legs there for most of a season. I watched it learn the sting from the scald by the set of the adult’s weight, the same way I was shown as a child on another bench, by a body and not by a rule. When the calf took its own place at a cooler rim I felt the house-rhythm in it, the fitting, and I did not wave. Waving is a shout done with the arm. It lifted its head once, salt at the mouth, and the steam took the shape of the look, and that was the whole acquaintance.

I eat what the rail brings, and twice I have eaten what the crust did not quite close on—once a calf that missed the stain-line in a hard rain, once a crawler-fat thing the length of my forearm that had poured onto a lid and stuck. Both were pulled clear. Both became leather or tiles or the pot. I did the pulling because I was nearest, and because my hands already knew the difference between a seep that wants a body and a seep that will give one back if you are quick and do not speak. There is no ceremony. Ceremony would need voices to agree on it.

At dusk I come in when the grazers drift toward the richer outflow and the narrow wings drop to the fins. I am not always the one awake over the pressure. That office rotates, and my turn is the third brown hour, when the knock in the floor is easiest to lose if you have been hearing it all day. I sit with my heel on the plate and my hand on the valve and I do not think of lowland rain. That dream belongs to people who still expect an answer from the air. What I think of is the doubled tick I have not yet walked to: a crack on the eastern bench that should have a brother near our cistern footing, and does not, yet. The basin is one skin. If the brother is late, the skin is deciding. I will be on it when the decision shows, rod down, book open, mouth shut.

If the car came tomorrow and the rim opened and someone asked me what I had been, I would not have the spoken shape of it. I would show them the outer latch, pale where my fingers have worn the condensate smooth. I would show them the rod’s tip, polished by asking. I would open the book to a page of ticks that match cracks half a kilometer apart, and I would set two fingers on the seam of my own palm and walk them, slowly, the way the crawlers pour, until the other person understood that this is a life the size of a seep-line, and that the water has not failed me, and that I do not intend to be audible again.
