I come up the outer rail with the tins, which is the wrong way to arrive if you mean to be remembered, and the right way if you mean to be used. The grain is hard and the oil is sealed and nobody on the loading spur looks at my face long enough to decide whether I will stay. They look at the lading marks. I look at the basin and the looking costs me a breath I do not yet know how to budget, so the first thing I am, here, is a person who has already spent what the morning required and must stand with a hand on a rail until the sweetness in the teeth passes and the yard stops tilting.

I wash, that first week, the way lowland people wash. The film on the pipes disgusts me in a clean, educated way. I scrub my forearms in fault-water until they sting, and by the fourth night I am feverish in a room above the condensation line where the heat has nowhere to go but into the pallet. Someone leaves a rinsed cake on the sill without a note. It tastes of hot coin and of a terrace I cannot name. I eat it because pride is a sprint and sprints die here before they become habits. In the morning the fever is less. I stop scrubbing quite so thoroughly. I do not mention this. Already I am learning the local economy of speech: what you can afford to say is smaller than what has happened to you.

They try me on the presses because I am new and the presses need bodies that can wait. I can wait. I cannot love the bite. Half a shift of watching a plate take the shape it was always going to take leaves my hands wrong—too ready, wanting a conclusion. The condenser crews will not have me; I flinch at the rime and I still drink as if water were a right rather than a sounding. A re-leveler lets me carry the rod for three days and then takes it back, not unkindly. "You trust your feet," she says, which is the worst thing anyone has said to me in any country. She does not explain. She does not have the breath, and I am not yet worth the explanation.

What they give me, when they are done testing what I am not, is the chalk and the slate and the hour.

The signal arm on the survey spur wants a keeper who will not improve it. It moves once an hour if the lower yards have anything that must cross the air without being spoken. Most hours it does not move. My work is to be there when it does, and to write what the angle meant on a slate that will be read at arm's length by people who already half-know, and then to walk the slate down through the terraces because the air will not carry a message and the arm cannot say anything twice. I learn the postures. A lift of two fingers is water, short. A palm turned out is a bearing warming past schedule. A stillness held one count too long, which I miss the first week and which costs a condenser hall an hour of scale, is the flock—someone on the rim has seen them, and the yards should know, not so they can do anything, but so they can go to the rails and be in the sentence.

It is not a trade with a flattened thumb. It is a trade with a pocket. I carry chalk, a rag for the sun, a second slate because the first will be taken from me on a landing and not returned until evening, and the small level the re-leveler pressed into my hand on the fourth day without taking the rod back. "Not for the lanes," she said. "For you. You still think the floor is arguing." I touch it the way they touch it, without looking, and for a long time it tells me nothing I can use. Then one evening the spur under the arm has taken a lean no one posted, a finger's width toward the basin, and the level agrees before my ankle does. I rehang nothing. I am not licensed to rehang. I write the lean on the slate as if it were a signal, and in the morning the re-levelers are already there, and one of them nods at me the way you nod at weather you have budgeted for.

So that is the first thing I become: the hour's memory. Not the city's—the city keeps itself in brick and in the rise of foundations, and it does not need me for the long count. I am the short count. What the arm said. Which terrace the cake came from, because the name is the taste and the taste is a location, and if I miswrite the terrace someone eats the wrong pipe and spends a day offended at a flavor they cannot blame. Which door was rehung. Whose cistern sounded honest. I write small. I have seen what the sun does to a schedule posted in pride, and I post low, in the damp band, where the chalk holds and the film tries to eat the letters and I scrape a margin each evening the way other people scrape their forearms. The letters last. The margin is kiln-fodder. Even my corrections enter the work.

The change does not announce itself. Outsiders would notice it before I do, but outsiders are mostly the tins, and the tins do not stay. What I notice is the walk. Disputes find me because I am already on the stairs and because I will not spend air taking a side before the third pass. They pace me the length of a lane: water-right stolen in chalk, a shift posted to favor one landing, a slight that grew a shell in the dark the way the cistern-things do. I do not judge. I do not have that office, and I do not want it. I walk. By the third pass the story that returns to the landing is smaller than the story that left it, and the smaller story is the one both of them can carry. Sometimes that is justice and sometimes it is only the thin air doing the billing, and I have stopped needing those to be different. When it is truly about a surface, I take them to the re-leveler and I hold the rod and I do not trust my feet, and this, more than the slates, is how I pay for the room.

I sleep against the south face of the spur, where the facing-stone still ticks after dark. The first months I sleep through it. Then I wake when the pitch changes—a higher tick, a pause, a slower one, as if the rock had turned over. I lie with my palm on the course and I do not call it intention. They are strict about that word on the rim, and rightly; a person can make a spirit out of anything he is too tired to measure. What I call it is schedule. The stone is on a shift I was not given and have started to hear. In the morning I write the change in the margin of the slate, under the signals, in a hand only I re-read. Most days it matches nothing. Some days a re-leveler, passing, glances at the margin and alters a stake by a hand's width and does not thank me, which is correct. Thanks would make it a favor. It is a sounding.

They ask me, once, at the evening meal, what I was before the rail. I open my mouth and the air taxes the answer and I find I do not want to pay. What I was was a voice that carried farther than an arm's length and thought that was a kindness. What I am is the distance the voice no longer crosses. I say the name of the terrace where the cake was scraped, and they accept this as an origin, which it is, by their rules. A person is a location that has learned to walk.

There is a night—the rare rain, the hour of silver, the cuts too shallow to find by morning—when I am on the spur because the arm has no hour assigned to weather and someone must see whether it needs to mean anything. It does not. The basin glitters and goes out. I stay. The ground under the newer stakes rings, thin and high, a bowl not fired all the way, and my level, set on the rail, takes a tremble that is not lean and not wind. Far below, a vent eases its long breath. I match mine to it without deciding to. The pause at the top lengthens until it is not mine. In that pause I understand the office more cleanly than the re-leveler would have wasted the words to explain: I am not here to stop the floor from arguing. I am here to write down, before the sun and the film and the rise of things, what the argument was this hour. Tomorrow the argument will have moved, and the movement will not be damage, and someone must have the short name for it so the long name can go on being stone.

Near the rim, where the city thins into stakes stacked in the shade, I have started to leave a blank slate in a crack that will not be a crack for long. No message. A test. If the face comes up plane, the way the newest faces do, it will close on the slate and carry it into the course the way a wall carries a brick that used to be a person. I will not be that brick. I am not dead, and I have not yet earned a course. But I like the idea that a blankness I chose might be taken up into the making and mistaken, by some later keeper, for a signal the arm failed to send. They will walk it down through the terraces. They will read it at arm's length. They will find it empty, and they will have to decide whether empty is water, or flock, or the honest sounding of a cistern, or only the hour in which nothing needed to cross the air.

I know what I would have them write.

Nothing moved that could not be borne. The floor held its quarrel without winning it. The cake tasted of the terrace it claimed to be. And in the margin, smaller than the rest, the tick of the south face, which changed, and which I heard, and which was not a spirit, and which was not mine, and which I answered anyway, by being awake, and by putting my hand on it, and by giving the morning a name the morning could afford.
