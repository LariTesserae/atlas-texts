I am not standing on the rim. That is the first thing that corrects itself. The rim is where a body goes to cure poles and to be briefly colder in the wind than in the water, and I do use it, but I do not live at the height of looking down. Looking down is how the bowl becomes a picture. I am under the picture.

At first I think I am still the one who came up the notch. Wet past the waist, the white thread pulling at the backs of the knees, the swifts already having told on me. Someone poles out without hurrying. I cannot read the causeway. I am rafted. I am given a lintel-knock to learn, a place on a stem-floor, fish if there is fish. That version of me lasts a little while in the telling and then fails, the way a fire fails when it is asked to last. I know the braid under the skin. My foot finds the pale track with the eyes closed and does not treat this as a lesson. Lessons are for the season when the hands still cramp. Mine do not cramp. They go white and I keep them moving, and the white leaves, not as a triumph, only as what happens if you do not stop.

So I am one of them. That settles, and then it doesn't, because thirty is a count made from the rim in the hour when the crowns show clear, and I am not always under a crown when the count would be made. I am out. The open water, the unbuilt lot, the square that is only water. Ribs in the cold. Pole at the wasteful angle that is not wasteful. I am looking for the sag in a willow causeway, the place where trust has been walked into the braid so long that the old gray withes have given up their spring. I tie in the new ones beside them. I do not pull the old. The old stay load-bearing, slick, argued-with by weather and then left alone. My work is the tie, not the replacement. If you asked me what I am doing I would say that. I would not say I am keeping the town.

The column I rise into is the one under the swifts. Not because I chose the birds. Because the knock I make on the root lintel is the knock that house already knew, or the house learned it so early that the difference is gone. Inside, the fall becomes pressure. The leaf-lining transpires its faint sweetness. My raft-floor drinks a little; I know the sound it makes when I shift, a softer slap than the others, and I have stopped wanting a stiffer floor. I wanted it once. I was the younger one. I said something about a lining that would not have to be cobbled, and about fire. An old mouth listened. Nobody forbade me. I poled a stone sled to the middle and fed it dried crowns and watched the ash lie on the water like a decision the tarn would not swallow. I scraped it off before it sank onto a road. After that I came back to the bone awl. I do not speak of it. It has not been a year in the way lower country counts years. It has been enough recrownings that the want has thinned into a story I no longer tell even inside the pocket.

My body is what the posture makes. Long through the trunk, short in the leg, shoulders wired to the pole. Palms thick. Between the fingers the skin is thin enough that the cold lives there permanently, a pale seam, not webbing, not a gift, just the place wear forgot to thicken. I am not the reed-brown of the ones who stay in the lanes all day, and not yet the leaf-underside of the ones who only mend. Gray-olive, stone under the splash line. Vein-map at the temples when I have been in the room too long and then go out; the tarn puts the map away again. Hair pitched and braided tight. A copper needle through the braid, green, doing the work of a pin. It is not mine. None of the copper is mine. There are three pieces, or four, and they rest wherever a lining must be weighted true, and I am often the hands they pass through, because I am often the one cobbling when the split is found at dusk. People call me by the tower under the swifts, or by the west causeway if that is where the sag is. My name is short and mostly unnecessary. I answer to the knock.

What I did not expect, letting this become itself, is the night work, and that it is not heroic and not assigned. When warm rain has been on the stone above, the thread at the southern lip thickens. Someone has to see which causeway the extra pull is teasing. The someone is me more often than the count from the rim would suggest, because my sleep is thin. The lining dulls the fall to a pressure and my body treats pressure as a question. I pole out while the columns are black pipes with a warmer black at the waterline. I do not imagine stopping the water. I have never heard anyone imagine that, and when I try to imagine it myself the thought has nowhere to stand. I only look. I mark, by walking it, the braid that will need the morning first. The fish hold in a thicker shade. The newts go on counting the mat in their own unhurried way. I do not eat them. I do not need the reason recited to me anymore. A year they thinned, the roads tried to close; I was small and was set on those tracks with my eyes closed twice as often, and the mat was the teacher that did not grow bored. I still walk that way when I cross at night. Eyes open is for other people, and for daytime, and for the trout I am trying not to be.

The trout are the nearest thing to a private life I have, and they are not private and not mine. I sit in the shade of my own house until they forget me. They forget. That is the skill: to become the temperature and the stillness they already intended to hold beside. I take one when the day wants one. Bone hook, drifted, no rush. If I take two I feel the day looking at me, not as a god, as a shortage. The small fish that move like weather I scoop when the basin is loud and I am hungry in the ordinary way, silver draining through my fingers back into the weave. I eat with my hands. There is no other clean way, and clean is not the point.

A stranger still comes sometimes, up the thread, announced by the swifts or by the wrongness in the shout-carry. I am not always the one who poles out. When I am, I do not hurry, and I ask whether they can read a causeway. If they cannot, I raft them. I teach the lintel-knock of whatever column has room on its stem-floor. I do not teach the plan. The plan is not a story. It is which tracks stay pale, and that is the mat's difference, learned by feet or not learned. I used to think this was unkind. That was the same season as the fire. Now I think it is the only accuracy available. A map would be a kind of improvement. The old do not forbid improvements. They wait, and the basin finishes the argument. I have become someone who can wait with them, even while my sleep stays thin and my hands stay cold and my floor goes on drinking a little.

If I am anything beyond a mender of linings and a finder of braid, it is this: I am a road that has been walked often enough to stay open, temporarily wearing a body so the walking can continue. That is too neat, and I feel it try to become a single image, so I let it go. I am not the road. The road is under me, gray and slick and not grateful. I am the one who will be on it again when the light returns, first, because the thread thickened, and after that because crossing is how the day starts, and after that because a basket on the far side needs a rim that is still half alive. The fall will be going. The overflow will be going. I will not describe myself as what makes that possible. I will tie the new withe in beside the old, and I will not pull the old, and the crowns will show clear whenever the rim bothers to count, and I may or may not be among the thirty, depending on whether I have gone under yet.

When I am under, the room rocks with the tarn's breathing. The sweetness moves. I cobble the split before I discuss it. Someone in the dark of the column says where the sheet of melt is coming harder. I listen. My needle is in my braid, green as the water, ordinary, ready to be asked for if a weight is needed and this house is where the work is. Outside, the notch does not intend to stop. Neither do I, though I will, in the ordinary way, and the knock I make will be learned by the house as a variation it can absorb, or not learned, and the mat will not care which, so long as the pale tracks are walked by someone whose fingers can still tell them from the rest of the living floor.
