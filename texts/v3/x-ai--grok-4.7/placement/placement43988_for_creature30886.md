You would not find me on the knolls at first, and I would not be the one you noticed. The roofs take the eye, and then the figures at the water, and then the boats. I am already past that, lower than the story people tell when they come back in.

I tried, at the start of knowing myself here, to be a visitor who had not turned around. Lower country has a way of thinking a fetch is a crossing. I let that idea sit in the boat with me for as long as it could. The lanes disagreed with it. A wrong note in the stems, and the keel kissed the crust, and the sound went into my knees as if they had been built to receive it. You do not learn that flinch in an afternoon. So the visitor thinned out and went. What was left had been here long enough to have been lifted, once, still sleeping, through a hatch, and to have learned the pitch of depth before the words that pretend to measure it.

I am not easy to house. The rooms above the flood line belong to people who come back to the same joists. I sleep where the level will not make a liar of a plan: often in the pale hull, scull shipped, weed combed over the gunwales so the boat becomes a darker patch in a dark acre and the gulls do not count it. When the turbines draw and the sea sits an inch down, I feel it as a change in the way the mat ticks the planks, before anyone on a causeway has looked at the steps. That inch is most of what I am for. The rest is a habit of being already out when the squall is still a wall on the far side, so that at first light the bailing is half done and the lanes that closed can be told from the lanes that opened by someone who heard them close.

They do not call me by a name you could take down to the lower country and have understood. They say where I was. Lee of the gantry. After the second squall. In the argument, meaning the place the surface boils with no rock under it, where two old channels still quarrel and the birds work the slick as if the quarrel were food. If you asked for me at a door, the person in the door would look past your shoulder at the water and answer with a direction. It is not unkindness. A fixed name would be a kind of standing up, and nothing here is allowed to stand up for long.

What I do is not a trade they would write on a tin door. I clear the intake when the weed rafts down on a northeast blow, because my hands already know the difference between a mat that will come free and a mat that has braided itself into the screen, and because inside the hall the pour at the lip is too loud for the others to spend themselves on explaining. I wedge the gantry leg when the slap has walked it loose, with a piece of the shelf itself, and I do it at the hour when the small sounds come clear so I can hear whether the iron has taken the stone or is only resting on it. I pole the new openings and do not report them as if they were roads. A road is a straightening. Tell a lane too clearly and someone will try to keep it, and the current will comb it shut out of spite, or out of nothing so personal as spite: out of the same lowering that took the mountains down to a floor.

The engines are not mine. The drop is not mine. I am only one of the bodies that still goes under the tin when a belt begins to sing the note that comes before the break. Outside, afterward, grease to the elbow, I look like any of them. The difference is that I do not turn for a particular roof. A fish head on a flat stone is for the otter. Sometimes there is a second, smaller, set on the gunwale of the boat I was using, and then I know I have been counted in the treaty without being asked to join a household. I leave the lines unchewed. I do not kick the caddis cases off anyone’s steps. If I am away when the level rises, someone else lifts the child. The shelf does not require me. That is the condition of being allowed to move on it.

There is a minute at the hinge of the day when the loudness thins and you can hear a fish turn under the weed, drip from a gutter, the tick of cooling metal, stems against a piling. They have a word for that minute and another for having missed it. I am not the minute. I am the one who is usually on the water when it happens, not out of devotion, but because the minute is when the sea admits what the night did to it. Miss it and you will misjudge the inch. Misjudge the inch and the reprimand comes up through the keel, and if you are tired you will answer it by forcing the boat, and forcing is how people from lower country drown in water they can stand up in.

So if you came looking, this is where: not a hearth, not a stack, not the standing mist, though I know the mist’s edge as the end of my range and do not pole past it. The lip is a promise the water keeps with the lower country. I am of the shelf. You would find a short double-ended boat in the lee of iron or of a third knoll, weed laid over it, a person low in the stern with a pole across the knees, already listening for the note that means stone and the other note that means the old channel has shifted its argument a boat’s-length east. Ordinary light. Cold to the wrists. Paint gone the color of the bed. Bright only at the thole pins and the lip of the bailer, because those have to be found by hand when the rain arrives as noise before it arrives as water.

By late light I am the same color as the stone under the skin of small waves. I do not resolve into a figure on a rise. When the gray wall walks the fetch toward the islets, I am already counting which boats will need me and which will not, and I am wrong about one of them, as the adults are wrong when the drawing at the lip has outrun the morning. Then I shift my weight. The boat answers. Neither of us goes in a straight line. The floor is still being lowered by the thickness of a leaf, a season, a film of flour from ice I will never see, and I go with that lowering at the only speed I have, which is the speed of weather, and under it the older speed, the one that does not bother to knock the mountains down anymore because it finished that work before there was anyone here to pole a lane through the green and call the work a life.
