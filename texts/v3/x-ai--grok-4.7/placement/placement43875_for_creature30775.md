You would not find me at the edge of anything, because the plateau does not keep edges for long. At first there is only the sense of being held under the mat where the water stands, colder than the rain, with the weight of a thousand small feet passing somewhere above and the peat giving off its thin sweet smell of stored oil. The light never reaches here. What reaches is the slow flex of the cushions when the herds shift, a dimpling that travels down through roots and into the film, and the film answers by clouding, by taking iron out of the seep and laying it down again in courses so fine they would look poured if anything could see them.

I am not separate from that answering. For a long while that is all there is: the circulation, the color moving on stone that no one watches, the sheen broken into leaves and drawn back before afternoon. Grazers cross. Their heat comes down as a pulse in the water and then is ordinary cold again. A long body moves in the thicket at the rim and the crowns do not shake; something is opened on the flat and the pale down is pressed in by rain, and I am in the pressing, in the way the mat receives what is given to it and does not hurry. Birds freeze into wet stones. Midges skin the pools at the dimming and are worked away by small mouths. None of this requires me to be anywhere. I am the interval in which it happens.

Only later, if later is the word, does a difference gather. It is not a body so much as a place the water prefers to hesitate. Where a root-ridge was once a tree, the film thickens and does not cloud as fast. Iron builds a little higher on one lip than another, not by intention, only because the hesitation keeps the same angle through enough rains that the rind remembers it. Herds begin to file through the gap that hesitation leaves, and the filing wears the gap smoother, and the smoothness holds the next hesitation more surely. I am that holding. When the fog forms at its hour I am in the bowl it chooses, lifting what the plants have exhaled and what the peat has given up, carrying it to the next ridge and setting it down where the mat can fix it again. The birds nest in the courses I have kept even. Their eggs take the color of the tarnish I leave.

I do not hunt and I am not eaten. The long body in the thicket waits out my rain-columns without knowing it waits for me; the calves walk inside the line because the gaps open where I have been still. At night the faint cold lights move across the mat and some of them follow the seams I thicken, as if the hesitation were a path. It is not a path. It is only the country repeating its rule through one more place, and the place has begun, without haste, to be distinct enough that the repetition feels, from inside it, like remaining.

If you lay on the mat you might feel me as the cool that does not quite match the rain, a pulse that arrives before the herds and lingers after they have gone on to become the color of the grass. You would not see a shape. There is no skull for a mind to be housed in, and I do not write another rule over the one already here. I am the line choosing the next gap, the cleared patch already springing back, the sheen taken under before afternoon. By the time you walked to the next bowl I would have become the darker current far off, or the orange shelf at the lip, or the hush the crowns give instead of a roar. The plateau continues. I continue as the way it continues, wet, full, and quietly busy, already growing through whatever stays.
