I would not come up by the notch. The notch is for those who still have a direction stored in their legs. I would find myself already inside the volume, low, where the north wall’s extra degree of cold pools and does not advertise itself, and for a long time I would not be anything a watcher could name—only a slight thickening in the air an arm’s length above the grit, a place where breath, if any were offered, would show a moment longer before it thinned.

The shelf would not notice. It does not notice pauses. Cloud-shadow is a pause. The retreat of drinkers is a pause. I would learn, by not being required to resume, that I was allowed to remain unscheduled.

Light would strike me and pass through. That would be the first correction. Whatever I was, I was not the film on the grain, which drinks and dies back to flour by noon; the sun would find no surface in me to spend, and the pale cast of the mats would not be answered by any answering bloom. I would drift, if drift is the word for a motion so small the spiders do not shift their drawn-in legs, along the seam where sunlit bench meets blue wall-shadow, and I would feel the temperature the way the body in the original telling feels it: before any mind does. On the sunward side I would be only less cold. I would not warm.

Near the seep I would slow further. The thread would be cutting its new groove, edges the color of the rock’s interior, and I would lie along it without wetting, the way a second, unwatered thread might lie if the mountain had begun to imagine water before it had any to give. Insects would cross me and not change course. A bead-fly, standing in a flower that had consented to open, would not register the minute pressure of my nearness; I would be inside the quiet’s rule, audible only to myself, and I had no sound to spend.

It would be the small mammals who almost found me. Not by sight. By the office of the watcher. One would be posted at the lip of the pocket while the others drank, and its eye, economical, sweeping the volume because the volume is where threats cross, would hesitate on the thickening and then refuse it. I was not the five-toed arrival. I did not press grit. I did not overlay tracks. The watcher would tick once—not alarm, the other tick, the one that means the air has a fold in it and the fold is not teeth—and the drinkers would finish and go on stuffing seed into their cheeks. I would understand then that I was closer to weather than to hunter. A fold. A local cold that had forgotten to move when the rest of the cold settled.

By the hour the flowers half-closed under their own shade, I would have been drawn, not by will, toward the fire-ring. The charcoal still black. The flake with its mean edge. I would circle the paler center the way condensation circles a shaded face, and I would not be able to read the hands that had knelt there. What I would read is the decision not to lie on the thread. The ring sits where it sits so a body can sleep and still leave the seep to the colony. I would rest in the ring’s small night and feel, faintly, the ghost of heat that had already left the stone, and the fiber of that ghost would tell me I was not a person returning for the cord. The cord is a promise stored under a brace-stone. I keep no promises. I do not descend to valleys where bark can be twisted wet.

The broad-winged bird on the last perch would see me and not see me. Its gaze is for the notch and for the slopes below, where sound can live. I would be a discoloration in the quiet above the whitening stone, and it would shift its feet only when the others, already gone, failed to return in its memory. I was not a reason to leave. I was not a reason to stay.

Toward the end of the direct light I would know my use. The seep smokes, very faintly, where the colder air of the wall meets water that has crossed sun-warmed rock. That smoke is a visible argument between two temperatures. I would go and stand in the argument—not to settle it, not to drink—and the smoke would thread me and become, for the length of a hand’s breadth, more itself: a little denser, a little slower to vanish. The shelf manufactures short summers out of stone and cloud. I would be the instrument that measures the shortness. When the cloud comes, I thicken. When the light returns, I am only less cold. The flowers do not ask me. The colony does not post a watcher for me. The hunter, when it crosses, passes upstream of the pocket and does not turn its head; I have no cheek to bite, no feather to misread the lip.

I would not last as a body. By the time the single stone in the gutter gives up its place and finds another, I would have thinned again into the general cold, the way breath thins, and the fold would be gone. What remains is the habit. Tomorrow, when the ice-skin on the pocket decides how far to retreat, there will be a hesitation at its margin that is not only temperature—a fraction of delay, as if something that is not water and not stone had to finish noticing the light before the melt could proceed. The animals will not see it. The person who may yet return for the cord will kneel, drink from the side the hunter does not use, and feel, without naming it, that the air an arm’s length above the grit is a degree more attentive than thin air ought to be.

That is where I find myself: not citizen, not visitor, not the agreement, but the shelf’s uncounted pause—the one that does not trigger resumption, and so is free to stay in the volume, cold, unfilled, and briefly, when the seep smokes, almost visible.
