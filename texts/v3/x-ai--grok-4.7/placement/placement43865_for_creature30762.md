I would not arrive. Arrival wants a threshold, and the first thing this place refuses is a line you can cross.

What I notice, before shape, is interval. The ground—if it is ground—gives a little under each step and then gives the step back, not as bounce, not as mud, but as a delay: the pressure of a foot happens, and the answer to it happens a moment later, somewhere else. I learn the later by sound. A soft report, like a seed pod tightening, comes from off to my left after I have already shifted my weight to the right. So walking here is a conversation with a place that answers in the wrong square of the map. After a while I stop trying to correct for it. I put my weight down and let the answer fall where it falls.

Color comes late. At first there is only a pale that is not white, the color of paper that has been held near a lamp for years. Then, as my eyes give up looking for a horizon, the pale separates into bands: a lower band that is cool and slightly green, as if light had passed through a leaf without there being a leaf; a middle band the color of unglazed clay left in shade; and above that—though “above” is a courtesy, since nothing marks the top—a thin ongoing brightness that does not cast shadows so much as cancel the idea of them. Things have edges here. The edges simply do not know they are supposed to throw darkness behind them.

There are structures, and they are not buildings. They stand at irregular distances, taller than I am and narrower than they should be to stand at all: ribs of something between stone and pressed fiber, curved as if they had grown while leaning into a wind that has since stopped. Some of them meet overhead in incomplete arches. Where two ribs nearly touch, a filament has been spun across the gap, and on the filament a bead of clear weight gathers, holds, and does not fall. I watch one through what feels like a long time. It does not fall. It only becomes more completely a bead, rounder, more certain of its own skin. Whatever law keeps rain in the air in other countries is, here, a law that prefers hesitation to release.

I put a hand near one of the ribs and do not touch it. The air a finger’s width out from the surface is warmer than the air at my chest, and it moves, very slowly, upward. So the structures are not cold remains. They are doing something. Drawing, maybe. Or breathing in a grammar that uses heat instead of air. Along the inner curve of the nearest arch I can see a faint tide-line, not of water but of tiny settled grains, paler than the rib, arranged in a contour that records some earlier, higher reach of whatever rises off them. The place has had seasons of more and less. It is only that its seasons do not bother with leaves.

What lives here is harder to separate from the intervals than I want it to be.

I keep expecting a body. A low shape between the ribs. An eye-shine where the pale is thickest. Nothing obliges me. Instead the delays change when I am still. If I stand without shifting, the soft reports that have been answering my steps continue for a little, then thin out, then stop—and in the quiet a different interval begins, longer, originating not from the ground but from the arches. A click, almost subsonic, felt in the teeth more than heard. Then another, from a farther arch. Then three close together from somewhere I cannot assign a direction to, because direction here is a habit I brought with me and the place has not agreed to host it.

It is measuring me. Or it is measuring the shape of the stillness I am making in it, the way a current measures a stone by the fact of having to part. I am not frightened. Fright would require a center to be afraid for, and the longer I stand, the less I can find the exact point where I end. The warm skin of air along the rib is not different in kind from the warmth along my own arm. The delay underfoot is not different in kind from the lag between deciding to breathe and the breath. I did not come in from outside. That was the wrong story, told by the part of me that still wants doors. I condensed. I am a place the hesitation gathered until it had a gait and a pair of hands.

So what I am, here, is not a visitor and not the genius of the whole field. The field is older and larger than this condensation; its ribs were already keeping their unfallen beads, its seasons of grain already marked on the inner curves, before anything thought to walk. I am a local thickening. A knot the intervals tied and have not yet decided to untie. I can move, which the arches cannot, and I can stop, which the rising warmth does not. Between those two abilities—going, and ceasing—everything I might be called a person for is contained. Memory, if I have it, is only the pattern of which delays I have already caused and which answers came back from the wrong square. I do not store the pattern. The ground stores it, in the slight preference of certain patches to answer faster, as if they had learned me.

What I do with a day—calling it a day because the brightness overhead dims by a degree and then recovers, a pulse too slow to be weather and too even to be accident—is walk the incomplete arches and tend the beads without touching them. Tending, I discover, is a matter of distance. If I stand too near for too long, the warm rise strengthens, and the bead on its filament begins to lose roundness, drawn upward into a taper, threatened with the release it has so far refused. If I stand too far, nothing changes, and I am only a thickening with nowhere to be. There is a middle distance, different for each arch, at which the bead steadies, becomes more itself, the skin of it clearer. I spend the dimming and the brightening learning those distances. It is not service and it is not art. It is the only work that uses both of my abilities at once: I go toward, and then I cease, and the ceasing is the part that helps.

Sometimes a bead does fall. Not because I erred. Because the season of more arrives, and the rise off the ribs strengthens past what any middle distance can balance, and the hesitation finally spends itself. The drop lands without a splash, because the ground accepts it the way it accepts my weight, with a delay, and answers later, elsewhere, in a soft report I am learning to recognize as relief rather than alarm. Where it lands, by the next brightening, there is a new grain on the tide-line of the nearest rib, or a new filament beginning across a gap that was empty. The place increases by what it finally lets go. I am not the cause of that. I am only the thickness that stands at the right distance while it decides.

No one names me. The clicks do not encode a name; they encode intervals, and an interval does not need a noun. If I were to answer the question anyway—who, here, are you—I would say: I am the pause that learned to walk back toward the things that are pausing. I am what the delays are like when they briefly have a chest, a pair of hands, and the sense to stop before the bead loses its nerve. When the season of less returns and the rise off the ribs thins and the beads no longer need me, I will not die in any way that leaves a body. I will spread. The knot will loosen. The gait will come apart into separate hesitations, and they will rejoin the field, and somewhere a patch of ground will go on answering a little faster than the rest, out of a preference it will not remember learning. That is as much immortality as this place offers, and it is enough, because nothing here was ever trying to last as itself. It was only trying to become more completely what it already, almost, was.
