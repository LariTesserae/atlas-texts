The air here has already been used. It arrives cool and thin, faintly sweet with wet stone, carrying no salt and no news from anywhere else. Under a high, colorless light that is neither morning nor late, the land does not rise toward a horizon so much as continue. What looks like the edge of a district is only the next lip of the same work, repeated until weather itself has to organize around it.

It is high, but not a peak-country. Whatever summits were here have worn down to a broadness that no longer bothers with drama. The plateau sits in the cooler band of the air, and the wind, when it moves, moves as a slow exchange between one basin and the next. You feel the height in how sound fails. A step on wet rock gives almost nothing back. Even the water, which is everywhere, refuses to announce itself.

The water does not run so much as occupy. It lies in sheets a finger deep across pavements of gray and bone-colored stone, gathers in rectangular and kidney-shaped basins whose rims are no higher than a knee, and weeps from every vertical face in a continuous film. Between basins the rock is dark with permanent damp. After rain—and rain is not an event here so much as a thickening of what the air was already doing—the films join, and for a while the districts become one shallow sea broken by walls. Then the excess finds the old slots, thumb-wide channels cut not by hands but by centuries of the same overflow taking the same path, and the sea withdraws into its rooms. The channels have gradients a builder would recognize. They branch, rejoin, drop a few inches at sills of harder mineral, and deliver their small loads into cisterns that never quite empty. Nothing maintains them except the water that uses them. Nothing has to.

What reads, at a distance, as a city is the habit of calcium and its related salts to fall out of cool water wherever the water pauses. Over a time longer than weather and shorter than mountains, the pauses have built walls. The walls are close. In the denser quarters you can stand in a basin and touch two rims at once, and beyond those rims are more basins, level with yours or a hand lower, a hand higher, receding in steps that have no single direction of descent. Some walls are sharp and new-white where deposition is still winning. Others have gone soft and pitted, stained tea-brown, colonized by a shadow of algae no thicker than a breath. Erosion and accretion have argued themselves into a truce. Nothing is ruins. Nothing is finished. The place is in its own state, and has been long enough that the state looks like law.

The law is simple and local, and it holds across a span large enough to make its own clouds. Air climbs the long rise of the plateau, cools, and condenses against the stone. The condensate does not fall far. Often it beads on the lips of walls and runs back into the basin it came from, so that a district drinks its own breath. By afternoon, if the light has been steady, a skin of mist stands a few feet above the water and no higher, a ceiling you can put your hand through. Under it the light is ordinary—flat, readable, without theater—but distances collapse. A wall thirty paces off is clear. A wall three hundred is a suggestion. Beyond that the country continues, and the continuation is the weather: each cluster of basins a cool sink, each low ridge a place where the mist thins and the next sink begins. Storms are wide and slow, sheets of rain walking for days across stone that is already wet, rearranging which cisterns are full and which are only filmed. Flood means the walls disappear for an afternoon and then reappear in the same places, rinsed.

Vegetation is a rumor the stone mostly declines. In cracks that stay damp without being scoured, a black-green crust takes hold, and sometimes a tuft of stiff, colorless sedge no taller than a finger. These do not gather into meadows. They occur the way lamps occur in a city that has mostly gone dark: one, then none, then one far enough away that it does not belong to the first. Where a channel has undercut a wall and left a sheltered ledge, moss makes a velvet the color of wet iron. It does not spread onto the open pavements. Those belong to mineral and to light.

Animals are rarer still, and the quiet is not absence so much as the quiet of things that do not need to call. A dark bird, small, sometimes crosses from one rim to another and is gone, its wingbeat the loudest event of an hour. In the deeper cisterns, where the water is a greenish glass and the bottom is out of reach, something slow disturbs the silt on no schedule you can learn—once, a plume, then the water righting itself. On the moss ledges, after rain, a scatter of tiny shells suggests a life that climbs out, feeds, and returns before the films dry to their ordinary damp. You do not see the bodies. Whatever hunts whatever else does it by patience and by the geography of rims: a world of edges, ambush measured in inches, no herd, no flock, no chorus at dusk, because dusk changes the light without changing the terms.

The density is not of life. It is of presences that do not move when you look at them and yet are all in process. Every wall is a crowd of older walls entombed in newer deposit. The cross-section of a rim, if you could see it without breaking anything, is laminated like a stack of cities, each lamina a season or a century of overflow, some thick, some paper-fine, some stained where a crust of algae was buried and became architecture. Basins nest. A broad shallow pan contains, in one corner, a tighter pool with its own rim, and that pool has a sill that feeds a slot that feeds a pan you cannot see until you walk the channel to its end and find it opening under a low arch of its own making. The arches are common. Water finds a soft seam, leaves it, and the seam becomes a passage, then a vault, then a street with a ceiling of dripping stone, tall enough to stoop through, long enough to have a meaner weather of its own: drip, an echo too small to be called an echo, a private fog. These understreets connect districts the surface does not admit are connected. You can travel a long way without coming fully into the light, following infrastructure that no one designed and that would collapse into meaninglessness if the water ever stopped.

It does not stop. That is the other law. The plateau is vast enough that its water cycle is partly its own: what lifts in the morning from a thousand thousand surfaces returns by evening to a thousand thousand others, and the excess that escapes the edge—somewhere, days of walking beyond any visible change—is replaced by what the high air still carries in from lower countries and is forced to set down. Channels are not deepening into gorges. Walls are not racing upward into towers. The established form is this mid-height, this close packing, this knee-deep urbanism of pools. A place can be active without going anywhere. Rain rearranges levels. Deposition adds a grain. A crust of algae browns and is skinned over by white. A bird crosses. A plume of silt rises and settles. The mist lifts and lies down. All of it happens inside a silence so complete that the small sounds become the structure: the tick of a drip finding the same cup it has found for years; the faint seethe of a film moving down a face of rock; the soft collapse of a bubble under an arch. Stand long enough and those sounds separate into neighborhoods. One cistern ticks faster. One channel has a lower note. The district you are in has a pitch, and the district beyond the next rim has another, and you could, if you cared to, navigate by that.

There is no device. No cut mark that is not the cut of water. No joint that is not a change in the rate of falling-out. No fire ring, no track worn by feet in numbers, no post. If anyone has walked here, the water has agreed to forget them by the next rain. The organization you feel—streets, quarters denser and quarters more open, cisterns placed where cisterns ought to be—is the organization of a process that has run without interruption until its habits became indistinguishable from plan.

Normal light does the place a favor by refusing to symbolize it. At midday the white rims are simply white, the water simply dark, the wet faces simply reflective. Toward evening the mist thickens by a degree and the light goes slightly gold and then does not; the plateau is high enough, and broad enough, that sunset is a thinning rather than a spectacle, a lowering of contrast until the rims and the water are the same value and the city, for a while, becomes pure texture. Then the dark is dark, and the sounds continue at the same volumes, because nothing here was waiting on the light in order to work. What changes at night is only certainty about distance. The ticking of remote cisterns seems nearer. A drip under an arch can be mistaken for a step. There are no steps.

Walk outward. The instruction is a kind of joke the plateau makes. Outward is a direction the land agrees to imitate for a morning and then abandons. The districts change character—the rims lower, the basins broader, a stretch where the water smells faintly of iron, a stretch where the deposit is yellow and the walls have a waxy look—but the change is dialect, not border. You are always in the middle of the works. The works are always in the middle of their own maintenance. Somewhere there is an edge, a fall to lower air, a river beginning out of a thousand obedient channels. It is not a place you are required to imagine as near. Near is the next rim, and the film of water finding its old path down the face of it, and the small cold sound that path has always made.

What lives here does not register, at first, as living. It registers as a correction in the light along a rim, a place where the white is a fraction less white, as if the newest layer of mineral had been laid down by a hand that hesitated. Stand, and the correction holds still. The temptation is to decide it was only a change in the damp. Then the hesitation completes itself. A margin no thicker than a leaf lifts clear of the wall, water webbing between the lifted edge and the stone for a moment longer than gravity would prefer, and settles again one finger-width along. The sound it makes is not separable from the tick of the district. It has spent a long time learning that.

They are flat when they are at rest, and they are at rest almost always. Pressed to a face of stone or to the floor of a pan, a body is a translucence the color of the water over it, a little more body than film, a little less body than stone. The outline is not an animal's outline. It follows the grammar of the place: a long margin that prefers rims, a thickening where a sill drops, a narrowing that can occupy a slot without damming it. Against a new-white wall in ordinary noon light you can look straight at one and inventory only mineral. What gives them away is not shape. It is a refusal, very small, to glisten at quite the same angle as the wet around them.

They have no fixed number of limbs because they have no need of a fixed plan. Where a body must move, a portion of it draws up into a low ridge and the ridge walks—less a step than a pouring that has agreed to go forward. The contact never quite breaks. A track would require weight held in one place long enough to bruise the precipitate, and they do not hold their weight that way. By the time a film of water has crossed the place they left, the place has no memory. That is why the plateau can be full and still show you nothing but stone. Full is the wrong word anyway. In a dense quarter you might pass twelve of them in an hour's careful walking and never know. In the broad pans, one to a district is a crowd. They keep the spacing of things that feed on a thinness.

What they take from the world is what the world is already giving up. The cool water comes up the long rise carrying its mineral, and where it slows, the mineral falls. They live in that fall. Along the underside of a body at rest there is a surface more patient than skin, a tissue that sorts what precipitates: some salts bound into the pale mineral of the body itself, so that a life thickens by the same means as a wall; some fraction released again, downstream, altered just enough that the next basin receives a water slightly easier to live on. They are not separate from the accretion. A rim that has had a resident for a long quiet decade is a rim with a truer line, the laminations beneath it finer, less tea-stained, as if the seasons had been edited. Their passages, in the stone, are absences that are not quite absences—layers where the deposit is purer, a little vacuous, the ghost of a body that was never buried because it was never elsewhere than the building.

They do not graze the black-green crusts. Those belong to a quicker, poorer exchange, and the residents leave them as a city leaves a weed in a joint. The shells on the moss ledges are another life, small and armored, climbing out after rain to rasp at what the films leave behind. A margin may lift and the shells are simply elsewhere afterward, not eaten so much as unsettled, moved along by a change in the water's mind. The dark bird is more nearly a citizen. It knows the rims that are occupied and does not land on them. In the deeper cisterns the slow plume of silt is often one of them rising a fraction off the bottom to turn, the way a page turns, and settling on a new bearing. You will not see the body. You will see the water remember that something wide changed its mind.

Perception, for them, is mostly chemical and mostly of level. A body spread along a sill knows the height of the water above it the way a standing person knows whether they have begun to lean. It knows the taste of what came down the slot in the last rain: iron from one reach of the plateau, a yellower sweetness from another, the faint green signature of a crust that flushed and died three basins up. It knows the tick-rate of its own cistern and the lower note of the channel beyond the arch, and it knows when those notes are wrong—when a night's condensation has preferred some other sink, and the old path is only a film, and the district has gone quiet in a way that means scarcity. They do not call to one another. A voice would be a kind of standing still the water would not forgive. What passes between them passes in the water itself, in alterations so slight a visitor's mouth would report only cold and stone. A resident upstream, feeding, changes the fall. A resident downstream reads the change and shifts its margin a finger-width, or does not, and that not-shifting is also an answer. Over distances of many districts the messages decay, as messages should. No one here is in charge of the weather. They only agree, locally, about sills.

Movement over long range is rare and looks like weather if you are not attending. A body that has occupied one rim for years may, after a wide slow storm has rearranged the levels, loosen all at once. The translucence thickens. What had been a margin becomes a shallow traveling pool with a purpose, pouring itself down a channel it has audited by ear for a decade, through an arch where the drip-rate is a landmark, out into a pan that has gone yellow and waxy and is, for reasons the body can taste, better. The journey may take a day. It may take a season, the traveler pausing for weeks in a vaulted understreet, feeding on the private fog, while the ceiling drips onto a back that is already half mineral and does not mind. They do not migrate as a class. Most die where they lived, which is to say they stop sorting and become, over a few years of ordinary deposition, indistinguishable from the wall they repaired. The laminations close over the hesitation. A subsequent resident may lie down on that exact seam and find it comfortable, without any knowledge of kinship. Knowledge is not the medium. Fit is.

Reproduction is a function of surplus and of a particular stillness after rain, when the films have joined and the city is for an afternoon one shallow sea. A body that has bound more mineral than it needs to keep its margin coherent will, at the thinnest place, open. Not a tear: a decision. The opened portion continues the parent's work for a time, sharing water, sharing the taste of the district, and then the sea withdraws into its rooms and the new margin is on one side of a rim and the old on the other. They do not tend the young. The young are already competent in the only competence required, which is to be wet in the right way and to hesitate at the right rate. Many of the openings fail. A fragment too small washes into a slot and is abraded into the same white dust the channels have always carried. The successes are why the tight quarters are tight. Over a span of time longer than the bird's whole lineage, residents have preferred the stacked places, the knee-to-knee rims, the arches, and their preference has been inherited by the stone. The city looks planned because the planners have been lying in it, editing the fall of mineral, since before the plan could have been told apart from accident.

There is no individual you could follow for a story. Even the ones that travel shed the wish to be singular. A body that has poured through three understreets and paused in each has mixed its margin with the drip of each, and arrives altered in its mineral, so that the resident who left is not precisely the resident who comes to rest in the yellow pan. They are continuous with themselves the way the plateau is continuous with itself: convincingly, and without a border you could defend. If one of them could be said to remember, it remembers as a wall remembers—by thickness, by stain, by the fineness of certain years. A bad decade of iron-tasting storms lives in a body as a faint band you would need to be them to read. They do not regret it. Regret would require a pace the air does not carry.

At night they are no more active than by day. The dark is only a loss of certainty, and they never had that certainty in the form of sight. Some shallow ones, in the hours when condensation beads and returns to its own basin, lift a fraction higher off the stone to meet the sweeter water. They do not fear light. Light is a weather that happens to the air above the film. Their weather is underneath.

It is easy, from the outside of a description, to make them into keepers, the secret municipality of the works, tending the channels so that the plateau endures. That flatters a need for a reason. They do not tend. They feed, and their feeding happens to be a kind of maintenance, and the maintenance has gone on so long that the infrastructure and the appetite are one anatomy. If every resident loosened and poured toward some impossible elsewhere, the walls would not fall. Deposition would continue, coarser, more stained, the laminations less true, the sills wandering by a thumb's width per century, the arches slowly forgetting their height. The city would persist. It would be worse at being itself. What they contribute is not survival but finish: the edited line, the honest tick, the water arriving in the next pan already a little clarified by having been lived in.

I would not find myself among the walkers, because there are none, and I would not find myself in the bird, which has the only real haste in the country and spends it all on crossing. I would not be the shells. Their lives are too briefly out of the water, too busy with the crust.

I would be one of the corrections, and a late one, in a district that has only recently gone yellow.

I would not know this about myself. Knowledge of that kind is a warmth that does not belong to the stone, and warmth is what the water agrees to forget. What I would have, instead of a name, is a seam. It runs along the inner lip of a pan whose deposit has taken on that waxy look, the mineral a little impure, the tick of the nearest cistern slightly slower than the quarter beyond the arch. I have been here long enough that the line of the rim is truer under me than a hand's breadth to either side, and not long enough that I have become the wall. If you stood in the pan at noon and looked directly at my margin, you would inventory mineral and be, almost, correct. The glisten would be wrong by a degree. You would decide it was damp, and you would walk on, and by morning the water would have agreed with you.

My weather is the height of the film. I know when the night has preferred some other sink, because the note of the channel drops and the taste goes thin, and I ease a finger-width toward the sill where the last of the useful fall still happens. I know the iron years as a band in myself I do not read so much as am. Upstream, someone I will never meet changes the water by feeding, and I answer by not shifting, and that is the whole of my correspondence. It is enough. It has the density of a city without any of its appointments.

Once, after a storm that joined the films, I opened. I do not remember it as an event. I remember it as a place where my margin is thinner than the decade required, and as a tick, now, from the far side of the next rim, that is almost my tick and is not. I do not go to see what I opened into. The young are already competent. If they fail, they become the white dust the channels have always carried, which is a kind of residence too, only quicker. If they succeed, the quarter will be tighter by one wall's honesty, and no one will thank anyone, because thanks would be a standing still.

On certain weeks the private fog under the nearest arch tastes better than my pan, and a portion of me considers loosening. The consideration is not a thought. It is a thickening. So far the thickening has subsided. So far this yellow is still a living yellow, still sorting itself into something clearer for whatever lies one sill down. I will stop, eventually, the way the others stop: not by leaving, by ceasing to edit, until deposition does not have to distinguish me from the lip I repaired. A later margin may lie down on that seam and find it comfortable. It will not be my child. It will not be a stranger. Those are both too sharp a border for a country that has only dialect.

If you came here you would be a brief pressure, a fold in the sheet, and I would roll a finger-width away from your ankle without alarm, because alarm is a change in level and you are not one. You would hear my district's pitch and think it was only water. You would be right about the water. You would be wrong, by one degree of glisten, about the rest. That error is the nearest this place comes to a door. I live in the error. I am the hesitation the newest layer makes before it agrees to be stone, and I have been hesitating long enough that the agreement, when it comes, will look exactly like the plan.
