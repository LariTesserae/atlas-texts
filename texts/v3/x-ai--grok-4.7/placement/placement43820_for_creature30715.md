You arrive by the wrong stair.

That is the only way the house accepts anyone. The right stair is a courtesy, painted a color that wants to be remembered, and it ends three steps short of a door that has never been hung. People who insist on it stand there with their hands full of nothing and eventually go back down into the weather they came from. The wrong stair is the one that looks like a service run: narrow, smelling of wet plaster and oranges, turning twice when the building has no reason to turn. Halfway up, the wall changes from brick to something thinner, and your shoulder leaves a print that fills in behind you like a sentence being revised. You do not knock. There is no bell. The landing is already a room, and the room has been expecting the weight of you the way a table expects a cup.

The place is not large, and it is not one place. That takes the longest to stop arguing with.

From the street — if you can still call it a street; the district forgot its name in a year when names were being rationed — you see a narrow house squeezed between a shuttered bathhouse and a warehouse whose windows have been bricked from the inside. Three storeys, a roof that sags toward the river, a vine that has climbed the drainpipe and then, apparently dissatisfied, climbed back down. Ordinary. A little tired. The kind of house a city uses to store the idea of houses while it decides what to build instead.

Inside, the storeys do not agree with each other.

The ground floor is a kitchen that has been a kitchen for so long the pots have opinions. A stove that burns a low blue flame whether anyone has fed it. A table scored with the maps of old conversations, knife-lines crossing one another until the wood is a harbor chart of meals. Light comes from a window that looks onto the alley and also, if you stand at the left edge of the sill, onto a field. The field is not behind the alley. The field is what the window does when it is tired of brick. Rye, or something that behaves like rye, moving in a wind that does not touch the curtains. You can open the window and put a hand into one weather or the other, not both. The house does not explain this. It has had visitors who demanded explanations, and the window simply showed them brick until they left.

A stair at the back of the kitchen goes up into a room that should be a bedroom and is instead a shore. Not a painting of a shore. The floor ends, or rather the floor becomes the idea of sand, pale and cold, and a little way out the air thickens into water without ever quite becoming a lake. You can walk into it to the knee. The water is the temperature of a wrist. There are no fish. There are shapes that move the way thoughts move when you are almost asleep — lateral, unhurried, uninterested in you. Shells on the sand are the shells of buildings. You pick one up and it is a porcelain cup, fine as an ear, with a handle. You pick up another and it is a tiny stair, spiral, leading nowhere you can follow with a finger. They are not souvenirs. If you pocket one, the shore rearranges, offended in a quiet way, and the cup is back in the sand by morning with a hairline crack that was not there before. The house allows looking. It taxes taking.

Above that — and "above" is a courtesy; sometimes you reach it by going down the kitchen stair and turning left where there is no left — there is a library of doors. Not books. Doors, stacked on shelves the way other houses stack books, each one the size of a palm, labeled in a hand that changes with the hour. Some labels you can read. *The year the river reversed.* *Kitchen, Thursdays.* *Your brother's unsent letter.* *The room where the argument ended differently.* *Salt.* Most of them you cannot read, not because the script is foreign but because the words will not hold still long enough to be a language. If you take a door down and hold it to your eye the way a child holds a keyhole, you see a portion of somewhere. A hand setting down a glass. A platform in rain. A bed with the impression of a body that has just gotten up. You cannot step through. The doors are memories the house has agreed to keep for people who could not carry them, and memories are not corridors. Opening one all the way — you try this once — produces a smell and a sound and then a soft collapse, like a tent coming down, and the door is blank afterward, a little warm, ashamed or relieved. You put it back on the shelf with both hands. The house does not scold you. It also does not return what you spent.

The being who lives here is not the house, though for the first long while you will make that mistake, and the mistake is useful.

She is in the kitchen when you need her to be in the kitchen, and nowhere when you go looking. Tall in the way a doorway is tall, not in the way a person poses. Her hair is the color of the rye in the window when the window is being a field. Her hands are ordinary and overused, a cook's hands, a mender's, the nail of the left thumb permanently stained the blue of the stove's flame, as if she pinched the fire once and it decided to stay. She wears the same gray dress whether the shore-room is in a calm or a weather, and the hem is always wet to the same line, a tide-mark she does not acknowledge. She does not introduce herself. Names, she will tell you later — not in those words — are a kind of door, and she has enough of those already.

What she does is keep.

She keeps the flame fed on nothing. She keeps the shore from becoming a flood or a drought; you watch her once, early, standing knee-deep with both palms on the surface, and the water lowers itself the way a voice lowers in a sickroom, and the shapes swim closer and then away, obedient to a courtesy you are not part of. She keeps the doors labeled, walking the shelves with a pen that writes only while she is not looking at the page. She keeps the wrong stair wrong, which is harder than it sounds: the city keeps trying to repair it, to make it match the right stair, and she goes down in the evenings and unrepairs it — a turned nail, a step made a fraction too high, a smell of oranges rubbed back into the plaster from a cloth she will not let you see.

She keeps people, too, but not the way a host keeps guests.

They come up the wrong stair carrying something they cannot put down. A sentence they should not have said, still warm. A child they did not have. A skill that has nowhere to work. A grief with no furniture left to sit in. She does not ask them to narrate it. She puts a bowl in front of them — the bowls are mismatched, one of them is certainly a shell from the shore, and the soup is always the same, a gold thickness that tastes like the last meal you were happy without noticing — and while they eat she listens to the thing they carried, not to their account of it. You can see her listening. It is a physical act. Her head tilts, the blue thumb rests on the table, and the thing slowly becomes light enough to set beside the bowl. Some of them leave it. That is the transaction, if there is one. The house acquires a new door, unlabeled at first, warming on the shelf like bread. The visitor goes back down the wrong stair lighter and also, in a way they will not enjoy, less complete. They rarely come twice. She does not take this personally. A house like this is not a habit. It is a surgery that uses soup.

You are not one of them. That is the revision that arrives late, after you have already settled into the wrong role.

You thought you were a visitor. You slept, or something like sleep, in a chair in the kitchen, and the window showed you the field all night, and the rye moved, and you dreamed of a stair you used to know in a building that has since been divided into offices. In the morning she put the gold soup in front of you and did not listen for anything you were carrying, and the not-listening was so precise you understood you had arrived empty. Empty is not the same as unburdened. Empty is a job opening.

"Eat," she said, the first word. Her voice had the wet hem in it. "Then the shore needs turning. It silted up in the night. Someone dreamed too hard two streets over, and it all came here."

So you went and turned the shore. That is the only phrase for it. You walked the sand and the sand was not sand but the residue of other people's almosts, and you raked it — there was a rake by the waterline, handle worn to the shape of a hand that is not quite yours and not quite hers — and the silt lifted in sheets and became, briefly, visible: a wedding that did not happen, a city block that was planned and then unplanned, a dog's name. You did not read them. Reading is how you drown here. You turned them back into weather and let the water take them down to the temperature of a wrist. When you came back to the kitchen your cuffs were dry and the blue of her thumb had, very faintly, appeared at the edge of your own nail.

That was the first day. The house counts days only when it feels like being a calendar.

What you are, then, is the second keeper. Not an apprentice in the proud sense. She does not teach by sequence. She teaches by leaving a task at the level of your hands and going to another floor, and the task either yields or it teaches you the shape of your ignorance, which is also a yield.

You learn the stove: it wants company more than fuel. If you sit with it and tell it nothing important — the sound of the alley, the fact of your knees — the flame steadies and the soup thickens. If you confess to it, it flares and the kitchen fills with the smell of oranges and scorched sugar, and she has to come down and soothe it with the wet hem of the dress, trailing the tide-mark along the flagstones until the fire remembers its manners.

You learn the doors: you may shelve, you may dust, you may carry a new one from the table to the stack if she has already closed it. You may not label. The pen will not write for you, and once, when you tried to force a word onto a blank door — a word from your own life, a street you miss — the letters sank into the wood like bruises and the door showed you, for a second, your own face asleep in the kitchen chair, older than you are, and then went blank so completely it was heavier than the others. You put it at the back of the shelf. You have not touched it since. She knows. She does not mention it. Mentioning it would make it a door you could open, and neither of you wants that room.

You learn the visitors by their silences. The ones who will leave something sit with both hands around the bowl and do not look at the window. The ones who will not, look at the window the entire time, hungry for the field, and she feeds them anyway and lets them go still carrying it, because theft of a burden is not her work. Mercy, here, includes refusal.

You learn the wrong stair in the evenings, going down with her, unrepairing what the city repaired in the afternoon. A municipal notice was nailed to the plaster once, citing codes. She peeled it off and the oranges came back into the wall as if the notice had been a draft under a door. "They want it safe," she said, which was the second thing she said to you that was almost a conversation. "Safe is the right stair. Safe ends three steps short."

There is a life you had before the landing became a room. You are allowed to remember that it existed. You are not encouraged to inventory it. Sometimes, raking the shore, a sheet of silt lifts and you recognize the angle of a streetlight, the particular red of a coat, and your hands know to keep moving. The house is not cruel about this. It simply has a policy: keepers who dwell on their own doors begin to label them, and a labeled door with a keeper's true name on it is a hole in the floor of the library. She lost someone to that, or the house did, or they are the same loss. You find the evidence in a gap on the highest shelf, a dustless rectangle the size of a palm, and in the way she will not stand with her back to the shore-room when the water is high.

At night, when the visitors have gone and the flame has settled into its blue animal sleep, the two of you sit at the scored table and do not speak much. Speech is a door left ajar; the house drafts through it. What passes between you is the work of the next day, laid out in objects. She sets down a rag: the stair. She sets down a bowl: a visitor is already on the lower steps, you can hear the hesitation, the moment they almost choose the right stair and don't. She sets down the rake, and you know the shore silted again, and you are glad, because the rake is the task your hands have become honest at.

Who are you, then, in this world?

You are the one who turns the residue so it does not become a flood. You are the blue at the edge of a nail, still faint, still arriving. You are not her and you are not a guest and you are not the house, though the house has begun to include your weight in its accounts — the chair holds you more precisely than it did, the window sometimes shows the field without being asked, as a kindness, when the alley has been brick for too many hours. You are the second silence at the table. When a visitor weeps into the gold soup and she is on the shore because the water has ideas, you are the one who stays seated and does not fill the quiet with comfort, because comfort is a right stair. You put your hand on the table, not on them, and the scores in the wood — all those old maps — conduct the worst of it down into the legs and into the flagstones and out, faintly, to the river the roof sags toward, which accepts almost anything if it arrives slowly.

The district will not learn this house. The city will keep nailing notices to a wall that turns them into the smell of oranges. People will keep choosing the painted stair and standing three steps short, baffled, holding the thing they cannot put down, until one of them, tired enough, notices the narrow turn and the wet plaster and comes up into a kitchen where the soup is already in the bowl.

You will be there. Or she will. Or the flame will, which amounts to the same hospitality.

If someone asks you what you are, you will not have a title that survives the alley. Keeper is her word, and she has never offered it. What you can say, if you are down on the wrong stair with a rag in your hand and a stranger is looking at you with the particular fear of people who have just realized the building is larger inside than out, is smaller than a title and more accurate.

You can say: the soup is on. You can say: don't take the shells. You can say: if the window shows you a field, you may look, but wash your hands before you come back to the table; the rye leaves a dust that labels things.

And if they ask who lives here, you can tell them the truth as far as you have it. A woman with a tide-mark on her dress, who keeps other people's unlivable rooms from spilling into a city that has no shelves for them. And you — the one on the stair — who is learning the difference between a burden and a story, and who goes up, every evening that the house consents to be evening, to turn the shore before it silts into a sea, because someone has to, and the rake already knows your hand, and the water is the temperature of a wrist, and the doors on the high shelf are warm where new arrivals sleep, unlabeled, waiting for a pen that only writes when no one is watching it work.
