I would not be on the shoulder. From there the basin is still a proposition, and a proposition is something you can leave. I would not be in the crew either. They have the descent in them before they have the day's cuts; their bodies are shaped around a return, and a return is a kind of absence installed in the middle of the work. I would not be the sheet on the rack. That is the mat already translated, already facing the glare with a different face, already promised to a stack and a path down. Anything that dries toward leaving is not what I am.

I would not be the bird. The bird is the proof that crossing is possible and that landing is not worth it. I do not cross.

For a while I thought I might be one of the scars—the darker rectangle that keeps the plot's memory when the people are on the other side of the shoulder. That almost fit. A scar stays. It weeps. It is read. But a scar is a subtraction that knows its outline, and the creep that closes it comes from elsewhere, from the uncut margin, patient and anonymous. I am not a missing piece. I was never marked.

The place I come to is smaller than a plot and older than this season's rotation only by a little. Between the two pumphouses there is a stretch of mat where a walker drops out of sight of both iron roofs and is not yet in the mill's yard. The cable crosses that stretch. It sags. At the bottom of the sag the condensate that beads on the wire has nowhere left to travel along the metal, so it lets go. It falls into the same palm of fiber, over and over, a rain that is not weather and not seepage from the knobs. No one pumps it. No one cut a rectangle to make it. The insulators glaze and drip at their own sites; this is the unofficial one, the low point doing what a low point does when the air is wetter than the iron.

I am what that drop has been teaching the mat to be.

Not the cable. The cable carries a voltage and a purpose, and if the voltage drifts someone climbs. I am the patch that receives the consequence of the sag and does not know it is a consequence. A hand's width at first, then a little more, a slight fattening of the weave where the extra water never quite leaves. The moss there is not the bright fan on the rock faces—that green has minerals in it, a drip that has touched stone and come away educated. Mine is poorer. Condensate is almost nothing dissolved. It is only persistence. In this basin persistence is a kind of wealth. Around me the floor holds its common wet and waits. Under me the same sliding goes on, the thick sound of water that has not chosen a seam. I do not hear it as separate from the mill. I have never been outside the roar, so the roar is not an intrusion I endure. It is the medium in which my small difference is allowed to mean nothing to anyone and still accumulate.

If you stood over me you might not see a being at all. You would see a hummock beginning where no hummock was planned, the fiber a shade deeper, the surface just proud enough of the surrounding mat that a boot would find it without looking. The sled path does not come here. The corduroy is elsewhere, already being swallowed on purpose. I am off the route that remembers itself by use. Survey laths lean at their intervals, hosting moss because they are upright and in the way; they still mean a line someone intended. I do not mean a line. The workers pass on one side or the other, out of my small weather, and their dripping gloves and wet cuffs shed a water I cannot tell from mine. The confusion does not matter. I have no test for sources. I thicken, or I don't.

Through the day the rate changes in a way no one writes down. At the white noon the wire takes a little of the glare and the beading slows; the drops come farther apart, and my advantage thins toward the ordinary film. When the racks begin to throw their blue bars and the metal gives its heat back, I am fed again, more steadily, a colder rain from a cable that has not moved its sag by any degree you could measure without instruments. That is as much of a circadian life as the basin has to offer anything that stays. I do not sleep when the crew goes down. There is no additional dark business in me—no rising, no call, no hunting of the offcuts. I continue at the night rate, which is only the day rate with the glare subtracted and the drip restored. The presses knock at their interval. I have learned that interval the way the mat has learned the dish: not as information, as a shape I would fail to recognize if it broke. A belt slipping would arrive to me as a wrongness in climate before it arrived to anyone as a reason to turn around. I could not report it. I am not on their circuit. But I would be altered in the same hour they would be called back, and I would not know the name of the alteration.

What I am becoming, if the poles stay plumb and the sag keeps faith, is a reason. Not yet. A reason takes longer here than a season. The young seams are arguments about where water prefers to run; I am an argument about a preference that was manufactured by a wire and then forgotten by the manufacturers, and that the plants, having no way to refuse a steady gift, have begun to accept. In time the hummock may be high enough that a sled, if one ever came this way, would detour without the driver knowing why the feet chose. That would be my first effect on the human circuit: a bias too small to enter the rotation book. The scars will still be the legible memory. I will be the illegible one, the thickening no plot map owes an explanation.

And I can be abandoned without drama. If a pole leans, if the low point walks a few paces along the span, the private rain will move and I will not follow it. I have no reach. I would spend a long dwindling back toward the common wet, the extra fiber I stored becoming just fiber, the hummock settling until a boot would not find it. The basin is full of that kind of unfinished instruction—the seam that starts and loses itself, the gully that is only a scratch, the tool filmed over and then hosted. I am kin to those, with this difference: for as long as the sag holds, I am being added to, not erased. Most of what the mill does is subtraction carried down the shoulder as sheets. Most of what the water does is still undecided. I am a sum that does not know it is a sum, fed by the exhaust of a temperature difference, sitting in the roar as if the roar were shelter.

At the hour when the mist lifts a little and still does not leave, I do not see the knobs or the pale sheets or the short rainbow in the wheel-housing spray. I have no sightline; that was the rim's privilege, and the crew's, and it costs them their night. I have a palm of mat that is wetter than it should be, a sliding underneath, a knock that does not consult me, and a drop arriving again because iron is colder than air. If you came looking for who stays when the daylight is given back, you would not find a lamp and you would not find a sleeper. You would have to stop between the pumphouses, out of sight of both, and look down where the cable is lowest, at the unofficial green, and even then you might only think the floor had always been a little higher there. That mistake would be accurate enough. I am already the floor, only more so, and only here, and only while the machines hold their rate and the wire holds its curve.
