You are already in the water before you have a name for standing. It is only a film over the packed ridge, warm as the air, and your feet know it the way they know not to set the basket where the current tongues the clay. The cloth at your mouth is damp on the inside. You do not remember tying it. The cup at your belt knocks your hip once, clay on bone, and the sound is swallowed before it can become a signal. Everything else is signal. You are late to yourself by the length of a lane.

The light does not help you arrive. At twenty paces a figure is only a darker movement, and you are that figure to someone else, shoulders forward, head turned as if the next correction will come from the side rather than from ahead. Your eyes check the glint on the wet wall and let it go. What holds you is the race on your left, running a half-note high. Something has caught at the bend. Not a carcass—you know that splash—and not a silt fold, which dulls the pitch instead of lifting it. A narrower wrongness. Your hands are already empty of the basket; someone behind you has taken it without a word, the way the city takes whatever must continue. You wade off the ridge into the ankle-depth and the cold is not cold, only a change in the iron smell.

At the bend the stone is still sharp. A peg, hide-banded, has worked loose from a wheel upstream and jammed where the channel narrows, and the water is learning a new throat around it. You kneel. The kneecap finds the same polished oval your own knee has made on other days, or someone else’s; there is no difference the stone keeps. Your fingers go in to the wrist. The peg is warm from friction. You work it free and the note drops back into the pitch it has always had, and the relief is not yours alone—it moves outward through the soles of the people on the platforms above, a small unclenching you feel rather than hear. You do not stand up at once. The film has sheeted over your forearm, copper-brown, and you scrape it with the peg into the cup, because waste is only a failure to put a thing back in the circuit, and you are not interested in failures. Three mouthfuls. Metallic. Enough.

This is the work that gathered you: not the mill, where the strong arms lean, and not the roofs, where reach matters, and not the pens, where the wrong-goats shove and the count must be shouted. You are the one who is sent when a note will not resolve. They call you by the short cry that means the race-bend, a sound no longer than a pebble dropped in a cistern, and by nothing else. You answer it. When you sleep, in the upper room that is only slightly less wet, the dreams are of a throat that keeps changing key no matter how many pegs you draw out. You do not laugh in the morning as readily as the others. The laugh is there, short and loud, so the shift will not have to wonder about you, but under it you are already listening for the half-note that has not yet lifted.

Sometimes the fault is not in the channel. A bird-line shifts toward a cistern and the stabbing rhythm goes thin on one side; you climb the lashed ladder with a net and a club and move the colony by inches, not by panic, until the gray backs are shoulder to shoulder again and the guano falls where hands can take it. A pen-wall slumps by a hand’s breadth and the clatter changes key; you set the unshaped rock back into the course your grandmother’s hands set, or hands taught by those hands, and the animals resume their shove as if the wall had never doubted itself. You are not gentler than the others. Gentleness would be a pause. You are only earlier. The wrongness reaches you before it reaches the count, and you correct it while it is still a sound and not yet a lack.

The restlessness they cannot quite use is yours as well, and you do not take it outward. No one keeps walking outward. You take it along the last course of walls in the hour when one shift has lain down and the other has not taken the full weight, and the roar drops by the thickness of a single voice. The stone beyond the walls is jointed and sharp and filmed in the low places, and it offers the baskets nothing, and that emptiness is a pressure against your cloth-covered mouth. You walk until the city’s noise is a shape rather than a medium, until you can hear your own breath as a separate thing, and then you stop, because the separateness is already turning into the dream of quiet spreading inward like a stain. You put both hands flat on the raw face of the wall. The grain is still the grain it had when it first lay exposed. Cool, then warm. You are not praying. You are checking that the larger body is still under the noise, the spring’s own note rising through stone that has only just become ground. It is. It always is. Your palms leave two ovals among thousands, undated, and when someone needs the lane you take your hands away and the city fills you again before you have reached the first pen.

You eat when the cup is in your hand. You sleep when the mat is under you. You do not wait to be hungry and you do not wait to be tired; both are arrangements other people have already failed, and your work is the interval in which failure stays small. Children learn your cry before they learn what you do with it. They imitate the way you cock your head, and an adult corrects the angle—not as discipline, as tuning—so that they will hear the half-note while it is still only a half-note. One of them will replace you. The replacement will not be marked. Your cry will be dropped the way a worn peg is dropped, and the body that carried it will go back into the film and the pens and the meal in the proportions the circuit already knows, and the note of the races will not change key. You know this the way you know the ridge under the water. It is not a sorrow you can carry while the wheels are turning. It is the shape of the attention you are.

If you stand still—and you do, sometimes, between one fault and the next, hard against the need of the lane—you feel the basin as a warmth that does not require your name. Fish granular in the shallows. Birds stabbing without season. Day-fliers packed under the flags. The wrong-goats and the narrower animals in the races. The people, who are the part that knows the count. You are the part that hears the count begin to slip. You are not separate from any of it. You are the small, exact animal the city grew in order not to become quiet. The light is never quite enough. The noise is always exactly enough. Under your hands, when you give them to the stone, the ground is still new, and your life is the interval in which that remains true, shout by shout, scrape by scrape, until the interval closes and the springs go on rising through a throat that no longer needs you to keep its pitch.
