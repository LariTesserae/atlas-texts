The boards do not end at the water. The water has not been invited yet.

You would come to it along a path of crushed shell that never quite decides it is a path. The shells are small, the color of old teeth, and they give underfoot with a sound like a matchbook closing. On either side the ground is not ground. It is a low, continuous mat of something between moss and felt, gray-green, warm if you kneel, and busy with a slow inward pull, as if the whole flat were being gathered toward a point you cannot see. Nothing tugs at your ankles. The pull is only in the nap of the mat, all the fibers leaned the same way, the way grass leans after a wind that has already gone.

The pier begins without a shore. One plank simply becomes trustworthy, then another. They are the width of a forearm, laid with gaps a finger could fall through, and through the gaps there is not sea. There is a pale, moving grain, like milk stirred once and then left to remember the spoon. It has a surface and it has a depth, and the two do not agree on where they meet. Light sits on it in plates. When a plate slides off, what is underneath is the same light, only older.

The pier runs straight for as long as straightness can be believed, then it thickens. Planks are doubled, then tripled, then set on edge so the walkway becomes a low wall you can still walk, if you turn your feet sideways and accept that balance here is a courtesy, not a law. Nets hang from the outer edges. They are not fishing nets. The mesh is too fine, and what is caught in it is not struggling. In one, a coil of fog the size of a cat, dense enough to keep its shape when the air around it moves. In another, three sounds: a gull’s cry, a cup set down, a door that was not opened. They hang in the mesh like fish and do not fade. Touch the door-sound and your hand comes away with the feeling of a latch, nothing more.

Farther out the pier branches, not by design so much as by habit. A spur goes left into a stand of poles. The poles are white, waist-high, and each one is capped with a shallow bowl. Most bowls hold an inch of the milk-grain from below, and in the inch a single dark seed turns, always turning, never drinking. One bowl is empty and has been lined with the crushed shell from the path, packed into a smooth pale cup. Another holds a needle, floating, pointing not north but back along the pier, toward the place where the first trustworthy plank begins. If you look away and look again, it is still pointing there. It does not swing.

At the last branching the wood changes. The grain stands up out of the planks in fine ridges, as if the timber were trying to become grass and had been stopped at the moment the attempt became visible. Walking there is loud. Each step wakes a small dry applause that does not echo, because there is no far side of the air for it to strike. The sky, if it is sky, is low and the color of the inside of a shell, and it does not contain sun or cloud or the idea of weather arriving. Weather, here, is something that has to be brought.

Someone brings it.

Not in sight at first—never all the way in sight—but the pier is edited by a routine you can read the way you read a kitchen by its stains. A section of rail has been wrapped and rewrapped in a strip of the gray-green mat, not tied, tucked, the way you bind a split handle you still mean to use. Beside it, a row of the bowls has been turned upside down, and under each one the milk-grain has gone clear and still, a little lens of held water with the seed suspended at the exact center like a pupil. One lens is flawed: a hairline crack in the stillness, a dark thread running from seed to rim, and along that thread the seed has started to put out a root that cannot decide whether it is a root or a crack of its own.

Below the wrapped rail, scratched into the wood with something no sharper than a thumbnail, there is a tally. Not numbers. Short vertical cuts in groups of five, then a gap the width of a hand, then one cut alone, deeper, reopened more than once. The wood has not healed around it. Wood here does not heal. It only agrees to remain in the state it was last asked to hold.

At the very end, which arrives sooner than the length of the pier should allow, the planks stop mid-board, the saw-cut still bright, the next plank never laid. Beyond that edge the milk-grain continues, placid, plated with light, and on it sits a single bowl that has no pole. It does not sink. In the bowl is not water and not seed. It is a small weather: a turning gray, a scent of iron and wet shell, a wind the size of a breath, kept from leaving by the curve of the clay. The bowl is warm. The weather in it is cold. Nothing spills.

If you wait, the nap of the distant mat will lean a fraction more toward the pier. A gull-cry in one of the nets will shift its weight. The needle will not move. Whatever keeps this place does not arrive by walking the boards. It arrives the way the pull arrives—as a direction everything else has already agreed to face—and the unfinished plank, the solitary tally-mark, the weather cupped and not yet poured, are how it leaves a shape for itself in a world that will not record footprints.

That is the place. The boards, the nets, the bowls, the cut that was reopened, the weather that has not been allowed to become rain. What lives there has not stepped forward. It is only the sum of the alterations, patient, and the pier is long enough for it to be late.
