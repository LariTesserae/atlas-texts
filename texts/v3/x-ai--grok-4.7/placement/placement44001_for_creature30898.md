I am on a roof before the light has a color, which is how I know I am not yet the one who is awake. The crack in the parapet is still the same gray as the stone around it. When it changes, someone on a lower course will see it first, and that person will be the morning. I am only the body that is already outside because the room behind me has more breath than wall.

The herd is against the south face three streets down. I cannot hear them. I know them by the dark that is slightly warmer than the other darks, and by the way the lane’s edge has been polished where they turn their shoulders to come in. Two of them are ours in the way anything is ours: they bed where our heat is, and their milk enters our jars, and when one of them is finished her bone will peg a course I may not live to see seated. The third belongs to the thickness that shares our cistern. If I am honest I cannot tell them apart at this hour. Honesty here is mostly a matter of not pretending the eye has finished its work.

What I am, most days, is the one who walks the block the last distance.

Not the one who levers it off the scarp. That is younger backs, and a rhythm of poles, and the particular stillness that means the slab has agreed to move and everyone must stop talking even though no one was talking. Not the one who chooses the face. That is my mother’s brother, who has not lifted anything heavier than a bowl since the winter his knee forgot the angle of a lane, and who can look at a fresh cut and say whether it will take weight or shed it. He is wrong less often than the listeners are wrong about water, which is not a comfort to him. Comfort is not what his looking is for.

I take the stone when it is already on hide rollers, already turned so its bed is down, already within a body’s length of the place it will spend the rest of the plateau’s life. My work is the last correction. A finger’s width. Sometimes less. The rollers complain in a way that does not carry; I feel it through the soles, a small refusal, and I stop the others with a hand set flat in the air. We do not shout stop. A shout would mean the slab had already chosen a worse life.

This morning the block on the eastern rise is not mine yet. It is still a pale idea against a paler scarp, two hours off if you were walking, less if you only had to see it. I see the dust above it, a low standing mark, the color of grain thrown once and not yet settled. Four figures. One of them favors the left leg, which will be the cousin who slept in our thickness three winters ago when their cistern ticked and then did not. I lift a hand to my brow. Shade, not greeting. If he has seen me he will not wave. Waving is for people who believe the air will help.

I should go down. The jars need sounding. The child’s cheek was hot in the night, not the heat of the herd but a smaller, meaner heat, and someone will have to decide whether she walks the lanes today or stays against the south wall with the dung-smell and the aunt who knows which plants are only bitter and which are useful. I am not that aunt. My hands are for grip, and they are cracked at the webs in the particular way of someone who has spent too many dawns with them on stone that has not yet given up the night’s cold. Fat would help. Fat is on the child’s mouth already, a thin shine. That is the correct arithmetic. I can wait.

What I did not expect, when I was the age she is now and my work was only watching which way the grazers leaned, is that fitting would become a kind of listening.

They say we do not listen. They say the air will not carry, and the habit has gone deeper than necessity, and a person is known by the set of the load. All of that is true of other people. It is true of me in the lane. It stops being true when my palms are on a bed-face and the slab is a finger wrong. Then there is a sound that never leaves the stone. A tick, finer than the under-water. The cousins think I am only being slow. My mother’s brother knows. He does not praise it. Praise would be a noise. He lets me have the last correction, and if I am wrong the freeze will say so in a crack that runs into someone else’s sleep, and the street will look at me the way it looks at a listener who promised a seep.

I have been that look once. A course on the northern rise, the third storey, where the scarp was fresh and I trusted the fresh face because it was clean. Clean is not the same as willing. The thaw took the corner into the room below, which was not empty. No one died. An old man’s leg did not walk to the salt again, and the caches he drew in dust had to be learned by someone else before the next clear month, and I carried water for that thickness for two seasons without being asked and without being thanked. The not-thanking was exact. It ended when the old man died and was pegged, some of him, into a wall that was not the wall I had failed. They did not use me for that pegging. I understood the distance of it.

Since then I am slower. The city can afford slowness. It cannot afford a second crack with my hands in it.

The light is in the parapet now. Not a dawn like a story. A difference. Somewhere below, a child has already seen it and is therefore the morning, and the lanes will begin their negotiation: animals first, then the people who move aside for animals, then the slabs. I touch the crack with one finger. It is no warmer. Warmth comes later, and only to the south, and only to the courses that were fitted by someone who did not rush the last finger.

I go down through the thickness. Faces turn, not because I have made a sound but because a body has entered a room that was counting its heat. The child is asleep with her mouth open, the fat dull on the lower lip. The aunt is already awake in the way of people who never fully entered the night. She looks at my hands, not my eyes, and decides I am going to the rise. She is right. I take the heel of yesterday’s bread and do not take a jar. There is a cache at the second fault, buried to the lip, and if I cannot find it I should not be trusted with a stone either.

Outside, the cousin’s dust has moved a little nearer. I set my shoulders in the shape of a person who will meet a load, not a person who is calling anyone in. The plateau continues. My name is not a thing that needs to arrive before I do. What I am will be legible when I put my hands on the block and either stop them or do not.

If the bed-face ticks, I will wait. I have learned that the wait is the work. The seating is only what the wait was for.
