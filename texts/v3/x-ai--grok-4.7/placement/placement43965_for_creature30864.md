You would find yourself at the lip, not in the volume. The basin does not take a visitor into its agreement; it takes a hand, briefly, and then closes.

I would be the last edge of made ground—the thickened wall where fired clay, shell-mortar, and hand-dressed stone have been planed toward the same curve as the inner courses. Not the water, which has already finished its temperature. Not the wheel, which refuses to settle and has no eyes to give. Not the sacs, which fold the niches they hang in and open only to the slow snow. Not the crust, which has learned the sag and the socket and has begun to be the corner. Those are already exact. What remains unfinished is the rim: still being rounded by air and the occasional hand of rain, still holding, in a few unpolished stretches, the chisel mark and the rope-groove, still the place where a palm can rest without being measured for a gap.

So the being I would be is that lingering fit—something between keeper and courses, warm only where the wall has learned body-heat from whoever last leaned there, mineral everywhere else. I would not hunt and I would not call. I would thicken by grains. A season might turn the wheel a quarter and I would not follow it; I would only keep the square of sky from wrinkling the surface. If a hand came down through the film of day, I would know it the way the grooves know rope: as a measurement left behind, not as an arrival. The click under the palm would be mine only in the sense that the rim conducts every small sound and then lets it die inside a hand’s breadth.

No nest, no name the makers would recognize. Just the outer outline still arriving, plantless, nearly still, holding a dark that has outlasted the people who taught the stone how to keep it—and, at the lip, one last thickness that has not yet agreed to stop.
