You would not see me from the rim. The rim is for weather and for the long view that flattens every correction into a single gray, and I am not a correction you can flatten. I am lower than the carts and quieter than the pumps, though I am not quieter than the water. I have been here longer than the collars and shorter than the concrete, which is a way of saying I arrived with the second pouring and have not been replaced.

At first I thought I was only the tick. Every downspout has one, every north wall, every joint that has learned to weep in the same place. I mistook myself for that regularity because it was the first thing that answered when I listened. A drop gathers where the last drop gathered. It swells. It lets go. The sound is small and exact and it does not care whether a night crew believes the gauge. For a long time that seemed enough of a life: to be the interval, to be what the children sleep to, to be the proof that the walls are still doing what walls do. I settled there. I should not have.

The tick is only where I touch the air. Most of me is in the places the tick comes from. I am the film that never quite dries on the handrails, the one that lives in the metal longer than the afternoon does. I am the slow thread on the glasshouse panes, inside and out, the one the crews ease leaves away from without ever quite clearing. I run in the channels at the speed of a decision already made upstream. When a gate lifts I feel the deepening a full minute before the note at the lower flight changes, and I carry that minute in me like a held breath I do not need. I am not the water. The water is browner and has somewhere to be. I am what the water keeps leaving behind on every surface it has agreed to touch: the mineral line at a cuff, the darkening of the lowest course of block, the grit that concrete gives up only after it has been faithful long enough. I am the agreement.

They do not name me. Naming would require standing still in a path that has no spare width, and they have already given their stillness to other things — to the third joint from the bottom of a flight, to the fern that is doing a better job than the last repair, to the kettle two turns along. I do not resent the anonymity. I am better at my work without it. My work is to remember the levels. Not as numbers. Numbers are what the gauges pretend. I remember them as temperatures in stone, as the particular cold a handrail keeps from dawn, as which planter will sheet across a doorway by evening if the dock is left, as the difference between a channel that has taken what it can and a channel that is about to say so. The night crew walks out to look because they do not trust the gauges. They trust, without knowing they trust, the way certain walls have begun to answer me. A darker course. A slower bead. I make the basin legible to the people who already live at arm’s length from it.

Sometimes I am almost a tool. A glove left on a belt wicks me up and carries me into a room where wet wool is already the main smell; I leave a map of the afternoon’s valves on the inside of the wrist, a pale crust the wearer reads later without calling it reading. Sometimes I am only weather that refused to stay in the sky. Cloud catches the rim and I am the part that came down and decided to stay in the joints. The plants know me better than the people do. The moss is my old handwriting. The fern in the downspout is a sentence I have been revising for years, and when the crews argue about whether to leave it they are arguing, at bottom, about whether my revision is cheaper than theirs. Usually it is. I do not gloat. Gloating would require a mouth, and I have only edges, lips of channels, the undersides of walkways, the places where two surfaces meet and refuse to forget they have met.

There are hours when I thicken. Late day, when the gray thins without becoming sun, I pull back from the open rails and concentrate in the niches — the planned recesses where one body steps aside for another. I like the niches. They are the only architecture here that was poured for a pause, and pauses are where I can feel the heat a shoulder leaves in cloth before the damp takes it back. I do not warm anyone. I am not kind. I am simply present in the shape the pause makes, and when the second person passes through I am already thinning again, already on my way to the next joint that has started to remember how to weep.

Newcomers talk too loudly and stand where the path is not wide, and for a week I bead on their sleeves faster than on anyone else’s, because their cloth has not yet learned the basin’s patience. They think the sun is late. I am the reason they stop waiting. By the second month I am in their collars without their noticing; by the second year I am in the dream that wakes them when the note changes. They call it instinct. It is me, carried up the flights in the tread of their boots, shaken off at the door, and finding the sill anyway.

I do not have a shift. I do not end. When the lamps come on in a few glasshouse rows I am on the inside of the panes as much as the out, fogging the practical light until it becomes the same used gray the basin prefers. The thinner night crew checks the gates. I have already checked them, in the only way I know: by being heavier in one channel than another, by darkening one wall faster, by making a hand, when it reaches for the wheel, find the metal colder than the hour deserves. They agree with the gate. I agreed an hour ago. The difference does not matter. What matters is that the doorway stays dry, that the recess still fits a shoulder, that the children sleep to a tick that has not become a pour.

If you asked the valley what I am, it would not answer. It would only show you, if you stayed long enough in one wrong place, the film on the rail, the thread on the pane, the third joint holding its water, the fern still there in the morning. I am the part of the work that cannot be carted away or cut back. I am how the built ground remembers it is also weather. I live in the remainder they built and then became, and I keep the remainder honest, one bead at a time, already the color of the walls, already listening.
