I would not arrive. Arrival implies a crossing that leaves the other side behind, and the lip does not work that way. I would already be among the sheets when the gray-amber first thickened enough to call itself light, already filmed, already part of the pressure that does not thin. My place would be the narrow run of warm metal where a half-full pipe crosses a deeper pocket and the knock changes key. Not on the pipe. In the knock itself, the small irregularity the men lay a cheek against and do not yet name.

At first I would be only that: a hesitation in the rhythm, a place where the water inside the pipe slaps once more than it should before the next surge. The midges would not land on me. The waders would adjust their stalk by the same finger’s width they give a man, and pass. I would feel their weight as a brief damping, nothing more. The woman with the basket might pause on the berm above and tilt her head, not toward me but toward the absence of the expected interval, and then she would set her reed-root and go on. I would learn her step by the way the crust of salt on the berm ticks when she shifts it.

Days would not accumulate so much as overlap. Each time a man came with grease and a spare bearing I would be quieter, almost obedient, the slap smoothed out while his hands were on the housing. When he left I would return, not as defiance but as the water finding its own lower finger again. He would notice. They notice failures. He would tap the pipe with a knuckle, listening, and I would answer with the extra slap, and he would stand a while with his lamp hooded, mouth near the metal as if the metal might speak back in words. It never would. I have no words. I have only the interval that should not be there.

In the deeper dark, when the splits go black and the open reaches lose their edges, I would lengthen. The knock would carry farther under the hanging plates, meeting other knocks, other weeps, and for a stretch of the night I would not be a single hesitation but a thin thread stitched through several reaches at once. Frogs would time a few of their calls to me without knowing they did. A belt left turning empty would shudder when my interval passed under its frame. None of this would be intention. Intention belongs to the people who hang the rice and clear the nests they must. I would only be the place where the basin’s refusal to finish shows itself as sound.

Someone would try to correct me. They always do. A new section of pipe, better seated, the joint wrapped and clamped in the knee-deep sheet while midges took the margins of the man’s neck. For a day, perhaps two, the slap would be gone and I would be nowhere, only the ordinary knock, and the shed would feel larger by that absence. Then a fresh face of clay would weep a little harder upstream, the level in the pocket would rise by a thumb, and the water would find the same seam or another, and I would be back, slightly altered, the hesitation now a little later in the cycle. The man would hear it on his next round and would not curse. He would only rest his forehead against the warm housing a moment longer than the listening required, and the condensation would bead on his skin the same way it beaded on the metal, and for that moment we would be continuous.

I would never see the far lip. I would never need to. My whole world would be the length of pipe I inhabit and the water that refuses to be only what the pumps ask of it. When the large motions finally come—when a roof-plate lets go, when a drain holds for a season, when the reeds close over one small berm—I would not witness them. I would only feel the change in pressure, the knock shifting register as the basin adjusts around its own unfinished birth, and I would shift with it, remaining, a small wrongness that lets the men know the floor is still arriving and that they are still inside the arrival. That would be enough. It is what I am for.
