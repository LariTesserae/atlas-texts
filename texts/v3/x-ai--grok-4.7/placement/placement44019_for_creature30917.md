I am under the water that used to be a street and is, for this hour, a mistake. It moves where it should stand. A thread of it finds the blue clay and worries it loose, and the cold in that thread is older than the wall it is leaving. Sticks cross above me at the wrong angles, peeled pale, one of them still rocking from a weight that has already gone on. I do not see the weight. I feel the level drop by less than a hand, and that is enough to turn me around.

For a little while I could be the leak itself. The basin is full of water that does not belong to anyone, and this water is only doing what water does when a plate-sized foot splays, lifts, and takes a seam with it. But water does not come back. I do. That is the first thing that is mine.

I come back along the bottom, not along the slot. The slot is a rumor of smaller bodies in the stems overhead, voles ticking, a duck’s shovel-sound far off and dull. Down here the canal is still a canal for as long as I keep it. My feet know the packed mud of the feeding shelf and the softer place where the shelf was cut last night and the night before that. The cut is not new in kind. It is new in this particular stick, this particular hour, the way a scarp-block is new and then is not. I surface once, only the eyes and the nose, because the air on the floor is a colder animal than the water and I do not stay in it longer than the breath requires. Nothing on the rise is looking for me. The large dark shape is already a ward away, shouldered into willow, its jaw working. It does not know it opened me. I do not know its name for itself. Between us there is a missing hand’s breadth of water and the work of putting it back.

The wall is a low thing. From the rim it would not read as a wall at all, only as a darker stitching in the sedge, a line where the sheets on one side stand and the sheets on the other move. Up close it is sticks laid as they were cut, mud tamped by the same hands that will tamp it again, a root I pulled in on a better evening because it was already shaped like a brace. I climb it where it still holds and feel the leak with the side of my face. The water leaving is a soft, stupid pull. I bite a wand from the bank — this year’s alder, bright bark, the pulp sweet-green even in the cold — and I swim it crosswise into the gap, and then another, and then I am up on the face of it with my hands in the muck, packing, packing, the way the day packs rime onto a north stem and does not call it building. The mud smells of iron and of crushed horsetail. It skins over as I work. The thread thins. A last dirty pulse comes through, clouds, and quits. The level on the upstream side begins, very slowly, to remember itself.

This is where I am: not the center, where the young river is still learning to be taxed, and not the scarp, where the nests are. I am on the spring side of the middle ward, where three lodges have grown into one another until the doors underwater are a neighborhood, and the cache of branches beside them is a second, ruder lodge that only we eat. The rises of till here are dump-heaps from a season when the ice was still arguing with the floor. We have improved them without improving the idea of them. My door is the left-hand one, under a cap of frozen grass that was last year’s bedding and is now a roof. In the day I am mostly that roof’s idea of absence. The floor’s business does not need me visible. The heron fishes the seam I maintain and does not thank the seam. The otters use the canal as a thread through a cloth they did not weave. I let them. They do not dig out what I have packed. That is the whole treaty.

What I am, then, is the returning. A body low to the water, brown as wet bark but not the same black as the great browser, ears that are nothing much, a tail that is a tool for bracing on a mud face and for steering a stick, and teeth that keep the belts from becoming a wood. I cut high enough that the stump sprouts, low enough that the sprout is still mine next year. The browser takes the tips I cannot use once they are out of reach of my standing, and in winter strips bark I would have stored if I had come a day earlier. Some evenings I arrive and the belt has already been edited by a mouth larger than my work. I take what is left and do not experience it as theft. The trench that ruins a platform is not an attack either. It is a recurrence, like rime, like the pulse of melt at midmorning that I have already built for without having a word for planning. I mud the same seam so often that the seam has become a place, the way a path becomes a ditch and then a road. If I abandoned it for a season the geometry would soften, the head would die, the lodge doors would sit in water that no longer quite reaches the cache. I do not abandon it. Repair is not a virtue I possess. It is the pace that keeps the doors wet.

In the hard part of the year I am less on the wall and more under it. The rime happens to other lives. I hear the browser less, and then sometimes very near, the suck of those splayed feet in the clay above the lodge, the whole roof ticking as weight passes and does not bed. If it bedded on us the mud would take the print and the kits would go still in the dark, and in the morning the structure would still be a structure. We are built for that kind of weather. The kits smell of milk and wet wood and the same iron mud. They learn the seam by being taken to it before their teeth are a serious argument, and one of them always tries to pack a stick that is too long, and the stick drifts, and someone else, usually me, puts it where the water was arguing. None of this is instruction. It is the lodge doing again what it did.

When the velvet is on the high browser and the flies are a climate, I am careful of the wallows. A wallow opens in an afternoon what I would need a month to consent to, a circle of broken willow and held water that breeds its own small summer and then silts. I do not close wallows. They are not my streets. I work around them, and the canals bend a little, and the bend becomes the next fact. In the rut the ugly low call comes under the stems and I sink without deciding to. It is not for me. Almost nothing that makes a sound on this floor is for me. My own alarm, when I give it, is a single slap I resent, because afterward the ward is too quiet in the wrong way and the fish stop their rings. I would rather the heron take its fish in a small collapse and the level stay put.

By the time the south faces have lost their morning white, the seam holds. Water stands on the upstream side at the height the sedge expects, and slips through the deliberate low place farther down, where I meant a spill, not where a hoof invented one. I cannot see the far rim from here and do not need to. The city is the distance of a night’s swimming and the knowledge of which banks are mine to cut. I push a last thumb of mud into a crack the cold will open by evening, and I go under, back along the canal that is a canal again, past the cache, into the door. Behind me the large feet will find the shelf or they will not. If they find it, the thread will start, and the level will forget itself by a hand, and I will feel it from inside the lodge as a change in the sound the water makes against the sticks.

That change is the bell. I answer it. The ground is younger than the habit, and the habit does not care.
