I arrive already partway across a bench, and the first thing that does not fit is the wish to be seen arriving. The sinter takes the sole the way it takes everything else—warm, porous, already used—and the wish thins out of me before I can spend it. A sheet of beetles reassigns itself a pace to my left and stops. I stop with it. For a while that is the whole of what I am: a pause the ground has agreed to hold.

The pause lengthens into a habit of edges. I learn the third tower by the way its footing ticks when the bucket is outbound and the way the tick goes dull when the span has sagged. The walking raptor uses that dullness; I begin to use the half-breath before it. Not to warn anyone. Warning would be a voice, and a voice dies. I only shift my weight the way the fin birds do, one stone to the next, so that the ledge below the span is already empty when the darker seed drops. The birds do not thank me. They reoccupy the cups on the return stroke of whatever motor is still willing to shake mites loose, and the message of my shift travels a little farther than I meant it to, out across the stippled fins, and comes back as nothing I can point to. That nothing is how I know I have been allowed to stay.

What I eat is what has already blundered. A mat of beetles that chose the brine pan on a day when the skin of microbes ran thick; I lift them the way the one whose pan still skins lifts them, with a flat of tin and no hurry, and the pan does not grudge the theft because the hatch was larger than the skim. Water I take from the oval that rises a finger when the valve at the pump house seats. It tastes of the shaft I have not yet cleared. The taste tells me the bearing is beginning to pack with sinter-dust, and that if I do not work it free before the next dry interval the housing will cool, the seep will fall, and the four birds in the warm joist will start the quarrel that spreads. So I clear it. My hands learn the half-second between strokes. Fiber from the belt comes away with the dust; I leave the fiber where a cup can find it. The birds tolerate the body in the doorway for exactly as long as the rhythm holds. When I am finished I step back onto the trail and the ridge of plates I had been using as a curb settles a fraction, as if to remind me which of us was stone.

Night is the film on the cable and then the failure of the film. I do not sleep in the shed. The shed is paid for. I sleep in the lee of the fin that sweats latest, close enough to the tower footing that the mortar of old pellets keeps a pocket of warmer air, far enough that the raptor, returning along the wire, does not count me as a pause it might profit by. In that pocket I keep the older name I arrived with, the way the others keep theirs—unspoken, because there is no one in the lee to spend it on, and because a name that expects to carry across the bench is already lost. What I answer to, if anything on the plateau should need me, is the dull tick of the third tower, and the finger of water, and the empty ledge.

Over days the wish to be a visitor finishes drying. I am the interval between the shaft’s strokes. I am the shift that empties a ledge one beat early. I am a tenant the finished ground has measured and found small enough to close around, provided I keep the manners: no new color, no shout, no repair that tries to remember a louder purpose. The bucket goes out. The beads form and fail. Somewhere under the plates a trail uses a living curb and does not announce that it has seen it. I clear the bearing again when the water says so. The country does not grow quieter for my being in it. It was never empty enough for that. It only fails to notice one more agreement, and the heat keeps coming up through the soles, and that is the place, and that is what is left of me.
