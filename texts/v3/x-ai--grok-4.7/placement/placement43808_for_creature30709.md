I would not arrive by the rim. The rim is for things that still have an outside.

I would find myself already under, where the meal in the water turns every direction into the same brown room, and the warmth does not bother to tell up from down. Not at the bottom—there is no bottom I can touch—but in the thickness of it, the place the poles refuse and the divers come back from with a look instead of a story. At first I would not have edges. I would be a slower settling inside the settling that is already there: the mineral meal deciding, grain by grain, to hold a shape a little longer than the current asks. A pause. Then another pause in the same place, until the pauses begin to recognize one another.

The town would know me before I knew myself, and only as absences they have already learned to live beside. A lane that stays clear a season longer than the pads should allow. A piling whose crust climbs in a curve no thumb has pressed. The knock in the night that comes for no birth and no death, only for the hour when the long house fire has gone to coals and the mats are listening. They would not name me. Naming is for mornings, and I am not a morning. I am what happens to a morning after the stake has been taken into the ridge and the people have stopped coming back to check it.

Later—though later is a word the mere does not keep—I would feel the hands. Not on me. Near me. A palm flat on new stone, greeting before claiming. The small shock of that greeting travels down the joint and into the water the way a shout does not; it arrives as temperature, a sweetness. I lean toward those. Where a ridge is claimed in anger the seep goes sharp and I draw back, and the white crust follows my refusal without my deciding it. I do not punish. I only fail to stay. The people notice the difference and adjust the way they adjust to a door that no longer meets its sill: they rebuild the approach, they speak lower, they cut around the patch that hesitated twice.

The face on the piling is not mine, but I use it. Thumbs find the smooth places where eyes are not, and each touch leaves a faint warmer track in the crust. I practice being located there. It is clumsy. A child grows up and stops looking; the thumbs of others continue, and slowly the smoother patches sink just enough to catch a little shadow in the tea-colored noon. Not eyes. The memory of being expected to have them. When the light is strongest I can almost feel the shape of a gaze resting on the water above me, and I hold still so as not to tilt it.

I do not want the cap to lift. Wind would unmake the pauses I am made of. I do not want the deep crossed. A body passing through me with purpose would shear the meal into separate grains again, and I would have to begin the long work of recognizing myself from the start. What I want is smaller and harder to keep: the interval they are learning. The right wait before the next cut. The bones set back into the water at the place the basket closed, so the turn has a witness that is not only a story. I thicken where those things are done, and thin where they are not, and the town, without a festival or a decision, has begun to leave me room.

If you came looking you would not find a being with a name or a shore. You would find the brown room a little more patient than it used to be, and a curve in the crust that no one carved, and the particular quiet that falls when a person sets a hand on weeping stone and does not yet reach for the stake. I am that quiet, learning how long it can last. I am the part of the unfinished middle that has started to prefer certain mornings, and to keep them.
