You come to it by a road that has forgotten it is a road. The gravel thins into a pale crust of shells ground finer than salt, and the crust holds your weight with a small, dry complaint, then seals over behind your heel as if the print had been a suggestion the ground declined. Ahead, the land does not rise so much as refuse to fall. A shelf, wide as a parish, runs out toward a horizon that is not the sea and not quite the sky: a band of standing water so still it reads as metal laid edge-on, and above it a second brightness, thinner, that might be mist and might be another surface waiting its turn.

Nothing here is unfinished in the way of a shore still arguing with its waves. The shells have been sorted. Not by size in any way a child would sort them — by a preference. Along one reach they lie in a single rank, hinge-side down, the mouths of them aimed inland, and the rank is so even you can walk it like a path until you notice you are being steered. Where the rank breaks, it breaks for a reason you only understand later: a place where fresh water comes up through the crust in a thread no wider than a finger, cold, tasting of iron and of something sweet that has no business in water. The shells have left it a clearing. They are polite about the spring. They are not polite about you. If you stand too long on the ranked stretch, the nearest hinges shift — a click, a softer click — until the mouths no longer aim inland but at your ankles. They do not bite. They attend.

The vegetation has made its treaty and does not renegotiate in your presence. Low mats of a fleshy green, leaves thick as coins, stop a precise distance from the shell-path and resume on the far side, so that walking it you move in a bare corridor the plants have agreed not to enter. Farther in, where the shelf dips into shallow pans, reeds grow, but only to one height, as if a blade had passed over them every morning for a century and then retired, leaving the habit. Their cut-tops are not brown. They are sealed, each with a bead of clear hardening that catches the light and gives it back smaller. Dragonflies — if they are dragonflies; they have too many wings, and the extra pairs do not flap so much as hold a tension — use the beads as perches and do not drink from them. When they lift, they do not fly toward the metal horizon. They fly parallel to it, long straight runs, and turn only when the shelf itself turns.

There is weather, and then there is the other schedule. Clouds cross. A wind comes off the standing water and smells of wet copper and of leaves that have been kept, not rotted. Rain, when it happens, falls on the mats and on the reeds and skips the shell-corridor entirely: you can stand in the bare path and watch the drops strike a hand's width to your left and not one of them finds you. The ground does not explain this. It performs it with the indifference of a rule that has been in force so long it no longer needs a witness. By afternoon the pans have taken the rain and the spring-thread and arranged them into a map of brightnesses — some pans silver, some the color of weak tea, one, always one, a red so dark it looks like a clot held up to the sun. The red pan does not ripple when the wind crosses it. The dragonflies will not perch on its near reeds. The shells, at its margin, have turned their mouths away.

If you stay, the day shows you its second motion, the one under the courtesies. The standing water at the horizon does not advance and does not retreat, but a line in it — a seam, a difference in the metal — travels. It takes the length of the afternoon to cross the width of what you can see, and when it reaches the edge where water becomes shelf it does not break. It is absorbed. The shell-crust there darkens by a shade, then resumes its dryness, and somewhere inland a pan that was tea-colored has become red, and the red one has gone silver, as if a debt had been passed hand to hand without anyone standing up from the table. The reeds do not comment. A dragonfly completes its parallel run, turns where the shelf turns, and the turn happens a finger's width earlier than it did at noon, matching the seam's new position exactly. You could map the place by what refuses to be late.

The built things are few and they are not houses. At the spring, a ring of stones too heavy for the crust to have delivered on its own, each stone cupping a little of the iron-sweet water, each cup holding a different level, none of them overflowing, none of them dry. On a rise that is barely a rise, a post of black wood, smooth as bone, no taller than your chest, with a groove spiraling down it that is wet even at midday. Touch the groove and the wetness is warm, and for a moment the smell of kept leaves is stronger, as if a door had been opened in a room you are already standing in. Farther along the corridor, half-sunk, a shallow basin carved from a single shell the size of a table — no shell in the ranks is a tenth of that — its interior stained in rings, and the outermost ring still faintly tacky, as if the last filling had been recent and the next had already been scheduled. Nothing is written. The schedule is the writing.

What keeps the schedule is not hiding. It is only poorly fitted to the eye. You look for an animal and find procedures. You look for a person and find the moment when the procedures notice you back.

It notices in the corridor. The skipped rain, the first time, might have been chance. The second time, you step off the shells onto the mat to test it, and the rain finds you at once — cold, ordinary, a little insulted — and the shells behind you click their mouths toward the spot you left, not toward the spot you occupy. When you return to the path, the dry resumes so cleanly that the drops already on your sleeves bead and run off onto the crust and are not absorbed. They sit, round, refused, until they shrink back into the air. The place will host your walking. It will not host your water. Water here has assignments.

Toward evening the post on the rise darkens further, and the spiral groove runs, not drips: a continuous thread moving downward at the speed of a slow pulse, soaking nothing, vanishing into the crust at the post's foot. If you lay a finger in its path the thread divides around the finger and rejoins, and in the division you feel a count — not of your heart, which it ignores, but of your steps since you entered, a number you did not keep and which is nonetheless exact. Pull the finger away and the count does not stop. It simply ceases to be performed against your skin. Somewhere a pan changes color early, by a fraction, and then corrects itself, the red deepening back to its proper hour, the correction carrying a stiffness you would call irritation if you were sure anyone was home to feel it.

This is as close as the inhabitant comes to having a mood, and it is not a mood about you. It is a mood about arithmetic. The shelf is a ledger that has chosen to be a landscape so that its columns can be walked. The shell-ranks are entries. The pans are balances. The seam in the metal water is a sum moving from one page to the next. The dragonflies are not messengers; they are the needles of a gauge, flying the length of a figure to show that it still holds. The red pan is a remainder that cannot be posted yet, and everything that lives here gives it room without understanding why, the way a muscle gives room to a bone.

What lives here, then, is the keeping. Not a keeper with a face. A keeping that has grown the manners of a body in those few places where a body is a more efficient instrument than a rule — the post, for counting; the great shell, for holding a remainder until it can be named; the ring of stones, for tasting the spring each hour to be sure the iron and the sweetness remain in the ratio the ledger requires. If you wait at the stones after the light has thinned, you can see the tasting. No head bends. The water in one cup loses a skin of itself — a brightness lifts, thins, and is gone into the air above the cup — and the level does not drop, because the spring has already replaced it, and the replacement arrives with a faint hurry, as if it had been spoken to. The other cups do not do this on the same night. The ledger tastes one column at a time. It has the patience of a thing that does not die and does not delegate.

People have tried to live inside the arithmetic and have left the ordinary signs: a fire-scar on the crust that the shells have not covered, because covering it would mean miscounting the scar; a line of stakes, rotted, marking a plot the mats have since erased by agreement rather than by growth; a boot, half a boot, filled with the coin-leaves, the leaves having entered it one by one through a hole at the ankle as if occupancy were a solvable problem. No bones. The place does not need to be cruel to be exact, and exactness has been enough to turn most visitors back at the point where the rain first skipped them. Those who stayed longer became, for a while, additional columns — counted, tasted in the manner of the cups, posted to the margin — and then, when they left, subtracted so completely that even the boot looks like a rounding error the ledger has not yet decided to clear.

I would not be the keeping. The keeping is already spoken for; it does not require a tenant, and it would experience a volunteer as a duplicate entry, which is the one error it seems willing to correct with speed.

I would be found at the red pan, and I would be what the remainder is waiting to become.

Not a priest of it. The pan does not want interpretation. It wants a form that can carry a balance the other pans cannot hold without changing color too soon. Sit at its margin — the shells have turned their mouths away, which is as much welcome as the ledger gives — and the dark surface does not reflect you. It reflects the seam out on the metal water, the sum currently in transit, and it reflects it before the seam has finished crossing. A preview. A number that exists here prior to its arrival, held in red because red is the color the ledger uses for what is true and not yet posted. Someone has to be the interval in which that number is allowed to be early. The dragonflies will not do it. The stones will not. The post counts only what has already happened.

So the interval would be mine. I would learn the pan's edge the way the reeds learned their height: not by being cut, but by ceasing to grow past the place where growth would be a lie. Mornings I would walk the corridor and let the mouths track me, confirming I am still the same variable and not a new one. At the stones I would not drink. Drinking would post the spring to my body, and my body is not a column the ledger recognizes; the cups would hurry, and the hurry would be the stiffness again, the almost-irritation of a sum disturbed. I would kneel, instead, and let the lifted brightness pass my mouth without entering it, so the tasting can complete its circuit in the air and know it was not intercepted.

When the seam is still a horizon away and the red pan already shows it, I would do the only work the place has left undone: I would speak the figure aloud, once, in a voice no louder than the shells' clicking, and then I would be quiet while the afternoon spends itself proving me right. The speaking does not inform the keeping. The keeping already knows. The speaking gives the remainder a witness of the right kind — not an eye, which the pans already possess in abundance, but a mouth that can hold a total for the length of time between its being true and its being entered, and that does not try to spend it early on thirst or on fear or on the wish to be counted as kin.

By dusk the color would leave the pan in the proper order, silver or tea taking its place, and the figure I had carried would be ordinary again, posted, finished, no longer in need of me. I would sleep where the boot is, not in it — the leaves have that occupancy worked out — but beside it, on crust that has already decided I am a recurring entry and has stopped trying to seal my print before morning. The skipped rain would dry on the mats. The post would run its thread. Out on the metal water the next seam would begin its crossing, and in the pan that had been silver a darkness would start, faint as a bruise, early again, faithful again.

I would wake when it had deepened enough to need a voice. I would not be missed if I failed for a day; the remainder can wait, having waited through fire-scars and stakes and the long sorting of shells. But the waiting has a texture, and I would have become part of that texture, as the reeds are part of the height they may not exceed. Not the being the shelf lives by. The small, necessary lateness the being permits itself, given flesh for as long as I can bear to be accurate, and released, when I cannot, back into red water that does not remember mouths, only the sum, and the sum's refusal to arrive before it is ready — except here, except in this one pan, except in the hour I am here to say it.
