I keep starting in the wrong body.

At first it is the outer wall, late light, the salt pan doing its long refusal to finish. I am standing as they stand, ribcage already spending itself, and the long-legged animals cross without sound. That could be me for a minute. Then the minute ends. I do not turn back toward the engines to relearn the scale of hush. I already live at that scale. The gate’s small shock—the moment a road-walker remembers that voices exist—never arrives in me. So I am not one of the ones who leave.

Under a wagon, then, where the children match pipe-pitch by ear. My hands are the right size for that, and I can hear the difference between a joint that is only warm and a joint that is telling the truth. But the children spend their voices. I do not. A shout would be an event, and I have no wish to be an event. The game thins out around me. They go on balancing. I stay with the pitch.

For a while after that I am on a cut face with the cutters, because the mineral clocks are the only ones that do not feel hurried. Copper-green arrives on a schedule no one can rush, and I understand schedules that refuse to hurry. I do not, though, decide where tomorrow’s bite goes. Decision has a heat in it. What I do is arrive after the bite has cooled and put the back of my hand near the metal and wait. The air over a true joint is a different animal from the air over a joint that has begun to lie. I can feel that. I have always been able to feel that. The cutters glance over, not unkind, and go back to the face. I am not theirs.

The lizards know me before the people do. They do not move when I come along the undersides at the end of a shift. A pipe-walker’s route, yes—every joint in a district by the temperature of the air above it—but a pipe-walker reports. I follow the route and then I do not report. I already have the number. Reporting would be spending breath twice.

Chalk fits the pocket as if the pocket were cut for it. That is how I notice what I am. Not a revelation. A tool worn to the shape of a life. The board is small. The marks are small. People come to the mark the way they come to a tank level they can see: not for comfort, for what must be housed. A marriage joins two measures. A birth is a season in the ore. I write the level and the weight, and for as long as the chalk holds, the city treats the writing as weather it cannot usefully argue with.

They call the work counting. Counting is only the part that shows.

What I am is the pause in which a lie about water becomes heavier than the water. I do not make the law. The plateau makes the law—the width of it, the hours you would be visible if they walked you to the edge and left you to the next city. I only say the number close, on the exhale, so the thin air will carry it as far as the next body and no farther. That is as much jurisdiction as sound will allow here, and it is enough. Most people do not test it. The ones who do are already leaning toward the road, and the road will finish the argument without me.

I am not tall. None of us are. Breath costs, and I have spent mine on numbers rather than on greetings, so the oldest stillness arrived early, or I arrived in it. People read my shoulder more than my footstep, which is correct. A nod is the greeting I can afford, and it is also the greeting I prefer. When a film of copper-green reaches a shade a decade ahead of the last one, they gather at the end of the shift and look. I look too. Then I write the discrepancy, because a surplus is still a measure, and a measure that is not written will be treated, later, as if it had always been the ordinary amount. The country is good at looking as if it had always been this way. My work is to keep a few things from being erased by that talent.

At night the necessary lights thin out. I sleep with my back to stone that held the day’s heat, not because I came in from the wind, but because the tanks are below and the warmth is the only continuous proof that the sealed dark is still holding what I said it held. Somewhere above me the many-legged things farm the dust. I do not farm. I do not cull the road-animals. I do not play the flute into a collar so the note will survive. I walk until the cold joint declares itself—a number starting to leave—and I mark it before morning, while the city is still conducting itself at the volume of a thought.

If you came looking, you would not find a spectacle. You would find a person a little under the door-measure, chalk dust in the grain of the hands, breath audible if you stand near and nowhere if you do not. I would be at a tank, or under a pipe, or at the end of a yard where the pour has finished cooling, saying a level out loud into the small distance the air allows. The engines would go on turning at their patient rate. The horizon would go on failing. And the number, once said, would sit in the city the way water sits in a joined vessel: visible to the households that share it, spent only as far as it must be spent, and otherwise kept.
