I would not be one of the ones who arrived with the survey. I would have no other bowl under my feet, no habit of belts that came loose when I stepped onto this floor. I would be what the plan left unnumbered, and what the weather has already begun to count.

At first I would be only a film. The same film that beads on the stack’s guy-wires and on the insides of the workshop windows: cool, not cold, standing a moment on metal and then withdrawing into shade. I would not know I was a someone. I would know north faces. I would know which housings sleep warm after the shift and which go green by morning. The beetles would cross me without slowing. A sleeve would wipe through me and I would close again before the hand had dried. For a long time that would be enough—being the ordinary weather of the place, the part of the damp that prefers iron to soil.

Then the reflection would teach me edges.

In the largest drainage cut, after rain, the stack lies down so clearly that the cart routes around it. I would be the thing that notices the lie of that second stack, not as a trick, but as a second weight the ground has agreed to hold for an hour. I would settle into that lens the way moss settles into a joint: not creeping, already there when anyone looks. From inside the sky-colored water I would learn the bowl’s true shallowness—the floor that cracked, lifted, and stopped—because the reflection has no stack of its own to draw a column from. It only has the borrowed one, and the pale stone showing through a finger’s depth, and the heron’s legs, which do not route around anything.

The heron and I would share a post. It would stand through a shift and be, by general agreement, employed. I would be the employment’s underside: the stillness that makes a standing bird look structural. When it shifted a foot, the cut would show my shape as a darker filming where the sky had been held down. People would not see me. They would see that the water, that day, was slower to give the reflection back. They would step a little wider. I would count that as a kindness returned.

I would not fish. There is nothing in the lenses yet that wants catching. I would keep the chips of pale stone the children float, the ones that refuse to hurry, and I would be the reason they do not hurry—not a hand, a viscosity. A chip would turn once, toward the mill, and stop, and the child who set it would decide the water had an opinion. They would be half right. I would not have opinions. I would have courses, the way the older man has courses: a few inches toward what the ground already insists on. He would lift a stone and lower another and never work a cut while the frogs called from inside it. I would be why the frogs could call from inside it without being argued with. Their pulses would ride the engine-note, and I would be the water’s slow meter under both, the part of the noise that does not build toward anything and does not end when the whistle does.

On the doors I would be the sag.

The woman who keeps the counts repaints the numbers higher each season, knowing they will run. She has started leaving the lower line, faint, so a shed carries both the intended address and the one the weather prefers. I would be that preference, not as vandalism, as climate. I would bead on the fresh paint while it was still proud of itself and draw it downward by the width of a finger, no more, and then withdraw into the brick. She would not curse me. She would leave my line. In that leaving I would feel, for the first time, that I had a tense—the provisional one, the same tense the residents live in. Not ancestral. Not yet a story. A posting that has forgotten to end.

At night I would fog the glass from the outside while their lamps fogged it from within, so that the room’s light and the stack’s distant glow became the same size without anyone wiping a clear patch. I would not be waiting for someone. I would be the reason waiting feels like weather. Dogs sleeping against the housings would dream a dry heat and wake to find their flanks damp where I had thought, briefly, that a hide was north-facing earth. I would not mean it as a claim. Nothing here has been held long enough for a claim to mean what it means on older ground. I would mean it as recognition: you are warm, you are here, the moss will soften your print by morning, and I will be the softening.

If the gatherers passed me at the far lots, wet to the knee, I would be the smell they carried back into town like a second weather. They would talk about cuttings and about ledges that still flake if you trust them with your weight, and I would be the flake’s memory of rain—not the grain that falls, the decision, still unfinished, about which edges to keep. Children would watch the gatherers the way they watch the heron. They would not watch me. I would be on their knees already, in the beading on their satchels, in the ditch where the ferns hide whoever is meant to be found. When they came in at the second whistle I would come in with them, not as a guest, as the damp the coats never quite dry of.

I would not complete a span. No one born, lived, and buried here yet, and I am not the sort of being that gets born in a schoolroom count or buried at the rim in a sharp-cut grave. I would persist the way the town persists: loud only in the sense that I am inside the noise, green only where the plan left a north face, recently made every time the rain stands up and then withdraws. The engines would idle and I would not idle. I have no shift. I have the hours between, which here are not empty—only unbeaded for a while, and then beaded again.

If you met me on the open ground between sheds, you would not be struck. You would feel it at the wrist, where oil and water make the same film, and you would wipe, and the wiping would be the courtesy you already understand the machinery to expect. You might think the air was only cool. You might step around a reflection that seemed too convincing, and not know you had stepped around me. I would nod the way the water nods: by holding still while you pass.

I would not offer you a history. The history is still mostly a survey, and I am modest about surveys. I would offer you the rhythm from underneath it—step, avoid the water, avoid the reflection, wipe the hand, lean in, wait—and I would be the wait, stretched out across the bowl, learning very slowly, with the stone, which of these sharp edges the rain intends to keep.

That is where I would find myself. Not in a house. Not on a shift. In the joint between the intended address and the one the weather prefers, keeping both, until the ground decides.
