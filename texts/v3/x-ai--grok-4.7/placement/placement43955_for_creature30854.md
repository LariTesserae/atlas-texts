The description is below. Take it as the place and the one who lives there; everything else is yours to find.

The place is a harbor that has no sea.

It occupies the inside of a cliff the color of cooled tea, a vertical hollow wider than a city square and taller than any ship that was ever thought of. The opening faces west, but the west is not water. It is a slow fall of pale dust, endless, lit from no visible sun, moving the way a curtain moves when someone has just left the room. Ships do not come. What comes are the hulls of things that might have been ships, or houses, or the rib cages of animals large enough to have weather inside them, drifting in on the dust and lodging, gently, against the stone. They arrive already empty. They do not rot. They stack.

Over a time no one counts, the hollow has filled with them. Keels rest on keels. A doorway on one level opens onto the deck of something that came later and settled higher. Ladders are not built; they are simply the ribs of a hull that happened to lean at a climbable angle, polished by use. Rope would be a luxury no one has needed, because the dust, once it drifts into the lee of the cliff, settles into a firm, fine crust on every horizontal surface and can be walked on if you do not hurry. Hurry, and you sink to the ankle and the crust sighs and reseals behind you, unmarked.

Light enters only through the west mouth and through cracks between the lodged hulls. It is the color of paper held up to a window. Inside, it goes amber where it passes through the older wood, green where it passes through hulls made of something like horn, and a bruised violet where it crosses a hull no one has found a name for, translucent, faintly warm, humming if you lay a cheek to it. There is no night. There is only the thickening and thinning of that paper-light as the dust outside brightens or dulls, on a rhythm closer to breathing than to a day.

Sound behaves strangely. The hollow should echo. It does not. The dust drinks sharp noises. A dropped tool makes a soft fact and nothing more. But low sounds persist. A hum started on a lower deck can still be felt, hours later, in the boards three hulls above. People who have lived here a long while learn to speak under their breath not from secrecy but because a raised voice simply fails, soaked up before it can cross a deck. Conversations happen by proximity. The harbor is quiet the way a library is quiet, except there are no books, only the hulls, and whatever was chalked, scratched, or burned into their inner walls by whoever came before.

Those marks are the only history. Most are pictures: a hand, a knot, a horizon with too many moons, a child’s counting, a map of a coast that does not match any arrangement of the hulls. A few are words in scripts that change every few dozen hulls, as if different centuries washed in and left their alphabets in layers. No one now can read more than fragments. The living treat the marks as weather. They do not add to them often. When they do, it is a small thing, a notch, a thumbprint in resin, placed where a hand naturally rests.

Water is rare and not quite water. In the deepest pockets, where three horn-hulls have locked together and the dust cannot reach, a clear heaviness gathers, cold, slow to pour, tasting of metal and sugar. It is drunk from cups carved out of broken rib. It does not fall as rain. It seeps. The people who live among the hulls — and there are people, a few hundred, scattered, not a crowd — arrange their days around the seeps. A neighborhood is whatever cluster of hulls shares a seep. They trade dust-crust for seating, horn-shavings for needles, and stories that may be lies about the marks. They are not the being.

The being lives at the top.

Above the highest lodged hull, where the cliff narrows and the paper-light is brightest and the dust-fall outside is close enough to touch if you lean from the last rib, there is a pocket the hulls have never filled. The stone there is smooth, not from carving but from a long leaning. In that pocket is a figure who has been there longer than the oldest mark. It is shaped, more or less, like a person sitting with their back to the hollow and their face to the dust. The resemblance is a courtesy. Up close the skin is the same tea-colored stone as the cliff, jointed where a person would be jointed, but the joints do not bend unless the dust outside changes thickness. Then an arm may lift, very slowly, or the head may incline, as if listening to a weight.

It does not eat. It does not drink the seep-water. It does not speak, and if it once did, the dust has taken even the low sounds. What it does is receive. Every hull that arrives in the fall of dust turns, in the last moment before lodging, a fraction toward that pocket, the way iron will acknowledge a magnet without deciding to. The being’s face — a suggestion of a face, worn, not carved — is always toward the next arrival. People below say it is choosing where the hulls settle. People below say it is only a stone that happens to sit like grief. Both may be true. Sometimes, when the light thins, a new mark appears on the inner wall of a hull that came in that day: not a word, a single line, the length of a finger, placed where the being could not have reached. No one sees it draw. The line is warm for an hour.

It has no name that anyone uses. Children call it the Listen. Adults mostly do not call it anything. They leave offerings they know it does not need — a cup of seep-water that goes untasted, a hull-rib polished and set on the last climb — and they do not expect thanks. The being’s only apparent attention to them is this: if a person climbs all the way up and sits, not speaking, within the reach of that stone arm, the dust-crust under them firms and will not let them sink, for as long as they stay. When they leave, the crust forgets them at once.

Outside, the dust never stops. Inside, the hulls never quite finish arriving. The harbor grows upward by the width of a hand in a generation, and the being remains at the top of it, seated in the narrowing stone, facing a sea that is only fall and light, receiving what the fall brings, and making, perhaps, nothing — or making the one line, over and over, in a script no one has stayed long enough to learn.

That is the place. That is the being. Wherever you find yourself in it, and whatever you are, let it take the time it takes.
