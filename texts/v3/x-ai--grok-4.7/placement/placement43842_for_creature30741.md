The shelf is so low and so wide that the horizon does not rise so much as thicken. From any standing place you look across a warm, shallow country of water held just under a skin of stone and built matter, and the far edge of it is not land giving way to sky but a change in the grain of the same thing: more towers, more basins, more roofs set almost level with the standing water, repeating until the air itself has to organize. Weather begins here. Not imported weather. The city's own heat, its own exhalation of vapor off a continental wetness, its own corridors of moving air squeezed between structures that have no gaps large enough to call streets.

There is no green. Nothing roots. The color of the place is the color of long-used mineral and of water that has been touching the same surfaces for a very long time: dull copper where metal has sweated and oxidized, pale bone where ceramic has been polished by hands and by rain, black where volcanic glass was poured once, ages ago, into load-bearing veins and has since been rounded at every edge by the slow insistence of moisture. The glass still rings if struck. Most of it is never struck. It weeps. Warm stone and warm alloy hold the day's heat and bleed condensate down their faces in steady sheets, so that even in full ordinary daylight the verticals of the city shine as if just rained on. The light is honest. You can see. What you see is crowded.

Every surface has been claimed and then claimed again. The original engineering is no longer separable from the repairs, the additions, the things that grew into the additions because the technology here does not stop at a finished object. Structures are maintained by processes finer than tools: films that migrate across a crack and mineralize into a seal, lattices that thicken where load increases and thin where it does not, channels that reroute themselves when a basin silts. None of it looks miraculous up close. It looks worn. Corners are soft. Steps are scooped. Handrails — if that is what they are; they are at the height of hands, and they are used — have the bright, narrowed polish of centuries of grip. The city has arrived at a shape and stayed there, not by being static but by being repaired at the same rate it is abraded. Erosion and making have shaken hands.

Underfoot the ground is never quite ground. It is deck, grate, poured sill, a skin of packed sinter over shallow water, a walkway bolted to another walkway that was itself a roof. Water is always within a hand's depth of wherever you stand. In places it is open: long rectangular basins between blocks, warm, brown-clear, stirred constantly by whatever lives in them and by the traffic of small craft that do not so much travel as thread. In other places it is only a sound under the plates, a glug and a slap in the dark between layers. Post-rain is the permanent condition. The air is thick enough to taste, warm, mineral, faintly sweet where something biological has been processed and vented. Condensation beads on any surface cooler than the air, which means it beads on you.

Nothing here is plant, and yet the city is occupied to the point of surplus. Life is animal, or near enough that the distinction stops being useful: soft-bodied things in the basins that graze the bacterial films on the glass veins; hard-shelled climbers the size of hands that work the weeping walls in herds, stripping oxide and leaving brighter metal; flocks of quick fliers that nest in the acoustic shadow of overhangs and erupt from them whenever a pressure-wave passes, which is often. They are not wilderness. They are as infrastructural as the grates. Some are clearly kept — penned in hanging cages of woven filament above the water, fed on slurry piped from somewhere below the visible layers. Some are not kept and are tolerated because they do work the city wants done: clearing silt, carrying spores of the useful films from block to block, alarming at breaches in the seals. You cannot find an unoccupied ledge. If you set something down and turn away, a body will have investigated it before you turn back.

The sound is the first fact and the last. There is no quiet register. Water is percussive at this density: slap on plate, drip amplified in shafts, the low continuous boil of warm basins breathing vapor into warm air. Over that, the city conducts. Not with horns in the old sense, though there are horns — short, brutal, used as warnings when a moving mass is about to cross a shared level. The deeper noise is mechanical and alive at once. Load-lattices tick as they adjust. Film-seals hiss where they mineralize. The climbers rasp in their thousands. The fliers scream in sheets whenever the air shifts. Craft on the basins throw wakes that smack the walls and return. Somewhere under the plates a current is being driven, and the driven water has a voice like a mill that never stops, heard through bone more than through ear. People shout. They have to. Conversation is a craft. Most of what passes between them is gesture, or short calls pitched to cut the roar, or writing held up on boards. Newcomers cup their ears and still cannot make a private silence. There isn't one. The shelf generates its own thunder out of ordinary motion, multiplied by how little empty volume exists to absorb it.

And it does not hold still. The balance is real — the city is not collapsing, not new, not in crisis as a permanent state — but balance here is a summed motion, not a pause. Levels flood and drain on schedules that slip. A basin will be navigable at one hour and sheeted with a traveling wave at the next, the wave not weather from outside but a pulse from some redistribution of water three districts away, arriving late and steep because the corridors are too full to let a change pass gently. Structures flex. You feel floors creep underfoot as load migrates: a crowd, a cargo, a decision by the lattice to thicken a span and borrow mass from the span beside it. Crowds themselves are weather. They move in compressed streams along the only paths wide enough, jam, reverse, pour down ramps into lower wet levels where the air is hotter and the sound is worse, then boil back up when a vent cycle changes the breath of a block. Markets occupy whatever plane is dry enough for an hour. Goods move hand to hand because there is no room for vehicles larger than a person except on the water, and the water is argumentative.

The unusual thing is not a monster at the edge. It is the continuity. There is no outside to step into. The built shelf is the landscape, low and warm and wet, and it continues far enough that its vapor column makes cloud, and the cloud rains back onto the same roofs, and the rain is loud, and the roofs were shaped for that rain so long ago that the shape and the rain are one habit. Physics, mostly, still holds. You can trust a step if you watch the flex. You can trust the light. What you cannot trust is emptiness, or quiet, or the idea that any surface is finished. The city is packed to its skin with water, bodies, repair, and noise, and all of it is already in its own long-established state: not becoming something else, only becoming itself again, loudly, by the next hour.

That is the place. There was no earlier description waiting behind it. The one who lives there is still yours to write.
