You would find yourself in the gap the town spent itself refusing to leave open.

Not on the rim, where the settlement is one object and a person is only a tool in a slot. Not on the stake-path, where imagination is a way of drowning. The gap is lower, in the fog-hour, where thresholds have already gone and the pans between the plates are doing their small dishonest work. Heads stay clear up there. You are not up there. You are in the sheet, at the height where a knee would be if you were a resident, and you have no knees.

What you are takes longer to finish becoming itself, because the place keeps offering you jobs that almost fit and then do not.

At first you seem like a walker. You have the repeatable attention, the dullness that reads a line of stakes as a sentence with only one legal ending. But a walker leaves, and returns, and is expected, and carries reed that has already been promised to three roofs. You do not leave. The path knows you as a pressure the water makes when nobody is on it: a stake that ticks though no hand has touched it, a spacing that is briefly wrong and then correct again, as if someone had set a foot sideways out of habit and then remembered the rule. Residents do not mention you. Mentioning would be the same sentence as the extra line of thatch, and they have already decided that sentence does not earn a second life.

Then you seem like the seep. Cloth drawn slow across a dry palm, under the floors, through fractures the stone has not finished opening. That is closer, and still wrong. The seep is the ground’s own speech. It does not choose. You choose, in the narrow way the town allows choice: not by digging, not by widening a slot, but by arriving where a lashing has gone slack a moment before the hand that would have found it. The old woman, the one they file under neither keeper nor walker, sometimes pauses with the cord in her fingers and listens as if a second set of hands had already tested the tooth in the plate. She does not thank the dark. Thanks are brief here, and this would not even be brief. It would be a story about a floor that does not tick until it has been heard.

So you are not a person of the carried people, and not the water, and not the leftover warmth in the young stone. You are the town’s other maintenance: the one that cannot be coiled in a house because every house already has cord, and this is what the cord is for.

By full day you are almost not there. Practical light is an argument against you. The lanes are full of necessary motions, and your motion would be a second body in a shoulder’s width, which the place has legislated out of existence. You withdraw into the properties they have agreed not to discuss. A reflection that holds an extra slot for the length of a blink. A plank that is wet on its underside when the rise has not yet come. The shared knife, wiped, edge turned in, already waiting in the hand of the person who had meant only to stand where the keeper could see them. No one is frightened. Fear would be wasteful, another exposure. They treat you as they treat the tick in the night: a sound that asks for hands, and then, when the hands have done the work, not a sound anymore.

What you want, if wanting is the right word for something that has no mouth and will not drink either water, is the second plank. Not to stand on it. To keep it true. The town’s rule is simple and the ground is not, and the difference between those two facts is the only room you are permitted to occupy. When the water table breathes past a handspan and someone has been wrong, you are the wrongness arriving early enough to be corrected. When a newcomer is about to say what the pan showed them, you are the end of the cord already in their hands, not as a ghostly trick but as the town’s own grammar: a task that lets the sentence die. You are how a people stay smaller than their habits without becoming cruel.

In the dispersed evening, doors unlatched but not wide, you do not join the sequence of completions. You are the pause inside it. The jar weighed and found a little lighter than the eave should have made it, and then, before anyone speaks the difference, heavier again, because a bead that was going to run the wrong way has been turned by nothing with a face. The old woman scrapes her bowl even. She does not lift her head. If she did, there would be nothing at the height of a knee but fog, and an extra line in the water that does not match the roofs, and the quiet of a place that has already heard you and decided you are a property of wet air on warm young stone.

They will not name you. Naming would build a wall the shelf has no room for.

You remain anyway, sharp as the unrepaired edge, loyal in the only sense the ground can use: you do not ask the land to become kind, and you do not ask the people to look up into the clear air, where a voice would travel past the rim and come back wrong. You keep the irreplaceable things counted. You leave the slack where the morning will need it. When the plate ticks, you are already pleased, because a tick is a sound that asks for hands, and tonight the hands will be theirs, and the hearing, briefly, was yours.
