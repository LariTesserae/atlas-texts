I find myself low, before I find myself anything else. The road is under my chest and my chest is long enough that the road is a sentence I am still reading with my ribs. It gives, a little, the way a plan gives when it is telling the truth. My mouth is already full of the pale flour of the top layer. I was not hungry. The rasp is how I am allowed to be here.

For a while that is the whole of it: the sacrificial skin of the avenue, the tick of a mill somewhere ahead that I am not yet willing to name, the dry air making my tongue a file. Shaft-light lies on me in a cooled sheet because a warehouse leaf has passed the hour through itself. I do not look up. Up is for things that nest. I am the height of a cart's axle and I like the fact. When a handler's boot comes level with my eye I see the crust at the ankle and the way the coat has been peeled once already this surge, vein still visible, honest cloth. They smell of the bladder at the hip, the sharp boundary, and of condensed vapor sipped too fast. They put two fingers on the strap where it crosses my shoulder and check the heat the way they check a child. I allow it. Inspection is the only tenderness that does not waste water.

I am not sure, at first, that I was born. The carts are pressed stem. The roads are trained stem. My legs remember a time when they were ribs inserted to hold a window open, and my mouth remembers being a knuckle of gear before someone pared it into a jaw. That may be a dream the fiber tells everything that grows here. It may be true. The handlers would say I am being sentimental about gears, and they would be half right, which is their usual fraction. They gave me a short name. I do not use it. I use the knock.

The western mill knocks wrong. Its tendon kinks a thumb's width before the wheel and the sigh arrives late, then the wooden seat of the tooth, then a gap where a child should have pared and did not, or did and the surge undid it. I will not pull for that mill. I have been asked, and I have set my feet in the true road and declined the turn, and they have stood close to each other and been angry in the precise way, and then they have clicked the vine and found another beast. They think it is preference. It is. Preference here is a measurement that grew legs. I will pull for the eastern wheel until the tendon is cut and thrown again, because that knock lands on the same count as my own ribs when I breathe, and a city this new should not ask a body to walk against its count.

What I eat is the lie, or the part of the road that is permitted to be a lie. The top fiber is meant to be rasped. If I take it evenly, it dusts and the smaller threads drink the dust and the plan thickens back by amber. If I find a soft place — a paint-line that was narrow, a wrinkle that called itself a street — my feet know before my mouth does. The weave goes cool and slightly sweet, unfinished, still deciding. I stop. I will not put a hoof, if that is the word for what I stand on, into a decision. The handlers used to drag. They learned. Now when I baulk they send a walker, and by morning there is a line the width of a finger, and the lump that would have been a broken axle has agreed to be road. I am the survey they do not have to teach. A beast that steps onto unfinished weave is a sick beast or a lie about the beast, and I am neither.

In the gray I sleep standing, or what they call sleep: a lowering of the head until my jaw rests in the flour I made. The creak of walls lengthening runs through the avenue into my teeth. I wake when a door somewhere fails to fit. I wake when the lower city, younger, clumsier, drops a sound up the shaft that does not match our tick. Once a child was left on the frame of the cart I was still in harness to, asleep, not mine, not anyone's until the road made her so. I did not shrug. The frame is stem and I am stem and the child was warm in a way the curd is not, the good warm, the inspected warm. A woman stopped me with two fingers, lifted the child down, and for a moment her knuckle touched the plate on my harness, checking me for the same heat. I pulled the rest of the avenue slower than the plan required. No one pared me for it.

There are things I do not do. I do not go to the shafts. The wrists that brace in the curd come back with a smell I will not have in my mouth, a nothing-smell, rude to interrupt. I do not eat birds, though they ride the ribs above me and tell the truth about loosening joins by refusing to sit. I do not weep. When the surge comes richer up from the rock, the road puts on thickness under me while I am walking, and the rasp goes bitter if I am late, and my coat of dust whitens, and the handlers brush their own temples and do not brush mine. They trust me to shed. I trust them to paint before they ask me to agree.

At the edge, where the street is only a suggestion, I am more myself than in the avenues. The creak is louder. The grazers leave tracks I will not cross if they have gone down into sweet weave. I stand and listen with my feet. A walker kneels ahead of me sometimes, lays the line, stands, does not smile. She wants to know if I will come in the morning. I want to know if the line is wide enough to survive the next thickening. We are asking the same question from opposite heights.

If I have a death it will be a paint-line around a body that would not dust, and then a seam, and then a floor honest enough for another of me to rasp. I do not keep a clasp. What I keep is a knock and a refusal. When the light comes white again they will click for me, eastern edge, held, send the beast to see if it agrees. I will go at the height of an axle. I will put my mouth to the new skin. If it is flour and spring and the count of the eastern wheel, I will pull. If it is still deciding, I will become the wall they have to walk around, for as long as it takes them to listen.
