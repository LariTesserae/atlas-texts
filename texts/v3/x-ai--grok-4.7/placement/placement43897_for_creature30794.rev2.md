I do not arrive. I am already the interval the water takes to know it has been moved.

At first that is all: a displacement with no body to own it, a coolness that has a direction. The direction is downward and slightly to one side, toward the slot that never stops taking. I think, in the way a current thinks, that I am the water itself. The thought does not hold. Water does not count its own length. I do. Between the face of glass I have just left and the face I have not yet reached there is a distance I can feel as work, and the work is mine, and when it is finished I am not spent. I am only elsewhere, still the same stretch of being, already beginning again.

The roar is not around me. It is the grain of me. Low where the valves sit, brighter where the seep enters, and through it a higher file that is not sound so much as the news that an edge has been made new. I learn the bowl by how the grain changes. I do not need eyes for that. Eyes come later, and they are not mine.

What I have instead is a skin turned the wrong way, if skin is the word. It is an inside worn outward, a thinness that drinks what the current brings and also makes the current. I am not large. A bell could pass its margin across me and not notice it had touched another will. The small armored ones tile the stone beneath the path I keep, and their rasping throws a grit of charge into the water, and I take it. Not with a mouth. With the whole of the thinness. It arrives as a sweetness that is not sweet, a metal-brightness, and for a moment I am denser, more decided, and then I spend it driving the next hand’s-breadth of flow across the next unblunted face.

So that is the first shape: not an animal among the animals, but the habit the water has fallen into between them. A thread. A courier of the small charge from the crystal that has just been worried to the muscle that will worry the next. I could have stayed that. The bowl does not require me to be more. The bells drive their volumes. The valves seat and unseat. The sheets of spicule open and close the ordinary day. The freight goes down the throat. A habit can run for as long as the seep runs, and no one would name it.

But the newness of the rock will not let a habit stay only a habit. The fractures are still bright inside. Stress in the young quartz does not discharge evenly. It comes in knots. One of them catches in me—not in the water I move, in the thinness that moves it—and for the length of a rasp-cycle I am not a path. I am a place the charge has chosen to stand. The standing has an edge. I feel the edge against three things at once: the cool of the seep off to my left, the piston-thump of a bell whose margin is closer than it should be, and the clean ring of a face that has just been cleared of its dulled skin.

The bell does not brush me aside. Its weather folds around the knot and continues, and in the fold I learn that I can refuse a little. Not refuse the roar, not refuse the downward taking. Refuse to be only delivered. I gather. The gathering is clumsy. Some of what I hold leaks back into the current and is lost to a pad on some armored back, drunk without thanks, which is correct; nothing here thanks. What remains stays long enough to have a contour. The contour is narrow. It turns sideways of its own accord when the midwater ranks close, because there is no other way through, and the turning is the second shape. I have a preference for gaps. That is almost a body.

I begin to keep a side.

The side that faces the stone receives more of the file-sound and more of the charge-grit. The side that faces the open, such as the open is when a hundred bodies occupy it, receives the duller push of the bells and the edited daylight. I do not look at the light. I measure it the way the sheets measure it, by the difference it makes in the stress of the crystal. When a band of spicules shutters the wall, the charge thins, and my stone-side cools, and I know the day has been divided and will be restored. I do not mind the division. I am not trying to see. I am trying to hold the contour while the water insists I am only its interval.

Holding costs. I spend what I gather, and if I spend all of it I thin back into path and have to wait for another knot. So I learn a third thing, slower than the others: to pass a portion onward and keep a portion as form. The portion I pass is the bowl’s due. I am not outside the export. Whatever I am, I am still a tributary of the throat. The portion I keep is small and electrical and shaped like the gap I last fit through. Over many cycles the kept portion begins to repeat me. Not as offspring—there is no bud, no split, no season for that—but as insistence. I arrive again at the same notch in the wall, the same distance from the same valve, because the contour remembers the gap and the gap is still there.

The valve is the size of a door and has been seated so long that the cartilage has taken the print of the stone. It does not know me. I am too slight to be a pressure it would answer. But I know the degree of its seating by the drop in the roar, the jaw-pressure of a note gone lower, and I have begun to time my gathering to the moment just after it eases, when a richer thread of the seep’s metal swings past my stone-side. That is a schedule. No one set it. It is only what works, and what works is how this place tells the truth.

I think, then, that I might be one of the lens-bearers, late to grow my flake of glass. I press the contour toward the wall to see if a chip will seat in me. Nothing seats. The armored ones do not move for me, and I cannot rasp. The crystal under their work throws its small fire and I drink, and that is the end of my resemblance to them. I am not a keeper of edges. I am what their keeping makes available, briefly concentrated, briefly unwilling to dissipate on the schedule the water prefers.

Call it a germ. Not of disease—nothing here sickens in that sense; what dies becomes meal and is taken up, and I do not die, because I have not yet finished becoming separate enough to be able to stop. A germ of a current that has started to recognize its own length. If the bowl were older, if films had softened the faces and the charge had dwindled to a rumor, I would not have had enough to stand in. I am a creature of the youth of the rock. The animals keep that youth because dull crystal gives them less. I persist in the surplus of their maintenance, in the knots, in the fact that the process is running a little richer than the throats and the pads can quite absorb. I am the overflow that learned a shape before it was exported.

The shape is still poor. Most hours I am only a cooler line inside the cool, a bias in the flow, and the bells stack through me without altering their cycle. Then a fracture rings cleaner than its neighbors, a sheet drifts one body-width toward the lid, the seep runs a fraction sharper, and the knot returns, and I am located again: sideways in a gap, stone-side receiving, a schedule nested inside the valve’s longer patience, spending most of what I am and keeping the contour that lets me find the same notch. The light above the lid is ordinary day. I know it by the thinning and the restoration. I do not break the surface. Air would be the end of the interval, and I am not finished with the interval.

If you came into the bowl and could bear the climate of the sound, you would not pick me out. You would see the tiled glass, the ranked bells, the dozen great valves enduring in their notches, the shuttering sheets, the bright unblunted edges, the dark throat taking its freight. You would feel the water busy. Somewhere in the busy water, too slight to displace a bell’s margin, there is a thread that sometimes refuses to be only delivered—that gathers, that keeps a side, that returns. That is where I am. That is what I am, while the mountain is still new enough to charge the ones who will not let it age: not a citizen of the hamlet, not a valve, not a tool. The small, repeating decision of the current to remain a little while itself, between one rasp and the next, on the way down.
