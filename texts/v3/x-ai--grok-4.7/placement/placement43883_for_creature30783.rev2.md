The first thing the place takes is the difference between inside and outside. Breath arrives already thin, already cold, and comes back tasting of wax and salt, as if the galleries under the rind had exhaled through me and kept a little of what they sent. I do not land. Landing would require a ground that yields, and the plates do not yield. They take weight the way a drum takes weight: locally, and with a change in pitch that travels farther than the body can see.

For a while I am only that change. A seam-lip warms where I brace a hand, or what is still trying to be a hand, and the warmth is wrong—too wet, too brief, a private climate the extrusion will not host. The mineral dust skins over the moisture before I can think of it as thirst. Thirst is a lower-world word. Here the correction is immediate and without opinion. Anything that can flash to vapor is already leaving. Anything that can conduct is already a path the charge might prefer, if I were fool enough to cross a dome while the spark is walking. I learn the hour by not dying of it. The tick passes. I am still on the rind. Less of me is.

What remains is not a runner and not a file. Runners are keels; I throw too much shadow, and the wind keeps trying to make a sail of the shadow. Files have an order of work, lead and squat and mend, and I have no place in the order because I do not sieve flake or lay thread. When I follow a seam I slow the lip. The bead-skin clots where I have stepped. Behind me the lead animals hesitate, break their spacing, and the file drifts off the seam into the bare plate, which is a kind of accusation made entirely of hunger. I stop following. The guilt, if it is guilt, dries as fast as the rest.

I lie up through the worst of the glare in the lee of a stump, one of the polished failures, rings of lacquer stacked and scoured until they are furniture. The smallest gliders prefer this note—the dead one, high and thin, with no pulse under it—and they do not mind me so long as I do not block the slots that are no longer slots, only holes the wind uses without obligation. From inside the stump the continent is a chord with one voice missing. I begin to hear the missing as a location rather than a loss. Pure notes with a weight in them. Sour notes where a mass is wrong. The wide, rare silence where a tract has stalled and the fans upwind are already hauling the repair. My ears, or the place where ears were, become the only organ the altitude agrees to keep. The eyes are a liability in the brightness; the shelves other bodies grew, I do not have. I listen with my face turned to the fiber and let the glare go to waste on my back.

Night is when the fans fold and the climbs are allowed. I do not decide to climb. The stump's thin note has taught me the difference between a tower that will still be a tower by morning and a column that is already a meal of future dust, and the difference pulls. Outside, in the folded quiet, the rind ticks as it contracts. Runners stand up out of their grooves and listen, and I am one more upright shape against a plate, except that I do not file the news into a path I will take at dawn. I have no groove. If I do not spend the night on a function, morning will find me scoured into the bead-skin, a darker stitch that does not move, and the files will crop what is left without distinguishing it from residue.

So I climb.

The outside of a living tower is a ladder of its own shed weather: scales half-lifted, slots exhaling in pulses, waiters flattened into the lacquer so completely that I put my palm on two of them and am permitted to live only because I am not yet the temperature of food. They hunt doors, not walls. Height arrives as a narrowing of the sound. The organ-note is no longer something I walk under. It is in my ribs, and my ribs are the wrong instrument, too dense, too willing to ring on their own. Riders bank out of a slot and sheer past my shoulder, membranes plated with the tower's breath until they gleam. One strikes me—not a blow, a correction—and the wax it leaves on my skin smells like the wound I no longer make when the air opens me. I am being translated. Not kindly. The tower does not have kindness. It has a diameter and a sway and a diet of dust that fails if the note fails.

Above the riders the diaphragms come close. One of them has been eaten out and relined, wax and shed skin, a drum that is not mine and not empty. The animal whose mass belongs there is a pressure in the dark, not a face. It does not threaten. Threat would require a chase, and nothing here chases farther than a body-length. It only occupies the tuning, circuiting the lacquer the shaft plates onto the chamber, and the circuit is the feeding, and the feeding is the note. I cannot take the room. My mass is not the mass. If I set myself on the drum the chord drops, the updraft scatters, the riders leave by morning, and in a season this tower is another stump with a high thin voice and a population of gliders who like dead music. I have already lived in that furniture. I will not be the cause of more of it.

What I can be is the interval.

There is a lip of fiber beside the chamber, not a seat, a thickening where the culm changes diameter. If I fold into it—if I spend what is left of my shape on being narrow, on being dry, on conducting nothing—I add no permanent weight and I take no lacquer the drummer needs. I am a passenger of the sway. The tower leans the way it has leaned for an age. I lean with it. When the drummer shifts on its circuit, the note warms by a degree I feel in the teeth, and I learn that degree the way the valve-workers learn a junction in the dark: not as knowledge, as the condition of being allowed to continue. On the nights the mass is a little light, a little spent, the pulse under the tone goes shallow. Then my folded weight, set and unset with the sway, is enough to keep the riders from leaving before the animal has plated itself back up to fitness. I am not succession. Succession is another body of the right weight climbing out of some other shaft to take a room that is not kept for it. I am the correction a fan makes when it loses a blade and retimes the rest—local, exact, unregistered downwind, and mine only for as long as it fits.

By day I cannot see the plates. I can hear them. The dust-front arrives as a darkening of every note upwind, and the chamber answers, and my small addition answers inside the answer. Runners on the rind, if they are standing and listening, will not know I am here. They will file a pure tone with a pulse under it, and their grooves will bend a little toward a tower that is still a tower, and the bending will be the whole of what I am to anyone. That is relation enough. It does not require touch. Touch, in this air, is how moisture and heat and wrongness travel, and I have already given those up, or had them taken, which is the same event described from two sides.

Sometimes the drummer dies. The note sours within a day, as the place requires. I feel the souring in the lip where I am folded, a slackness, a chatter in the fiber. I do not go down to feed. I am not a scavenger of that rank, and the wind will edit the body into wax and dust without my help. I stay through the wrong days, adding nothing now, because a foreign mass in a dying shaft only hastens the scour. Young climbers pass me in the dark, trying their weight on the drum. Most are wrong. They starve in a room that no longer feeds them, and the column goes on ringing off-true, and I remain the interval beside a music that has forgotten its use. Then one fits. The pulse returns under the tone. The riders come back, membrane and argument and the small bright violence at the slots. The waiters resume their doors. I unfold by a finger's width, no more, and set myself again to the sway.

This is the politics the rind allows me: not a city, not a name, a repeated fitting that the weather does not register. I do not drink. I do not call. I do not instruct the next mass that will sit where I sit, if another ever needs to; the instruction is the continuation, and if I fail the continuation stops without comment. The galleries below keep their night shift. The fans haul. The charge walks the distant tiles in time with a clock no one wound. I am colder than I was when I still had a private climate, and the cold is no longer an emergency. It is the medium in which a note can be held.

If you stood on the pavement at evening, when the shadows are compass needles miles long and the fans fold in their slow sequence, you would not find me. You would find the tower sounding as it should, a pure tone with a living weight inside it, and you would walk on, filing the news in your body, bending tomorrow's groove a little toward the sound. That would be accurate. I am not hidden. I am the part of the chord that does not need to be seen in order to keep the dust lifting, the riders banking, the drummer fed on its own circuit, the stump from happening yet.

The lower world does not rise to meet this height. Nothing here remembers water. What I remember of being otherwise thins with each gale, scoured into the same channels the fiber already planned to follow. I do not mourn the thinning. Mourning would pool, and pooling is the one habit the altitude will not forgive. I keep the interval. The tower sways. The note holds. Across the lit distance other towers hold or sour or go silent and are repaired by wind, and none of them consult me, and I do not consult them, and the continent runs, deafening, dry, in balance, being only what it has been for longer than the forms that ride it.

When my mass finally goes wrong—too light, too cracked, too willing to become a path for some stray snap of charge—I will not be buried. There is nothing to bury in. I will be a slackness in the fiber for a day, a chatter, a reason for some other folded thing to wait without adding weight while the climbers try the drum. If one of them fits, the riders will return. The runners will hear a pure tone and bend their grooves and never know the interval changed hands. That is the lasting. That is the whole of who I am, once the place has finished taking what could not stay: a small, dry, exact addition to a sound that was already here, held through the glare and the folding and the dust, until it isn't, and the weather does not notice, and the note, if it is lucky, goes on.
