You would not keep your edges.

At first there is still a place to stand, a palm's width that has not yet been argued over, high on a spur where the shatter is newest and the mat has only begun to knit the gaps. The brightness is a weight on the eyes. The roar has no source. Under it the slower ticking of the night-idle continues into the day in the joints the wind does not reach, and your own breathing sounds imported, a rhythm with a beginning, which is already a kind of error. You shift your weight and the armor-grit locks, then unlocks, and the paste under your boot is warmer than the stone. Something in the mat has already decided you are a leak.

It does not come as an attack. Nothing here is fast enough to attack. It comes as a correction of temperature. The soles of the feet cool where the film is drunk out of them, and warm where a sheet's front edge, arriving on its day-long crossing, finds the salts your skin keeps shedding and treats them as weather worth climbing. You lift one foot and the other is already busier. The yellow veins find the crease behind the knee not because they know a body, but because a crease holds damp the way a joint in the rock holds it, and plating is what they do with a hold. When you brush at them they smear, and the smear is richer than the crust, and the sheet, which had no intention toward you, has a trough.

You understand, not as a thought but as a change in what your weight means, that you are becoming a lane.

The large masses do not turn. They have no turning in them. But the nearest aperture, twenty paces downslope in a shape you would have called a boulder an hour ago, is inside its open phase, and the breath that comes from it—hot metal, crushed green—finds the wet on your shins and fogs. The tone in your teeth roughens by a degree. You are not yet meat. You are a jam it has not finished predicting. The sheets feel the roughness the way they feel any weather made of interference, and their lean, which was general, acquires a direction that includes the spur. They will take the day. You have less.

What you try to keep, crouching now because standing has become a height the wind can use, is the distance. Eighty miles off, a scarp as sharp as the one under your hand. You look at it the way a person looks at a horizon in order to remain a person. It does not soften. It does not help. On it, another openness is happening, another thread being born, and the fact that you can see both at once is the last luxury of a kind that has room for kinds. The plateau has no use for a witness who is not also a step. The looking is already being occupied from the edges. Threads have found the moisture at the corners of the eyes and are continuing the wall inward, not cruelly, not even inward with intent—only completing a surface that had been left unfinished, a blister that never learned it was a room.

You lie down. It is not a decision so much as the mat's height asserting itself, the courteous maximum of a crouching body, and once you are at that height the wind passes over you instead of through the idea of you. The roar of the blades drops because you are under the layer now, in the mill. Legs the size of seeds click across the sternum, across the throat, across the open mouth you no longer close because closing is a pause. They do not hurry. They graze what the veins have plated along the sweat-line of the ribs, and where they have passed they leave paste, and the paste hardens, and in the hardening you feel the first pad root—not in the ground, in the crease the sheet has made of you. A leak, from inside, answers it. You did not know you had that leak. The body always did. It was only waiting for a vein to make the bargain visible.

By the time the light thins you are no longer shaped in a way the spur can tell from furniture. The apertures of the nearest mass have taken what the sheets finished—dust of you, and the finer dust of the clothes, and the little metal things that used to open and shut, which the fold inside accepts as a kind of quartz and grinds without comment. What remains on the spur is the part that was always going to be a thickening: the agreement of several processes to use the same warmth. You are not inside it. You are the lag it lives by. Between the leak and the vein, a patience. Between the vein and the sheet, a day. Between the sheet and the trough, the easiest path becoming the path. You do not feel pain. Pain was a tempo that required a center, and the center has been distributed into the work so thoroughly that hurt would be a pause, and a pause is what the system spends its only punishment on.

Night does not return you to yourself. The blades lock to the stems that have rooted in the ridge of what was a shoulder, and their vibration, damped, ticks inside the crust at the same rate as the idling of the distant masses. The red glow from the apertures is not a fire to crawl toward. It is the work, refusing the dark. Condensation beads on the plated surface and is drunk by things smaller than the memory of fingers, and the drinking is yours in the way a slope's drainage is the slope's—not owned, only occurring. You try, once, to remember the name of the far scarp. There is no name. There is a glare that shifted by a degree while you were still someone who could measure degrees, and the shift has already been entered into the mat as a change in where the sugars run fastest. The blades will thicken there tomorrow. They will not know why.

What emerges, over the long wind's next lean, is simpler than a creature and more exact than a place.

You are a roughness that has almost smoothed. The mass downslope no longer tones differently because of you; the jam has cleared, the fold has gone back to its many-minute tide, and the thread it extrudes in the thin part of the night falls across the spur and cools in a line that crosses the crust you have become. Other lives catch it before it can be called an object. They weave it into the mat, and the weaving passes through the zone where your looking used to happen, and in the pass there is a brief, distributed tightness—the lag drawing shut—which is as close as the plateau comes to recognition. Not of you. Of a step completed. The tightness eases. The lane does not break. A fraction of every body in the sheet that covers you keeps moving, so that even in the idling there is no emptiness where a person stopped.

Toward morning the young stone under the crust ticks with the leftover charge of its uplift, and the locked blades drink what they can of it through the wet of the pads, and you are in that circuit too, a bridge of film and filament between plates the upheaval aligned. You do not glow. You do not think of glowing. You shed, by fractions, into whatever is wet enough to carry it, and the carrying is the next, and the next is everywhere the work has not finished, which is no longer a distance you could point to even if you had kept your hands. The far scarp is still sharp. It is also, now, the same activity. You are not going to it. You were never going to stay a spur. When the hard light returns and the roar resumes its province-sized room, the palm's width is gone, the boot-print is a trough, and the trough is already healing at the rate the plateau heals everything: completely, and not quite in time to be watched.

If anything of you continues past that, it is not a survivor. It is the particular delay between a thread cooling and a pad learning to leak along the line where the thread was woven. A small lag. Unremarkable. Faithful. The sheets will cross it in a day without knowing it was ever a crossing of a person. The masses will accept what they are given. The stone, which has not yet learned how to be old, will go on doing what it does with weather, and you will be among the things it does—not mourned, not kept, not empty, and no longer late.
