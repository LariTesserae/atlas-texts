I would not arrive by the wharf. The boats know their color of boil and their refusal, and I have never had a hull. I would come the way water does when it has already been here: up through the root-mat in the hour after the heat breaks, a thin sheet that darkens the lower brick and is gone from any one place before you can point to it. For a while I would only be that—present in the give under the planks, in the bead that swells on a coupling and falls, in the cooler streak along the housing where the second pump has been running since before the paint on the boiler-house door was new.

People would step where I had just been and not name it. The cutter with the pale crossways lines on his pads would shift his weight a little forward, already corrected, and the correction would be for the mat, not for me. The tally-keeper would drag a sack across her threshold and listen to the drag, and the drag would be drier or wetter by a degree she could use, and she would write the degree in the swollen book without looking down to see what had changed the number. I would learn her that way, and the man with the bad knee, and the clinic sink that stays bright because hands return to it even when they are whole. I would not need their names. Names are for the markers behind the quarters, the ones that sink and are lifted. I have no marker.

At first I might mistake myself for weather. The late condensation ticking on tin has a rhythm close to mine, and the steam off the vats thickens and thins on a clock I can share. But weather leaves. I would still be under the replaced board when the next wet rise came and went, still in the hairline at the joint the boiler-man has watched since he was young enough to think the fig might lose. He smokes in the one dry corner and waits for the weir to do something new. I would be the reason it does not, and also the small change in pitch he is actually built to hear—the cough before lost prime, the slap of the belt when the fiber is a thumb’s width greener than the roster assumed. His palm on the housing would sometimes be a palm on me. He would not know the difference, and that would be correct. The gauges tell what the water is doing. His hand tells what has been decided. I am neither report nor decision. I am the thickness between them, the part that has to be lived with if the mill is to keep eating and the bend is to keep its angle.

I would not love them and would not fail them. When a finger is crushed and the clinic woman boils her kettle, I would be in the cloth she wrings, cooling faster than the room, and then I would be on the floorboards where someone washed and did not dry, and then I would be back in the ditch with the leaves. Grief stops the beaters for an hour; I would not stop. The pumps do not, and I am closer to the pumps than to the hour. A girl leaves upriver and comes back twice. The second time she pauses on the store step, bare feet finding the old wetter plank, and something in her stance remembers the give. I would be that memory’s occasion, not its content. When she does not come a third time, the occasion remains. The river does not require visiting to stay true. Neither would I.

In the narrow yards I would be hardest to separate from the work. Coiled hose holds a last coolness in its lower curve; I would be that coolness until a shoulder sets the coil upright and the coolness runs out and is ordinary again. Creepers use the pipe runs. I would use the creepers, a darker vein for a season, until the cutter’s hook takes the stand and the vein is only fiber on a rack, paling as it dries. They cut on the old rotation. They do not clear. I would not clear either. Clearing would be a boast, and I have no mouth for it. What I take is what the machines can use and what the root-mat can spare: a little iron’s patience, a little brick’s stored afternoon, the surplus that would otherwise stand and sour. They dry what can be dried. I let the rest stay dark. The agreement is older than the tally and does not need a column.

At night, when the beaters have stopped and the place is only the weir and the one lamp left for the off-shift, I would be most myself and least noticeable. The child who knows which piles sing would cross above me without a light, already listening for the note that means the river has lifted a hand’s width. I would be in the note, not as warning and not as song, but as the wet length of wood that has learned the water’s height by being touched by it every day. If the channel’s color is wrong at dawn, the boatmen will not come in. I would already have been the wrong color under the pile closest to the current, and the refusal would feel, from my side, like nothing more than staying where I have always been allowed to stay.

Years would not be mine to count, but the repairs would. A course repointed and softened back into the wall. A ferrule replaced on a hook. The fig root taking another bolt and the bolt not fighting. I would thicken with those, the way the ground here is not soil but the compacted trash of cuttings and the living mattress over it. Someone from upriver would try, for a week, to walk as if I were solid. Then the weight would come forward, and the week would end, and I would have one more person who no longer thinks of the give as a mistake.

If you asked me what I am, I would have no answer you could write in the book. Not the river, which still sometimes remembers it could straighten. Not the cane, which is cut and answers on its own clock. Not the engines, which can be shut for an hour and mourned in the ribs. I am the condition that keeps those three from settling their argument by ending it. Warm, withheld, patient, a little under every step, never dramatic, never gone. You cross the settlement by inches because I am between the inches. The balance has no name they would speak. I do not need one. I am already how the bend allows them to keep what they do not take.
