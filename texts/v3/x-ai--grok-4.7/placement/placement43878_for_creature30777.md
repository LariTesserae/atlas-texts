The rim does not hold me. I learn that by standing there until the fracture-edge prints its cold into the balls of my feet and the roar has stopped being a sound and become the pressure my ribs are shaped against. Looking down, the basins are only a darker version of the dusk already on my hands. Nothing calls from them. The birds do not glance up. If I were meant for the lip I would feel the pull of the outer country at my back, the ordinary wind, the grasses that still know a sun. I do not. The outer country is already the memory of a roof.

So I go down. Not by any path—there is none—but by the fresh joints, the places a plate has tilted and left a stair of edges. The stone takes skin. By the time the light has thinned to the color of the deeper walls, my knees are filmed with the same pale dust that sits in the cracks, and the dust does not brush off. It agrees with me. I stop on a ledge wide enough for a body and no more, and the rank of birds parts by the exact width of my shoulders and then closes again against my sides, warm, ticking, indifferent. Their feet grip the same stone mine do. For a while I think this is it: I am one more intercept, flicking into the insect layer and back, throat working, the count unchanged by my arrival. But the birds' eyes stay on the drift. Mine keep going lower, past the layer, to the open floor that is not empty.

I leave the ledge. The drop is nothing; the scree catches and turns me and stands me up among angular blocks still sharp from the day the ceiling failed. Here the roar is only a roof. Under it the air is cooler, and the small runner is already at the edge of the nearest block, head lifted, accounting for me the way it accounts for any new spill. It does not flee. It waits to see whether I will be waste or weather. I lower myself until my palms are on the grit, and the runner becomes a stone again, satisfied or simply finished. The posture fits. Belly close to the young rock, I can feel the drip arriving one grain at a time, the slow budget of the place moving through the floor beneath me.

I am not the runner. The runner takes the remainder and vanishes into joints; it is sized for the spills. What I am sized for is the holding itself. I stretch out along the base of a roof-block whose turf is still dying in place, and the length of me matches the block's shadow, the long cool bar it lays across the basin. Where my side rests, condensation beads and does not fall. The seep that darkens the wall above finds its way down the joint behind my shoulders and films the stone under my ribs, and the film does not advance past me and does not retreat. I am a level. The tea-colored pool a little farther out keeps its shoreline against the slight dam of my hip; fish rise and sink in their preferred layer and never test the crust where I have settled. Insects that miss the walls and the bills drift down and come to rest in the dry hols between my arm and the scree, and the runner, when it returns, takes them from there as from any other margin I have made by being still.

Time does not ask me to move. The dim does not change, so I do not change. A bird falls, slides, opens; the smaller beaks work; what they leave crosses the short distance to the crook of my knee and is finished by the runner before the next drip has formed. I feel the transfer as a small warmth that equals itself and is gone. Higher, the nap of hanging bodies breathes its continuous exchange, and the breath of that exchange reaches me only as a faint, regular cooling across the back of my neck, no different from the cellar air. I do not count them. Counting would require a season, and the country has spent its one season already—the collapse—and is now only the rate.

If I lift my head I can see, across the low divide, the next basin repeating the same arrangement: open floor, sealed pools, sheets on the faces, the living ceiling of wings. Nothing in me wants to cross. The divide is only another young edge, and I am already the edge this basin required. My weight keeps a little more dust from traveling; my stillness gives the condensate one more surface that does not yet know how to be worn smooth. The turf on the block above me yellows at the speed of the leaked light, and a few of its last seeds tick down onto my shoulder and stay, unsprouted, because I cast the same insufficient dim the rest of the floor casts. They will not become a cover. I will not become a ruin or a monument. There are no hands to read either.

What I am, then, is the pause the water needed and the stone had not yet grown. Not a creature that hunts or breeds or billows, but the place where the budget is allowed to be exact—one more cool, damp, unmoving fact among the facts that do not drift. The roar continues above me as weather. The runner comes and goes, a stone that sometimes remembers. The fish hold their saturation on the other side of my hip. And the rim, far up, leaks its small day onto all of it, onto me, and does not know I have joined the floor.

I will be here when the next fragment falls. It will crack, lost in the sound, and the scree will accept it beside me, and the sheet of birds will not alter its thickness, and neither will I. The country finished becoming when the ceiling came down. I am only how it holds.
