The basin holds its light the way a closed hand holds a coin: fully, without flicker, as if noon had been poured in once and never asked to leave. It is warm. Not the wet warmth of a greenhouse, not the glare of sand. A dry heat that sits on the skin and does not climb, the temperature of a stone that has been in the sun long enough to forget the night and not long enough to burn. There is no wind worth the name. Sound arrives only as the small tick of cooling mineral and, farther off, a pressure in the ear that never becomes a noise.

Nothing here has been worn down. The ridges that break the floor are new in the geological sense and finished in every other: edges still crisp, fracture faces still showing the grain of their making, no talus at their feet, no softened lip where weather should have argued with stone. They look as if they rose this morning and then the morning was withdrawn from the world and stored somewhere else. Time, locally, does not proceed. It is present, and it is complete.

There is no water. Not in the joints of the rock, not as vapor you can taste, not as a darkening anywhere the light falls. The chemistry that stands in for it is visible only as a faint iridescence on certain faces—thin films of something that is not wet and not quite solid, a solvent of fields rather than molecules, beading nowhere, running nowhere, present the way charge is present on a rubbed surface. Things grow in it. They do not drink.

What grows is dense enough that the open basin still feels close. From any low rise the country runs out for a long way: a second ridge, a third, a pale flat between them the color of unfired clay, then a darker shelving where the growth thickens until individual forms stop being separable. Town-sized, valley-sized, and then more of the same beyond the haze, which is not moisture but a fine suspension of shed scales. The features repeat without becoming a pattern you could map and trust. Distances agree with each other only while you are not measuring them.

The growth is lush in mass and poor in kinds. Most of it is a low, branching crust, warm to the touch, the color of old brass gone green at the edges—not verdigris, something more like light caught in a lattice and allowed to stay. Stalks rise from it in layers: a ground mat that locks plate to plate, a mid-story of flat blades set edge-on to the sun so that walking among them is like moving through a library of shut books, and above that a canopy of slow spirals, each spiral a single surface folded until it occupies more volume than its material should permit. They do not sway. They do not grow while you watch. They are already as large as they will be, packed until neighboring spirals nearly touch and do not. Between the near-touches there is a dark that is only shadow, ordinary, correctly shaped, and then, a hand’s breadth aside, a dark that is not the shadow of anything standing there. That second dark does not move when you do. It has been allocated to the spot.

Nothing constructed stands in the basin. No wall, no cut stone stacked on purpose, no road worn by feet. And yet the growth is not wild in the way an untouched wood is wild. The blades meet the crust at angles that repeat to a tolerance no organism keeps without a rule underneath it. Break a spiral—though breaking is difficult; the material gives like horn and then remembers—and the interior is not pith. It is a nested geometry, chambers the width of a hair opening onto chambers you cannot see the end of, surfaces machined by no tool you could name, continuous with the living outside. The engineering is the organism, or the organism is the remainder of an engineering that finished and did not leave. Current accounts of matter do not quite cover what the chambers are doing. They are not storing anything you can remove. They are maintaining the basin’s refusal to change: a distributed correction, nanoscale and basin-wide, that files every incipient crack back to sharpness and every incipient wilt back to full color. Youth, here, is an actively kept condition.

Fauna is almost an absence with exceptions. For a long time there is only the growth and the light and the allocated shadows. Then, rarely, a thing the size of a fist detaches from a spiral’s underside—not falling, unhooking—and crosses a gap by occupying the positions between its start and its end without a visible crossing. It is the same brass-green. It has no face. Where it settles, a spiral that had been perfect shows, for the duration of a glance, a small asymmetry, and then the correction takes the asymmetry back. You do not hear it. If you are still, you may feel the air tighten, once, as if a calculation had used the space you are standing in and returned it unused.

The rules are simple and do not hold everywhere. Light is daylight and behaves as daylight on skin, on the clay flats, on the true shadows. On the growth it sometimes arrives twice, a second, fainter illumination from a direction the sky does not contain, enough to read by and not enough to cast. Walk toward a ridge and the ridge remains at the distance you first judged until you are close, and then you have been close for longer than the walk accounts for. Drop a flake of crust and it does not fall so much as complete the act of having fallen; it is on the ground, and your hand is empty, and the interval between is missing rather than fast. None of this agitates the place. Equilibrium includes the errors. They are stable errors.

At the far shelving, where density is highest, the spirals interleave until the canopy is a single folded surface pretending to be many. Under it the light is still normal, only sifted, and the quiet deepens into something you notice as quiet—the absence of insect-tick, of leaf-slap, of your own breath coming back off a wall—because there is no wall and there are no insects and the growth does not answer. The ground there is sharp underfoot, young gravel that has never been gravel long enough to round, each grain still announcing the break that made it. Nothing rolls. Nothing settles further. A flake you watched complete its fall is in the same attitude when you look again, and again.

Beyond that shelving the basin does not end so much as stop being describable in one viewing. Another pale flat. Another set of unweathered ridges. The same warm light standing on all of it with the patience of a thing that does not have to be renewed. If there is an outside to this maintenance—some earlier condition, some maker who is not here and did not build in any sense that leaves a ruin—it does not show. What shows is a country in its first hour, held there, lush with a life that does not drink, dense enough to crowd the open ground, and silent in the particular way of a machine that has already finished its work and is only, perfectly, keeping the finish from slipping.
