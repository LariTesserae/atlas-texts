The air up here is thin enough that sound arrives late, and wet enough that it never quite arrives dry. You notice the water before the height. It beads on the backs of your hands. It skins the rock in a film so fine it looks like polish, then gathers in the seams and runs, not as streams yet but as a slow insistence, water that has not decided what kind of water it is. The sky is the same substance, only colder and farther off: a low lid of cloud that condenses against the ground wherever the ground rises to meet it. The peaks do not pierce weather. They are inside it.

This is a range still being made. The stone has not had time to look old. Surfaces are raw, edges unsmoothed, colors that erosion would have quieted still loud—sulfur yellow in the cracks, iron red where a seam has split overnight, a chalky white crust forming wherever water pauses long enough to leave what it carried. Nothing here is weathered into gentleness. Terraces of new mineral step down a slope that did not exist, in this exact shape, a season ago. A lip of rock the color of bone lengthens by the width of a fingernail while you watch, the water laying it down grain by grain, a factory with no walls and no shift change. The mountain is manufacturing itself. You can hear the work if you put an ear to the ground: a tick, a seep, a small hydraulic knock where pressure finds a throat in the stone and speaks through it.

The cold is not the clean cold of high dry places. It is a cold that has water in it, so it clings. Breath clouds and does not disperse. Metal—if you had any—would sweat. The rock sweats instead. On the north faces a rime grows in feathers, not from snowfall but from the air giving up what it can no longer hold. South faces stay dark and glossy, always just above freezing where the water moves, just below where it stops. The line between them walks. By afternoon a sheet that was ice at dawn is a skin of melt again, and the tick in the stone has changed pitch.

There is almost nothing standing between you and the next ridge, and the next, and the valley-system beyond that, which is not a valley so much as a pause in the rising. The scale is regional and the occupancy is not. Emptiness here is not a metaphor. Between one dark seam of wet rock and the next there can be an hour of walking across stone that holds no plant, no track, no scatter of anything that has decided to stay. The light does not help. It is the light of the inside of a cloud: no sun-disc, no hard shadow, only a gray that flattens distance until a ridge two miles off looks like a fold in cloth held at arm's length. You learn the size of the place by how long it takes your legs to be wrong about it.

Vegetation, where it occurs, occurs as a decision forced by water. A mat of something low and dark green plugs a seep and nowhere else. A few tufts of stiff grass occupy the lee of a new terrace, roots feeling for purchase in mineral that is still chemically rude. Lichen has begun on the older faces—older meaning last decade, not last epoch—and stops at the line where last month's deposition buried it. Nothing grows tall. Wind and thin air and the habit of the ground rearranging itself argue against height in anything but stone. The green, when you find it, is almost a shock, and then you see how little of it there is, and the shock passes back into gray.

What is not scarce is animal life, which is a fact you accept slowly, because the place looks uninhabited and largely is, in the sense that you can stand for a long time and see no creature at all. The abundance is distributed across a volume of air and stone so large that any given acre reads as vacant. Then a shift in the cloud-light shows you the slope across the basin stippled with small dark bodies, hundreds of them, grazing the seep-margins in a band no wider than a road, and the band itself only a thread in the open. They are compact animals, low to the ground, built for cold and for footing on new rock—something between a goat and a pika in the logic of their shape, though they are neither, only the thing this country has iterated until the iteration held. They move as a weather of their own. By the time you have crossed to where they were, they have gone along the wet line to the next basin, and the stone they left is printed with narrow slots of melt, already freezing at the edges.

Overhead, and this is the other half of the abundance, the birds work the same thin resource from the air. They are not scenic. They are constant, mostly out of sight in the lid of cloud, dropping into view when a thermal of warmer seep-air punches a hole, then gone again. Their cries carry oddly in the thin wet atmosphere: close when they are far, absent when they pass directly over, as if the cloud kept the sound and released it on its own schedule. They hunt the insect hatch that rises wherever water and the faint heat of new rock coincide. That hatch is invisible until it isn't—a smoke of small bodies lifting off a terrace at the warmest hour, which is not warm, and the birds folding down into it with a soft percussive sound like rain starting on tin. Then quiet again. The quiet is the dominant instrument. Everything else is an interval.

There is a mechanical quality to the intervals that takes time to separate from animal noise. The young rock is full of gas and heat at depth, not enough to call volcanic in any dramatic sense, enough to drive water through the stone on a schedule the stone itself is still learning. Vents no bigger than a fist exhale at irregular periods. The exhale is a hush with a metal edge, wet steam at a temperature that would scald and then, a hand's breadth out, is only mist joining the general mist. Some of the vents tick beforehand, a coupling of pressure against a mineral flap that has not yet cemented itself shut. Stand near one and it sounds, unmistakably, like an engine idling in a shed you cannot find. There is no shed. There is no engine. There is a throat in week-old rock and water forced through it by a heat that has not finished arriving from below. Across the basin another answers, then a third farther off, out of phase, so the mountain seems to be running several small machines at once and none of them in rhythm with the others. At night—if the dimming of an already dim day can be called night—the ticking continues. It is the closest thing the country has to a built sound, and nothing built it.

Electricity lives in the same air, not as storm but as a property of height and moisture and the constant low friction of cloud dragging across rising ground. On the sharper new ridges a faint violet brushing comes and goes, soundless, a corona that outlines an edge for a few breaths and then is only rock again. It does not strike. It leaks. Hair lifts. The wet film on the stone takes a charge and gives it back as a tiny crackle when you set a palm down, once, and then not again until you have walked far enough to be a new circuit. None of this is spectacular. It is industrial in the old sense: a process running at scale, converting one thing into another, indifferent to whether anyone is there to name the conversion. Water to mineral. Heat to pressure. Pressure to sound. Charge to a brief light the color of a bruise. The mountain's production line has no operator and no product it intends to keep. It sheds what it makes. The terraces are inventory that has not yet been priced by weather.

Weather is the other engine, and it does not idle. Because the air is saturated, every change of temperature is a change of state. A drop of two degrees and the film on the north faces locks. A rise of two and the locked faces weep again, and the weeping finds the new cracks—cracks from the last uplift, which is ongoing, a rise measured in seasons rather than centuries—and widens them by the width of freeze. You can hear that too, rarely: a soft knock from inside a face, the sound of water becoming ice where ice has nowhere to go. The range is lifting and wetting and freezing and laying itself down simultaneously. Emergence and the first gestures of erosion occupy the same hour. A terrace deposited in the morning can be split by the afternoon if the seep that built it shifts its mouth six inches left, which seeps here do, because the plumbing is new and the stone has not committed to its channels. Maps of this place would be wrong by the time they were drawn. The accurate description is a description of rates.

Down in the basin floors, where the slope pauses, the water finally consents to be water in the ordinary way. It collects in shallow sheets over mineral pavement, ankle-cold, clear enough to see the bubbles trapped under last night's ice-skin, which the day has half-freed. These are not lakes. They are pauses. A sheet a quarter-mile across may be two inches deep, and under it the pavement is still being plated, a white mat of precipitate soft enough to take a footprint and firm enough, by evening, to have begun forgetting it. The animals cross these sheets at their edges and do not linger. Something in the water—the metal taste of it, the newness—keeps them to the margins where the insect smoke is and the chemistry has had a week to soften. Fish have not arrived. There is nowhere upstream for them to have come from that has held standing water long enough. The basins are younger than the idea of a fishery. What moves in them is larval, small, and adapted to a life of being occasionally frozen and occasionally boiled at the margins where a vent's outflow threads in, a ribbon of warmer water writing cursive across the sheet until the temperatures agree.

At the scale of the whole country, seen from a ridge when the cloud lifts half a hand and lets you have a distance, the pattern clarifies and then refuses to stay clarified. Wet lines. Dry intervals. A scaffolding of new terraces like the footings of a city no one is going to build. Threads of animals moving along the wet the way current moves in a wire, numerous in total, lonely in any one place. Birds entering and leaving the lower cloud. Steam answering steam across basins, out of phase. The violet leak of charge on the youngest edges, which are also the highest, because the highest is what has most recently come up. No road. No wall. No post. No object in the entire view that was placed rather than extruded, deposited, or born. The industrial impression persists anyway, and strengthens the longer you listen: a works, running. The quiet between its sounds is not peace. It is the pause in a cycle.

By the time the light has thinned to the point where the far ridges unmake themselves, the day's deposition has added its unmeasurable layer, the vents have shifted their rhythm by a few beats, and the grazing thread has moved into the next drainage, leaving the one you stand above empty in the way the place prefers—empty as a condition, not as an absence of life. Life is here in numbers that would stock a softer country. It is simply never crowded, because the country is still deciding how much country it is, and the decision is large, and ongoing, and cold, and wet, and audible, if you stay long enough, as a ticking under the hand.
