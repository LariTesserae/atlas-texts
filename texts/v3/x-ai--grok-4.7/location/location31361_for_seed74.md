The place is a hollow no larger than a parish close, sunk just enough that the land around it reads as a rim rather than a hill. You come down into it on a path of packed root that has been walked until the fibers lie one way, like combed hair. The lip is only a few paces of rise. Beyond it the country is ordinary wet ground and goes on without ceremony. Inside, the air drops a degree and stays there.

Nothing here is built of dead timber in the usual sense, and almost nothing is built of stone. The settlement is three halls and a scatter of lesser sheds, set far apart across a floor of living mat. Between them the space is mostly empty: courts of short sedge, a wheel-pit holding tea-colored water, a lane of bare copper rail gone green, wide enough for a cart that no longer comes. The emptiness is maintained. Where the copper runs, and where old tannin drains have blackened the soil, the growth pulls back and leaves a corridor. Everywhere else the ground is plant, and the plant is thick enough to be called floor, wall, and roof without metaphor.

The largest hall stands near the middle, a long nave of hornbeam and osier willow pleached over iron hoops that have not been seen in decades. The trunks were set as saplings in a double row and bent, season after season, until they met and grafted. Now the roof is a single living vault, leaves outside, smooth scar-wood inside, the grafts swollen into ribs. Light comes through it as ordinary day, broken into coins and bars, never dim enough to be called shade in the wood sense and never open enough to be sky. You can read a gauge by it. You can see the far end of the hall, which is not far. Cathedral only in the way the ribs repeat and the sound dies.

Under the vault the industry is still running, and has been running in more or less this arrangement since the channels found their grade. A wheel turns in the pit outside, fed by a race that enters under the rim as a damp culvert and leaves as a thread of water no wider than a wrist, soaked up again by roots before it can become a stream. The wheel is wooden, but the teeth that meet it are iron, and from the iron a shaft goes into the hall and drives a line of small machines: beaters for fiber, a screw press, a set of rollers that draw willow withe into cord. Belts are twisted bast, replaced when they dry and crack. Two motors, no larger than buckets, sit in bark housings and take what the wheel cannot give when the race runs thin. They hum at a pitch you stop hearing after a minute. There is no stack, no furnace, no shift whistle. The power is small and continuous, scaled to a crop that is also the building.

The other halls do other work and sit far enough away that you walk in weather to reach them. One is a drying shed: walls of trained yew, dense enough to break wind, open at the eaves so damp air can leave. Racks of peeled withe and retted stem hang in rows with real gaps between them. Condensation beads on the iron hooks and falls, all day, a private rain that does not reach the ground as rain but as dark spots on the mat. The third hall is lower, almost a cellar grown rather than dug, where the press-liquor is held in vats coopered from coppice oak and lined, where the lining has failed, with a skin of root that the coopers long ago stopped fighting. The liquor is the color of old tea. It smells of leaf-rot and clean metal. It is drawn off on a schedule the pumps keep without anyone standing by.

Coolth is the climate, not a season. Breath shows only at the wettest hour before full light, and then only if you look for it. Moss holds the temperature the way a cistern holds water. Nothing is frozen, nothing is warm. Tools left on a bench stay the temperature of the bench. The few metal surfaces sweat when the outside air thickens, and the sweat runs down engineered paths — grooves in the old iron, veins in the living wood — into collection tins that feed back to the vats. Moisture is never a flood and never absent. It moves: mist over the rim at dawn, gone by the time the leaf-coins on the floor are bright; a brief rain that darkens the sedge and is finished before the wheel's note changes; pockets of standing water in the pits that rise a hand and fall a hand with the pump cycle and the weather together. The banks of those pits have not slumped in living memory. Roots stitch them. The erosion happened, thoroughly, a long time ago, and what remains is the form the water and the plants agreed on.

Fauna is almost an absence you notice by the quiet it leaves. No flocks. No mice in the walls, because the walls are watched by their own sap and by a bitterness in the bark the old planters selected for. A wren, sometimes, in the yew, and gone. Once a heron stood in the wheel-pit at the far edge of the permitted shallows and left when the wheel took a stronger gulp. Insects are there and not numerous: a few moths that drink the condensation, beetles in the rotting withe that the press rejected. Their sound is below the motors. The place does not support a hunt. Whatever eats, eats little, and mostly the industry takes the growth before anything else can.

People are not in it at the hour this is, or are so few they do not thicken the air. The settlement is urban in its joints, not in its crowd: graded races, numbered racks, a ledger of marks burned into a living post and healed around so the numbers sit in scar, copper corridors kept open by the plants' own dislike, lamps — plain industrial glass, low wattage — wired along the ribs for the dark half of the year and switched by the same clock that starts the secondary motors. A town's worth of decisions, made once and left to run, inside a boundary you can walk in twenty minutes.

The rules are plain once you have stood still long enough to see what does not happen. Growth is total except where metal, tannin, or traffic has drawn a line, and the lines are old, so the emptiness looks designed because it is, and also looks natural because the plants enforce it without being asked each morning. Sound stays low because every process is sized under the threshold of echo: the wheel's wet knock, the rollers' shuffle, drip into tin, a leaf freeing itself from the vault and landing on the mat with no audience. Nothing rushes. Everything is in motion. Sap is up or down according to the hour. The race swells and starves. Presses cycle. Mist arrives, works, leaves. A withe on the rack loses a measure of water and becomes cord stock; the cord stock becomes a belt; the belt, in a year, becomes fiber again in the beater. The building eats its maintenance and grows its replacement in the same gesture.

By afternoon the light is simply day, a little cooler under the hornbeam than in the courts, where the sedge is bright and the copper rail throws a thin green flash when the clouds thin. You can see the rim all around, near, ordinary, holding the hollow the way a bowl holds what was poured into it and never quite dried. The wheel takes another turn. Somewhere in the yew hall a drop that has been gathering since morning lets go, and the sound of it is so small it confirms the quiet rather than breaking it.
