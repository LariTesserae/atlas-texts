The ground does not remember being anything else. Every surface you can put a foot on was laid: packed fines the color of baked clay, courses of split reed pressed into mats, low curbs of a pale stone that takes a polish where hands have passed and stays chalky where they have not. There is no bedrock showing through, no parent soil, no place where the making stops and a prior world begins. If you dig—and the digging is easy, the stuff yielding under a wooden blade—you find only earlier layers of the same work, slightly misaligned, as if the basin were rebuilt on itself without ever being allowed to fail.

It is low, and it stays low. The horizon is a bright smear rather than a ridge. What relief exists is made relief: long berms no higher than a person, sharp along their crests, unrounded. Nothing has had time to slump. Nothing is going to. A fresh cut in a berm shows the same crisp arris it showed when the cut was new, and the cut is always new. Dust does not gather in the angle. Rain, which is frequent and warm and seems to fall out of a sky that is already full of water, beads and runs and vanishes into channels without staining the stone darker for more than a moment. Then the stone is the color it was.

The heat sits on the water. Not a dramatic heat—no shimmer that walks, no wind to organize it—just a steady, skin-close warmth, bright enough that squinting becomes the local posture. Light comes from everywhere above the berms and from the water itself, which throws it back in flat sheets. Shadows are short and oddly decisive, cut with the same unused edge as the curbs. They do not lengthen. If you watch one across what ought to be an afternoon, it remains the shadow of the hour you arrived in.

Water is the architecture's other half. It stands in wide, shallow pans between the berms, not quite a marsh, not quite a canal: made ponds with square corners that the reeds have not been permitted to round. The water is clear for a hand's depth and then only bright, and it weeps from the stone in threads so fine they look drawn. Nothing overflows. Nothing falls. The level is a rule. Frog-prints in the marginal silt—if you can call it silt, when it was sifted and placed—remain printed, unblurred, while the frogs themselves are everywhere: green backs in the pans, dozens to a pool, throats still, eyes open, bodies arranged with the spacing of objects set out rather than of animals that chose a spot. They do not jump when you approach. They are already where the jumping would have landed.

Between the pans, the planting is lush and strangely legible. Broad leaves, almost too green for the heat, overlap in ranks that someone staked and never had to restake. Vines hold to trellises of lashed reed in patterns a hand could parse: repeats of three, then a gap, then three. Flowers open and stay open. Fruit hangs at the weight it had when it was ready, and does not drop, and does not soften. You can walk a long time between one planted island and the next. The sparseness is deliberate the way the planting is deliberate—wide pads of bare laid ground, hot underfoot, separating groves that would otherwise have closed. The basin reads, at the scale of a town or a small valley, as a single work: water, berm, grove, water, repeated until the bright distance eats the count. You never quite arrive at an edge. The next berm is always the same distance off, which is not how distance works, and which you notice and then fail to keep noticing.

Life is abundant and almost without voice. The frogs blink. Herons stand in the pans on one leg that never tires. Insects traffic the leaf-surfaces in visible streams, a glittering that should roar and does not. Wingbeats happen; the air does not take them up. When something moves—and things do move, continuously, in the way of a scene that has been set in motion and left—the motion does not accumulate. A heron strikes. The minnow is in the bill. The minnow is also still in the water, in the place the strike began, and both facts are calm about each other. Downstream of that, if downstream is a word that applies where nothing drains, another heron holds the same strike at a different instant, or the same instant wearing a different body. You cannot decide, and the decision is not required of you. The quiet is not emptiness. It is the sound of a rule that does not include noise.

The making is primitive in every joint you can inspect. No metal flash at the lashings. Pegs, fiber, stone dressed by pounding rather than by saw. The reed mats fray at their ends into the exact tuft a mat frays into on its first day of use, and the fray does not advance. Someone, or many someones, or a method that only resembles someone, built this with hands and stopped at the moment the last surface was true. There are no builders now. There are no tracks that are not part of the design. A path of packed fines runs between two groves, worn to a smoother pale, and the wear is uniform along its whole length, as if it had been walked by a single step repeated rather than by journeys.

What breaks is not the weather and not the work. It is sequence. You set a cup—you had not known you were carrying a cup—on a curb, and when you look back it is on the curb and also in your hand, both dry, both the right weight. A leaf you turned over is turned over and is also face-up, and the insect on its underside is on the underside of both. The basin does not flicker or tear. It simply permits two finished states to occupy the finished world, because finishing is all it knows how to do. Nothing rots. Nothing completes a fall. The bright heat does not climax into storm; the storm is already accounted for as the wet that is already here, held at the degree the channels were cut to hold.

Farther out, where the repetitions should dwindle into wild country, they do not. The made ponds keep their corners. The herons keep their poses at different points along a gesture that will not be allowed to end. The horizon stays a bright smear at the height of the berms, and the smear, if you study it, is only more water, more sharp crests, more planted green set apart by empty laid ground, continuing at the same density, under the same noon that does not move. You can walk toward it. You will cross another channel, another grove, another pad of hot bare making, and the cup will still be in your hand and on the curb you have not yet left, and the frogs will watch with the patience of things that were placed, in a country that was built all the way down and then declined, forever, to change.
