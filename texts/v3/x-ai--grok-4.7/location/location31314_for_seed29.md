The chamber is no larger than a room that has never been measured, and it has no door. It sits high in a ridge that is still deciding what kind of rock it is—fresh fracture, edges not yet rounded, a blister of heat that split the face and then almost closed again. From outside there is nothing to see: a seam, a warmth if you put your palm to the stone, a sound you might take for wind in a crack. Inside, the air is packed to the walls.

There is no water. The heat has already taken whatever moisture the rock once held and driven it out through hairline vents that sealed behind it. What remains is dry mineral breath—iron, salt crust, a thin bitter dust of oxides that never quite settles because something is always moving through it. The temperature is a constant pressure against the skin, not a flare. It comes up from below, from rock that has only recently stopped being softer than this, and it has nowhere to go. The chamber holds it the way a closed fist holds heat.

Nothing green has ever taken here, and nothing green could. The surfaces are new. Glass-smooth runs of cooled melt cut across duller fracture faces; in places the stone is still sugary, grains not yet locked, so that a scrape of a body leaves a pale scar that will not weather for a long time. Erosion has barely begun. This is not a cave worn out of older country. It is a pause in the making of the mountain—an interior that opened and has not yet been filled back in by the same slow shove that opened it.

And it is full.

The life is small, hard-bodied, and everywhere at once. They are not plants and they are not visitors. They live on the mineral film that heat keeps sweating out of the freshest faces—thin, metallic, almost invisible until a cluster of bodies darkens it by feeding. They pack the floor until the floor is no longer visible as floor. They stitch the walls in overlapping mats. They hang in the upper dark where the air is hottest and thinnest, a ceiling of small restless weight. When one shifts, ten shift. There is no empty interval large enough to call a path. To be in the chamber is to be among them, shouldered by the same heat, breathing the same dry dust they kick up and never let fall.

They make most of the noise, and the rock makes the rest.

The sound does not rise and fall with weather. It is industrial in the old sense: continuous, mechanical, scaled past any single body. Mandibles work stone. Wing-cases tick in a rhythm so dense it becomes a drone. Claws find purchase on surfaces that are still micro-rough, and the collective scrape is a mill running without a building around it. Under that, slower and lower, the chamber itself works. Heat expands a face; the face checks with a bright crack; a slip of grit runs; somewhere in the seam a dry gas jet—sulfur, metal, nothing wet—hisses at a pressure that never quite becomes a roar and never quite stops. No engine was set here. No wall was raised. The processes simply run at a pitch and a constancy that the ear files with factories: production without a product anyone named, electrified in the small way of hot stone and moving charge, a static that lifts the finest dust and keeps it burning in the dark like a faint incorrect snow.

Light is almost an absence. What little there is leaks from the hottest seams—a dull red that does not illuminate so much as stain the nearest bodies and then fail. Most of the chamber is known by contact and by sound. You learn the new glass by how it refuses a grip. You learn the raw grain by how it powders. You learn the animals by the way the drone changes pitch when the mat is disturbed, a brief local brightening of noise that spreads and then settles back into the general labor.

Nothing here is in a hurry that a person could watch. The blister opened across a span longer than attention. The mineral film thickens by a patience that makes feeding look frantic only because the feeders are small. Cracks propagate, pause, propagate. A generation of the hard-bodied life turns over while the walls move a distance you would need a mark to believe. The mountain is still arriving at this shape. The chamber is an early room in a house that does not know it is being built, and may close again before it ever becomes a cave, a vent, a name.

High above whatever valleys the ridge will eventually overlook, sealed in new stone, dry, dark, loud, and slow, the place continues its work: heat, mineral, bodies, and the unbroken sound of a process that has not yet needed anyone to construct it.
