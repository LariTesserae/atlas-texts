The place is smaller than it sounds. From the low lip of the bank you can see the far rim—a rise of packed root and stained iron no taller than a shoulder—and the whole interior sits in a warm, shallow bowl no wider than a chapel nave. What fills it is not ground. Ground, if it remains, is a rumor under a floor of living tissue: stems welded sideways into planks, mats of leaf so thick they take weight, a surface that gives and then holds, warm as a body left in the sun.

Water never leaves it. It does not stand as a lake. It weeps. It films every green face, beads along the undersides of leaves the size of shutters, and collects in the seams where metal still shows through. A boot, if you could find a place to set one, would press a dark well that fills at once and does not drain so much as sink into the plant and reappear somewhere else, a slow percolation with no true current. The air is close and ordinary in its light—daylight, not dim, not theatrical—but the light arrives already wet, broken into a thousand small reflections off wet cuticle and wet scale. Nothing glitters. Everything glistens, patiently, as if the weather ended years ago and the moisture simply decided to stay.

The vegetation is the substrate, the walls, the roof where a roof has formed, and most of what used to be machine. At this density there is no understory and no clearing. A gallery of hollow stems runs where a walkway once was; you can put a hand into one and feel air moving, slow and heated, smelling of sap and hot oil. Leaves overlap until shadow is only a thinner green. Roots have taken the bolt-holes. Vines have learned the pitch of pipes and kept it, so that here and there a curve of living wood still describes the arc of a conduit that has entirely gone. The plants are not lush in the way of a jungle that reaches. They are settled. Thicknesses have stopped increasing. Bark, where bark occurs, is smooth from long rubbing against iron that no longer moves enough to cut. The forms are finished. They have been finished for a long time.

What remains of building is scattered and partial, and all of it is inside the mass rather than on it. A section of boiler-plate, copper gone green-black, shows as a shoulder in the western wall, its rivets still proud, each one ringed by a callus of tissue that grew around the heat and then cooled with it. A flywheel stands three-quarters buried near the center, teeth packed with fiber, turning so slowly that the motion is easier to hear than to see. Somewhere under the floor a pump keeps its stroke. You do not find a factory. You find the factory’s persistence: an engine that was never switched off, or that no longer possesses an off, scaled down by neglect into a single working heart the size of the bowl that contains it. Pipes surface and vanish. A gauge glass, miraculously whole, holds a line of brown water that rises and falls with the pump’s breath. A short run of rail, industrial and absurd, disappears into a trunk and does not come out the other side. There are no roofs left that are only roofs. Where workers once stood, the standing-room is now a sequence of narrow, warm cavities lined in leaf.

It is loud, and the loudness does not hurry. Drip is continuous and close, a thousand small strikes on metal, on taut leaf, on the skin of water that never quite becomes a pool. The pump has a deep, uneven knock, iron on iron padded by something soft that grew into the gap. Steam, or what behaves like steam, sighs from a seam near the boiler-plate and condenses before it can rise, so the sound is a wet exhalation rather than a whistle. Leaves the thickness of hide flex against one another with a sound like oiled leather. Nothing startles. The noise is the place’s ordinary speech, and it does not crest or fall with the hour. If you stay, it becomes the silence you measure other sounds against. There are almost no other sounds. Fauna is nearly absent. No birds work the canopy—there is no canopy separate from the floor. No tracks cross the living planks. Once, low in a pipe-mouth, something small and pale shifts and is gone, too quick and too seldom to name, and the motion does not repeat. The ecology is plant, water, heat, and the machine they have finished digesting without having killed.

The strangeness is not a spectacle. It is a rule. Heat from the engine does not burn the tissue wrapped around it. The plants nearest the boiler run a few degrees warmer than the rest and grow, very slowly, in plates, like scale, as if they have taken the habit of metal. Sap in the hollow stems is faintly conductive to the touch—not a shock, a presence, a low thrill along the wet, as if the old wiring found a new sheath and kept a fraction of its work. Condensation on certain leaves beads in lines that follow vanished diagrams: straight runs, right angles, a geometry no leaf invents alone. Cut a stem—if the mass allowed you purchase enough to cut—and the wound does not bleed so much as weep the same brown water the gauge glass holds, and the lips of the cut thicken over days into a flange, neat as a fitting. The place repairs itself in the language of the works it swallowed.

Time here is patient and already mostly spent. Erosion has arrived at its balance and stayed. Rust is a patina, not a process you can watch. Silt does not gather because there is no open water to drop it in; minerals travel instead through the wet films and come to rest as pale rimes on the oldest leaves, which do not fall. Seasons, if they pass, pass as a slight change in the pitch of the pump and a slight sweetening or souring of the air. Growth and decay have the same speed, which is nearly none. A leaf that was a door last year is a door still, only darker, only more exactly fitted to the opening it grew across. The bowl does not spread. The far rim does not advance. Whatever appetite built this density has been met, and the meeting holds.

You do not explore it so much as inhabit a few possible postures inside it. Kneeling is possible in the gallery of stems. Standing is possible only where the flywheel’s housing left a vault, and there the ceiling is a single leaf so broad it reads as plaster, veined, dripping on a count you could learn. Lying down would mean agreeing to be grown around, and the mass is slow enough that the agreement would take longer than a visit and faster than a life. At the lip, on the only true bank—a low sill of older, harder root, warm, wet, established—you can sit and hear the knock, the drip, the leather-flex of leaves, and see, under ordinary daylight, the whole small interior shining with water that has nowhere else to go. It is a marsh that stood up. It is a machine that continued by becoming garden. It is neither, exactly. It is the size of a room that remembers being a works, and it is full, and it is finished, and it is still, very quietly and very loudly, at work.
