The basin is still deciding what it is. From the rim you can see all of it—two low shoulders of pale rock, a saddle between them, and a floor some kilometers across that has not yet learned to drain. The rock is new in the way stone can be new: jointed, sharp at the edges, unstained except where water has been sitting on it long enough to darken a band. It has been lifted and not yet cut. Gullies are scratches. The streams have not found their jobs. What should be slope is still a shallow dish, and the dish holds water the way a tray holds it, everywhere near the surface, nowhere in a hurry.

The light is the first hard fact. At this height the air is thin enough that noon arrives without softness, a white glare off wet surfaces, metal, and the pale faces of the rock knobs that break through the cover. There is mist, but it does not shelter you. It hangs in sheets over the standing water and condenses on anything colder than the air—roof iron, pipe, the back of your neck—and the sun goes straight through it. Shadows are short and blue. You squint even when the sky is only bright, not clear. Distance is easy. You can pick out a shed on the far side of the basin and count the posts of the wire that runs to it.

Underfoot there is almost no mineral ground. The rock is there, a hand’s depth down or less, cold and unweathered, but the surface you walk is plant, continuous, a mat of moss, sedge, and low cushion growth so thick it has become the floor. It gives under a boot and then holds. Water rises into the print and does not sink away; it simply waits in the fiber. In the open stretches the mat is knee-high at most, often less, so the basin reads as empty even though it is entirely occupied. Nothing stands up to break the sightline except what has been put there, or the occasional hummock where the plants have built on themselves for longer than the ground around them. The green is not one green. Close in it is olive, rust, and a bleached yellow where last season’s stems have not yet been pressed into the mat. From the rim it is a single dull living color, bright where the water films it, dark where the mat is thick enough to hide the rock.

The water has no main channel because it has not been given time to make one. It seeps from the joints in the knobs and runs a little way in films, then loses itself in the plants. Around the knobs the rock weeps in steady lines, each line a dark stripe on the pale face, and the stripe ends in a fan of brighter moss. Farther out, the mat holds pools with no clear banks—marsh edge all the way around, a softer black where the growth has drowned and a firmer rim where it has not. If you stand still you can hear the water moving under the mat, a thick sliding sound, not a brook. Here and there a preference is beginning: a slot a few hands wide where the plants are torn and the flow has started to keep a line open. These are the first streams, embryonic, still losing themselves after a short run. In another age they will be valleys. Now they are dark seams you can step across, and the rest of the basin remains a wet plain.

What breaks the plain is small and widely spaced. A corduroy track of split stems, already half swallowed, runs from the near shoulder toward the middle distance and gives out. A row of short poles carries a single cable, sagging, with insulators glazed by the damp. Survey laths lean in the mat at long intervals, some of them already hosted by moss. There is a pumphouse of corrugated iron no larger than a room, raised on a crib of rock so it does not sink, and another like it far enough away that a person walking between them would be out of sight of both for a while. Near the outlet side of the basin—where an outlet is only just becoming true—there is the works itself: a low mill, a penstock of riveted pipe, a wheel housing, and a drying yard of racks. That is the settlement. No town. A handful of buildings and the marks of people who come up, run the machines, and go down again before the light fails, because there is nowhere here to stay that is not wet.

The mill is the center of the noise, though the noise does not have a center you can escape. The beaters run without letup, fed by sods of the mat cut in long strips from marked plots and hauled in on a sled that does not need runners so much as a broad base. Inside, the fiber is pulped, pressed, and sent out to the racks as pale sheets that stiffen in the cold bright air. A generator in the same building takes the fall from the penstock—the only fall the basin has, and that one made, not found—and the cable carries what power the pumphouses need to lift seepage into the pipe. Engines, belts, the knock of the presses, water in the housing: all of it is ordinary machinery, sized for a works and not for a city, and all of it is loud. On the open mat the sound does not die. It travels. The wind at this height is steady rather than gusting, and it brings the mill with it, and under that the sliding of water in the plants, and the tick of condensate on iron when you stand near a roof. Close to the beaters you cannot hear a voice. Far out, you still cannot hold a low conversation. The place has a roar the way other places have weather. It does not rise and fall with the hour. The machines are set to a rate and left, and the water does not hurry, and the wind keeps its one note.

Almost nothing alive answers it. The mat is a plant world in the strict sense: production, storage, and decay are mostly the plants and what works in them unseen. The water is cold and poor and new, and it holds no fish you would find with a hand. There are no tracks crossing the seams. No flocks work the pools. Now and then a single bird crosses the saddle high up, silent against the mill, and is gone over the shoulder; you do not see it land. The plots where the sods are cut do not green over in a season. The raw edges weep, the moss creeps, and the scar remains a darker rectangle for a long time, which is how the cutters know where they have already been. Whatever would eat the offcuts or nest in the racks has not arrived, or cannot stay. The food, such as it is, is fiber and cold water. The ecology is the mat eating light and the mill eating the mat, and between them a thin traffic of people who do not live here.

The rules are simple because the place is unfinished. Water stays near the surface until someone pumps it or the young seams take it. Weight sinks unless it is cribbed on rock. Metal wets and stays wet; the bright air does not dry a roof so much as glare off it. The mat will close over a dropped tool, slowly, and the tool will be there next season if you remember the plot. Cutting is done on a rotation longer than a year, because the growth that replaces a sod is patient and the rock beneath offers nothing to hurry it. The mill runs in the same temper: not in surges, but as a continuous load, sheets accumulating on the racks, the generator holding one voltage, the penstock taking whatever the pumphouses can raise. Change, when it comes, is the kind you measure by walking out to a seam you knew last time and finding it a little longer, a little less willing to lose itself in the plants. The basin is under construction by water and by the works at once, and neither has gotten far.

Late in the day the glare lowers but does not warm. The racks throw long blue bars across the yard. Spray off the wheel housing still carries a short colorless rainbow, gone when you step sideways. On the knobs the weeping lines keep their dark stripes, and the mist lifts a little off the pools without ever leaving the basin. You can see the whole of it again from the shoulder: the living floor, the few iron roofs, the cable, the first dark threads of drainage, the mill still knocking at the same rate. It is a small region and an open one, loud in every part, and slow enough that if you came back after a long absence you would know it, and you would still have to look carefully to say what had begun.
