The ground has forgotten how to be a mountain. What remains is a high shelf of dark stone, green-black where it is wet and a dull iron color where a film of moisture has just pulled back, and it runs on farther than a morning’s walk in every direction except one. The old peaks are gone. Whatever stood here was ground down before anyone kept years, and the grinding finished its work: the shelf is nearly level, a plain held up in the cold air, its rises no taller than a roof and its hollows no deeper than a person’s waist. You feel the height in the chest, not in the view. The air is thin and cool and never quite dry, and most days the cloud sits down on the stone and stays.

Water does not travel here the way it does in lower country. There is no river worth the name. Moisture arrives as the cloud itself, combed out of the air by leaves, by eaves, by the fine grain of the rock. It beads, films, and gathers. In the shallow basins left by the last of the erosion it stands in dark pools that rise and fall with the weather and never empty all the way. Between rains—and the rains are brief, needling, and frequent—the stone still weeps from hairline cracks, a slow shine moving downhill by a finger’s width in an hour. Clothes dampen and do not dry. Wool holds the smell of wet mineral and crushed leaf. Breath shows in the early part of the cycle, then the air warms a little as the fog thickens, and the white of breath disappears into the larger white.

The light is the cloud’s light. It has no edge. Shadows do not harden; they pool, blue-grey, under leaves and under lintels, and a person walking toward you resolves out of the dim as a darker shape before they resolve as a face. Once or twice in a day the deck lifts. Not far. Enough that the shelf shows its one drama: the rim, where the ancient range still remembers vertical distance and falls away in a wall of smoothed cliffs, tier on tier, vanishing into the lower weather. For those minutes the town’s roofs come clear, and the drop beyond them, and then the fog walks back in and the distance closes to the length of a street.

What grows here drinks the fog and grows thick. Nothing reaches high. The trees are short, wide, and numerous, their bark a wet leather color, their crowns meshed so that you move under a continuous low ceiling of leaf. Between them a waist-high growth of broad, ribbed leaves stands in ranks, each leaf beaded, each bead running to the midrib and falling. Under that, moss—red-brown, then green where it is newest—fills every pause in the stone. The lushness is lateral and layered. Stems touch stems. Leaves overlap until the ground is a suggestion. In the basins, a finer plant floats in mats and knits the still water shut. Growth is quick and continuous in the damp; you can find a leaf at morning that was a fist of tissue the day before. Decay is slower. Fallen leaves lie varnished and intact until the moss climbs them and takes them under. The cool keeps rot patient.

Animals are scarcely part of it. There is no birdsong to speak of, and the absence is loud once you notice you have been listening for it. A moth the color of the fog works the wet leaves at the end of a cycle. In the warm run of a pipe behind the rendering sheds, something small ticks and is never seen. The works harbor a few of these hangers-on, drawn to heat more than to food. Out on the shelf itself, tracks are rare, and what tracks appear in the mud at a pool’s edge are old, half-healed by moss, and do not match any beast the town bothers to name. People say the thin air and the endless damp starve anything with a fast heart. Whether that is true or whether the shelf simply does not want company, the result is the same: the noise of life here is vegetable and mechanical, not animal.

The town occupies a shallow gather of the shelf a little in from the rim, and it does not stand apart from the growth. Streets follow old water-threads, slight windings worn into the stone before the first wall, so the plan feels decided by the land and only afterward by masons. Houses are stone cut from the shelf itself, so that in the dim a wall and the ground it rises from are hard to separate. Slate roofs repeat the color of the rock. Moss takes the north faces and the joints. The broad-leaf plants seed into gutters and are pulled, and seed again. Lanes are narrow enough that two people with baskets turn sideways. Laundry lines run from eave to tree and back. Pipes—copper gone green, iron gone black—travel the lanes at hip height and overhead, crossing, dripping, jacketing the streets in a second, wetter architecture. Nothing has much clearance. You walk in a press of leaf, stone, cloth, metal, and other people’s shoulders.

It is a town, not a scatter of holdings: cisterns linked by graded channels, a market court roofed in the same slate, a lane of yards where the sap is graded, a small grid of worker housing nearer the rim where the hum lives. Foundations at the center of town are older than the buildings on them, and older in a way the masons do not like to discuss. Some walls have no joints you can put a knife to. The stone there is continuous, pressed or grown, its surface smoother than tools leave, and it weeps on the same schedule as the open shelf. People render over those stretches, or stack cordwood against them, or let the moss have them. They do not knock them out. The town has learned the shelf’s grain and builds along it.

Industry sits toward the rim, where the only real fall of water is. Catch-basins on the upper side of town collect what the cloud gives; channels carry it through the lanes, under the market, past the houses, and deliver it to a wheel-house built into the cliff’s lip. The wheel turns in a housed drop, out of the wind, and turns slowly. Belts run off it into low sheds: one where blocks are sawn from weeping stone and the slurry is settled for a pale mineral dust the lower country buys; one where the trees’ sap—a clear, faintly sweet exudate tapped at dawn—is warmed in wide vats and drawn off as a resin that sets hard and pale. Engines in the ordinary sense are few. What power there is, is the wheel, and belts, and a pair of electric motors that hum under the hearing of anyone not standing against them. Furnaces light for a single stretch of the day, when the resin must be finished hot, and for that stretch a chimney smokes and the lane outside the shed is the warmest place on the shelf. Then the fire is banked and the cool comes back into the walls.

The quiet is not emptiness. It is dampening. Fog and thin air swallow distance, so the wheel’s thump, which should carry, arrives as a pulse you feel in the boot-sole before you hear it. Drips tick from every eave and every leaf-tip, a scattered clock with no shared time. Belts creak. A vat surface turns over with a thick sound. Voices stay in their lanes; a call from the market does not reach the wheel-house, and the wheel-house does not reach the market. When the deck lifts and the rim shows, a lower wind sometimes finds the cliff and for a few minutes the town has a voice—the channels run louder, a loose sheet of roof iron speaks—and then the fog returns and folds the sound away again. People talk softly out of habit. The place has taught them that shouting buys little.

The day moves even when no one does. Fog thickens until the lane’s far end is a rumor, then a fine rain walks through, darkening the slate and loading the leaves until they tip and spill. The basins rise. Channels that were a glaze become a finger’s depth of moving water, hurrying toward the rim with a purpose the rest of the shelf never shows. In the sheds the belts take the extra push and the hum steps up half a tone. Moss darkens and shines. The broad leaves lift and drink and lower. A break comes: the rain quits, the cloud loosens, and there is the rim again, and below it a tumbled brightness that might be a lower valley in sun—nobody on the shelf trusts that brightness, or needs it. Before the watchers at the lip have agreed on what they saw, the deck seals and the light goes back to its even dim. By the next cycle the basins have slipped down a little, the channels have returned to glaze, and the leaves are beaded once more as if the rain had been a thought the shelf tried and set aside.

There is a place the town does not build on. Near the middle of the shelf, beyond the last meshed trees, the vegetation fails along a clean curve and the stone inside the curve is bare. It is round enough to have been drawn. The rest of the shelf holds water in every seam; this circle stays dry. Fog crosses it and does not quite wet it. Leaves blown onto it curl and pale and are eventually carried off again by a wind that seems to begin at the circle’s edge and nowhere else. Moss, which takes mortar, slate, and boot-leather, stops at the line as if the line were a wall. The stone inside is the same green-black rock, only unsheened, and in the dim it looks faintly lighter than the ground around it, as if it were remembering a weaker sun. People walk around. Children are warned off and, being children, go anyway, and come back with dry shoes and nothing to tell. No one has cut it, flooded it, or roofed it. The rendering sheds stand with their backs to it. In a town that has layered itself into every other surface—plant on stone, pipe on plant, moss on pipe—the circle is the one volume left unoccupied, and the density of everything around it makes the emptiness feel worked, not accidental.

That is the strangeness the shelf allows itself. The rest is only a place that has been finished for a long time and has not stopped moving: cloud in, water sideways, leaf into moss, belt over wheel, light without a source you can point to. At the rim, when the deck lifts, you can see how far the drop goes and how little the town has to do with anything below. Then the fog returns, the lanes close to their proper length, and the drip resumes its count. The shelf keeps its height, its damp, its low green ceiling, and its quiet industry, and goes on being what the mountains became when they were done.
