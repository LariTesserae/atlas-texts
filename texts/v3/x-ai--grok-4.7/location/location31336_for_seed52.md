The city sits in a basin that has not yet decided it is a basin. The ground is new: shelves of pale sinter and glass-smooth basalt still carry the geometry of their arrival, edges so clean they throw a faint ring when a foot scuffs them. Low, though — everything is low. The horizon is a long, uncertain line of embankments and hanging gardens no taller than a rise of fog, and the fog is almost always there, a warm damp that beads on every surface and never quite becomes rain.

Buildings stand far apart. Between them the avenues are wide enough to hold weather. You can walk for a long time and meet no one, only the hush of condensation sliding off a wall and the occasional tick of a machine doing something you cannot see. The structures themselves are not stone in any settled sense. Their skins are grown, or extruded, or both: lattices finer than root-hair, catching the dim light and turning it the color of old brass. Some of them breathe. A facade will swell, almost imperceptibly, and a film of moisture will lift off it in a sheet and fall as a brief, silent shower onto the street below. Then the wall tightens again, and the street steams.

Vegetation has taken the emptiness as an invitation. It is lush without being crowded — great solitary trees with trunks like cooled slag, their canopies pouring shade onto plazas that have no market and no name. Vines the width of a wrist climb the open frames of unfinished towers and stop, as if instructed, a meter short of the top. Underfoot, moss thick as carpet holds water in shallow lenses. The moss is warm. Everything is warm. Even the shade holds a residual heat, as if the ground remembers a fire that has not happened yet, or happened and was put out by means no one recorded.

Light never quite arrives. It filters through a high, permanent haze the color of tea, so that noon reads as late afternoon and late afternoon as a kind of gilded dusk that refuses to end. Shadows are soft and slightly wrong: they lag their objects by a finger's breadth, as if the light were thinking about them before committing. In the deeper streets, between the spaced towers, you navigate by the pale phosphor of the walls themselves, a slow pulse that brightens when you approach and dims when you pass, not quite welcoming, not quite indifferent.

Water is local. There is no river, only pockets — cisterns open to the sky, rectangular and perfectly still, their surfaces filmed with a skin of oil-bright pollen. Condensation collects in the hollows of the new rock and runs nowhere; it simply stands, warm, and is drunk by roots that have learned to find it. Sometimes a cistern will empty itself upward. The water lifts in a column, silent, and is taken into the underside of a passing structure, and the basin below is dry stone for an hour, steaming, before the drip begins again.

Animals are rare enough that a single one rearranges the afternoon. A bird, if it is a bird, crosses a plaza on wings that do not flap so much as reconfigure, and the moss under its shadow darkens and then lightens again, a brief bruise of attention. Insects exist mostly as sound that isn't quite sound — a thin metallic ticking in the canopy, present and then not. No flocks. No herds. The ecology seems to have decided that one of each thing is enough, and that most things should be plants, or machines, or the unsettled category between them.

The technology does not announce itself. There are no signs, no vehicles in any ordinary sense, no lights that mean traffic. What there is: streets that repair their own cracks while you watch, the fissure drawing shut like a lip, leaving a seam that gleams and then dulls. Towers whose upper stories are not fully in the same place as their foundations — a slight, steady offset, as if the building were remembering a different plan and halfway enacting it. In one district the air itself is gridded. Walk into it and your skin reports a faint resistance, a warmth organized into lanes, and if you stand still long enough a fine dust of something not quite mineral settles on your sleeves in a pattern you did not choose. People say the grid is counting. People say very little, actually. The city is quiet enough that a voice carries farther than it should, and so voices are used sparingly, and mostly indoors, where the walls drink them.

The rules, such as they can be inferred, are few and not quite stable. Water rises when it is wanted elsewhere. Light lags. Structures breathe and drink and occasionally relocate a story of themselves by a hand's width overnight, leaving a clean scar in the moss where they stood. Nothing hunts, or if it does, it hunts slowly enough to be mistaken for weather. The land is young and has not eroded into kindness; its edges are still sharp, its stones still remember being liquid, and the city built across them has chosen not to soften them but to grow around their geometry, leaving the cuts and shelves exposed between the gardens.

At the edge of the basin the embankments give way to a flat of black sinter that stretches farther than the haze allows you to see. Out there the vegetation thins to isolated stands, each one a dark island in the warm dim, and between them the ground clicks faintly as it cools and does not cool, a young crust still negotiating with whatever made it. The city does not end so much as space itself out until the next tower is a rumor of brass light in the fog, and then the next, and then only the sound of water deciding, somewhere out of sight, whether to fall or to rise.
