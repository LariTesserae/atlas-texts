The plateau is not stone that plants have covered. The plateau is the plant, and the plant is new.

It rose, or grew—those are not different events here—faster than weather could round it. The ridges are still edges. A rib of living tissue the width of a river-valley stands up out of the dark with a crest so sharp it would cut a hand if a hand could reach it, and the crest is not bark and not bone. It is compressed fiber, pale where a recent split has opened it, beading with a sap that freezes before it can run. Touch is rare. The distances are wrong for touch.

Between the ribs the air is almost nothing. You can walk, or the things that walk can walk, for a day inside one hollow and not meet a wall, only the slow curve of a single fiber-arch going up into weather. The arch is so large it has its own cloud. Moisture gathers on the inner face, holds, lets go. Rain here is not a sky-event. It is the plant deciding, in a thousand places at once, to stop holding water. The drops fall through a darkness that has no floor you can see, and some of them freeze on the way down and arrive as a fine dry tick against whatever is below. Other drops find pockets—old splits, the cups where two ribs fused while both were still soft—and stay. Those pockets are the only standing water. They crust at the rim with ice no thicker than a leaf, and under the crust the water moves, because something in it is moving.

The cold is not weather passing over a place. The cold is the elevation, and the elevation is still increasing. In the newest growth, at the crests, the fiber is warm for a few hours after it splits—you can smell it, a green smell cut with iron—and then the heat leaves and the split seals under rime. Lower down, in the older hollows, the cold is settled and damp. Breath hangs. Sound does not.

Sound is the first thing that is wrong, and then the thing you stop noticing, and then the thing the place is. The hollows are instruments. A split opening somewhere you cannot see arrives as a crack that fills the whole volume, then as a long tearing that might be fiber or might be ice giving way along a rib three valleys off. Wind does not blow through. Wind is made here, by the ribs shedding heat at different rates, by the cloud on one arch collapsing into the hollow of the next. It comes in walls. Between the walls the air is so still you can hear an animal shift its weight on a fiber a kilometer away, the small wet sound of a pad unsticking.

They are abundant, and they are almost never where you are. The space is too large for crowding and too loud for silence, so the animals live as events in the noise: a flock that uses the updraft inside a splitting rib, a scatter of clicks from something that walks the undersides, a low continuous booming that is either a herd moving along a crest or the crest itself growing. You learn the difference by whether it stops when the wind-wall hits. The animals stop. The growth does not. At the scale of the plateau the growth is weather—fronts of new fiber shouldering up, hollows pinching shut over years that, from inside, feel like seasons. A pocket that held water last month is a seam. A seam that was a path is a wall, sharp, still beaded.

Almost nothing is built, and what is built looks like an argument with the plant that the plant is winning slowly. On a few ribs, where the fiber has grown dense enough to take a point, there are ladders of pegs—bone, or the harder knots of the plant itself, driven in by hand. They do not go far. A platform lashed with twisted fiber-cord sags over one of the ice-crusted pockets, and on it a coil of the same cord, a blade of knapped something that is not stone, a heap of dried cups cut from the inner wall. Nobody is on the platform. The pegs below it have been overgrown at their bases, the living tissue closing over the wounds the way it closes over everything, leaving the pegs as a line of small dark eyes. Higher up, on a crest that split recently enough to still smell green, someone has cut a mark: three strokes, then a fourth that stops where the sap welled and froze the hand's work in place. The mark is already sealing. By the time the next wind-wall passes, it will be a scar, and the scar will be louder than the mark ever was, because scars on these ribs catch the air and whine.

Light never quite arrives. What passes for day is a grayness in the cloud the arches wear, a paler dark moving along the inner faces when a gap opens between two ribs and then closes as they grow toward each other. In that brief paler dark you can see, once, how far it goes: rib after rib, hollow after hollow, each hollow big enough to hold its own rain and its own flock and its own single abandoned platform, diminishing not into a horizon but into a repetition that fog and fiber make identical. The plateau does not end in a view. It ends, if it ends, where the newest crests are still splitting open in the cold, sharp as the day they rose, and the sound of that opening is already on its way down through the dark to wherever you are standing, small, in all that living empty space.
