The first thing the eye accepts is distance. Not darkness—distance. A pale, even brilliance fills a volume so large that the far wall does not arrive as a wall at all, only as a slow thickening of the light, the way weather thickens on a horizon you will never walk to. The air is cold enough to sting the wet of the eyes. It does not smell of earth. It smells of cut green and of frost, a clean bitterness, as if a leaf the size of a weather-system had been split and left open.

There is no sky in the ordinary sense, and no ground that is not also a growth. You are below the surface of something, and the surface is not stone. Overhead, and underfoot, and off to either hand where the distances finally tire and close, the world is made of the same substance: a living mass, pale where the light comes through it, darker where it has thickened into rib and spar and keel. It reads, at first, as architecture—vaults, flying buttresses, a nave repeated until the repetition itself becomes the climate. Then a rib shifts. Not much. A hand’s breadth in an hour, with a soft, wet sound no louder than a sleeve drawn across a table. The edge it presents is sharp. New. The tissue has not yet been worn. Where it has most recently opened, the faces are clean as a split celery stalk, beaded, geometric, still deciding what they are.

The light is the plant’s own conduction. Somewhere above the living roof—how far is a question the place does not answer usefully—day falls on an outer skin, and the mass drinks it and passes it inward the way a leaf passes it to its own shaded underside. By the time it reaches these halls it has been sifted to a cold white-gold, shadowless, bright enough to read by and bright enough to exhaust the eye, because there is no contrast to rest in. Shadows exist only where one spar crosses another, and even those are thin, blue, and moving. The sun, if it is a sun, never appears. What appears is its consequence: a brightness with weather in it. High in the vaults, sheets of illumination brighten and dull on a cycle of minutes, as if cloud were crossing an outer face you cannot see. The dimming is never night. It is only the plant deciding, for a while, to keep more of the light for itself.

Scale makes a climate of this. The chambers are not rooms. A single hollow can hold a pressure system. Cold air slides down a living wall in a sheet you can watch, a faint shivering in the brightness, and pools in the lower thirds until it spills through a gap into the next volume and the next. Mist forms where the descending air meets a warmer film of breath off the tissue, and the mist does not fall as rain so much as wander, lose itself, and reappear as a glaze on whatever surface has cooled enough to take it. Water, here, is a rumor that keeps being confirmed. A seep along a vein. A pocket cupped in a fold of new growth, skimmed with ice at its edges by morning-that-is-not-morning. A dark thread of melt moving inside a translucent spar, visible for a hundred paces and then gone into thickness. The greater dryness is the rule. The air is damp against the teeth and dry in the throat an hour later. You can cross what would be, on a map, a province, and drink only what you wring from your own sleeve.

Sound does not keep up with the size of what is happening. The place is turbulent and nearly quiet, which is its particular unease. Growth at this scale ought to roar. Instead the changes arrive as pressure in the ears, as a sweetness or a bitterness on the air, as a spar that was a path and is, by the time you return, a sealed membrane with a bead of condensate along its fresh lip. Collapses, when they come, are local and soft: a span of tissue that has overreached thins, goes from gold to gray-green, and folds inward with a sound like heavy cloth. The volume it vacates is immediately too large. Wind—there is wind, internal, born of the temperature differences between a sunlit roof-mass and a shaded throat of root—moves through the new opening and then forgets itself in the next hollow. Echo barely exists. The living surfaces drink it. A shout travels a little way and comes back as warmth on the face, nothing more.

Emptiness is the other law. The plant is total—there is no soil that is not already root, no wall that is not already stem, no “outside” of the organism that a person inside it could reach without cutting—and yet almost none of the volume is occupied. The living mass spends itself on the boundaries. Between the ribs the air is vacant for what feels like miles. You walk a spar because the spar is the only continuous thing, a pale road of tissue maybe three paces wide, springy, cold, faintly sticky where a duct has wept and then skinned over. To either side the drop is not a drop so much as an invitation to the scale. Things drift in it sometimes. Shed scales of outer cuticle, curled, bright, turning as they fall for so long that they stop being debris and become birds, and then stop being birds and become litter again when they touch a lower face and adhere. There are few actual birds. Fauna is a scarcity the plant seems to enforce without hunting. What lives here lives on the margins of the margins: a moth-sized thing that grips the freshest cuts and drinks the bead before it freezes; a slow, armored crawler no bigger than a hand, the color of the tissue it matches, found perhaps twice in a month of travel; once, far off and motionless on a spar that had no business holding weight, a shape that might have been an animal and might have been a knot of growth and was gone behind brightness before it could be settled.

The youth of the place is in the edges. Nothing here has been filed down by age. Every new chamber is a wound that decided to become a room, and the lips of the wound are keen. Travelers—there have been travelers; the evidence is small and does not accumulate—learn to keep their hands off the freshest faces. A cut plant of this kind weeps a clear, cold sap that crusts into a sugar-glass by the end of a dimming and then, if the air stays cold enough, powders away. The powder drifts. In the lower hollows it gathers in pale drifts against the windward sides of spars, not snow, but kin to it, and it creaks underfoot with a sound almost too fine to hear.

The built things are scattered the way sparks are scattered: each one implies a fire you are not standing in. Lashed to a spar, somewhere, a platform of split ribs—harvested, it seems, from tissue already thinning toward collapse, so that the cutting was half a kindness. The lashings are cord of twisted fiber, hand-worked, uneven, the knots simple and redone where the living wood beneath them swelled and split the old binding. A stone, inexplicable until you understand it was carried, sits as a weight on a membrane that would otherwise billow. There are notches in a keel, evenly spaced, where someone counted dimmings or counted spars or counted nothing and only needed the marks to be there. A ladder of pegs goes up a wall for thirty paces and ends, not broken, simply unfinished, as if the next peg had been in a hand when the wall decided to grow a flange across the route. No metal. No mortar. The tools that made these were edges of the plant itself, dried and sharpened, and perhaps a few stones brought down from whatever surface exists above the drinking-skin. Whoever made them did not stay in one hollow long enough to found a city. The place will not hold a city. A spar that is a road in one dimming is a wall in the next, or a thread, or gone. The marks are statements of having been here between two changes.

Rules, then, as they declare themselves if you remain:

Light is a fluid the organism rations. Brightness is constant in kind and inconstant in amount, and the dimmings are not time so much as appetite.

Water is film, pocket, vein, and mist. It is never a river you can trust, and it is never absent. Ice forms in the stillest cups and does not last; the tissue beneath it is warmer than the air by a fraction, and the fraction is enough.

The solid world is temporary and sharp. What looks like stone is fiber under tension. What looks like a path is a decision the plant has not yet reversed. Young growth cuts. Old growth, where it exists, is only growth that has not yet been asked to move.

Sound lies. The great events are the quiet ones. If you hear a tear, it is small and near. If the pressure changes in your ears and the taste of the air goes sweet, something the size of a district has opened or sealed, and you will not see it until the wind arrives, mild, cold, and already forgetting.

Distance is inhabited by almost nothing, and that nothing is the point. The plant fills the category of place so completely that it can afford to leave the interior hollow. Weather lives in the hollow. Travelers, when there are any, live on the lines. Between the lines is brightness, cold, and the long fall of a curl of cuticle that will not strike anything for an hour.

If there is an outer world, it is present only as the light’s source and as the occasional stone. People speak, when they speak at all, of a surface of low green under a hard sun, wind-scoured, where this same organism shows only its back—a continent of leaf lying on the planet like a closed hand. They say the ways down are accidents: a split after a surface storm, a throat that opened and did not immediately heal. They say most such throats close. The ones that remain do so because the mass below has found a use for the draft, the way a body finds a use for a nostril. Whether any of that is true is hard to test from inside. The brightness does not point upward reliably. Up is wherever the tissue is thinner. Down is wherever the cold has pooled. The compass is the next spar, and the next, and the knowledge that both of them are already, quietly, becoming something else.
