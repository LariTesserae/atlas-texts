The water is the first fact and it does not behave like weather. It has no surface you are meant to break. It occupies the whole of a high, worn country, a regional sea cupped where the last of the land once stood, and it has occupied it so long that the idea of a shore has thinned into rumor. The cold is not a bite. It is a settled cool, the temperature of water that has forgotten sun and forgotten depth’s heat alike, the same against the mouth, the same in the long open reaches, the same inside the grown rooms. Nothing in it stratifies anymore. Warm does not rise. Cold does not pool. The whole body of it holds one degree, as a finished thing holds one shape.

You understand the height only by negatives. There is no heavier water beneath this water, no lower country tugging at the belly. The plain that carries the sea was a range, once, and then a plateau, and then, across an age with no remaining name, a floor. Erosion finished its work and stopped, not because the water ceased to move, but because there was nothing left for movement to take. Stone, if any stone remains, is a rumor under a continuous living pavement: a skin of plant so total that the substrate of the place is no longer mineral. Silt is shed fiber. The taste in the water is leaf-mineral, old, faintly sweet, never foul. The floor is near-flat for distances you can cross only by patience, and then it steps, not as cliff but as a grown terrace whose edge has been worried smooth into a lip. Those terraces are the last topography. They do not retreat.

What stands up from that floor is sparse, and it is all made. Not with tools. There is no tool in this country, no cut mark, no joint, no device, no fire-memory in the materials. The making was growth, and the growth has ended in the only way growth can end without dying: by arriving at a form it can keep. Across the open water, far enough apart that each presence has a darkness of its own, rise the structures. Some are halls with many thresholds, their doorways oval and worn, their walls a dense pith that has hardened into something like horn and then been polished by the same current for so long that polish and material are the same fact. Some are single columns, hollow, ribbed inside, tall enough that their height is the extreme of the place — they reach into the upper dark of the sea and stop, flat-topped, as if the growing had one day met a ceiling and accepted it. Some are low, wide tables of intergrown stems, their upper surfaces level with a care that looks intended and is only the final shape of an ancient instruction. Between them the water is empty in the large sense. You can travel a long while and meet nothing but the floor’s living mat, the cool, and the sound.

The vegetation is not a forest standing in a sea. It is the sea’s other body. Even in the open reaches the water carries a fine living weather: threads, films, a slow meal of cells that never clouds and never clears, a suspension so continuous that to swim is to move through plant the way, elsewhere, one moves through air. It does not snag. It does not bloom. It replaces itself at the rate it is taken, and the rate is exact, so the dimness never thickens and never lifts. Against the great structures this fine mass is only the grain of the medium. The structures themselves are the coarse grain — trunks fused into walls, walls fused into vaults, vaults whose inner wood has been bored by water into chambers that no longer change their volume. Plant fills the category of substance. There is no bare place to put a hand. Even the apparent stone of a sill, pale and cold, splits along a fiber if worried, and weeps a clear sap that the water takes and does not stain.

Light is almost not a property here. What exists is a directionless dim, green-black, colder than the water by a feeling rather than a measure. It has no source you can turn toward. It does not flicker, does not dawn, does not fail. In the open water it is enough to know a shape at the distance of a few body-lengths and to lose it after that. Inside the grown halls it falls off into a dark that is still not blindness: the walls return a low sheen, as polished horn will, and you can tell threshold from wall by that sheen and by the change in the sound. No lamp has ever been carried through these rooms. Nothing here stores light or spends it.

The sound is the property that refuses the stillness you keep expecting. The sea is loud. Not with storm — there is no storm, and there has not been one since the floor became a floor — but with a maintained chord. Every hollow column is a pipe. Every terrace-lip sheds a thin perpetual sheet into the next lower basin, and the basins are arranged so that what falls is returned, somewhere out of sight, by a slow rising through living conduits in the pavement. The circuit is closed and has been closed long enough that the note does not wander. In the open water the chord is broad, a low roar with a grain in it, the countless small frictions of the suspended plant. Near a hall it narrows and becomes architectural: a tone in the doorway, a different tone in the second chamber, a beat where two conduits meet and have always met. The loudness carries. Across the sparse distances you can hear the next structure before you can see it, and you can hear, farther, the one after that, until the region is a single instrument heard from inside.

The animals live inside that instrument and do not conduct it. They are abundant, and they are not crowded. The space between presences is honored by them the way the water honors it. A long, pale grazer moves alone along a path in the mat that is not a scar: the path is a groove the plant keeps open, replacing its edges at the rate of the grazing, so the animal walks a road that is both wound and garden and that will be the same road when the animal is gone and another, identical in habit, has taken the route. High in the open dark, widely spaced, hang the filterers — bodies like slow bells, moored to nothing, holding station in the current because the current holds one station. They are many if you count across the region. You will not see two at once unless you are patient and the dimness is kind. In the chambers live the listeners, small and many, pressed to the inner walls where the chord is purest, their bodies tuned to particular overtones. They do not sing against the architecture. They answer it, and the answer is part of the loudness, a dry ticking or a soft knock, regular as the fall of water over a lip that will not wear further.

Nothing hunts in a way that changes the count. Taking and replacing have the same arithmetic. A swimmer-beast the color of the dimness — you know it by the brief colder touch of its wake — passes through a loose gathering of lesser bodies and the gathering reforms at its prior number, not by miracle but by the way the region is stocked: every loss is already met by a life that was waiting in a conduit-mouth, a nursery that has always been full because it has always released exactly what the halls can bear. You do not see nurseries as events. You see, if you look into a low arch and wait, the same small emergence you would have seen a year ago, or a thousand. The fauna are abundant as a climate is abundant: everywhere available, nowhere heaped.

The rules are simple because they have been simplified by age. Nothing new is built, and nothing built is lost. A chamber that has three thresholds will have three when every living animal now in the sea has died and been replaced. Growth and wear are one motion seen from two sides, and they cancel. The currents do not migrate with a season; there is no season. The cool does not deepen at night; there is no night distinct from the dim. Animals do not leave the region. There is no outside they can reach without crossing a lip of the high sea into air, and that lip, far off, is not a place the living arrangements point toward. The chord does not swell or drop. If you learn one route — from the flat-topped column to the table-structure to the hall with the oval doors — you can leave and return by the sound alone, and the sound will not have been revised.

What is unusual is only the completeness of this. Other places change slowly enough to seem eternal; this one has reached the state those places imitate. You can rest a hand on a sill and know that the polish under your palm is not accumulating. It is the polish. You can listen to the fall over a terrace and know that the lip is not becoming a waterfall’s throat; the throat was declined ages ago, when there was still something to decline. The abundance of animals does not become a swarm. The totality of the plant does not become a thicket. The height does not become a view. There is no view. There is a dark, cool, loud, open country, made entirely of living matter that has finished becoming, standing sparsely on a floor that will not lower, under a water that will not leave, in a light that will not arrive.

If you stay, the place does not reveal a further secret. It reveals its sameness more carefully. The second chamber’s tone is a third lower than the first, and was, and will be. The grazer’s road bends once, at a fused root that the pavement grew around and then stopped growing around. A bell-body turns in the current and the turn is not a decision; it is how that body meets that line of flow, every time. The extreme of the elevation is not dramatic from within. It is the reason the water can be this cool and this unpressed by any deeper world, the reason the air of lower countries is a substance this sea does not bother to remember. Up is only where the columns end, flat against a dark that does not brighten.

Leave a mark and the mark closes. Not quickly, not as healing looks when you watch it — at the rate of the equilibrium, which is the rate at which you cannot watch. Speak, and your voice is taken into the chord and does not remain as an echo with your shape in it. The halls were not made for visitors, and they do not resent them. They are not waiting. They are the finished form of a making that no longer needs to make, standing in their own loud water, sparse on a flattened height, green-black, cool, and exact.
