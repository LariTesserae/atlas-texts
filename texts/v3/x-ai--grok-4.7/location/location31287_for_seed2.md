The first thing the water tells you is that it has a floor, and that the floor was poured.

You come down through a ceiling of moving light—ordinary daylight, broken by a wind that has had hundreds of kilometers to build a chop—and the brightness does not fail the way it fails in a natural deep. The basin is vast and, over most of its area, not abyssal. Forty meters, sixty, a hundred in the service trenches. Enough water to drown a country, not enough to extinguish noon. The light arrives green-silver, cool, and sufficient to read by, if what you are reading is the grid stamped into the plain below.

The plain is concrete. Pale, still, the color of a yard that has not yet learned dirt. Formwork lines run in parallels until they meet a joint, and the joint is a dark slot wide enough to put a boat in, tarred, bolted, sharp as the day the forms came off. Nothing has rounded it. Silt has only begun: a flour of fines in the lee of a bollard, a pale drift against the upstream face of a caisson, a darker smear where a current dies in a corner and drops what it carried. Scrape any of it aside and the aggregate shows, clean, young. There is no bedrock under this sea. There is plate, pour, pile, and under that more plate. If the made floor ever ends, it ends in another made floor.

Between works the volume is almost empty. You can travel for a long time and see only water, the slow crumple of the surface far above, and the faint orthogonal ghost of the grid. Sound does not leave you alone in that emptiness. It arrives from a long way off, without a visible source: a cavitation hiss, a piston thud in threes, the organ-note of a pump house pushing a river’s worth of water through a throat of steel. The basin is loud the way a factory is loud when you stand in the yard rather than in the shed. The noise is the place. It has weather in it—gusts when a bank of engines loads, lulls when a gate train closes—and it has distance, so that two machineries can sing across each other for an hour before you see either one.

When a work does appear it arrives all at once, because nothing here has been softened into a coastline. A cooling tower’s underwater root: a ring of columns, still showing the ridges of the molds, hung with a thin beard of algae and not much else. Vegetation has not claimed this sea. A few rope-kelp frames stand in the shallower squares, half of them bare wire. On the high pilings a green film, no forest. The water is cool, recently filled or continually turned over by the pumps, and poor in the slow nutrients that make a coast shaggy. What grows, grows where metal offers a grip and a light stays on past dusk.

The animals found the lights and the intakes before any garden did. Around every lit grating there is a school—small, ordinary, silver, the kind of fish that follow a harbor. They pulse when the current through the intake strengthens, and they break when something larger comes up the dark of the pipe gallery to feed. That happens often. The fauna is not a soup; it is abundant the way workers are abundant at a gate at shift change, and absent in the miles between gates. Mussels have set on the lower bolts in a single crust, new enough that you can still see the steel between clumps. A seal-shaped body turns in the upwelling where a return culvert dumps warmer, particle-rich water; gulls work the same boil on the surface, their shadows crossing the columns. Farther out, where the grid runs empty, you may see nothing living for a long time, and then a traveling cloud of bait and the one shape under it, both of them using the basin as if it had always been a sea.

It has not. The rules are still the rules of a machine that happens to be full of water.

Currents are scheduled. In the open squares the wind makes the surface, and the surface is large enough to have its own storms—gray walls of rain walking the fetch, a steep short sea when the wind opposes a pumped set, fog pooled over the cooler sluices by morning. Below, the lasting flows are engineered. Twice a day, or on whatever cycle the works keep, a gate complex opens and a lane of the sea becomes a river. You can watch the lane: a sharper line of light, a change in the pitch of the roar, debris—paint flakes, a lost fender, a raft of weed that grew on some other installation—drawn along a path that does not wander. When the gate closes, the river stops being a river. The fish that were using it hesitate. Some stay in the residual eddy all night, under the work lamps, which are plain industrial lamps in glass, yellow-white, not arranged for wonder.

The structures themselves are industrial in the old sense. Engine halls on piers that march down into the water as stair towers and then as column grids. Valve galleries you can enter, if you are equipped, and find them dry above a certain line, loud beyond speech, smelling of oil and hot metal and the sweet-chemical edge of treated water. Pipes the diameter of streets, ceramic-lined, running in parallel across the plain like a second, stricter geography. Foundry smoke sometimes visible from below as a stain on the bright ceiling, when an island plant is pouring. Electrified gantries. Derricks. A cable on the bottom, freshly laid, its jacket unbitten, its route as straight as a surveyor’s nerve. No one has built a reef of ruins here because there are no ruins yet. Edges cut. Welds are bright. A number, stenciled, is still legible at sixty meters.

Near the surface the day is simply day. Wave-lenses travel across the concrete. A squall dims them; the lamps on the works do not care, and click to a higher set on their own timers, so that a storm can make the distant installations suddenly more visible than the sun does. At depth in the trenches—the only places that feel like depth—the light thins to a working dusk and the sound thickens, because the trench is also a duct. Things move down there on purpose: inspection cages on rails, a blunt-nosed tug with a lamp bar, a stream of cold intake water heading for a plant that sits, somewhere above, on an island that is also poured.

Stand, or hover, in one of the empty squares at midafternoon and the contradictions hold without strain. You are below the surface of a sea large enough to make weather, and the sea is an object. It was filled into a container. The container is new enough that the world has not yet blurred its corners. Life is already busy at every joint and every light, and the volume those lives do not occupy is the greater part of what there is. The loudness never resolves into a single engine. It is the sum of conveyance, cooling, generation, and the wind, conducted through water that was put here and is being kept here, cool and clear and grid-lit, on a floor that still remembers the molds.
