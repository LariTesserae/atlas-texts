The basin holds its daylight the way a cistern holds water: at a level, and without expense. From the rim the sun is simply there, a hand’s width above the western knife-edge, bright enough to read a face by and cool enough that the light never gathers into heat. It does not climb. Shadows of ridge and wall and standing animal lie where they have always lain, dark and exact, with crisp margins, as if cut from hide and laid down. Nothing lengthens. Noon is not a place this country visits, and neither is dark.

The rim itself is new stone that will not become old. The ridges broke upward recently enough that their edges are still edged — plates of gray rock with pale, unlichened fractures, scree fans whose smallest chips have not yet walked downhill, a gash of a pass whose walls show tool-fresh corners though no tool finished them. Weather has been refused. The fractures stay bright. A pebble balanced on another pebble is not waiting to fall; the waiting has been concluded, and the balance is the fact.

Below the rim the ground opens into a valley of a walkable size, town-sized, with three shoulders of highland and a long tilt of open floor between them. You can see across it. You cannot see out of it except at the pass. The floor is not crowded. Stonework stands in it the way trees stand in a park that was never planted as a park: compounds, lanes, cistern rims, a market terrace, a second terrace above that, all of them with ground between. The city is real — planned, walled in places, channeled, named by its water — and yet most of what the eye crosses is unbuilt. A person could lose a house in the interval and take some time finding it again.

The air is damp without being wet. Moisture lives in pockets. Along the north foot of the rim a seep writes a dark line down the rock and then stops, and the line is always the same length, always the same depth of black. In the hollows, water stands in stone basins that people have only regularized, not invented: round pools with lips of unrounded rock, cold, still, reflecting the fixed sun as a coin that does not travel. Ceramic pipes, hand-rolled, clay-joined, run from pool to pool through the open ground and under the lanes. They do not drip. Inside them the water is moving and not moving; it occupies the pipes completely, at every moment, in the same arrangement. Condensation beads on the shaded side of every standing wall, a regular stipple, and does not gather into drops that fall. Touch a wall and your fingers come away cool and faintly slick. Leave the print. In time — though time here is only the order of noticing — the print is gone and the stipple is as it was, bead for bead.

Where plants have taken, they have taken thickly. Mats of fern, cold-country and heavy, fill the seep-line and the margins of the pools, fronds overlapping until the ground disappears, each frond entire, none yellowing, none in fiddlehead. Between these islands the floor is grit and pale grass in separated tufts, and the tufts are lush at the root — dense, sappy, wet to the thumb — and then simply absent for a stretch of open stone. There are small trees in the compounds, broad-leaved, their canopies exactly occupied, no bare twig, no new shoot. Fruit hangs on some of them, the same fruit, colored through, not ripening toward anything. A child could pick one. The branch would be as full afterward. No one tests this often. The people who live here learned the cost of testing early, which is not punishment but tidying: the valley returns itself, and whatever you moved is either back or exactly replaced, and your effort has nowhere to lodge.

The animals are many and hard to count, because they do not flock and do not hide. Goats in twos and threes occupy the open turf, heads down, mouths working, the grass under them as long when they raise their heads as when they lowered them. Their bells, if they were belled, would be the loudest thing in the basin; they are not belled. On the cistern lips, fish hold in the upper water, scarce enough that each has a handspan of room, numerous enough that every cistern has them. They flick, and the flick does not disturb the coin of the sun. Birds sit the ridges of roofs — ordinary birds, dark, small, abundant on the scale of the city — and sometimes one leaves a ridge and returns to it by a path you could draw, the same path, a short arc that spends itself and restores the bird to the same grip on the same stone. Insects move in the fern shadow. You hear almost none of them. The valley has a sound, but it is the sound of a place that has already finished every noise it intends: a low, directionless hush, stone giving back a little air, water touching clay inside a pipe without striking it, the soft pull of a hoof resetting its weight. Speech carries. It does not echo. Raised voices feel indecent, not by law but by fit; the air does not accept them. People talk at the level of the hush, and at that level a voice can cross a lane and no farther.

They built in the stone of the rim, which splits true. Walls are dry-laid, courses so tight a knife will not enter, corners left sharp because nothing rounds them. Lintels are single slabs. Bronze appears where a joint must hold against a pull — pins, a few straps, the odd hook — and nowhere else. Timber is used as timber, not as architecture’s idea of timber: posts, simple frames, shutters of boards that have not silvered and will not. Rope is fiber, thick, replaced by identical rope when a strand would have frayed, so that replacement and fraying occupy the same instant and neither wins. There is no mill that turns. There is no fire that is let to consume; hearths, where they are lit, burn at a size that matches the wood’s refusal to become ash, a technical puzzle the cooks have stopped treating as a puzzle. Cooking is placement and warmth. The food is always at the point of being ready.

The plan of the place is older than any urgency. A main lane enters from the pass and does not hurry: wide enough for two carts that are never both in it, paved with fitted grit, bordered by channels that are full. Off it, the compounds sit apart, each with a yard, a tree, a tank, a sleeping room and a workroom and a wall of a height you can see over. The market terrace is a broad step of dressed rock with lines cut in it where goods are set. The goods are set. Grain in open sacks, salt in cakes, hides, bronze pins in rows, leafy stems bunched and wet. No one haggles in the way of places where amounts change. You take what your hands already know is yours to take, and the row is full when you look back, or else someone has already walked the other way with the exact measure that keeps the row full. Accounts, such as they are, are memories of arrangement. A mark on a post would be a lie within the hour, or less than an hour; there is no hour. The sun does not make them.

Above the market, the civic terrace faces the fixed sun and holds the whole basin in one look: rim, seep-line, pools, fern islands, the scattered roofs, the goats like knots in the open weave, the pass. People come up to it and stand where their shadows already are. Children run, because children do, and the running is real — breath, impact, laughter kept small — and when the running is over the terrace is not scuffed. A scraped knee scabs at the rate the child notices, and then the knee is as before the fall, and the child has the memory without the mark. Memory is the only erosion permitted. It does not alter the stone.

This is the unusual thing, and it is not spectacular. The basin is not a spell someone is holding. It is an equilibrium that has reached the end of process and stayed there, high and cool and only partly watered, lush in its pockets, bare between them, full of animals that spend themselves in closed paths, built by hands whose tools are simple and whose city is large enough to have wards and quiet enough that a ward is mostly distance. Change is not forbidden. It is unsaturated. There is no remaining gradient for it to fall along — not of heat, not of water, not of hunger, not of stone wanting to be round. What you do here is an eddy. The eddy closes.

Stand long enough on the terrace and the hush starts to seem like a surface you could lean on. A goat shifts. A bird redraws its arc. A bead of condensation on the wall beside your hand is the same bead. The sun, unmoving, looks like ordinary afternoon paused between one thing and another, and the pause is the climate. Somewhere in a pipe the water occupies its shape. Somewhere a fern frond is fully itself, and has been, and is. The city keeps its intervals. Nothing is coming. Nothing has just left. The place is complete in the way a held note is complete, and the note is almost too quiet to call a note at all.
