The plain has no horizon in the ordinary sense. Distance simply continues until the eye gives up, and what it gives up on is not haze but repetition: the same low register of made ground, warm and pale, running on under a sky so bright it seems manufactured to a specification. There is no water anywhere in it. The air is dry enough that the mouth forgets saliva. What passes for weather is a slow rearrangement of heat and dust, and both of those are part of the construction.

Everything underfoot was built. There is no bedrock that was not poured, sintered, grown, or laid. Ages ago the distinction between foundation and landscape was abandoned, and the ages since have only confirmed the decision. Erosion here is not a river’s work. It is thermal breathing, the crawl of fine mineral, the patient subsidence of structures that were never meant to stand tall. What remains is the oldest stable state the builders could reach and then, apparently, leave: a continental floor worn nearly flat, its original ambitions reduced to a skin of seams, lips, and shallow basins no deeper than a person’s knee. Elevation barely exists. You do not climb this place. You cross it.

And yet it is dense. The flatness is a lie told by height. In plan the ground is saturated—modules, trays, lattices, buried vaults whose roofs have become the walking surface, conduits pressed so close they touch along whole kilometers. A step can land on three different materials: a pale ceramic that rings faintly if struck, a dark sintered mass that drinks the light, a film of grown mineral no thicker than paper and older than any name you could give it. Nothing towers. The density is lateral and layered, a geology of intention compressed until intention looks like stone. In places the layers have sheared, and you can read a cross-section as if it were cliff: courses of infrastructure, each one a former surface, each one subsequently buried by the next campaign of making, until the campaigns themselves settled and stopped adding height.

The light is excessive and even. Shadows are short, pale, and reluctant, as if the illumination arrives from more than one agreement with the sky. At midday—if midday is the right word for a brightness that does not much vary—the plain goes almost white, and the seams between materials turn into a fine dark script running to the edge of sight. Heat sits on the skin without quite burning. It is a warm world, not a furnace: the temperature of metal left in sun, of dust that has stored the day and gives it back slowly after whatever passes for evening. Nights, when they come, do not go fully dark. Residual panels in the ground keep a low luminescence, maintenance rather than mercy, so that the plain never quite releases you into true night.

Sound is nearly absent. Wind moves, but the surface is so continuous and so low that it does not whistle; it only shifts a veil of powder from one tray to the next. Footsteps die within a few paces. There is no drip, no insect tick, no far machinery with a voice. What activity there is announces itself by other means. A panel warms under the sole and then cools as some buried exchange completes. A line of dust sorts itself, grain by grain, into a cleaner band along a seam—an old sorting law still running, silent, indifferent to whether anyone is watching. Far off, once in a long while, a section of the plain exhales: a soft collapse of air from a vault that has equalized pressure, gone before you can turn toward it. The dynamism is real. It is simply not loud. Processes continue at the pace of materials that have already decided what they are.

Vegetation is a rumor with a few proofs. In the seams, where two ceramics have pulled apart by a finger’s width over centuries, a dry film sometimes takes hold—ocher, gray-green, not quite plant and not quite scale. It does not drink. It photosynthesizes on trace volatiles and on the light, which is never scarce, and it grows so slowly that a patch the size of a hand may be the work of a human lifetime or longer. Elsewhere, in the shallow basins where dust collects and bakes, you find brittle rosettes of a mineral-organism that looks grown and feels fired. They crumble if you are careless. They are not common. Most of the plain is bare of them, and the bareness does not feel like aftermath so much as policy: life was never the point of the floor, only an occasional tenant of its cracks.

Fauna is rarer still. What lives here does not flock and does not call. Now and then a low shape crosses a pale tray at the limit of vision—something the color of the ground, moving with the economy of an animal that cannot afford noise or waste water it does not have. It may be animal. It may be a mobile repair form that outlasted its instructions and kept the habit of patrol. You will not get close enough to settle it. Smaller things occupy the film-lichens: mites of chitin or of articulated ceramic, indistinguishable at a glance, harvesting whatever the films fix from the air. Their presence is a density you infer from a faint pitting in the growth, not from any sound they make. The ecology, such as it is, runs on light, dust, and patience. Predator and prey, if those words still apply, meet so seldom that the meeting is an event in the weather, not a pattern you can watch.

The strangeness is not theatrical. It accumulates. Distances do not fail dramatically; they fail by a small, repeating discrepancy, so that a feature you have been walking toward for an hour is either nearer than the light suggested or has been, all along, a repetition of a nearer feature, printed at continental scale by a construction method that preferred modules to monuments. Some surfaces remember contact. A palm pressed to the dark sinter leaves a warmer patch that lingers, then migrates—not smearing, but translating, a few centimeters an hour, along the grain of the material, as if heat here were a message the floor is still willing to carry to a receiver that no longer exists. Compasses, if you trust them, drift toward certain seams and then lose interest. The sky, examined long enough, shows a faint tessellation, a brightness broken into cells too large to be clouds and too regular to be atmosphere in the old sense. Whether the sky is an enclosure or a field projected across the upper air is not a question the ground answers. The ground has its own work.

That work is the last active law. The plain maintains itself without ceremony. Dust is sorted. Heat is shunted along buried runs that never surface as pipes, only as a change in the warmth of a tray. Failed modules are not repaired in any way a visitor would recognize as repair; they are isolated, their load taken up by neighbors, their surfaces left to the same slow flattening that has already had millennia. You can walk for days and never see a tool, a vehicle, a hand. You can still tell that nothing here is finished in the sense of abandoned to nature, because there is no nature left to abandon it to. The substrate is the building. The building is the climate. The climate is a set of quiet cycles—bright, warm, dry, dense, almost still—running on a floor so old it has become the definition of level.

If you stop and wait, the place does not perform for you. A film of dust advances across your boot and is corrected, grain by grain, back toward the seam the sorting law prefers. The light does not soften. Far away, another vault exhales, and the sound does not reach you; only a brief, inexplicable looseness in the air, gone as soon as noticed. The plain continues. It has already become what it was built, over an age, to be: not a ruin, not a city, but the final form of a made world, low and endless, bright enough to hurt, and quiet enough that the ongoing work of it feels like silence that has learned how to move.
