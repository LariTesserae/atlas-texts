The chamber is not a cave that later became a room. It was cut, and then it stayed cut, and the cutting has been finished for so long that the tool-marks have gone soft at the edges and the rock has taken the shape as its own.

You come to it from below, which is the only way. A seam in wet stone, narrow enough that a shoulder has to turn, opens into a pocket no larger than a market square and no taller than a low hall. The ceiling is close. The walls are close. Everything in it is close to everything else, and has been, and will be. There is no weather here in the ordinary sense. There is only the condition the place has already reached.

Water is in the rock rather than on it. The stone weeps in sheets so thin they look like polish until a drop gathers and falls, and another is already forming in the same place. Channels were cut when the settlement was made—shallow rills, finger-wide, running along the bases of walls and under thresholds—and they still run, always the same volume, always the same cold. The water is not rain. Nothing falls from above except what condenses on the ceiling and slides down the same paths the first builders taught it. Pools sit in depressions worn by that constancy, not by flood. Their surfaces barely move. They are loud anyway.

The loudness is the first thing and the lasting thing. The pocket is a struck bell that never stops ringing, except the strike is water on stone, and the stone was chosen, or found, for how it answers. Every drip lands in a bowl that was hollowed to a particular curve. Every rill drops a thumb’s height into the next rill across a lip that was left sharp on purpose and has only rounded a little since. The sound does not rise and fall. It does not have a storm-voice and a dry-voice. It is one voice, dense, layered, near enough to touch: a low rush in the under-channels, a bright ticking from the ceiling-bowls, a hiss where water skins down a wall that was dressed almost smooth. People who live here stop hearing it as sound. They hear the absence of it when they leave, which they rarely do, because leaving means the seam, and the seam is the whole world’s door.

There are no plants. Not a moss-film, not a pale root in a crack, not the green that usually comes to any wet stone and stays. The wet here does not feed that kind of life, or the life never arrived, or it arrived and could not hold. What grows, if the word still fits, is mineral. Pale crusts bloom on the lips of channels where the water slows—thin, chalky, built grain by grain, the same way frost builds except it does not melt. In some corners the crust has been allowed to thicken into coralline shelves. In others it is scraped back on a schedule older than any living memory, because if it closes a channel the sound changes, and the sound is not allowed to change. The scrapings are piled in baskets of split reed-root brought in from somewhere else, or of bark, or of nothing living at all: most of the baskets are woven from strips of the same pale crust, dried and peeled, a material the place makes from itself.

The settlement fills the pocket the way a hand fills a glove. There is no open center left for gathering in the way a town square gathers. The floor is a stair of platforms, each one a house or a work-room or both, separated by curbs no higher than an ankle and by the channels that double as streets. You step across water to visit. You do not step far. A child’s reach from one sleeping-ledge touches the next family’s wall. The walls are stone left in place and stone set back: slabs levered, wedged, bedded in a mortar of crushed crust and clay carried down the seam in skins. No metal shows, or so little that a buckle is an heirloom and a knife is a rumor. The tools are stone on stone, bone where bone can be had, and the hard shells of whatever rare thing sometimes comes up the seam and does not leave. Edges are ground, not forged. Joints are lashed with wet-sinew that tightens as it dries and then, in this air, never quite dries.

Light is a ration. A few oil-cups burn, fat from those same rare visitors, wicks of twisted fiber from outside. They are set in niches cut at sitting-eye height, and they do not travel. People move; the flames stay. Between them the chamber is dim in a way that has nothing to do with night. The wet stone takes light and keeps a little of it, a gray sheen on every dressed face, so that even the unlit corners are not black. They are the color of water held in the hand. Shadows do not jump. Nothing here jumps.

What lives besides the people is scarce and does not announce itself. Now and then a pale many-legged thing the length of a finger walks the underside of a channel-lip, always the same route, always ignoring the hands that could take it. It is not eaten. Eating it would be like eating a hinge. Higher, in the dark of the ceiling where the condensing water begins, something no one has properly seen makes a softer click than the bowls, on a longer interval, and has made it for as long as the bowls have rung. Children are taught the interval the way other children are taught a count. If the click comes early, you mention it. It does not come early.

The unusual thing is not a monster and not a miracle. It is the fit. Every surface has been answered by a use, and every use has worn itself into the surface until you cannot say which proposed the other. A seat is a hollow that a back fills exactly. A shelf is a bedding-plane that already wanted to be flat. The door—the only door, the seam—has had its edges polished by shoulders into a curve that matches the turn people make without thinking. The water never rises over the curbs and never falls enough to expose the channel floors. The oil never quite smokes the ceiling black; the ceiling sweats the soot away and carries it into the rills, which take it somewhere under the lowest platform and do not bring it back. The place is in balance the way a millstone at rest is in balance: heavy, seated, not about to become another shape.

If you stay, the loudness settles into your ribs and becomes the measure of the room. You learn which bowl is which by pitch alone. You learn that the left-hand wall weeps faster than the right, and that this has always been true, and that the left-hand houses are built a little higher on their platforms because of it, and that no one discusses this as a decision. It is how the pocket is. Cool air moves only where the channels move, a thin breath you feel at the ankles and nowhere else. Above the ankle the air is still, damp, and the same temperature it was when the first lamp was lit, whenever that was. Time here is not a change in the room. It is the number of times you have crossed the same water to the same ledge, and found the hollow still fitted to your back, and the click still on its count, and the roar still unwilling to become quiet.
