You hear the place before the ridge will admit it exists. The sound comes up through the soles first, a pressure more than a tone, as if the stone underfoot had been struck once, long ago, and had never been allowed to finish ringing. Higher, where the air has already gone thin and mean, the pressure sorts itself into strands: a dry ticking, a wetter click, a rustle with no single author, and under all of it a note so steady it could be mistaken for the mountain’s own breathing. Nothing on the outside of the ridge explains it. The outside is what extreme height usually is — scoured, bright in the wrong way, almost vacant, wind with nothing to catch on. The sound belongs to a room the wind cannot quite enter.

The room is not large. A cathedral would be flattering it; a chapel cut for a rite no one attends is closer, and even that overstates the floor. Call it a bell. Some old thaw, or a softer seam worried at for an age and then abandoned by the climate that made it, hollowed a chamber just under the crest. Later a slab — the size of a barn roof, edges no longer sharp — settled across the hollow and stayed. It has been there long enough to stop being an accident. Its underside is smoked with mineral, its contact-lines with the walls healed over by a slow welding of grit and lime, and the whole lid has taken on the settled look of something that will not shift again in any life that could listen to it. What remains of the entrance is a slit, not toward the sky exactly, but toward another face of the same summit, a second wall standing off by little more than the length of an arm. Daylight arrives already spent, reflected, narrowed, insulted. At the threshold it is a gray blade. Three paces in, it is a rumor. In the body of the bell it does not count as light at all.

The air, against every instruction the altitude tries to give, is warm. Not hot — nothing vents, nothing smells of sulfur in the theatrical way, no breath of the mountain’s anger. Warm as a shut room that has been occupied without interruption, warm as stone that has forgotten how to be cold. The heat is older than the occupation. One wall, the inner wall, away from the slit, holds it most faithfully: a low, patient warmth in the palm, the leftover of whatever intrusive body built this height and then went quiet. The living add to it. They do not create it. If every animal left, the stone would still be faintly, stubbornly warm for a long time, and the cold thread slipping through the slit would still die a few paces in, unable to organize itself into weather.

Moisture here is never a stream and never gone. It beads on the warm ceiling where the thin outside air brushes it, gathers along old seams, and lets go. The drips are few and they fall where they have always fallen, each into a cup the size of two hands. The cups no longer deepen. The stone has reached an agreement with the water: a skin of pale mineral forms, flakes, reforms, and the hollow stays the hollow. After a summit storm, a brief veil is driven through the slit and darkens the threshold grit for an hour; then that too is pulled into the same slow ledger — film, bead, cup, breath. Animal exhalation does as much work as rain. In the darker pockets the damp is a slick rather than a drip, and your fingers come away tasting of stone and a faint metal, nothing vegetal.

Growth has little purchase, and knows it. Along the seep-lines a black moss holds on, more crust than plant, advancing by seasons you would not notice standing still. Near the slit, where the secondhand light is least dishonest, a single shrub has jammed itself into the crack and lived a stunted, argumentative life — leaves few, one side warmed by the room’s breath, the other burned by the outside wind, neither side winning. Deeper in, what passes for vegetation is a threading of pale fungus through the old organic crust, and even that is sparse, a rumor of appetite rather than a cover. There is no soil to speak of. Nutrients arrive — constantly, obscenely — and go unused by anything with roots. The dark spends them elsewhere.

The elsewhere is everything. The bell is full. Not decorated with life, not visited by it: occupied to the point of contact. The upper air is a shifting layer of small birds, dark, short-winged, too many to count by any method but volume, flying in a mill that does not panic and does not land all at once. They click as they fly, short hard notes that the curved stone returns as a wash. Beneath them, and sometimes through them, a second layer of smaller wings — moths, midges, a long-bodied fly that seems to prefer the warm wall — turns the space between bodies into more bodies. You cannot extend an arm and bring it back untouched. The walls are shelved by old erosion into ledges rounded like the lips of jars, and the ledges are paved with roosting weight: birds shoulder to shoulder, heads tucked or not, a few larger ones among the small, all of them using the stone as the stone was left. Nothing has been built under them. No cup of mud, no stick, no daub. Eggs, when there are eggs, sit in the water-cut hollows as if the mountain had prepared a nursery and then forgotten why. Claws have polished the rims. That is the only amendment.

Lower, the floor seethes at a different speed. What looks at first like a stained pavement — granular, faintly ringing if struck, the color of old tea — is a crust of generations, worked and reworked by things that do not fly: beetles armored in grit, pale cricket-shapes in the damp seams, larvae that sleeve themselves in a single layer of sand and walk as tiny stones. That sleeving is as close as the place comes to craft. A bird, sometimes, will pick up a loose pebble and strike a hard nodule the wind brought in, or the shell of a rival’s egg, two or three blows, then drop the pebble back into anonymity. Nothing is kept. Nothing accumulates into an object you could point to and call made. The tools are whatever was already loose, and they return to loose. There are no walls but the mountain’s, no path worn smoother than the general polish of use, no mark that intends to outlast the hour.

The smell is the smell of a system that has already burned through its excess. Ammonia is present and already spoken for — taken up, oxidized, folded into the crust — so it reads as musk and warm yeast and wet mineral rather than as a sting. The balance is audible in that, if you have the patience: this is not a colony in the first rush of finding a cave. The numbers are outrageous and stable. Birth is matched, more or less, by the appetite of the floor. The crust does not thicken toward a future collapse. It turns over. Established, the way the cups are established, the way the slab is established. Loud, crowded, and in no hurry.

Sound is the medium the room thinks in. The slit contributes one note, a throat-whistle broken by the opposing wall so that it never becomes a clean wind. Drips contribute their small clocks, each cup with a different interval, none of them dramatic. Wings contribute the broadband hush that is also, up close, a battering. Clicks return from the dome focused, so that there is a place near the center, head-height, where the noise piles up and the flying layer thins — not a forbidden zone, only a knot in the air that bodies skirt without ceremony, the way a current is skirted. Under it all the insect sift, and the occasional scrape of a claw resetting on polished stone, and the low organic rustle of the floor, which you feel in the teeth more than you hear. It does not spike. It does not relent. Conversation, if any creature here dealt in conversation, would be impossible; announcement is constant and therefore cheap. The loudness is not an event. It is the room’s condition, as darkness is, as warmth is.

Change, when it comes, comes as a bias, not a blow. Over weeks the cold thread at the slit strengthens or weakens and the line of condensation migrates a hand’s breadth across the ceiling. The mill of birds loosens by a fraction when some portion of them spends longer outside, working the barren slopes below the crest for windblown insects and whatever else the high country grudgingly offers; their return is not a swarm-arrival but a gradual fattening of the sound. Molt drifts into the crust and is processed. A cup clouds with mineral and clarifies again. The shrub at the slit puts on a leaf, or declines to. None of this is visible in an hour. The gradients are weak, and the place seems to prefer them that way. Even the animals’ restlessness is local — a shift along a ledge, a brief quarrel the echo enlarges and then forgets — inside a census that holds.

What the bell does to a body is mostly subtraction. The eyes give up early and are not rewarded for lingering; shapes exist as interruptions in the dark, and after a while as memories of where interruptions tend to be. The lungs notice the height all at once, in the work of a single breath, and then notice the warmth contradicting it, so that the body stands divided against itself: high, and not cold; enclosed, and not quiet; dry-mouthed from the thin air, and standing in a room where every surface is faintly wet. Crossing the floor means contact. There is no aisle. The crust gives, slightly, with a living compliance, and small things go out from under the step and return behind it, unhurried, as if footsteps were only another slow weather.

No one has claimed it. There is no arrangement that implies a watcher, no offering, no soot of a fire, no chipping of the wall into a sign. The primitive gestures — the pebble struck and dropped, the sand sleeve, the egg set in a cup the water finished centuries ago — do not rise to the level of a site. They are manners. The mountain did the only building that was ever going to happen here, and then it stopped, having reached a form it could keep: lid, slit, warm wall, ringing floor, cups that no longer deepen. Life arrived because the form was kind, in a narrow and unsentimental sense, and having arrived it became the rest of the form. Dense, loud, dim, damp, almost plantless, small enough to be one thought, high enough that the outside world enters only as a broken thread of cold.

Stand long enough and the knot of sound at the center seems less like an accident of shape and more like the room’s attention. It does not regard you. It regards the ongoing, the mill and the drip and the slow skin of the cups. When you leave — you will leave; the place does not gather guests, only residents — the ridge takes you back into brightness and scour, and the pressure in the stone fades underfoot over a dozen paces, as a bell fades when a hand is finally laid against it. Behind you the note continues, unchanged by your having heard it. The slab does not shift. The cups accept their next drops. In the dark, something small lifts a pebble, strikes once, and sets it down again among all the other stones that were never tools.
