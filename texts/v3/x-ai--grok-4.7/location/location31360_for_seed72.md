The heat is already in the room before any other fact sorts itself out. It is not a draft from somewhere else, and it does not move. It occupies the cut the way water occupies a jar: full to the walls, the same in the open middle as against the stone, slightly worse where iron has been working. Breath goes in warm and comes out without having cooled anything. Metal that has stood an hour is unpleasant to hold. The concrete pads give heat back through boot soles. Sweat starts and does not do its job; the air is already too full of water to take more.

The place is a single gallery, newly opened, with two side bays and a low sump. You could walk its length without hurrying and be done with it. The crown rises enough, near the middle, that a lamp on a stand leaves the highest brick in brown dusk; at the sides the ceiling comes down to a height a tall person notices. One end is a working face: claystone and a band of sandier rock, drill holes still round, the last course of spoil not yet gone, chalk marks and a snapped string line. The other end is a bulkhead of municipal brick, pipes leaving through collars that have been packed but not yet neatly finished, a steel door with a number stenciled at eye height. Above, off-center, a shaft rises to a grate. Daylight comes down it as a weak coin, already tired when it arrives, and does not travel. The rest of the light is three work lamps, one of them filmed with mud, and a caged bulb over the switchbox that hums and throws a yellow pool no wider than a table.

Nothing here has been weathered into a landscape yet. The walls are still the inside of a cut. Tool marks run in parallel and stop where the shift stopped. Corners are sharp. Fissures are construction cracks and bedding the excavators happened to meet, not channels anything has had time to carve. Silt is only beginning: a skin on the sump floor, a paler line where yesterday’s water stood, a grit that has not agreed to be a bank. The room is pre-erosional. It is being born, and birth, at this scale, is mostly waiting.

Water does not flow so much as arrive. The claystone weeps. A film thickens on the face, loses its hold, and goes down in slow threads that darken the brick courses already laid along the spring line. Seepage collects in the low joints and steps, without hurry, toward the sump — a rectangular pit, board-lined on one side, concrete on the other, half made. The water in it is tea-colored and warm. It smells of mineral and of the town: a little soap, a little iron, the faint sweetness of waste that has been diluted past naming. Drips fall from a sleeve joint and from the lip of the shaft. Each one is a small sound; the vault adds them up until they are a continuous ticking, loud enough to follow you from the bulkhead to the face. After rain above, the ticking thickens. The level in the sump climbs by a finger, by a hand, and then holds, because the pump will take it, eventually, on a cycle longer than attention.

The machines stand far apart, and the emptiness between them is the main material of the floor. A centrifugal pump on a pad, inlet not yet elegant, discharge rising to the bulkhead. A second pump, smaller, still strapped to its skid, as if the room had not decided to keep it. A generator under a tin hood, grease darkening the concrete around it in a neat ring where someone has been careful and then, beyond the ring, a smear where someone was not. Chain hoist on a rail that runs only partway down the gallery and stops in midair, the rail’s end sawn, waiting for an extension that is not this week’s work. Gauges with glass already hazed. A hose coiled and not coiled well. Conduit along one wall, strapped to the brick at regulation intervals, with gaps where the brick itself is still a stack and not a wall. This is urban work: specified, numbered, meant to meet other work at known inverts under known streets. It is not a settlement of people. The cot in the near bay, the kettle, the tin of sugar, the chalkboard with a date and a level written and not erased — these are the signs of a crew of few, who come down, do a slow thing, and leave the room to its other population. Between the pads the floor is bare and new, and you can see all the way across.

What fills the place is animal, and it does not require any strangeness to do it. The food is not grown here. It falls, leaks, and condenses: grease, a crust of ration dropped and not all swept, the warm film on mineral, whatever the pipes carry a trace of, the bodies of the smaller things. On that imported calorie the room is oversubscribed.

Drain flies hold a trembling skin above the sump, so dense the water looks dusty. Midges work the lamp beams in visible ropes. Mosquitoes lift off the warm shallows when a boot nears and resettle before the boot has moved on. At the wall base the cockroaches are past counting; the clay color of them makes the spring line look as if it is breathing, and when the generator is off you can hear the dry stir of them, a third sound under drip and wing. Crickets occupy the unlit crown and the gaps behind the pipe collars — ordinary cave and cellar crickets, long-legged, innumerable, their ticking almost mechanical until a lamp is lifted and they scatter up the brick in a wave. Silverfish on the paper tags. Beetles in the grease ring. Rats in every pipe mouth that does not yet have a grille, and along the bulkhead where the packing has left a warm run; not a flood of them, but enough that the eye keeps finding one, and then another, and understands the margins are occupied in full. A few bats use the darker bay by day, street bats that found the grate and stayed for the insects; their clicks sit inside the louder noise the way a thread sits inside cloth. Higher in the shaft, on the iron rungs, the white stain of pigeons that will not come all the way down. Flies find the sugar tin if the lid is imperfect. Something small and many-legged crosses the new concrete in the open — the center is not empty of them, only less claimed — and is gone into a joint.

They are the residents. The crew are visitors on a slow schedule. Stand still and the room’s true density declares itself at the edges: wall, waterline, pipe shadow, lamp heat. The middle looks sparse only if you are counting machines.

Almost nothing grows. A green film oils the sump lip and the wet course of brick, algal and bacterial, slick, odorless until a finger breaks it. Mold feathers the boarding that has stayed damp. In a crack where the shaft lets down a little soil and a little of that tired light, one fern has committed itself, small, yellow-green at the edges, absurd against claystone. It is the only plant you will find if you look carefully, and looking carefully does not make a second one appear. The ecology is not a green one. It runs on heat, leakage, and the town overhead, which sheds calories and lives downward whether it means to or not.

The sound is loud in the way a works is loud, not in the way a collapse is. When the generator is up and the pump takes its draft, the gallery becomes a bell: a low roar, regular, with a knock in it where the impeller meets something the screens have not yet been fitted to stop. You can shout and be understood at the length of the room; you cannot talk at ease. The chain hoist, if anyone runs it, adds a bright clatter that the brick keeps. Between cycles — and the cycles are long, timed to levels and to fuel, not to restlessness — the loudness does not leave. It thins into insect-shriek and multiplied drip and the generator’s absence, which the ear keeps hearing for a while as pressure. Wings against lamp glass. A rat in a downspout. The steel door ticking as it heats and cools by a degree no one would bother to measure. Echo makes a small room sound larger than its walkable length, then gives the lie as soon as you move: the far wall arrives too soon.

Change, when it comes, comes as accumulation. The working face advances by a course, by a drilled round, then waits on brick, on a part, on the weather above that makes the shaft a chute for hot rain. Silt thickens in the sump by a faint layer and is pumped out as slurry, and begins again. Algae extends a hand’s width along a joint in a month. Tool marks lose their palest dust and keep their shape; nothing here is old enough to have been softened. The rats learn the new pipe the week it is set. The crew’s chalkboard gains a number. Heat does not break. Night and day are a rumor from the grate — a slight change in the coin of light, the pitch of street sound very far away, never a cooling. Seasons would register, if at all, as more water after rain and a slightly different smell in the seep, not as relief.

The rules are ordinary, and the room obeys them without instruction. Water goes to the lowest finished invert and, lacking one, to the hole that is becoming an invert. Heat stays, because the way out is a shaft too narrow and too still to carry it, and because the stone is warm and the machines add their share. Animals follow warmth, film, and waste, and they arrive before the finish work; grilles and lids are how the town will argue with them later, and the argument will be partial. Brick goes in courses. Bolts go in the pattern the drawing names. What is not yet built does not yet hold: the unsleeved joint weeps, the ungrilled mouth houses a rat, the unpoured corner of the sump slumps a little and has to be laid again. No other law is operating. The strangeness, if a person from the streets above feels any, is only the coincidence of a new cut, a high warm water, a closed vault, and a town that leaks life downward — a nasty job site, recognizable, unfinished.

Toward the end of a long off-cycle the sump surface is almost still. Midges reconstruct their skin. A thread of seep reaches the edge and does not ripple the middle. Roaches work the lip. The fern does nothing visible. Lamps burn in their small pools, and between the pools the fresh stone is dim, raw, still marked by the drill, not yet a wall that water has persuaded into any shape of its own. Somewhere in the bulkhead a gauge sits at a number that will, by its own patience, become the number that wakes the pump. When it does, the room will shout, the warm water will drop by a hand’s width, the flies will lift and settle, and the gallery will look, afterward, almost as it did before: sparse in its machines, unfinished in its geology, loud in its lesser register, inhabited overwhelmingly at every margin, and hot, wholly hot, below the street, with the next finger of water already on its way.
