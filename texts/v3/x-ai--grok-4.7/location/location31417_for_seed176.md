The place is a notch, not a country. You can cross it in a few minutes if the growth lets you, and you cannot miss the edges: a fresh lip on the open side, and on the other three a wall of stone that still looks cut. Nothing here has had time to round. The joints are clean, the gravel underfoot still angular, the faces pale where the last shift tore them. Moss has begun, but it lies on the rock like a cloth just thrown down, not like a skin grown over centuries.

It should be cold. It is not. The stone holds a low heat, the sort that never quite becomes steam and never quite leaves your palms dry. Stand with your hand on a face in the shade and the warmth comes through before the damp does. The air above the shelf is heavy with it. Water does not arrive as weather so much as it condenses out of that thickness—beading on every underside, threading the roots, standing in pans no deeper than a wrist between stones that have not yet learned to hold a pool. After rain, which is often, the whole notch weeps. Veins open in the cliff and run for an hour, then seal to a shine. The lip drips in a broken curtain into the gulf below, and the gulf takes the water without giving anything back. From the shelf you do not see a valley. You see air, and then, very far down, a color that might be forest or might be weather.

The growth has taken the warmth and the wet as permission. In a space no larger than a hamlet’s green, the vegetation is packed to the point of contact: broad leaves overlapping broad leaves, ferns shouldered into the joints, vines using whatever will hold them. You do not walk through it so much as part it. Light is ordinary daylight—sun, when the mist thins, falling in the usual way—but it reaches the ground only in coins and blades, because there is always something between. The leaves are larger than the height allows. Their veins sometimes stain a dull copper where the seep touches them, and the copper does not wash off. That is the only wrongness that announces itself. Everything else is only unlikely: a greenhouse jammed into a wound on a peak that rose faster than weather could plane it down.

Sound does not match the size. The lip-fall is constant, not loud as falls go, but the bowl takes it and will not let it go. Closer, and louder, is the works. A single engine house sits against the warmest face, concrete stained the color of the seep, a pipe run following one joint up into the cliff and another stepping down toward the lip before it vanishes into a gallery cut just under the edge. Inside, a turbine turns on the fault-water and does not vary. The note is industrial and patient: a geared, electrical hum under the water-sound, fans, a relief valve that coughs on a long interval and then forgets itself. No shift whistle. No voices unless someone has climbed. The place was built for the water, which should not be here, and for the heat in the fracture, which should not be here either. Both are. The machines run as if the notch were a room in a larger plant. It is not. Beyond the lip there is nothing to connect to except cable: a single anchored span, swaying, going down out of sight to wherever the power is wanted.

Other building is scattered and already losing. A timber stair climbs partway up the back wall and ends at a joint that opened after the stair was cut; the last three treads hang over nothing you would trust. Iron bolts with survey numbers oxidize in the moss. A shed of boards, older than the engine house, has been entered by roots through the floor and by a vine through the ridgeline; inside, a bench, a coil of hose, a logbook whose pages have gone to pulp except where a pencil dent still shows a date and a figure for flow. Someone meant to live adjacent to the work. The adjacent did not last. What remains is the engine house, the pipes, the cable anchor, the stair that fails, and the shed that is becoming a trellis.

Animals are almost absent. The leaves move when water moves them. Insects exist—small, many, uninterested—and sometimes a bird crosses the lip and does not land, the turbine having already filled the pitch it would have used. No tracks in the mud that mean anything larger. The food is here. The quiet is not. The land itself is too new for a web to have settled: sharp stone, mineral seep, a noise that does not cycle. Whatever will live here is still deciding, and deciding slowly.

Change, when it comes, is the kind you would miss if you came only twice. The seep migrates a hand’s width in a season, abandoning one stain and opening another, so that the copper in the leaves is a map of where water was rather than where it is. A fern that was waist-high is head-high, and the pipe it used as a stake has disappeared into the crown. The fault-water stays the same temperature; the operators, when they come, record it and record it again. The ridge is still rising. You cannot see that. You can see only that the lip’s edge is still sharp, that no scree apron has gathered below the freshest face, that the gravel has not yet gone to sand. Stand long enough and the loudness becomes the climate: fall, turbine, valve, the soft tick of drops on broad leaves, each drop the same drop. The notch does not perform. It persists, small and full, warm where it should be cold, wet to the touch, green to the point of pressure, with a machine in it that has agreed to run at the speed of the stone.
