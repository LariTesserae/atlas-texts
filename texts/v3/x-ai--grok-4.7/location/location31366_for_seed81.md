The road arrives already tired of itself. It has been climbing for so long that the climb has stopped being a fact and become a condition: the stone underfoot is the same pale, fine-grained stuff it was an hour ago, only wetter, and the air has not thinned the way high air should. It has thickened. Heat sits in it the way heat sits in a closed room above a stove, patient, uninterested in wind. There is wind, sometimes. It does not cool anything. It only moves the damp from one surface to another.

What the road climbs toward is not a peak. The high ground here is a broad shelf, a shoulder of country maybe a day’s walk across, rimmed on three sides by drops that the dimness will not let you measure. The fourth side is where the road comes up, and where, if you turned, the lower country would already be gone into vapor. The shelf itself does not feel like a summit. It feels like a floor that happens to be far above other floors. Long settled. The edges have worn back into curves. Gullies that once cut sharp have rounded into shallow troughs, and the troughs hold water that does not run so much as occupy them, dark and still, skimmed with a film that is not ice and not oil and not quite scum. Nothing grows in the film. Nothing grows anywhere the eye can use.

That absence takes longer to notice than it should. The wet suggests otherwise. Water is the ordinary state of surfaces: rock faces glazed, the road’s center a continuous sheen, droplets forming on any metal left in the open and falling, after a while, in a slow private rain that has no cloud of its own. You can stand in a place where the air is visibly condensing on a pipe and not feel rained on. The wet arrives by contact. Stone weeps from hairline seams that have been weeping long enough to stain themselves permanent colors — rust where there is iron, a pale chalky bloom where there is lime, and in a few cuts a yellow that smells, faintly, of something the nose files under medicine and then refuses to name. Pools collect in every low, and the lows are everywhere because the shelf has been worked, leveled, cut, and then left to weather back toward its own geometry. The water is warm. In the shallows it is the temperature of a bath left too long. Deeper, where a trench drops past the arm, it is hotter, and a thin skin of steam walks off it without ever becoming fog thick enough to hide the far bank.

There is no moss on the lips of those trenches. No weed in the cracks of the paving. No fringe of anything at the waterline, not even the black velvet of the simplest growths that usually claim wet stone. The mineral world has not been colonized. It has been finished, and then kept. Whatever chemistry lives in the heat and the seep — sulfur in some vents, a sweetness like hot metal in others, a flat mineral taste in the pools you would not drink — it has either never permitted a leaf or has long since finished with the experiment. The palette is stone, slag, concrete, oxidized plate, and the several colors of standing water. Under the dimness those colors go gray at a distance and return only when you are close enough to smell them.

The light never commits. It is not night. Shadows exist, soft-edged, pooled under eaves and inside the mouths of culverts, but they are shadows of a brightness that seems to come from the air itself rather than from any quarter of the sky. The sky, when the vapor thins enough to admit it, is a low lid, ochre-gray, sometimes bruised darker as if weather were crossing far above the shelf and declining to descend. Noon, if the word still applies, is a slight lifting of the lid. What passes for evening is the same light with the heat pressed closer to the ground. Nothing casts a hard line. Distances lie. A structure you have been walking toward for twenty minutes is not nearer in the way nearer usually works; it clarifies, detail by detail, as if the air were a lens being slowly focused.

The structures are the city, and the city is the shelf’s other fact. It is not a ruin and not a camp. Someone planned it at the scale of a settlement that expected to be lived in by thousands: a grid not of streets exactly but of service cuts, alignments, and pads, following the old drainage where drainage could be followed and forcing it where it could not. Foundations repeat. Platforms repeat. The same proportion of wall to opening appears often enough that you recognize a type — long sheds, square houses of heavy block, a narrower building with a clerestory that was meant to pull air and now only pulls condensation down its inner walls. Metal roofs, where they remain, are dark with perpetual dew and loud, in their way, with the small sound of drops leaving them. Many roofs are gone. The buildings stand open to the lid of the sky, and inside them the floors hold water in the same patient way the gullies do, reflecting a rectangle of dim light that is the brightest thing in the district.

And yet the city is empty in the only sense that matters here: volume. Between one block and the next the distance is wrong for a town. Streets run true for a long time with nothing on them. A warehouse occupies its pad and then there is a hundred paces of wet paving, a channel, another hundred paces, a stair that goes up to a platform on which a single engine-house sits alone, its brick stained to the course, its stack intact, its yard unbounded by fence because a fence would have been a claim on space the builders seem to have considered already claimed by the plan. You can see three such engine-houses from the platform, each far enough that a person standing in one doorway would be a rumor to the others. The infrastructure is dense in design and hollow in fact. Pipes leave the houses and run, elevated, on iron trestles that stride across empty lots as if the lots were rivers. Some pipes are large enough to walk through, and their interiors tick with cooling and heat in alternation, a joint speaking, a hanger shifting, then quiet. The trestles themselves have settled. Footings have sunk a finger’s width into the softened ground and stopped. Rust has bloomed and then stabilized into a crust that sheds only when you put a hand on it. Nothing here is new. Nothing is collapsing at a rate you could watch. The city and the weather have agreed on a state, and they are keeping it.

The industry is still in that agreement. Not all of it. Most of the sheds are inert, their floors traced by channels that once carried slurry or wash-water and now carry only the seep. But the engine-houses are not memorials. In the nearest, a mechanism turns. You hear it before you see it: not a roar, a cadence, slow enough that you can count the beats if you care to, each beat a wet compression, a release of vapor from a seam, a brief brightening of the heat against your face. Inside, the light is worse. Boilers or their descendants — riveted drums, patched, lagged in a mineral wool that has gone black and brittle — sit in a row, and only one of them is in service. Gauges with glass gone milky still have needles. One needle moves. Valves with wheels the size of shields stand at half turn, packed with something that has swollen in the damp and sealed them more honestly than their gaskets ever did. A drive shaft disappears through the rear wall into a trench of hot water and does not come back into view. Whatever it drives is elsewhere on the shelf, out of sight, still coupled, still asking for the cadence. The stack above breathes a little. The breath is clear, wavering only where it meets the cooler air, and it does not smell of smoke so much as of hot wet stone. Fuel, if fuel is the word, is not visible. The heat may be borrowed from under the shelf. In places the paving is warmer than the day can account for, and a grate set into an alley exhales without a machine anywhere near it, a steady animal warmth, the ground itself doing the oldest work in the district.

Elsewhere the work is waterwork. Locks and penstocks, built for a flow that is now a seep, still function in miniature. A gate left partway down meters a trickle from a higher basin into a lower flume; the trickle has polished a path in the concrete so smooth it looks poured yesterday, though the walls to either side are etched and pitted to the aggregate. Flumes cross flumes. Some are dry and cracked and have been dry long enough for dust — the only dust, a pale mineral flour — to collect in the cracks and then be pasted there by condensation into a mortar of their own. Others run. At a junction near the western drop, three of them meet in a chamber open to the sky, and the water arrives at three temperatures. You can tell without touching. The hottest throws a shiver of steam that dies at the surface. The middle one is clear and fast for a few feet where the fall gives it speed. The coolest, still warm, carries a faint rope of bubbles that cling to the wall and then let go one by one. They mix in the chamber and leave by a single throat toward the rim, and over the rim they go without drama, a sheet no thicker than a wrist, falling into the vapor that hides the lower country. The sound of that fall does not rise. The shelf keeps its own noises and declines the rest.

Sound is mostly the inventory of small agreements. Drops leaving a roof. The cadence in the engine-house, which you stop hearing once you have walked a hundred paces and then, unaccountably, hear again when the wind — that uncooling wind — turns and brings it. A tick in the trestle pipes as the day’s slight lift of light changes how fast they shed heat. The grit of your own steps, louder than anything civic. Now and then a deeper note, not quite a knock, from under the paving, as if a large volume of water had shifted in a main and found a new level. It happens, waits, happens again a long time later. No voices. No wheels. No bells. The city was given signals — you find the posts, the empty brackets, a horn the size of a barrel gone green on a roof — and the signals are not in use. Quiet here is not the quiet of abandonment so much as the quiet of a system that runs below the threshold of announcement. It does not need to call anyone. It is not clear it was built for anyone still expected to answer.

The living things, when they appear, appear as exceptions the shelf has not bothered to correct. High on a trestle, in the crook where a pipe meets a hanger, a knot of pale filaments clings. Not a plant. It has no leaf, no green, no direction of growth you can defend as upward. It looks like something precipitated, a lace the water drew out of itself and forgot to redissolve. Touch it and it collapses into slime and a smell of hot coins. Lower, in a culvert where the light is worst, something moves at the edge of a pool and is not a reflection: a low shape, many-legged or legless, it is hard to say, the color of wet concrete, crossing from shade to shade with a deliberateness that reads as animal only because it stops when you stop. It does not flee. It subtracts itself into a crack. You will not see another for a long time. On the rim, once, a scatter of small husks — hollow, light, like seed cases if the world had seeds — bleached and stuck to the rock by the same mineral film that rims the pools. They might have been blown up from below. They might have been shed by whatever made the lace on the trestle, in some season this place still keeps and does not advertise. Fauna, if that is the category, is not absent. It is rare enough that each instance feels like a clerical error, and the shelf’s main business continues around it without reference.

What the error reveals, if you stay, is that the rules are consistent and slightly to one side of the rules you carried up the road. Water seeks the low, but the low is warm, so evaporation and return are a closed loop conducted across the whole shelf: weep, pool, flume, fall, rise as vapor, condense on the next cool plate, weep again. Nothing drinks it in the way a root would. The heat does not dissipate upward; the lid holds it, and the ground donates more. Stone erodes, but slowly, and along paths the builders already cut, so the city is not being unmade so much as being edited toward smoother versions of its own intents. Machines persist where they are coupled to that heat or that water, and go still where the coupling broke, and the still ones are not scavenged. There is no one in the volume to scavenge them, or the one who would is not present at a density that leaves marks. Marks are the other absence. No graffiti, no trails worn through buildings, no midden, no bones in the doorways. The paving between districts is wet enough to take a print and empty enough that your own prints are the first you see, and by the time you look back the film has closed over them.

The strange part is not a monster at the end of a street. It is the competence. The shelf knows how to be a city. Alignments hold. Water goes where the flumes ask. An engine turns because a condition under the paving is still true, and has been true long enough that the brick around it has baked to a different color in a neat rectangle, a local climate the size of a room. You can walk the long empty axis from the eastern pads to the rim and feel, at every correct interval, the place where a crossing should be, and the crossing is there, and it serves no one, and it is not in disrepair. Purpose has not collapsed into mystery. Purpose has remained, and tenancy has not. The weirdness is administrative. A town-sized machine, in its own balance, performing a reduced version of its work, in dim heat, in wet silence, with the spaces between its organs left hollow as design.

Near the western drop, where the three temperatures meet, there is a platform built out over nothing. The rail is gone; the bolts remain, each with a collar of pale crust. Standing there you are high enough to be out of the shelf’s own breath and still not in any cleaner air. The vapor below is a floor. Sometimes it convects, a slow boil seen from above, and a warmer smell comes up — metal, and the medicine-smell, and something like wet ash. Sometimes it sinks, and for a few minutes the beginning of the fall is visible, rock walls dark with water, ledges, a lower flume still aligned with the one under your feet, as if the builders had continued the city downward and the shelf had simply declined to follow. Then the vapor rises again and the continuation becomes a rumor.

If you wait through one of the slight lifts of light, the engine-house cadence comes across the pads cleaner than before, and you can count, and the count is the same as it was. The shelf is active the way a tide is active, or a kiln left at temperature: processes in motion, none of them hurried, none of them finished, none of them requiring an audience. A drop detaches from the bolt at your foot. It hits the platform. The film accepts it. Somewhere under the paving a main finds its new level and speaks once, and the city, which is also the weather, which is also the heat the ground will not stop giving, goes on being the size it is — open, settled, urban, and almost entirely unoccupied — under a lid that will not brighten and will not leave.
