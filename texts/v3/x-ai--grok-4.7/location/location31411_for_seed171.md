The first thing the height does is take the heat out of the air, and the air answers by giving up its water. Not as weather arriving from somewhere else—there is nowhere else close enough to send it—but as a standing condition of the plateau itself. The continent sits high enough that the day never quite warms, and vast enough that its own breath makes the climate. Cool vapor thickens against everything that has a surface. It beads on new stone. It films the undersides of leaves that have not yet decided whether they are leaves or roof. It hangs in the narrow places between stems until those places stop being air and become a slower kind of water. You do not walk into rain so much as into a landscape that is always just after rain, and always just before the next sweating of the air.

Underfoot the ground is wrong in a quiet way. It is not worn. Nothing here has had time to be worn. The mineral surface is embryonic: skins of precipitate, pale crusts still damp from the chemistry that laid them down, sheets of stone so new they ring when a root shifts against them. Channels exist only as intentions. A seep finds a lower place, wets it, and the wet place darkens and softens, but the cut has not yet become a stream, and the stream has not yet become a valley. Erosion is a rumor the water keeps starting and the living mass keeps interrupting. The plateau is being assembled faster than it can be taken apart. What would, on an older world, be bedrock is here a floor still curing—cool, slick, faintly translucent at the thin edges, smelling of metal and wet cellulose.

Everything else is plant, and the plant does not leave gaps. There is no understory because there is no overstory distinct from it; the mass is the story. Mats of tissue quilt the new crust and pin it in place before wind or water can lift it. From those mats rise columns, not trunks exactly—pressure-held, jointed, cool to the hand, beaded along every seam. Between them the space is occupied by blades, by aerial roots drinking the condensate directly, by horizontal shelves of growth that function as floors without ever having been built. You cannot see far. Density does that, and so does the dimness: light arrives already tired, scattered by vapor and by the million wet surfaces, so that noon is a gray-green interior and the idea of a horizon survives only as a change in the sound.

The sound is the true scale of the place. Up close it is hydraulic—sap under pressures no ordinary stem should hold, clicking through valves the size of your wrist, sighing through tissues that flex and reset. A little farther off it becomes weather: sheets of condensate letting go all at once from a shelf of leaves, striking the shelf below, and the shelf below that, a cascade with no waterfall, only impacts. Farther still, the plateau generates its own low operations. Cells of air rise where the mass respires and sink where it has shaded itself into cold. The collisions of those cells do not sound like wind in trees. They sound like weather arguing with a living instrument the size of a country. Growth adds its own note, continuous and structural: fibers taking load, new crust cracking as roots seat into it, joints seating with cracks loud enough to feel in the teeth. There is no animal voice in any of it. Nothing cries, calls, or flees. The deafening is the work of water, tissue, and a geology that has only just agreed to be solid.

Stand in one place long enough and the processes separate themselves from the roar. A shelf overhead loads with film until its surface tension fails; the drop is brief, cold, and total, and then the shelf begins loading again. At your feet a pale crust clouds and firms where a root has breathed minerals out of the seep—land appearing in the time it takes you to watch. Nearby, a column thickens by a visible margin, its skin splitting in a wet line that seals itself as it opens. None of this is construction in the sense of anything made and left. There are no walls, no tools, no ruins, no evidence that a hand or a plan ever passed through. The engineering is the growth. Tissues here do things ordinary plants do not: they hold metastable films of water against gravity, nucleate stone out of seep on a schedule, brace themselves into architectures that carry weather-loads across distances too wide for wood as wood is understood. The physics is local and biological, and it does not announce itself. It simply fails to match the physics you brought with you. A droplet hangs too long. A stem bears too much. A patch of new crust throws back a sound with the timing of a mechanism.

The rules, once you have stood inside them, are few and strict. Water stays near the surface because the surface is cold, new, and infinitely folded; it does not run away so much as it is caught, used, and sweated back into the air that made it. Light remains dim because the mass and the vapor have first claim on it; what reaches the crust is enough for the chemistry and not enough for a vista. Nothing grazes, nothing hunts, nothing tunnels except roots. The plateau is occupied entirely by its own making. Motion is everywhere and none of it is animal: the flex of loaded shelves, the slow seating of crust, the rise and collapse of air the continent breathes for itself. You can move through it only where the mass has, for its own reasons, left a yielding seam—never a path, only a place where two pressures have not yet met.

At the scale of a body, it is a cool green dark full of dripping and impact, slick new stone under a carpet that is also a machine. At the scale the sound implies, it is a single high continent still coming into being, roofed and floored and walled by one continuous life, loud with the weather it manufactures and the ground it has not yet finished laying down. The unusual thing is not a marvel standing apart from the rest. It is the steadiness of it: that all this force, all this water, all this unfinished mineral, remains packed, living, and without a builder—emergent, deafening, and already, in every direction you cannot see, continuing.
