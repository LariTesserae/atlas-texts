The basin is small enough that its far wall is always in the same light as the near one. You can see the whole of it from the lip: a pocket cut into a rising shoulder, no wider than a town market and its streets, and not much longer. The shoulder itself is still coming up. The stone has not learned roundness. Joints stand sharp, faces pale and granular, edges that would cut a hand if the moss had not already claimed them. Nothing here has been worn into gravel. The mountain is younger than the habit of mountains.

Water does not run so much as arrive. It beads on the new faces and finds the cracks the rock opened while it lifted. In the cool of the height it never quite dries. A film moves down every wall, clear enough that the grain shows through, loud enough that the basin never rests. Where a joint widens, the film becomes a thread, then a sheet, then a small fall that strikes a ledge and starts again. After rain — and rain is frequent, a cold fine rain that does not so much fall as thicken the air — the whole pocket rings. Even in dry hours the weeping continues. The water table sits just under the soles of the feet. Step on a mat of moss and it gives, and a darker seep pushes up around the print and does not hurry to close.

The growth is lavish and local. It does not blanket the basin. It packs the wet lines: ferns in every fissure, cushions of moss thick as a wrist, dwarf shrubs rooted in fractures no wider than a finger, their leaves small, waxy, crowded, drinking the film and the mist. In the deeper pockets, where three joints meet and hold a permanent pool the size of a room, the green rises higher — canes, hanging mats, a low canopy that never quite becomes forest because there is nowhere level enough for a forest to stand. The color is deep, almost black-green in the seams, brighter where the normal daylight strikes the wet leaves flat-on. The open stone between the seams stays bare. Pale rock, dark stitchery. From the lip it looks embroidered.

What was built follows the same stitchery. The town is not set upon the basin. It is the basin, worked. Someone — many someones, over a long patience — cut the joints wider where they already wanted to open, dressed the faces with hand tools, and turned the natural ledges into floors. You can still see the tool marks: shallow, overlapping, the bite of stone on stone, no metal sheen, only a harder grit than the rock itself. Walls are ashlar only where a mason decided a joint should become a corner. Elsewhere the mountain remains the wall, and the film of water runs down the inside of rooms exactly as it runs down the cliffs. Channels have been nudged, not invented. A fall that would have struck useless stone now strikes a basin cut to hold it, overflows by a lip cut one finger lower, and is led along a street that was a crack. The work is organized. Streets meet. Stairs follow the rise. Storage rooms stack where the rock stacked. There is a center, a broader joint opened into a hall whose roof is the overhang the mountain made and whose floor is flagged so that water crosses it in a thin, deliberate sheet and leaves by three mouths. It is a town’s worth of intention in a grove’s worth of ground.

Almost no one is in it.

The volumes are large and the presences are not. A corridor will run the length of the basin and hold one drip, one mat of ferns at a windowsill that is only an unfilled joint, and nothing else. Rooms open onto rooms. Their floors are wet and untracked. Sleeping platforms — stone, raised a hand’s breadth so the sheet of water can pass underneath — stand empty in rows, blankets long since taken or never brought. A market shed roofed with split canes contains no goods, only the sound of the fall at its back wall and the slow swing of a cane end the wind worries. You can walk the circuits and meet no one, and still feel you are inside a settlement rather than a ruin, because the water is being conducted. Someone keeps the lips clear. A new course has been started along the eastern joint, blocks set but not yet seated, a line of pale fresh cuts beside the older, moss-softened ones. The work proceeds. It proceeds the way the mountain proceeds.

Sound fills what the people do not. The falls are many and close, and the stone is unsoftened, so each strike comes back off the facing wall before the next strike leaves. In the hall the sheet of water on the flags adds a hiss under the louder drops. Wind comes over the lip in long pushes and leans on the cane roofs until they knock. Drips in the empty rooms tick at different rates, and in the narrower corridors they stack into something almost voiced, a low crowded mutter with no speaker. It is not chaotic. The basin is small; every sound has a short life and a hard return. Stand still and you can separate them: the eastern fall, deeper because it drops farther; the western seep, a fine rain on moss; the hall sheet; the cane. They do not blend into one noise so much as occupy the same air without yielding. At night — ordinary night, ordinary dark, stars if the mist lifts — it does not quiet. It only loses the wind’s part for a while.

Living things are scarce, and the ones that remain seem to have accepted the sound as weather. Midges work the pools and do not sing above it. A few dark birds nest in the high unworked joints on the lip, visible as brief cuts across the daylight, gone into cracks before you can count them. In the moss, if you part it, a pale many-legged crawler the length of a thumbnail inches along the wet, unhurried, armored in what looks like the same grit as the tools. You will see two, or five, in a day. Nothing herds. Nothing calls loud enough to matter. The lushness is plant, not animal. The emptiness is animal and human both.

The unusual part is not spectacular. It is a mismatch of ages. The rock behaves as if it has only just become rock: joints still opening by a hair across a season, a new seep starting where last year’s wall was dry, a block that was level in spring sitting a finger proud by autumn because the floor under it rose. The town is built to that. Courses of masonry include a gap on purpose, a waiting joint, so that when the mountain offers the next face there is already a place for it. Lintels are not fixed tight. They rest, and can be lifted and reset when the ground changes its mind. No one hurls themselves at the work. A course might gain three stones in a month. Children — there are a few, you hear them rarely, far off, their voices small against the falls — learn the water paths before they learn the streets, because the streets are the water paths, and both will be slightly elsewhere when the children are old.

Rules, then, as the place keeps them. Water stays near. It may be guided and must not be stopped; a blocked lip floods a room within a day and is cleared, not punished, the way you clear a drain. Stone may be dressed but not powdered; there is no quarry apart from the basin itself, and every block removed is a block the mountain has already loosened. Growth is left in the seams. Pulling ferns from a wall-joint is considered the same error as pointing a wall solid: it starves the film and starts a dry crack, and dry cracks here split, because the rock is still deciding its shape. Building is slow and is not optional. To live in the pocket is to keep answering the rise. People come and go — the town can hold many and currently holds almost none — but the channels are maintained by whoever is present, a duty that does not require a crowd. Sound is not a signal. No bell could outspeak the falls, so messages move by walking, or by the arrangement of a cane, or by the simple fact of a newly seated stone, which anyone who knows the circuits will notice within a day.

Stand in the hall at midday. Normal light, high and clean, mist burning off the lip and returning by evening. Your feet are cold. The sheet of water crosses the flags and divides around them and joins again. Ferns spill from a joint at shoulder height and drip onto the sheet, each drop vanishing into the hiss. Across the hall an unfinished course waits, three stones set, a fourth lying beside its bed, tool marks fresh, no mason in sight. Beyond the far mouth of the hall the eastern fall speaks against the facing cliff, and the cliff answers, and the answer comes back through the hall and leaves by the western mouth toward the cane roofs, which knock once and are quiet and knock again. Nothing in the pocket is in a hurry. The mountain is still arriving. The town is still being taught its next room. Between the two, a great deal of loud, wet, green, empty space, small enough to hold in one seeing, and not yet finished with itself.
