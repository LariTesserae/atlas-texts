The city sits where the mountain has not yet agreed to be a mountain.

From a distance that keeps rearranging itself—sometimes a morning’s walk, sometimes a view you only get by standing still long enough for the crest to rise into your eyes—it reads as a broken comb of stacks and gantries along a ridge that is still being pushed up out of a darker country below. The ridge is open. You can see past one works to the next, and past that to a shoulder of raw stone, and past that to weather that has not decided whether it belongs to this elevation. Nothing crowds. The settlement is large enough to have districts, drainage law, shift bells, and a name the bells almost say, but the ground between its parts is mostly absence: long pale terraces, cables sagging across gulfs of air, a tram that crosses emptiness as if emptiness were a scheduled stop.

The stone is new. That is the first wrongness, and it never becomes familiar. Pavements are warm in a way the air is not. Curbs have a skin, a tightness, like cooling fat. In the cuts where crews have opened a street to lay pipe, the rock shows no bedding, no old fracture, only a granular brightness and a faint give under the pick, as if the mountain were muscle that had not yet learned bone. Erosion here is not a history. It is a rumor. Gullies are sketches. Talus has not had time to fall and stay fallen; it gathers, reconsiders, and is drawn back into the slope overnight, leaving bootprints that end in smooth ground. Geologists—there are some; the city employs them the way a body employs a fever—say the ridge is extruding. They mean it literally. Survey pins walk. A benchmark set at dusk is a hand’s width higher by the following week, and the week is not short.

It is cool, and never quite dry, and never quite wet. The sky is low and the color of unlit iron. Light does not so much fall as leak, a dimness with a direction, so that noon is a paler species of dusk and the stacks throw shadows that are only slightly darker than the air they stand in. Moisture arrives as condensation more often than as rain. It beads on cable sheathing, on the lips of hoppers, on the wool of animals bedded against boiler walls. It gathers in the joints of young rock and stands there in thin, metallic sheets that do not flow so much as wait. Now and then a pocket gives way and a short river is born already cold, already losing itself between two unfinished piers, already gone by the time anyone has named it on a works map. Higher up, where the crest is still mostly intention, frost forms on surfaces that were pouring yesterday. Breath smokes. Steel sweats. The damp has a taste of mineral and hot oil, even where no engine is near.

Vegetation has taken the invitation without filling the room. In the seams—around flange bolts, in the lee of a retaining wall that is retaining something which has not yet arrived, along the drip-line of a condenser—growth is sudden and thick. Moss the color of wet slate. A sedge that cuts the hand. Low shrubs with leaves like oiled leather, crowding a single footing until the footing looks like a reef, then stopping, as if some ordinance governed green the way another governs smoke. Between these pockets the ground is bare young stone and clinker and the long pale fans of material the mountain shrugs off and then reclaims. You can walk twenty minutes and see only industry and raw geology, and then turn a corner into a grove so dense the dim light goes green, and the grove will be no wider than a house.

The city is real. That should be said plainly, because other things here are only partly real, and the distinction matters to anyone who lives by it. There are streets, mostly. There are mills with names painted in letters that blister in the damp. There are lifting engines, cage works, a foundry whose hearth has been banked at the same level for longer than the ridge beneath it has existed—which the night shift treats as a joke and the day shift does not. Power runs. You hear it in the cables, a low continuous note under everything else. Production is slow on purpose, or by necessity; the processes the place can sustain do not like to be hurried. A pour that begins on a Monday may still be finding its level on the Friday after next. Boilers cycle across days. A press descends with the patience of geology, which is an insult the geology is in a position to answer. Nothing is rickety. The construction is heavy, overbuilt, as if the engineers understood that their foundations were opinions.

Animals are everywhere, and nowhere crowded. This is the part visitors notice last, because the animals do not perform the role of wildlife. They use the city as range and weather and, in some cases, as tool. High on the unfinished shoulders, herds of long-necked grazers—pale, almost the color of the new stone, with hooves that do not scar it—move between the cable towers at a walk that matches the speed of the ridge’s rise, so that from the mills they appear stationary for hours. Birds nest in the mouths of stacks that are between firings, and abandon them, without panic, a day before the schedule says the stack will draw. Something larger lives in the condenser sheds: you hear the shift of a body against tin, and in the morning the catwalks are beaded not only with water but with a mucus that melts the frost under it and smells faintly of ozone. Insects persist in the steam, indifferent to height. At dusk, which is only a deepening of the existing dimness, flocks cross the open gaps between districts in such numbers that the cables sing, and then the sky is empty again, and the singing takes a while to stop.

The sound is the city’s constant argument with its own slowness. It is loud. Not chaotic—patterned, industrial, large—but loud enough that conversation happens close to the mouth, and the herds have learned to keep their ears angled away from the press halls. Whistles do not blast so much as occupy a note and hold it until the note becomes architecture. You can be in a quiet grove, hand on wet bark, and still feel the foundry in your sternum, a pulse slower than your own. Sound travels badly here and also too well. A bell struck on the eastern terrace may arrive at the western stacks before it arrives at the street below the bell, as if the air had a grain and the grain ran along the ridge rather than across it. Workers navigate by this. They trust a noise that comes early more than they trust a map.

The maps are the second wrongness, and they worsen if you look at them kindly. A district surveyed in spring has, by autumn, a street the survey did not include and cannot quite exclude. The street is there. Carts use it. It has a gutter full of condensation and a name stenciled on a plate bolted to young stone. But the plate’s bolts are seated in rock that, under a glass, shows flow lines still faintly active, and the street’s far end does not meet the terrace it is drawn as meeting. It meets a thickness of air that has the resistance of a wall and the appearance of ordinary weather. People who walk into it do not vanish. They continue, and are later found on a lower switchback, damp, mildly embarrassed, having lost an hour and gained a smell of hot metal they cannot account for. The city has a procedure. It is posted in the gatehouses. It is three sentences long and does not explain anything. It works often enough that no one has rewritten it.

Other procedures are less successful. Shadows sometimes fall toward the brighter part of the sky, which is never very bright, and then correct themselves with a sound like a sheet being shaken out. Distances along the crest are stable in the morning and negotiable after the second shift bell; a walk you timed at forty minutes may take twenty, or ninety, and your boots will show the same wear either way. Weight is locally optional at the edges of platforms that overhang ground the mountain has not yet supplied. Men have learned not to set a crane’s outriggers on those edges. Animals have not learned this, or have learned something better: the pale grazers will step onto an unsupported lip and stand there, chewing, while the lip decides whether to become cliff or street. Usually it becomes street. Sometimes it becomes a long slowness of falling stone that never quite finishes falling, a curtain of gravel hung in the dimness for a day and a night, ticking against itself, before the ridge draws it back in and the grazer is simply elsewhere, unbothered.

Rain, when it comes, comes upward first. Condensation lifts off the hot young rock in sheets, rises into the iron-colored air, and returns hours later as a fine cold fall that wets the upper surfaces of things and leaves their undersides dry. In those hours the mills look as if they are exhaling the weather. Vegetation drinks during the return and ignores the ascent. The abundant small life in the steam rides both directions. There are moths, or something that occupies the office of moths, that only exist while a particular boiler is in its long heating, and that are not present in the world before the fire is lit or after the fire is drawn. The boiler crews refuse to call this a problem. They call it draft. They have charts. The charts are accurate and also, if you compare last year’s to this year’s, drawn for a building that has gained a storey without anyone adding one.

No one is rebuilding the city upward on purpose. The storeys accrue the way the ridge accrues: a lintel a finger higher, a window whose sill no longer aligns with the memory of the sill, a stair that has inserted a step so gradually that only a stranger counts them and comes up wrong. Interior doors stick, then swing, then stick again, not from swelling but from the frame’s slow argument with plumb. Plumb is a courtesy here. The mountain does not offer it, and the engineers have stopped demanding it in the foundations, reserving their rigor for the machines, which still obey ordinary metallurgy and ordinary fire. This split—mechanisms that keep faith, ground that does not—is the practical mysticism of the place. You can trust a gear. You cannot trust the hour it takes to walk to the gear.

At the far end of the open reach, where the settlement thins into survey stakes and a single lighted hut, the ridge is visibly unfinished. You can watch it, if you have the patience the place requires. Stone surfaces in the cut like a shoulder surfacing in water. It is dark, then it is pale, then it is load-bearing, and a crew that has been waiting with timbers they cut last season steps in and begins, without hurry, to receive it into the plan. They do not celebrate. The hut’s dog—or the animal that sleeps where a dog would, and answers to a name, and is sometimes, in certain dimnesses, shaped more like the long-necked grazers than like any dog—does not even lift its head. Below the new stone, if you lie on the platform and look past the edge, there is no valley yet. There is a darkness with weather in it, and sounds that may be rivers preparing themselves, and a pressure against the ears that is not wind. The city does not extend downward. It waits for the ground to arrive and then builds, at industrial scale and industrial slowness, exactly as much city as the arriving ground will hold.

By night, which changes little except the quality of the dimness and the schedule of the whistles, the whole ridge seems to breathe on a period longer than sleep. Stacks bloom and thin. Condensation runs, stops, runs the other way along a gutter that has subtly reversed its fall. Herds shift one tower to the west. In the groves, leaves drip onto metal and the metal answers with a tick that joins the larger music. Somewhere a press completes a descent it began before the last upward rain. Somewhere a street that was not on the map this morning carries a lamp and a walker and a small, localized correctness, as if reality, which has been leaking all day along the crest, had found a seam and decided, for the length of that gutter and that lamp, to hold.

It will not hold everywhere. In the morning the lamppost may stand in a thickness of air that has remembered it is not a street, and the walker will be on the lower switchback, damp, smelling of hot metal, already late for a shift that the bell, running ahead of itself along the grain of the air, has not officially called. The ridge will be higher by a degree no one will bother to measure. The animals will be where the weather is. The city will be louder than the speed of anything in it, and cooler than its forges, and only as wet as the stone has decided, this week, to be.
