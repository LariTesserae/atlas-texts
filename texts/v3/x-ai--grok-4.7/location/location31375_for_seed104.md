The water is the height. That is the first fact that will not settle, and after a while you stop asking it to.

You come up through cloud that has already forgotten it was cloud and become a warm, slow body, and the land is still under you—old land, worn past the idea of peaks—but the land is also above you, or beside you, or held in the same volume as the water so that elevation and submersion are no longer opposites. Somewhere a shelf of stone that was a continent’s spine lies drowned and yet not lowered. The water sits on it the way weather sits on a range: immense, local, generating its own climate inside itself. Far off, if far still means anything, the surface bends not with horizon but with a long, tired fold, as if the sea had been taught the shape of mountains and kept the lesson after the mountains were gone.

It is warm. Not tropical in the bright sense—there is almost no bright—but warm the way stone is warm after a day that never quite arrived. The light is a residue. It comes from nowhere you can point to and fails before it travels far, so every view is a near view thickened by what it cannot show. In that nearness the water is dense with plant. Not scattered kelp, not a polite forest: a saturation. Blades, fans, ropes, plates, things that are half leaf and half slow mineral, grown so close they touch along their whole lengths and still find room to grow between one another. They move. The motion is continuous and almost silent—pressure changes, a flex, a release—so the place is active without ever becoming loud. You hear, if you hear, a soft interior tick, like wet wood deciding whether to split, and then nothing for a long time.

The stone under and among the growth has been worked by water past any drama. Ridges are suggestions. Valleys are the memory of valleys, filled and then filled again until the filling became the form. This is an end-state that still lives. Erosion here is not a process you watch; it is the grammar everything already obeys. Edges do not cut. They have been persuaded, over a span that makes spans feel rude, into curves that continue into other curves until you lose the place where one thing stopped being another. In some stretches the rock is so smoothed it reads as skin. In others it has gone granular, a flour of ancient ranges suspended and resettling in sheets so fine they look like dim light until your hand—if you still have a hand in the ordinary way—passes through and comes back filmed with a warmth that is not quite temperature.

Life is mostly the plants, and the plants are mostly the place. Animals are rare enough that a single passage rearranges the hour. When something does move that is not vegetation, it is usually small, or so large and slow you mistake it for a change in the water’s own intention. A pale flank the size of a weather system drifts between the stems and does not return. A scatter of eyes, or of structures that only behave like eyes, opens along a frond and closes again without having looked at you. The ecology is motion without a crowd: cycling, shedding, regrowing, the constant exchange of films and spores and dissolved stone, predator and prey so thinned out that predation itself has become occasional, almost ceremonial, and mostly the system feeds on its own slowness.

There are buildings, if that word can be made small enough. Not cities. Not roads. Here and there a wall that was never finished or was finished so long ago the finishing eroded into the same curves as the bedrock. A lintel with no door. A ring of posts whose spacing suggests a room and whose material has gone halfway back to reef. They do not imply a people so much as a hesitation the world once had, a moment when something tried to stand apart from the water and was gently, over ages, refused. No tools remain. No devices. Whatever hands there were have been edited out, or were never the kind of hands that leave mechanisms. The marks are biological in their patience: shell-binding, root-wedging, the way a living mat will hold a stone upright long after the reason for uprightness has dissolved.

Scale misbehaves. You can swim—or drift, or be carried by one of the slow internal weathers—for what feels like a day and still be inside the same stand of growth, the same warm dim, the same ancient flatten. Then, without a crossing you can name, the stand opens onto a vacancy wide enough to have storms. The storms are quiet. They are walls of rain that are already underwater, curtains of denser water moving through less-dense water, carrying warmth and shredded leaf and a darker dim. They pass. Behind them the vegetation closes again, layered so thick that distance becomes a kind of touch: everything near enough to brush, nothing far enough to be seen whole.

The weirdness is not an event laid on top. It is the medium. Directions do not stay loyal. Up is sometimes the way the plants lean, and the plants lean toward a light that is not where the light is. A passage you took inward brings you to a broader dark, as if the interior of the thicket contained an ocean larger than the ocean that contains the thicket. Sounds arrive late, or early, or as the memory of sounds you have not yet failed to hear. Once, the silence itself seemed to have a surface, and you were under it and above it at once. The water does not explain. It has had time enough to stop explaining. What remains is a stable strangeness: a high drowned country, lush and warm and nearly mute, ancient past peaks, vast enough to breed its own rain, dense with green and almost empty of beasts, marked only by the occasional bone of a structure that could not outlast the smoothing.

If you stay, the staying changes you less than you expect and more than you can inventory. Breath, if breath is still the right exchange, becomes a conversation with films. Time thickens where the plants touch. You begin to understand that the height was never a place you climbed to. It was a condition the water achieved by being so old, so full, and so unwilling to come down.
