The basin holds its heat the way a kiln holds yesterday’s firing. You come over the rim on a road of packed lime and iron grit, and the air thickens before the city quite appears: warm, wet, ordinary daylight gone slightly pearled, as if the sun has to pass through a cloth that never quite dries. Elevation should have made it thin and cold. It did not. Something under the stone keeps the bowl a few degrees too kind, and the kindness has nowhere to go. Moisture beads on the eyelashes. Jackets darken at the shoulders without a cloud having decided to rain.

What spreads below is a city, but not a crowd of one. The works sit as islands—long, low sheds, a chimney, a rank of cooling frames, a yard of dressed block—and between them lie avenues wide enough to turn a train of carts without anyone speaking. Most of those avenues are empty. Density here was never a matter of pressing buildings together. It was a matter of how much pipe, flue, and dressed stone a single shed could carry, and then how much quiet distance the next shed required so that its water would not steal the first shed’s fall. From the rim you can count them. Seven principal islands, a scatter of lesser ones, a pale sheet of standing water in the lowest crease, and beyond that the far wall of the basin, terraced by old cuttings, a half-day’s walk if the road were straight, which it is not.

The stone is the color of weak tea where the sun hits it, and of wet iron where it does not. Limestone, mostly, shot through with seams that weep. The weep is constant and unspectacular: a dark line down a wall, a thread finding the joint in a pavement, a glaze on the underside of every lintel. In the hollows the threads agree with one another and become marsh-edge—not a wetland with a name, only a width of ground where the water has stopped pretending to be in transit. It stands warm, brown-clear, ankle-deep over a crust of mineral that clicks underfoot when the crust is thick enough to trust. Cattails do not grow there. Almost nothing does. A black moss follows the drip-lines with clerical patience. A salt-pale lichen plates the upper faces of walls, where spray reaches and soil does not. In the lee of a high curb, where the roar is a little less, a stiff grass keeps a dozen clumps and looks embarrassed by the concession.

The roar is the civic fact. It does not arrive and it does not crest. It was here when the first flues were cut, and the flues were cut because the roar was already in the rock—water dropping through solution cavities, air pushed ahead of it, a geological throat-clearing that the builders chose to house rather than oppose. They housed it in stone channels dressed to a tolerance that has since been lost, then in iron, then in ceramic throats banded with copper. Engines were set into the same fall: slow turbines, belt-halls, pumps that do not cycle so much as continue. Relief valves stand part-open by doctrine. To close one fully is considered a kind of vandalism. The sound that results is deafening in the plain sense—you cannot buy a sentence outdoors—but it is not violent. It has the steadiness of a millrace heard from inside the mill. Layers sit inside it. A low organ-note from the oldest cavities. A drier clatter from the belt-halls, softened by distance and by the fact that the belts are old and run slack on purpose. A hiss that is steam and also, indistinguishably, water turning to film on hot plate. Wind contributes when it finds the empty frames on the ridge sheds, and those frames are empty because the city no longer needs the sails they held; it leaves them, because taking them down would change the note, and the note has been tuned, over generations, to a balance the stone will accept.

People live with it by not fighting it in the open. Speech outdoors is a grammar of hands, learned early, sufficient for work and insufficient for grief. The buildings that matter—sleeping rooms, counting rooms, the long halls where disputes are settled—are lined with cork, wool, and a plaster mixed with shredded fiber until the walls feel soft as a horse’s nose. Inside those rooms the roar becomes a pressure in the teeth rather than a sound, and people say that is the difference between living and being weathered. Doors are heavy, double, and always a little wet at the sill. Lamps are ordinary: oil, and in the newer islands a sour electrical light on cables that sag between poles set too far apart, so that night pools in the avenues and the lamps look like claims rather than a system.

Industry, such as it is, does not hurry. The basin yields three things worth the machinery: mineral water condensed and casked, a silica crust baked into crude glass and into brick, and cut stone for a trade that has mostly become internal, the city repairing itself with itself. Shifts are long because the machines are not restarted lightly. A turbine that has run for nine years is a known creature; the day it is stopped for a bearing is marked, and the bearing is replaced, and the stopping is as short as the fitters can make it, because the neighboring flues have shaped themselves to the presence of that flow. Change the flow and a weep migrates. A weep that migrates stains a new wall, undermines a curb, arrives in a silence room where it is not wanted. So the fitters work as surgeons work on a body that must keep breathing. Nothing is demolished if it can be absorbed. A cracked housing becomes a planter of moss, then a buttress, then, after enough wet years, a hoodoo with bolt-holes still visible in its flank if you know to look. Visitors spend their first hour trying to separate the built from the geological, and then give it up. The separation was the original error. Erosion here is not an attack. It is the style in which the place consents to continue.

Fauna is an absence you notice the way you notice a stopped clock. No flocks cross the rim. The standing water does not boil with midges; vibration and mineral together keep it nearly sterile, though in one canal a pale amphipod is said to live, and children are taken, once, to see the place where a grandfather saw it, and they see nothing, which is the lesson. On the warm iron of a south-facing tank a single kind of lizard holds its station, the color of the rust beneath it, so still that the eye discards it as a flake. It does not seem to hear. Perhaps it hears only the part of the roar that has always been there, and treats the engines as weather. Cats are kept indoors, and badly. Dogs are rare and unhappy. The sparseness is not a purge. It is what remains when a landscape has been loud and wet and mineral for longer than a breeding season can bargain with.

The strangeness is modest and local, which makes it harder to file. In certain courtyards—never marked on the rim maps, always known to the people who carry water—the roar cancels. Two stone returns and a flange of iron throw the note back on itself, and the air goes thick and private. In those pockets you hear your pulse as if the basin had leaned down to listen. The city treats the pockets as infrastructure. Silence rooms are not built over them; the cancel is left intact, and a bench is cut into the curb, and the bench is civic property in the way a well is. People come to them to finish arguments, to tune an instrument they will not play outside, to sit with someone who is dying and who wants, once, to hear a voice at ordinary volume. The bylaw is short: you do not pipe a flue through a cancel, and you do not raise your voice as if the pocket were a stage. The pocket is a tool. Tools are not for display.

There are other small refusals. Water beads on the undersides of bridges in lines that hold for an hour and look, if you are tired, like a bookkeeping hand—columns, ticks, a crossing-out—and then the lines fatten and fall, and the next beading is not the same sentence. Copper sheet on the ridge sheds grows a verdigris that at dusk, in normal failing light, seems a shade more present than the metal around it, not glowing, only unwilling to dim on schedule. Compasses near the oldest engines drift a few degrees and then behave. No one has built a doctrine out of it. Doctrine is reserved for water-path and for sound. A drifted compass is a personal inconvenience, corrected by the sightline to the rim, which does not drift.

Walk the lower crease at midday and the light is simply daylight, warm, a little flattened by vapor, sufficient to read a stencil on a cask and insufficient to make a drama of shadows. The shadows that do fall are honest. What unsettles is the scale of emptiness they cross. A figure pushing a handcart along an avenue looks provisional, a correction the city has not yet absorbed. The cart’s wheel strikes the same tar-filled joint every time; the joint has been there longer than the cartwright’s shop. Laundry, where it is hung, is hung inside screened wells, because cloth left in the avenues grows a mineral film before it dries, and it does not dry. Breath does not smoke. Sweat does not evaporate so much as relocate, from skin to collar to the perpetual damp at the waist. The air tastes of wet stone, of hot metal gone quiet, and of a faint sweetness from the condensate sheds, where the casked water is not pure and is not meant to be. People drink a milder grade, boiled, from cisterns that collect roof-weep above the mineral line. The strong grade goes out by road, slowly, in casks branded with a chimney and a drip, to towns that want the taste of depth and do not want the roar that makes it.

Seasons hardly argue with the place. A cooler month thins the vapor enough that the far wall comes forward, sharp, and people remark on it the way other cities remark on snow. A warmer month increases the weep until the marsh-edge advances a curb’s width and then, by some agreement with the pumps, stops. Storms break on the rim and arrive below as a change in register, a few hours when the organ-note deepens and conversation indoors requires a step closer. Then the basin returns to its sentence. Repairs accumulate the way sediment does. A patch on a ceramic throat, paler than the throat, becomes the color of the throat, becomes the place where the next patch will be justified. Ledgers record stoppages, not transformations. The city is not young and not dying. It is in its form, the way a river terrace is in its form: still working, no longer inventing the shape it will keep.

At the western island, if you are allowed onto the catwalk—the gesture for permission is two fingers laid along the forearm, a question about vibration—you can look back across the whole concession. Normal sun. Warm iron. Sparse green confined to drip and lee. Water in every joint and in no hurry. Sheds separated by distances that would, in another city, have filled with houses, and that here remain distances because the fall between them is doing work. The roar does not swell to meet your attention. It was already meeting you. A lizard shifts on a tank and becomes, briefly, an animal. Somewhere in a cancel you cannot see, someone is speaking at a human volume, and the words do not carry, and that is correct. The far rim holds. The road you came by is a pale thread with no one on it. Nothing in the basin is about to happen. That is the event: a settled loudness, wet and warm and high, urban in its pipes and empty in its streets, continuing.
