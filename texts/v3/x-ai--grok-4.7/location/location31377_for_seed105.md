The water is older than the flood that made it. It sits in a high saddle of the range, a cold inland sea the size of a parish of valleys, and it has been there long enough that the stone no longer remembers being dry.

You come to it from above, which is the only way. The ridges that hold it are worn blunt, their edges rounded by an erosion that finished its argument centuries ago and now only keeps the peace. No fresh scars. Talus lies where talus decided to lie. Between the cols the land drops, and what fills the drop is not a lake in the ordinary sense but a standing weight of water, dark as wet slate, cold enough that the air above it smokes in thin, reluctant threads. The surface is hard to find. Rain has been falling into it for so long that the boundary has gone soft: a fathom of falling water, then a fathom of water that has already fallen and not yet settled, then the sea itself. Stand on a ridge and the sound arrives before the shape does. It is not weather-noise. It is the whole basin speaking at once.

The rain is the loudest thing, and it does not pause. It hits the skin of the sea and the skin answers, a continuous low hammer that carries for miles along the drowned troughs. Under that, other voices. Where a ridge still breaks the surface as a dark island of stone, ice has glued itself to the rock in shelves, and those shelves calve on their own schedule — not dramatic, not rare. A crack, a tilt, a boom that travels through water faster than through air and arrives in your chest before your ears have agreed on a direction. Farther down, in the narrows between two sunken spurs, the sea breathes. It draws and returns on a slow seiche, and each return shoves a tongue of water up a stone throat the old flood cut and then polished. The throat whistles. On stiller days — there are no still days, only less loud ones — you can hear the whistle from the high path, a single note that rises and falls as the basin inhales.

Light does not do much work here. The rain is a roof. What little sky exists above the roof is the color of iron left out, and by the time anything gets through the falling water and into the standing water, it has already given up. A few fathoms down it is dusk. Below that it is the dark of a shut eye. You learn the place by sound and by the pale things that grow in it, not by seeing far.

The plants are the exception to the dark, and they do not crowd. This is not a thicket. The sea is mostly empty water and bare, long-weathered stone, and the vegetation arrives as events: a meadow, then a long reach of nothing, then a single stand so tall it seems to have mistaken itself for weather.

Nearest the surface, where the drowned shoulders of the hills still catch a rumor of day, there are mats. Not grass. A cold, tight growth, olive going to black, plastered to every slope that isn't quite vertical, lush in the way moss is lush — dense in itself, indifferent to company. It drips. When the seiche lifts, sheets of it belly out and settle again with a wet slap you can hear from the ridge. Between the mats the stone shows through, grey, rounded, honest. The flood did not strip this country. It finished it, and then the plants agreed to the finish.

Deeper, the stands begin. They root on old streambeds and on the flats where soil once sat and has since become a cold silt the color of tea left overnight. Each stand is a city of blades. The blades are the width of a belt, the length of a mast, and they do not branch so much as fray, edges splitting into lesser ribbons that never quite detach. They are green only at the tips, and only when something — a thinning of the rain, a lens of clearer water — lets a little light down. The rest of each blade is the color of deep tea, then of old bronze, then of nothing. They move. Not with any will, but the currents here are many and they do not agree with each other, so a stand will lean one way at its roots and another at its crown, and the sound of that argument is a knocking, blade on blade, soft and enormous, like a crowd of people shifting their weight in a wooden hall. You can sit on an emergent rock and listen to one stand for an hour and never hear it repeat itself.

There are no animals. Nothing grazes the blades. Nothing hides in the mats. Nothing rises to the rain or turns under the ice. The absence is not a stillness — the place is too loud and too busy for stillness — but it is a kind of cleanliness. No tracks on the silt except the tracks of water. No shells. No guano on the rocks that break the surface. The mats are unbrowsed, so they grow until their own weight peels them, and the peeled sheets drift until a current pins them to another rock, where they root again if they can. The stands shed ribbons the way trees shed leaves, except there is no season for it; a ribbon simply finishes, silvers, and comes away, and for a while it hangs in the midwater like a dropped strap before the dark takes it down to the silt. The silt is deep. It has been accepting ribbons for a long time. In the narrows, where the whistle is, the silt has built bars, and the bars have grown their own small stands, and the stands have slowed the water, and the slowed water has dropped more silt. The basin is arranging itself. It has been arranging itself since before anyone cut the first step.

Someone did cut steps. Not many, and not recently.

On three of the emergent ridges, and on one drowned shoulder you can only find by wading the mat until the stone rises under it, there are stairs. They are not fine. Blocks levered out of the local rock, set into the slope, each tread dished by feet and then by rain and then, lower down, by the sea, until the dish became the point. The lowest steps vanish under the mats and do not come back. Whoever cut them understood water well enough to work with it and not well enough to keep it out, which suggests they were here while the basin was still filling — or while they still believed it might empty. Alongside one stair a channel has been pecked into the stone, a finger-wide groove that once perhaps led melt or rain to a cistern. The cistern is there, a round hole no wider than a person's reach, and it is full, and has been full so long that a mat has sealed it like a lid. If you kneel and press, the mat gives, and under it the water in the hole is the same water as the sea. The groove still carries rain. It no longer has anywhere to put it that the sea has not already claimed.

Lower, on a flat that is only a flat at the bottom of a long wade, there is a ring of stones. Not a wall. A ring, knee-high where the silt hasn't swallowed it, the stones chosen rather than cut, fitted by patience. Inside the ring the silt is cleaner than outside, as if something was swept, once, and the habit of sweeping left a shape the water still respects. A mooring pin of hammered pin-metal — poor metal, bubbled, the color of a bruise — stands at the ring's lakeward side, bent by some old pull and never straightened. Fiber rope would have rotted. Nothing is tied there. A stand of blades grows just outside the ring, close enough that on the inward seiche the ribbons drag across the stones with a sound like a broom. The ring and the stand have reached an agreement. Neither is winning.

There is another kind of work, easier to miss. In the throat that whistles, the stone has been worried at by hands as well as by water. A series of notches, evenly spaced, running up the polished wall above the present high-water of the seiche and continuing down below it until the dark and the silt disagree about which of them owns the rest. Not a ladder. Gauges. Someone stood here and cut a mark each time the water did a new thing, and then the water did the same thing often enough that the marks became a texture, and then the texture became part of the polishing. You can still feel them with a palm, under the water, until your hand goes numb from the cold and you have to take it out and hold it in your armpit and listen to the whistle instead. The highest notch is far above any water you will see today. The basin has been both fuller and this full. It has settled near a level it likes. The rain that enters it leaves by paths under the ridges — siphons the flood found or made, humming in the stone if you put an ear to the right place on the high path — and the leaving matches the entering closely enough that the shores do not travel much from year to year. An established wet. A balance with a sound.

The cold is not the cold of a season you wait out. It is the cold of height and of water that has nowhere warm to have been. Your breath is a brief local weather. On the rocks, anchor ice grows in beards where spray lands and does not run off fast enough, and the beards break and reform, and their breaking is a small bright crackle under the larger hammer of the rain. The sea itself does not skin over. Something in it — pressure, depth, the long stirring — refuses the last degree. At the margins, in the lee of an emergent rock, you sometimes find a lens of slush, grey, rotating slowly as the current worries it, and the slush hisses against the mat, and the mat does not mind. Plants here are built for this. Their lushness is a cold lushness: slow, waxy, thick in the leaf, spending nothing on flowers because nothing is coming to a flower. If they fruit, they fruit into the water as knots and swellings that detach and roll along the bottom until they lodge. You see the knots sometimes in the shallows, pale, fist-sized, motionless. They are not eggs. There is nothing to hatch.

The weirdness is not a creature and not a ruin. It is the way the basin fails to be only one thing.

There are places, midwater, where the dark thins without any reason you can point to in the sky. A column of water gone faintly lucent, the color of a bruise healing, and the blades that pass through it burn for a moment at their tips — not with light stolen from above, but with something they seem to have been keeping. The glow lasts as long as the column lasts, and the columns drift. They are not warm. Put a hand in and the cold is the same cold. But the blades know them, and lean, and for the length of a breath a stand becomes a dim forest of candles, and then the column slides on into empty water and the candles go out, and you are left with the knocking and the rain and the sense that the sea has rooms in it that move.

There is also the matter of the upward rain, if that is what it is. In the lee of the highest emergent ridge, where the falling water is broken by stone and the air should be merely wet, threads rise. Thin, continuous, as if the sea were giving back a little of what it is given, in lines too straight for steam. They make no sound of their own. They disturb the falling rain: a stutter, a brief hole in the hammer, a place where drops fall into an upward thread and cease to be drops in any way you can follow. The plants nearest these places are taller. Their tips break the surface and stand in the mixed air, black and beaded, and the beads run up them as often as down. No one has left a gauge for this. The notches in the throat measure level, not behavior. Whatever the threads are, they have been happening long enough that the ridge in their lee is fluted — vertical grooves, evenly worn, as if the rising water were a file the stone has agreed to.

At the far end of the basin, a half-day's walk along the ridges if the rain lets you see the next ridge, the sea narrows into a drowned glen and the sound changes register. The rain is still there, but under it a new note: low, rhythmic, not the whistle of the throat and not the boom of ice. A gate. Two walls of stone, close, both of them worked — crude pilings of block, primitive, the joints open, the whole assembly tilted a few degrees by the long slump of the slope beneath it. Between the walls the current is faster. It has to be. The glen is the start of one of the under-ridge siphons, and the old builders, or the old markers of water, put a throat of their own in front of the natural one. Not to stop the sea. Nothing here stops the sea. To make it speak in a count. Every few breaths the pressure behind the pilings overcomes the angle, and a pulse goes through, and the pulse hits a slab they set on edge beyond the gate, and the slab answers with a thud that the water carries back up the glen like a drum. You can walk toward it for an hour and the interval does not change. The basin has a clock. It is not keeping time for anyone. The fiber lashings that once may have adjusted the slab are gone; the slab has seated itself; the thud is simply what this much water does to this much stone, forever, at this height, in this cold.

Between the clock and the ring of stones, between the stands and the empty reaches, the scale of the place asserts itself slowly, which is the only way a place this dark can assert anything. It is not a world-sea. From the right ridge, on the rare thinning of the rain, you can see the far rim: a line of worn peaks, their snow dirty and permanent, their bases drowned so that they look like animals standing in a trough — except there are no animals, and the resemblance does not survive a second look. What you can see is a country. Several valleys, their dividing spurs submarine now, their old streams become the paths the currents prefer. A town's worth of distance, a shire's worth of water. You could not farm it. You could, perhaps, once, have crossed it in a hide boat or a raft of lashed ribbons, poling between the stands, using the mooring pin, sleeping on the emergent rocks under a skin against the rain. The evidence says someone tried a little of that and then stopped trying, and the basin did not chase them and did not preserve them. It took the stairs as far as it wanted them, kept the ring clear by accident of current, learned the gate's thud as one of its own voices, and went on being full.

Night is not different from day except that the iron sky goes black and the blade-tips, when a lucent column wanders through a stand, become the only lamps. The rain does not respect the distinction. Ice calves when ice is ready. The whistle rises and falls. The gate thuds. In the dark the sparse stands feel farther apart than they are, each a private knocking, and the empty water between them feels like distance you could get lost in without moving, which is almost true: the currents are local and multiple, and a ribbon shed at dusk may be a glen away by the time the iron comes back. The silt accepts it either way.

If you stay, you learn the map by ear. The whistle is north of the ring, the gate is down-glen of both, the ice-shelves speak from the western islands in a cluster of cracks just before they boom. The mats slap when the seiche is in its generous phase. The upward threads stutter the rain in one place only, and when you hear the stutter you are near the fluted ridge and can find the high path with your hands. None of this is marked anymore, except by the notches, and the notches are underwater, and the water is cold, and the plants have no opinion. They go on being lush in a country that is mostly absence: absence of light, of animals, of any new building, of any plan the sea is willing to learn. What is present is enough to fill the ear and, slowly, the idea of a place. A high, drowned, long-settled basin, loud with its own maintenance, growing great green engines in the dark and widely spaced, tended by no one, finished by no one, and not, in any sense that matters, waiting.
