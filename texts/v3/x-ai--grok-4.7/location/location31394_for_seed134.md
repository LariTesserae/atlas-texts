The place is a single bowl of water held high in new stone, small enough that its walls can be felt as a limit and full enough that there is no air inside it to stand in. Daylight comes down through a bright, uneasy lid—the surface, close in the way a cathedral roof is close—and does not fail. The water is cool the way a high spring is cool, not the cold of depth. It has not been here long.

The rock around it is still sharp. Nothing has had time to round. Faces of glass and pale crystal meet at edges that would cut a hand, and the fractures that opened the bowl are bright inside, as if the mountain had only just decided to split and had not yet learned regret. A thin seep enters at one wall, colder than the rest, tasting of metal that has not oxidized into softness. What leaves does so through a throat in the floor, a slot too narrow for any large body, and the water is pressed through it without hurry and without pause. The bowl does not empty. It also does not rest.

There are no plants. No films of green on the ledges, no drift of weed, no brown reassurance of decay. The light arrives as ordinary day and stays ordinary: gray glass, white spar, a bottle-green translucence in the newer breaks, and the first rumor of rust only where a edge has been worried. Color here is mineral and animal. The water itself is clear. What limits sight is crowding.

The animals are many, and they are near enough to touch. Small ones tile the glass in overlapping coats, each no larger than a thumb, each carrying a flake of the stone it sits on cemented into its back. Larger ones hold the midwater in slow ranks, bells of muscle stacked so close their margins brush. A few great ones—the size of doors, no more than a dozen—sit in the wider notches of the wall like valves seated in a works, and do not swim so much as endure. Between all of them the gaps are narrow. A body moving through must turn sideways, and the animals do not make way quickly. They are not territorial in any hot sense. They are simply present at a density the volume can only just bear, layered from the bright lid down to the dark slot, each layer feeding on a different refusal of the stone.

Nothing here was built. There is no pier, no chain, no cut mark, no slag. The resemblance to industry is a fact of process, not of authorship. The bell-animals contract on a cycle longer than a breath and shorter than an hour, and each contraction drives a measured volume of water across the faces of crystal they face. The small armored ones rasp. They do not graze a crop; there is none. They take the dulled skin of the rock—the microns that water and time would eventually soften—and leave the edge fresh. Under their grip the crystal answers. Young quartz, stressed, throws off a small charge, and the animals drink it through pads that are as much metal-salt as flesh. The great valve-beasts regulate the seep and the throat. Muscle and cartilage seat and unseat against stone with the patience of equipment that has no operator and no shift-change. The bowl exports, continuously, a thin freight of dissolved mineral and spent charge down into the mountain’s unlit plumbing. Scaled production, mechanized, electrified: all of it native, all of it unmade.

They are strange in the way a mechanism is strange when you recognize the function and not the lineage. Some of the midwater bells have no mouth you would trust; they absorb across an inner skin turned outward, and the current they themselves maintain is their weather. Some of the small ones carry a lens ground from a flake of the local glass, aimed at a patch of stone, and what gathers there is not a plant but a heat-thin electry bloom, simpler than fungus, which they lick on a schedule you could set a slow clock by. A few animals are mostly spicule and nerve, schooling in sheets that behave like shutters: they open and close the light on the wall in broad deliberate bands, and the bands do not flicker. Eyes, where eyes exist, are dull and accurate, more gauge than gaze. No one hunts in a rush. Enclosure is a matter of sitting until the smaller body is simply inside the larger current. What dies becomes mineral meal and is taken up again. There is no wood to rot and no leaf to stain the water brown.

The sound is the other wall. It does not spike. It occupies. The throat in the floor is an organ pipe with no silence between notes; the pitch is low, steady, loud enough that a shout would be a smaller fact inside it. The bells add a piston-thump in overlapping time, never quite unison, so the rhythm is dense rather than musical. The rasp of the edge-keepers is a high continuous file. The rock rings. Because the fractures are new, it rings cleanly, and the animals’ grips—tightening, loosening, tightening—play it without intention. Near the seep the tone brightens and cools. Near the great valves it drops, a pressure in the jaw more than a tone in the ear. You could cross the bowl in a few minutes if the bodies allowed a straight line. The sound does not cross so much as already be everywhere. Loudness here is not an event. It is the climate. Change, when it comes, comes as a gradient: a valve seated a fraction deeper, a sheet of spicules drifting one body-width toward the light, the seep running a little richer after some unfelt adjustment in the stone above. Cycles are long. Nothing darts. The roar does not hurry, and it does not dim.

Rules settle out of the arrangement, not out of any decree.

The youth of the rock is maintained. Left alone, the water would begin, slowly, to blunt what it touches; the animals will not leave it alone. They harvest the dulled layer because dull crystal gives less charge and worse purchase, and so the bowl stays sharp, a young place kept young by appetite. Erosion is not absent. It is harvested before it can become history.

Light is allowed to be ordinary. Nothing here hides in the dark as a strategy, because the dark is only the slot and the undersides of bodies. The bright lid wavers, and the sheets of spicule-animals edit it, but the day is the day. Shadows do not pool. They slide upward and come apart in refraction, so the floor never learns a permanent night.

The boundaries hold. This is not an ocean and not a flooded country. It is one feature: a high, recently opened chamber, hamlet-sized in its population of great valves, grove-sized if the bells were trees, cathedral-sized in the way sound and light share one room. Beyond the walls is mountain, still airless to this water, still dry in its outer stone. Beyond the throat is a darker circulation that does not belong to the bowl and is not visited. The surface is a ceiling you can rise to and not a shore you can cross; break it and the air is there, thin and high and useless to everything that lives below, and the animals do not break it. They treat the lid as a condition, the way a mill treats its head of water.

Nothing migrates in any ordinary season. Colonists arrived when the bowl first filled—recently, as geology counts—and the abundance is local increase, not visitation. Young parents, already crowded. Food is stone, charge, the bloom that charge and the animals’ own waste permit, and the bodies of the slow. There is no season of leaves to wait for. There is a longer weather: the seep’s metal content, the day’s angle through the lid, the degree to which the throat is pinched. The animals answer these with adjustments so gradual they read, from inside one hour, as stillness.

Stand in it—if standing were possible—and the first impression is enclosure and noise and too much life. Stay, and the enclosure becomes legible. The noise is a map: pitch for distance to the throat, rasp-density for the richness of a face, the interval of a nearby bell for how hard that animal is working the current you are floating in. The life is a factory that does not know the word, running a product of dissolved mountain into a dark pipe, keeping its own tools sharp, electrified by the stone it refuses to let grow old. The water is cool on the mouth. The light is day. The edges have not yet agreed to be old. And the whole small place, packed and ringing, changes only at the speed of a process that intends to continue.
