The plateau does not rise so much as continue. From any place you stand, the land runs out past weather and comes back as weather again, a high country broad enough to make its own rain and then lose it in its own moss. The stone under it is old in the settled way: ridges worn to the angle they can hold, cols opened just enough for wind to pass without cutting further, bowls whose lips have not moved in a long time. Erosion here is not an event. It is the shape things have agreed to keep.

It is cool without being sharp. Breath shows and then doesn't. The air is heavy with water that has not quite decided to fall, so that every surface is already wet before the rain arrives, and the rain, when it comes, is only a clarification. Rock faces weep in slow sheets. Water stands a finger deep over black peat and does not drain, because there is nowhere lower nearby that is not also full. Pools mirror a white sky and then cloud from beneath as iron and tannin rise into them. You can walk for a day and not find a dry sole, and you can walk for a week and not find an edge.

What grows here grows into every gap. Cushion plants lock shoulder to shoulder until the ground is a single breathing mat, and out of that mat come thicker things: waist-high scrub with leaves like oiled leather, stands of short trees whose trunks are green with film, curtains of moss that hang from branches and then root again where they touch. Nothing is sparse. Stems compete for the same palm of soil and solve it by sharing, or by growing through one another. Flowers are small and many, the color of cold metal and old blood, opening in the normal light and closing when cloud thickens, which is often. The light itself is ordinary daylight, only softened, as if it has passed through wet cloth. Shadows exist. They are simply never hard.

The abundance is not noisy. Sound goes into the moss and does not return. Wind moves the crowns and the crowns answer with a hush, not a roar. Water ticks in the rocks at a pace you stop hearing. Birds are everywhere and almost mute: they flick between stems, a dark comma, a pale throat, and settle again without a call. Something large moves in the scrub at intervals, unhurried, and the only proof is the slow collapse of a wet fan of leaves behind it. Insects work the flowers in a continuous small industry, their wings a dry whisper that never rises to a hum. The quiet is not emptiness. It is absorption. The place is packed past the point where sound can travel.

Fauna fills the seams the plants leave, which are few. Things live in the water-film on the moss, in the hollow stems, under the peat-skin that looks solid until a hoof or a paw dimples it. Herds of low, heavy grazers cross the wet flats in lines so dense they look, from a rise, like a darker current in the grass. They do not stampede. They feed and shift and feed. Predators keep to the dripping thickets at the rims of the bowls, where the scrub is tallest and the light fails earliest. You see their work more than their bodies: a cleared patch, a scatter of pale down, a trail of pressed cushions already springing back. Everything eats, everything is eaten, and the exchange is constant without ever becoming loud. At dusk, which arrives as a dimming rather than a line, the pools take on a skin of midges, and the small hunters come up from the wet to work that skin until it is gone.

There are no walls, no roads, no roofs. Nothing here was set down by a hand. And yet the country runs like a works. Every acre is in production. Peat compresses under its own weight and under the weight of water, sour and cold, giving off a thin sweet rot that smells, in certain hollows, like a shed where oil has been stored too long. Seeps bleed orange and rust where metal comes up with the groundwater and drops out again on the lips of pools, building rinds, terraces, delicate shelves that look poured and are only chemistry repeating. Fog forms in the bowls at the same hour each day, lifts, reforms on the next ridge, and in the lifting it carries what the plants have exhaled and what the peat has given up, a slow distillation with no vessel. The cushion mat fixes, stores, sheds, and fixes again. Grazers process it by the thousand. Iron bacteria stain and restain the same stones. Nothing is manufactured, and the throughput would shame a mill.

The unusual part is how finished it all feels. Patterns repeat at scales that suggest intention and are only the same rule applied again: the way the weeping faces always find the same angle, the way the orange rinds build in even courses, the way herds file through gaps that are not paths and yet are always there. A hydrocarbon sheen appears on some pools after rain and breaks into leaves of color, then is gone by afternoon, taken back into the mat. You can mistake it for runoff. There is no runoff. There is no elsewhere for it to have come from. The country is the source.

Weather belongs to it. A front can be born over one province of moss and die over another without ever leaving the plateau. Rain walks in gray columns you can watch approach for an hour. Between columns the light is clear enough to see, far off, another rain already standing on a further reach of the same land, and beyond that a pale shine that might be open water or only wetter grass. Distance does not resolve. The horizon is not a line. It is the place where detail becomes weather and weather becomes the same country again.

Underfoot the balance holds. Banks slump and are caught by roots before they travel. Pools silt and are scoured by the next seep. A tree falls, and within a season it is a ridge of moss with water running through it, indistinguishable from the ridges that were never trees. Nothing is young in the sense of unfinished, and nothing is ruined. The land has been this wet, this full, this quietly busy for long enough that every process has a counter-process already in place. You can lie down on the mat and feel it cold through your clothes, and the mat receives the weight, compresses, and begins, without haste, to grow through you if you stay. You do not stay. Something in the scrub shifts, the leaves settle, and the only sound is water finding, again, the path it has used for as long as the stone has known how to weep.
