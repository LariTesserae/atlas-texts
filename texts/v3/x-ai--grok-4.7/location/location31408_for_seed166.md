The place is a chamber, not a sea. It sits below the grade of the ground that once held it open to weather, and the water has been in it longer than any living memory of the works that keep the level where it is. You could swim the long way across it in a few minutes and still have room to turn. The walls close it. There is a floor, uneven, and a ceiling of stone that only fails in one place, toward the higher end, where a throat of water rises to daylight. That throat is not wide. It is how the light arrives, and it is enough.

The light is ordinary daylight, cooled and thickened by a short column of water. It does not shimmer in patterns that suggest waves above; the surface up there is kept flat. Down here the light falls as a pale column and then loosens, so that most of the chamber is lit the way a large room is lit by one tall window on an overcast day: you can see the far wall, you can see the grain of the rock, and nothing is dramatic. Colors stay in the range of wet limestone, iron-blush, and the grey-green of water that has something dissolved in it besides itself. No lamps. No glow that needs explaining.

The rock has finished the arguments it used to have with water. Edges that were once sharp are thumbsmooth. A ledge along the western wall has the settled look of a step worn by nothing but time and seepage, though nothing walks it. Finer sediment has found the low pockets and stayed. There is a single slab, tilted, half-bedded in the floor, its underside pale where it has not been turned in any lifetime you could measure by standing here. The forms are in balance. Nothing is mid-collapse. Nothing is freshly broken. If you came back after a year, or ten, the arrangement would convict you of having misremembered rather than of the place having moved.

What moves is almost nothing. The water is cool, not bitter-cold, the temperature of a deep cellar that has forgotten the season. It does not stratify in any way you can feel by passing through it; the whole volume is held at one coolness, as if whatever feeds and draws the basin does so with a patience that has already won. There is no current you could call a current. A mote, if you had one, would hang. The surface far above does not telegraph weather. Pressure is simply the pressure of this depth, modest, steady, a weight on the body that does not vary from one side of the chamber to the other.

The water itself is the industrial fact, and it does not look like a machine. It is clear enough to see across, with a faint body to it, a softness in the distance as if a very little clay or a very little metal were always in suspension and never settling further, because the amount is already the amount the water can carry at this temperature and this stillness. On the rock, in the zone where the light is strongest, there is a thin mineral skin, rust-to-ochre, continuous, not growing in crusts that flake and fall. It has reached the thickness it will have. In the darker margins the stone is only stone, darkened by long wetting. You would not point to a pipe, a housing, a bolt. There is no structure in the chamber. The works are elsewhere, upstream and aside, and what they have done here is to decide the water: its level, its coolness, its chemistry, and then to keep deciding those things so quietly that the decision has become a property of the place, like the grain of the limestone.

Nothing animal lives in it. No fish turn at the edge of the light column. No snails track the walls. No insects stitch the surface above, or if they do, they do not belong to this room and do not enter it. The water will not support them. That is not a curse and not a mystery; it is the same fact as the iron blush and the unchanging coolness, a process-water held past the tolerance of gills and soft bodies, stable enough that decay has nothing left to finish. The quiet has that in it too: no ticks of feeding, no taps of shells, no body displacing volume except yours, if you are there.

Vegetation is not absent, but it does not make a community. On the lit ledge, and on the upper face of the tilted slab where the column grazes it, there are a few dark green films, barely raised from the stone, and two or three short blades of something that might be a moss adapted to drowning, spaced so far apart that each one reads as an incident rather than as growth. They do not spread while you watch. They do not look recently dead. They persist at the density the water allows, which is almost none, and the distance between them is the ordinary emptiness of the chamber asserting itself. Most of the volume is unoccupied water. Most of the floor is bare settled silt and bare stone. You can feel the sparseness as room: room between the walls, room under the ceiling, room around the few stains of green.

Sound is mostly the sound of your own presence, and then not even that. The chamber does not drip. It does not gulp. There is, if you are still for a long time, a low continuous note at the edge of hearing, too even to be weather, the sort of sound a large pump makes when it is far enough away and well enough seated that it has joined the silence instead of interrupting it. It does not rise. It does not cycle in any rhythm you can count. After a while you stop calling it a sound. The water does not lap, because nothing here disturbs a surface you can reach, and the surface you cannot reach is kept flat. Small sounds die quickly. The rock gives nothing back.

Nothing in the rules of the place is unusual once you grant the water. Light falls off with distance and with silt the way light should. Stone holds its shape. Cool water stays where it is put when the putting is constant. Metals and carbonates have finished their exchange and sit at the skin they have made. The absence of animals is a chemical outcome, not a prohibition written on the walls. The scale is the scale of one hollow that flooded and was then kept: not a district, not an ocean, a single feature with boundaries you can see. Emptiness is part of the composition, not a failure to fill it. Time, here, does not so much pass as fail to find a purchase. The chamber is not waiting. It has already become the form it will keep, and the unseen engines, wherever they are, spend their work on not letting that form be revised.
