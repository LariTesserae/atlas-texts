The chamber is not large. You could walk its long axis in a few dozen breaths if the floor allowed walking, which it mostly does not. It sits under a lip of old stone, a room the size of a small grove or a parish nave, sealed on three sides and open on the fourth only as a low throat that lets air in and almost no weather. The roof has sagged over a long time into a shallow vault, stained where water once ran and then stopped running in the same places. Nothing here looks young. The shapes have already finished becoming what they are.

Heat comes up from the rock itself. Not a vent you can point to, not a spring you can drink from without caution, just a steady warmth held in the walls and given back into the air so that the chamber never cools, even when the throat breathes night air inward. The heat sits low. Ankles feel it first. Higher up it thins into a closeness rather than a burn, the kind that makes cloth stick and makes the idea of a breeze feel theoretical. There is no breeze. The air turns over slowly, in soft exchanges through the throat, and whatever enters stays long enough to take on the smell of wet mineral and leaf-rot and something faintly sweet that has no single source.

Moisture is not a pool and not a rain. It is a habit of the place. Condensation beads on the cooler underside of the vault and falls when a bead grows too heavy for the stone to keep. Some of it never falls. It travels instead, a thin film sliding along ledges that have been polished by exactly this motion for longer than any living thing in the chamber has been alive. In the lower third, where the floor dips, the film gathers into pockets: shallow dishes of water no wider than a torso, tea-colored, still enough that a fallen leaf can float in one place until it sinks. Between the pockets the ground is only damp, dark, and firm in the way packed leaf-mold is firm. A dry season would not empty this room. A wet one would not flood it. The water has already found its measure.

Light arrives the same way the air does, grudgingly, through the throat, and dies a short distance in. Midday outside is a gray-gold smear on the near wall. The rest of the chamber lives in a dimness that is not darkness. You can see edges. You cannot see detail until you are close enough to touch it. Shadows do not jump. They thicken and thin as bodies pass through them.

What grows here has learned the dimness and the heat together. Vegetation is thick without being tall in the way a forest is tall. It layers. Mats of small-leaved creepers hold the damp floor and climb the first handsbreadth of every wall. Above them, strap-leaved plants rise in clumps, their blades overlapping so that from a step away the clump reads as one dark mass and from inside it reads as many narrow rooms. Vines do not hang so much as braid. They have used the same holds for so long that the stone under them is smoother than the stone beside them, a pale track where rootlets and stems have worried the surface grain by grain. Flowers, where they occur, are small and dull in this light, the color of old brick or of bruised fruit, and they open low, near the warmth, not toward the throat. Nothing here reaches for a sun it cannot use. The green is deep, almost black in the far corners, and so continuous that bare stone is an event: a shoulder of rock, a ledge, the lip of a water dish.

The life that moves is everywhere and nearly silent. That is the first odd rule of the room, and it holds. There are many bodies. They do not announce themselves.

On the mats, small things with too many legs work the film of water, pausing when a drop falls, then resuming a path that looks random until you watch long enough to see it repeat. They avoid the open dishes. Something in the dishes does not like to be crowded. A low shape, flat, the color of wet bark, rests at the margin of one pocket with only a rim of it above the surface. When a creeper-insect blunders to the edge, the shape does not lunge. It tilts. The water accepts the insect. The shape settles back. Elsewhere, between the strap-leaves, pale moth-sized fliers drift rather than dart. Their wings make no tick against the foliage. They land, feed on whatever sugars the dull flowers keep, and lift again on air that seems too thick for them and is not. Higher, in the braid of vines, a slower animal keeps a territory no larger than a cloak: a climber with a narrow head and feet that never quite leave the stem. It eats the moths. It also eats a soft fruit the vines set in the heat, a fruit that splits without falling and smells, briefly, like the sweet note in the air. The climber is not rare. There are several. They do not call. They turn their heads when another passes on an adjacent braid, and that is the whole negotiation.

Closer to the throat, where the light is least poor, a different set of bodies keeps the boundary. Long-legged waders no taller than a hand stalk the damp between dishes, spearing whatever the mats give up. They freeze so completely that a falling bead can strike one's back and still not move it. Deeper in, where the light fails, the waders do not go. Something else has that dark: a bulk that reads, when it moves, as a rounding of the wall rather than as an animal. It travels the perimeter on a route worn into the leaf-mat, a shallow trench you only notice once you have watched the bulk complete it. The trench is old. The bulk is not the first to have used it. What it eats is unclear at a glance and then not unclear. It takes the soft fallen fruit, the drowned insects, and occasionally a wader that has misjudged the dim. It does this without haste and without noise beyond a wet compression of the mat under its weight. After it passes, the mats close. The trench remains.

There are bones, but not many, and none of them arranged. A scatter of fine white in one dish, cleaned and then left, slowly yellowing in the mineral water. A larger curve, half-swallowed by creeper near the back wall, the size of a rib from something that no longer seems to live here. The chamber does not keep trophies. It keeps what the heat and the damp have not yet finished.

The built things are easy to miss and then, once seen, hard to unknow. They are not structures in any living sense. On the left wall, at about the height of a shoulder, a line of small cups has been cut or worn into the stone. Seven, maybe eight. They hold nothing now except, in two of them, a skin of the same condensate that films the ledges. The cuts are even. Time has softened their rims until they look almost like the natural dishes in the floor, except that natural dishes do not align. Across from them, half-buried where a fall of rock met the mat and was then grown over, a flat slab sits level. Someone, once, wanted a level surface here. The slab's near edge is chipped in a repeating way, not from collapse but from use, the way a step wears where a foot always lands in the same place. No foot lands there now. A strap-leaf has rooted in a crack along its center and split the slab by the slowest possible means, which the slab has accepted. It is still level. It is no longer one piece.

Near the throat, just inside the dim, a set of marks runs vertically on the stone: short strokes, grouped, then a gap, then strokes again. They are not deep. Lichen has begun to prefer them, so the marks read as darker lines on dark stone, visible only when the outside light reaches that far and only if you already suspect a mark. They do not resolve into anything you can read. They resolve into the fact of having been made. Nothing else in the chamber suggests a tool. No metal, no fired clay, no cord. Whatever hands worked the cups and the slab and the strokes did that work and then left the room to the heat. The room has been finishing their work ever since, which is to say unfinishing it, at the pace of condensation and rootlets.

The oddness is not spectacle. It is a set of small refusals. The quiet, first: so many bodies, and the loudest sound in an hour may be a bead of water letting go of the vault and finding a leaf. Even the bulk does not grunt. Even the waders do not startle audibly. The air is warm enough, and the foliage dense enough, that sound dies within a body-length of its source. A struggle in the far corner would not reach the throat. Things here have stopped spending energy on noise that cannot travel. Warning, if it happens, happens by stillness, by the sudden freezing of every wader at once when the perimeter trench is occupied, a silence inside a silence.

The second refusal is the light-behavior of certain leaves. A few of the strap-plants, only those rooted nearest the warmest rock, carry a faint paler vein on the underside. In the dim it is easy to think this is moisture. It is not. If you still yourself and let your eyes adjust past the gray-gold of the throat, the veins hold a low, steady sheen, not a glow you could read by, more like the memory of a glow. Insects that feed at night outside do not come in for it. The moth-fliers do. They land preferentially on those blades, and the climbers know this, and so the paler veins are also hunting-grounds, and have been long enough that the blades beneath the busiest veins are more tattered than their neighbors. The plant does not seem harmed. It sets the same dull flowers. Whatever the sheen is, it is ordinary here, a property of heat and chemistry and a leaf that has lived a long time against warm stone. It does not spread to the creepers. It does not appear on the vines. It stays where the rock is kindest.

The third is the water's taste, if you were to touch it to your mouth, which the waders do not do and the flat shapes in the dishes do not need to. The pockets are drinkable in the sense that they are water, and not in the sense that you would want a second mouthful. Mineral, warm, a little sweet from the split fruit that falls into them and dissolves, a little bitter from the leaf-mat. Animals that need freer water use the film on the walls, licking upward, or they take it from the surface of leaves before it falls. The dishes belong to the flat shapes and to whatever drowns. This division is stable. Nothing in the chamber is currently renegotiating it.

Processes run without interrupting one another. A bead forms, travels, falls, is absorbed or joins a pocket. A moth feeds, is taken, is replaced by another drifting in from the braid. The bulk completes its circuit and rests where the rib-curve lies, not on it, beside it, as if the bone were a marker it recognizes and does not disturb. Fruit splits. The smell sweetens and fades. At the throat, the light smears and unsmears as clouds pass far above, and the waders adjust their freezing-places by a handspan, no more. Heat does not spike. It does not drop. The mats grow at the rate of mats. A new cup will not be cut. An old cup will hold its skin of water until the film finds a lower path, and then it will dry, and lichen will take the rim, and the alignment will remain, which is all the alignment has left to do.

If you stayed, the room would not perform for you. It would continue. That is the settled fact of it. The chamber has already had its catastrophes, whatever cut the cups, whatever left the rib, whatever sagged the vault. Those are over. What remains is a hot, dim, small interior, dense with quiet lives, damp in its own measure, lush in layers rather than in height, active in every process that does not require a witness. The throat breathes. The beads let go. Somewhere in the braid a climber turns its head, and does not call, and the other does not answer, and the agreement holds.
