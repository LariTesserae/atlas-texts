The place is a cup. Not a valley yet—too new for that—only a sharp-sided hollow near the top of a ridge that has not had time to soften. The rock is pale, almost chalky, and its edges still cut. Nothing here has been rounded by centuries. The walls rise in clean planes, and between them the floor is a shallow bowl no wider than a small town, ringed so that you can see the far rim from anywhere if the mist thins. It rarely thins.

Cool air sits in the cup and does not leave. Moisture does not fall so much as arrive. Some mornings a fine rain walks across the rim and is gone before it wets the center. More often the damp simply condenses: on stone, on the undersides of leaves, in the mouths of pots left out overnight. There is no river. Water lives in pockets—basins the people have cut, natural cups where two planes of rock meet, and the swollen joints of the plants, which hold more than they should. Walk the lanes and your sleeves darken. Stand still and a drop finds the back of your neck. The ground itself stays only damp, never mud, because the young stone drinks little and sheds the rest into the channels.

The channels are the town’s spine. They are hand-cut, narrow, and exact, running from the high walls inward to a ring of cisterns around a central court. Primitive work: stone wedges, fire, braided fiber, a little soft metal for the edges that must stay true. No wheels worth the name. Loads move on poles and on the backs of whatever will consent to carry them. The buildings follow the channels rather than a grid. Low rooms of fitted block, roofs of overlapping slate and living thatch, doors set deep so the dim does not quite enter. It is a settlement, organized, layered where one house has been built against the shoulder of another, but it does not crowd. Between structures there is open rock, deliberately left. You can cross the cup in a short walk and still feel, between one doorway and the next, a stretch of emptiness the builders refused to fill.

Light never quite commits. The rim cuts the sky to a band, and the mist takes the rest. What reaches the floor is gray-green and directionless, stronger on the upper terraces, weaker in the court, where the cisterns hold a dull shine and give nothing back. Lamps are rare and small—fat burned in shallow dishes—and even those seem apologetic. People work by the hour the mist is thinnest, which is not bright, only less absent.

The vegetation has no interest in the emptiness the builders kept. It takes every joint, every unused ledge, every roof that is not scraped weekly. Ferns the size of doors. Vines with leaves like oiled leather, cool to the touch, beaded even at midday. Moss in shelves thick enough to sit on, though sitting sinks you. The growth is lush to the point of excess, and yet it does not make a forest. It makes rooms: a grove against the north wall, a hanging garden on a fracture that split last season and has not weathered, a single tree—if it is a tree—whose trunk is a braid of younger stems, standing alone in a court the size of a chapel, its canopy touching all four roofs and nowhere else. Between these presences the rock shows, sharp and almost new. Soil exists only where roots have made it.

The quiet is the first wrongness, and it holds. Wind moves the high leaves and does not speak. Water in the channels makes a sound so small you notice it only when it stops, which it does, for an hour at a time, when the cisterns are balanced and nothing feeds them. People talk low, and not often outdoors. Tools knock, then don’t. The town has learned the cup’s manner.

Against that quiet the life is almost indecent in its number.

They are everywhere, and they do not announce themselves. On the moss-shelves, pale grazers no higher than a knee, too many for the forage you can see, moving in slow files along routes that are not paths. Their mouths work. Their feet make no report on the stone. In the cisterns, things like segmented leaves drift and occasionally flex; they are not leaves. The hanging gardens crawl—literally—with a layer of small bodies the color of the vines, so that a wall appears to breathe when you are not looking at it directly, and goes still when you are. Moth-sized fliers fill the dim in drifts. They do not beat. They tilt, and the air carries them from roof to rim and back. At the tree in the court, what might be birds roost so thickly the braid of the trunk is thickened again by their weight, and not one of them calls. When they leave, they leave all at once, a silent unburdening, and the canopy springs.

Nothing here seems to hunt in the way lowland animals hunt. Or if it does, the method is patient past noticing. You will see a grazer stop, not in fear, and a vine-tip rest against its flank, and by evening the grazer is less, and the vine is not. You will see the segmented things in the cisterns gather at a channel-mouth when the water resumes, and the water that continues downstream is clearer, and something that was in it is not. The town’s rules are built around this. Channels are swept at the joints where the small climbers congregate, not to kill them—killing is loud, and also useless—but to keep a lane. Doors have sills raised a hand’s breadth, because what walks the rock at night is numerous and low and does not turn aside. Roofs are scraped because a week of neglect becomes a habitat, and a habitat becomes a weight. Children are taught early which moss sinks and which holds, which flier-drift can be walked through and which must be waited out. The waiting is never long. The drifts have errands.

The place is active in every register except sound. Moisture arrives, pools, is taken, is released. Leaves unfurl on a schedule you can almost catch if you sit: a frond at the north grove opens while you watch, not dramatically, simply done by the time you have finished a thought. Grazers file, pause, file. The cistern-things flex and clarify. A fracture in the east wall, still sharp, still shedding grains when the temperature drops a little further in the late day, grows a new lip of moss between one dawn and the next. The builders mark such lips. Young stone is still deciding what it is, and the plants are helping it decide, and the animals are helping the plants, and the town is the thin agreement that certain lines—the channels, the sills, the court around the braided tree—remain legible.

At the center the tree does something the rest of the cup only imitates. Its bark, if bark is the word, beads continuously. The beads do not fall. They travel, slow as the grazers, along the braid and out the leaves, and at the leaf-tips they hang until a flier tilts into them and carries one away, unbroken, toward the rim. People collect the ones that drop, which happens rarely, when a leaf is jostled by the weight of roosting things. The beads are drinkable. They taste of cold stone and green. No one has decided whether the tree is producing water or only organizing the damp the cup already has. It does not matter for use. It matters for the feeling, which is that the town is not the organizer here. The town is a guest that has learned the house rules, and the house is still being built, sharply, quietly, by everything that moves and does not speak.

Toward evening the mist lowers until the rim is gone and the cup is a room. The lamps come on, few and small. The files of grazers continue, visible as paler streaks against pale rock. The wall-crawl subsides into a texture. Somewhere a channel starts again, a thread of sound, and stops. If you stand in the court and look up through the braid, the fliers are a slow weather of their own, carrying beads you cannot see toward a ridge you cannot see, and the only proof of their numbers is that the dim is never empty. It is only quiet, and full, and cool, and not yet old.
