The floor is already warm before it is finished.

You feel it through the soles first: a low, even heat, not the strike of sun but the stored heat of something fired and not yet allowed to cool. The air above it is thick enough to lean on. It tastes of mineral steam and of clay that has been wetted, dried, wetted again, and never quite committed to either state. Light comes from nowhere you can name. It is the color of the inside of a kiln after the door has been shut—amber thinned by vapor, enough to show edges, not enough to show distance. Distance, anyway, is one of the things still being decided.

Everything underfoot has been made. There is no bedrock showing through, no pocket of unworked ground, no bank where the world forgot itself and left a raw face. The plain—if plain is the word for a surface that sometimes refuses to stay a surface—is a pavement of hand-set units, none of them alike for long. Some are bricks the length of a forearm, pressed with a grain that looks like finger-drag through wet slip. Some are slabs of dark glass, volcanic in the way glass is volcanic, but sawn, or rather worried, into rough polygons and bedded in a mortar that never skins over. Some are courses of tamped earth, banded pale and rust, each band the width of a single tamp, the tamp-marks still crisp, as if the mallet has only just lifted and the next blow is late. Between these, and under them, and in places instead of them, water stands. Not in channels. In the joints. A dark line that beads, swells, and runs nowhere, because the fall has not been graded yet, or has been graded and then ungraded in the same hour.

The water is frequent and unconvincing. It gathers on the new faces the moment a face exists, a film bright as oil and thin as breath, and it weeps from stones that were dry when you looked away. It condenses out of the heat itself, as if the air were a sponge being wrung by a hand you cannot find. Where it pools it is only finger-deep, and the bottom of every pool is another floor, older by a course or two, still warm, the mortar in it soft enough to take a print and then close over the print before you have finished lifting your foot. Drink it and it would be hot and sweet with lime and bitter with whatever metal is bleeding from the simple fittings—pegs of hammered copper gone green, pins of iron already orange at the edges, lashings of fiber that have no plant to have come from and that steam, slowly, into nothing. No one drinks it. There is no one.

The works stand far apart. That is the first mercy and the first unease: the vastness is mostly interval. You can walk until the heat makes a private weather around your head—shimmer, a small rain that falls upward off your scalp, a thunder you feel in the teeth and do not hear—and still the next structure is only a darker clot in the vapor, no larger than when you started. A wall, perhaps. It runs for a distance that your legs believe and your eyes dispute, courses of basalt and brick in a bond no tradition would claim, because the bond changes midway without a break in the work. The top of the wall is unfinished in the specific way of a job left at the end of a day: a toothed edge, stones waiting in a line to be lifted, a string-line sagging between two pegs. The string is wet. The pegs are warm. No foot has darkened the mortar. When you come back along the wall, the toothed edge has become a different tooth, and one of the waiting stones is now three stones, or the same stone remembered badly, occupying its place and the place beside it without overlapping, which is a thing stone should not do and does here without sound.

Sound is the other scarcity. The turbulence does not announce itself. A course settles and the air takes the noise away, or the noise was never produced, only the consequence: dust lifting in a sheet and hanging, then settling upward onto a surface that was not there, bedding itself as if it had always been the next layer. Far off, something gives—the long soft complain of a span finding it has no abutment—and the complain stops halfway, not fading, stopping, as if the rest of the sound had been used for something else. Mostly there is the kiln-hush, the pressure of hot wet air, and a tick, irregular, like cooling ceramic, except the ticks sometimes precede the crack and sometimes belong to a crack in a wall you have not reached and will not, because between you and it the floor is revising its count of steps.

Nothing grows. You keep looking for the exception, the way a person looks for weeds in a yard, and the yard does not permit the category. No moss in the joints, though the joints are wet enough. No film of green on the standing water. What you take at first for a stain is sinter: mineral grown in feathers and rinds, white and rust and a black like cooled slag, layered in the drip-lines under every coping. It looks organic until you break a piece. Inside it is banded like agate and gritty as poorly ground grout, and it crumbles warm in the hand, and the crumbs, set down, begin very slowly to align with the nearest joint, as if even the waste still knows the bond. There are no tracks. There are no shells, no castings, no coughed pellets, no feathers worked into the mortar by accident. The fiber in the lashings, examined, is a twist of drawn glass, fine as hair, made by pulling, the way a person pulls taffy or a thread from a hot lump. Someone, or something with the patience of someone, drew it. The twist is uneven. A hand did that. The hand is not here, and was perhaps never a hand.

The tools remain, which is worse than ruins, because ruins imply an afterward and this is not after. Mallets of dense stone, handles worn where a grip would go, the wear not quite the shape of fingers—slightly too long in the span, slightly too shallow in the cup. Chisels of smelted metal so simple the smelt shows: bubbles in the cheek of the blade, a bend where the quench took badly, edges redressed with a harder stone until the bevel is a scalloped record of effort. Wooden—no. Not wooden. Handles of fired clay, hollow, wrapped in the glass thread where a palm would sweat. They lie where a worker sets a tool down without looking: on the last course, in the angle of a corner, beside a pile of unsized rubble that is itself half-sorted, the sorting abandoned mid-gesture. The piles are warm. Some of them are warmer at the top than at the bottom, which means the work is still arriving, stone by stone, from a direction that is not up and not along the ground. You watch a lump of dark glass ease out of the vapor at about the height of a shoulder and settle onto a pile with the softness of a thing being put down, not dropped. No arm. The vapor closes. The lump clicks once, cooling, and is thereafter only a lump.

Low as it is, the place has weather, and the weather is architectural. The steam does not drift so much as circulate in slow cells the size of districts, each cell with its own failed light and its own temper of heat. In one, the air is clear enough to see a rank of piers diminishing—round in section, built up of drums, each drum a single turned or ground piece, imperfectly circular, seated on lead pads that have not yet been poured, so the drums hover a finger's width above their marks, obedient to a placement that gravity has been asked to wait on. Gravity, locally, waits. In the next cell the same piers, or their continuation, have slumped: not fallen, slumped, the stone gone briefly willing, like wax near a flame, and then remembered itself and frozen mid-bow. The bowed pier is still a pier. It carries nothing. What it was meant to carry is visible overhead as a faint grid, a soffit of undried clay hanging in the steam, sagging between lines that have not been struck, dripping a grey water that prints the floor in perfect rectangles and then is blotted up by the floor, which uses it.

The sky is the largest unfinished work, and it is low enough that on the hotter reaches you can put a palm to it. It is a lid. Not stone. A continuous screed of clay and ash and powdered glass, troweled from beneath—you can see the arc of the trowel-strokes, overlapping, hurried, some of them crossed by a later stroke that tried to correct the fall and made it worse. It has not been fired except where it has. Those places are pale scars, crazed with heat-fracture, and from the fractures small panes loosen and fall without hurry, turning once, and land on edge, and are, by the time you reach them, already stacked in a neat bond at the foot of nothing, seven high, the stack damp, the top brick carrying a single chisel-mark as if to say this one is faced, this one is ready, take it. Take it where. The stack does not care. If you lift the brick it is almost too hot, and lighter than its size, and in your hands it is both one brick and the place the brick will occupy, a brief double exposure that makes your wrists ache with the effort of holding a location. Set it down anywhere but the stack and the floor accepts it with a wet sound and begins, quietly, to cut a joint around it, scoring itself to match, eager, wrong, because you have interrupted a count.

This is the turbulence, once you learn to see it: not storm, not upheaval, a constant small disobedience of sequence. Causes arrive after their effects, or beside them, or not at all. A wall unbuilds from the top in silence, each course becoming the pile it was taken from, the pile then becoming the wall again a few spans to the left, where the foundation has just decided to exist—a trench of wet darker than the wet around it, squared by a line snapped in red oxide, the oxide still bright, the snapping of the line audible only as a change in the pressure on your eardrums. You can stand in the trench. The water there is hotter, near to scalding, and it moves with a purpose that is not current: it sorts. Fines drift one way, grit the other, until the bottom is a bed fit to lay stone on, and then a stone is there, bedded, and you did not see it bedded, you only notice that your ankles are now against it and the trench has become a course and you are standing in the work, which continues around you without discourtesy and without pause, laying the next unit through the space your body occupies and also not through it, a polite contradiction, the masonry knitting on the far side of you as if you were a temporary opening left for a pipe that will not be installed.

There are rules, and they are the rules of a site, not of a country. Joints must break bond, except where the bond is the thing being revised, in which case a stack bond runs for a hundred paces like a dare and then remembers and staggers itself all at once, every other unit shoving sideways with a soft collective grunt of stone on stone, the only loud sound you will hear all day, and then quiet again, and the wall straighter, and your ears ringing with a noise that already feels historical. Water must be kept out of the work, and so the work drinks it, constantly, a maintenance without maintainers. Heat must be managed, and so the kilns—low domes, far apart, each one a hill you could walk around in an hour if the ground would stay—burn with no feed hole you can find and no smoke, only a seam at the springing of the dome that breathes. Put a hand near the seam and the air pushes out, furnace-dry for a heartbeat, then sucks, and on the suck it takes the moisture off your skin so fast the skin tightens. Inside, if you find a crack wide enough to look, there is no fire. There is light the color of the sky's pale scars, and shelves, and on the shelves greenware: vessels, tiles, blocks with holes for pegs, all of them simple, all of them slightly failed, warping as you watch, and some of them succeeding backward, slumping into better forms than the ones they were pinched into. No potter's thumbprint. In its place, a dent like the dent a thumb would leave if the thumb were made of the same clay and had drawn back into the wall.

At the scale of the day you can believe it is a site, abandoned mid-task by a people who used only what a hand can lift and a pit-fire can harden. At the scale of the walk the belief fails. There is no edge to the works. The vapor never opens onto wild country. Every horizon, when a cell of weather briefly thins, is another rank of unfinished things at the same stage of unfinished: walls at shoulder height, floors poured to the day-joint and stopped, stairs that climb four rises and end in a tread still soft enough to take the print of rain that is not falling. The stairs are the clearest violation. They ascend, and the ascent does not gain elevation. You climb, the heat thickens, the sky-lid brushes your hair with a wet clay kiss, and when you look back the floor you left is level with the tread under you, and the four rises are still four rises, and also a single continuous ramp, and also a stack of treads not yet assembled, leaning where a laborer would lean them. All three are true in sequence too quick to separate. Your knees report the climb anyway. The body keeps accounts the place has not opened.

Night, if it is night, is only a dimming of the dim, a shift in the steam from amber to the red of a covered coal. Nothing hunts. Nothing settles to sleep, because nothing is alive to sleep, and the work does not keep hours. In the redder hours the revisions come faster and smaller: a joint widens, a peg oxidizes through its whole thickness and powders, the powder lifts and re-compacts as a new peg of slightly different alloy, still primitive, still full of slag, still adequate. You can hear, then, if you hold still, a myriad of tiny actions at the edge of hearing—the sift of fines, the tick of contraction, the kiss of a film of water closing over a fresh face—and none of them add up to a voice. The quiet is not emptiness. It is the sound of a vast intention operating below the threshold where intention becomes noise.

Toward what you decide to call morning, a section of the sky fires. There is no warning beyond a change in the taste of the air, metal brighter, lime sharper. A pane of the lid goes pale, cracks in a map like dried mud, and lets down a harder light, white and brief, and in that light you see the true scatter of the place: not a city, not a ruin, a continental floor of made ground, sparse with works, each work separated by walks of an hour or a day, the walks themselves paved, drained, re-drained, the water writing and erasing its thin maps. The light lasts long enough to show you that the works are all at the same age—embryonic, toothed, waiting on the next unit—and that this sameness is not neglect but phase. The whole floor is a single pour that has not reached its set. You are standing in the middle of a thing the size of a weather system, and the thing is a floor, and the floor is being laid by a method no more complex than tamp, bed, butter, lay, and the method is occurring everywhere at once, including in the moment of light, including in you, in the sense that the light finds the moisture on your skin and begins, very gently, to score a joint along the line where your shadow should be. Your shadow is late. It arrives after the scoring, and lies down in the groove prepared for it, and is warm, and is the same substance as the mortar, and when the pale pane overhead fails and drops in shards that stack themselves, the shadow remains a moment longer than the light, a dark damp course in the pavement, fitted, broken bond, correct.

Then the steam closes, the amber returns, and the course that was your shadow is only another joint weeping hot water, indistinguishable, already being revised. Somewhere a mallet rests in its print. Somewhere a lump of glass is set down by no arm. The floor under you considers, softly, whether to be the floor, and for now it is, and the heat does not lift, and there is no wind, and the next work is no nearer, and the water beads on every new face the moment the face consents to exist.
