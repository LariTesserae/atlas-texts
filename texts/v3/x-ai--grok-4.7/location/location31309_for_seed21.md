The place is smaller than the sky it sits in. From outside you would miss it: a notch in a young ridge, no wider than a cathedral nave is long, walled on three sides by rock that still looks poured. The fourth side is open to a drop, but the drop does not behave like air. A skin hangs there, faintly brighter than the day, and anything that crosses it — a seed, a thread of mist, the last of a sound — arrives changed or does not arrive at all.

Inside, the stone is hot under the hand even in what passes for shade. The heat is not the heat of sun on a southern face. It comes up through the joints, a low, even warmth, as if the ridge had not finished being born and the birth-heat had nowhere else to go. The faces are sharp. Edges cut. There is almost no grit, no talus apron, no softened lip where weather has had centuries to work. Fresh fractures show mineral the color of wet iron and, in places, a pale rind like cooled glass. Water stands in those fractures without having fallen. It beads, gathers, and lets go. The rock weeps on a schedule that is not quite the sun’s.

The air inside the notch is thick. Outside, at this height, breath should come thin and cold; here it is close, sweet-metal, and damp enough that cloth never quite dries. Condensation finds every surface a few degrees under the air’s temperature: the undersides of leaves, the lee of a stone, the inner curve of anything left standing. Droplets fatten, tremble, and fall. That is most of the sound. A spaced ticking, then a softer answer from the shallow pools that never grow into a stream because the stone drinks them back as fast as it gives them up. Between drops the notch is quiet enough that you hear your own eyelids.

The plants do not make a thicket. There are not many of them — a scatter, each with room to be alone — but each one is lavish. Broad leaves, almost black-green, held out flat as if to bargain with the light. Stems the color of new copper, jointed, and warm if you touch them near the base. They root in almost nothing: a fan of pale threads forced into a crack, drinking the weep-water before it can run. Soil, where it exists, is a dark paste the plants seem to have made themselves, heaped at their own feet and nowhere else. Between them the pavement is bare, hot, and sharp, with space enough to walk without brushing a leaf. The lushness is local. The emptiness is the rule.

A few things were put here, or grew here in the way made things sometimes do. None of them form a settlement. Near the open side, three posts stand in a loose arc, chest-high, matte, not quite the color of the rock and not quite refusing it. They have no seams you can trust. At their bases the stone has flowed a little and then stopped, as if it tried to swallow them while it was still soft and lost interest. One post is canted. From it a filament no thicker than a hair runs to the condensation-skin and disappears into it without a fastening. When the light is ordinary — and the light here is ordinary, noon-clear, cloud-shadowed when cloud happens to cross — the filament throws a shadow that falls the wrong way for an hour and then corrects itself without anyone seeing the moment of correction.

Farther in, against the back wall, a partial floor: hexagonal plates, some missing, some lifted by a root or by the ridge’s slow rise. They are warm like everything else, but they do not weep. Water that lands on them slides to the cracks and is gone, not downward in any way you can follow. A low bench of the same material sits half-fused to the plates. Sitting on it changes nothing you can name except the drip-rhythm in your ears, which slows, as if the bowl had leaned a fraction toward listening.

Nothing hunts here in any way you would recognize. Fauna is a rumor with a few proofs. In the leaf-cups, which fill and empty all day, a pale thread of an animal — neither worm nor insect, jointed only at the head — drinks and then pours itself into the stem, and is gone until the cup fills again. Higher, in the ordinary sky beyond the skin, birds turn and do not cross. Once, at the lip, a moth-sized thing hovered with wings that did not beat so much as hold a shape against the thick air, then folded into a droplet and fell. It did not break on the stone. It soaked in.

The place is not still. It only refuses noise. Through the day the weep shifts from the east wall to the south as whatever keeps the air dense drifts a few degrees inside its fence. Leaves tilt, not together. A joint in the back wall ticks as it opens another hair; dust that is not dust — mineral, bright, new — sifts out and is beaded over before it can settle. At dusk the condensation-skin thickens until the drop beyond it is only a rumor of colder blue. The pools on the plates rise a finger’s depth and no more. The posts warm, or the hand decides they have. Somewhere under the notch a process continues that the ridge has not explained: heat arriving, water arriving, both spending themselves, neither flooding the bowl nor leaving it.

There are rules, if you stay long enough to stop calling them moods.

Sound does not cross the open side. A word spoken toward the skin comes back smaller, late, and stripped of its consonants. A word spoken at the back wall goes nowhere at all; the leaves take it.

Water will climb a fresh joint if the joint points at a post, and will refuse a slope that should have carried it to the lip. Following a trickle is a way to get lost inside a place too small to be lost in.

The plants do not like to be shaded by a standing body for long. A leaf that stays in your shadow past a certain number of drips folds, not in distress so much as in bookkeeping, and the stem under it cools. Step away and it opens on the next cycle, wetter than before.

The ridge is still rising. Not as a drama — a pressure in the soles, a plate that was flush at morning sitting proud by afternoon, a new edge somewhere you had already mapped. Sleep here and you wake with the bench a little higher relative to the weep-line, and with the sense that the notch has rehearsed your absence without mentioning it.

At full day the light is simply light: hard, high, able to show every unsoftened edge. It does not sanctify the bowl or darken it. What makes the place strange is not a wrongness in the sun but the way ordinary sun is spent — on wet new stone, on a few extravagant plants standing far apart, on instruments that keep a hot, damp room standing open to a cold altitude and do not explain the expense. You can see the whole of it from the bench. That is the scale. A grove with walls. A machine with weather. A birth still bleeding, quietly, into its own shadow.
