The basin does not announce itself with a horizon. What arrives first is noise, and the noise is already the size of a valley.

It is not one animal. It is a weather of animals: clicks, wet bellows, wingbeats like sheets shaken in a closed room, the slap of something heavy turning in water you cannot see. After a short while the separate sounds stop being separable. They mill at about the height of the canopy and do not escape upward. The air is warm enough that cloth does not cool you. It only grows heavier. Condensation beads on the broad leaves and falls again, a second rain that outlasts the first. True rain does come—short, warm, loud—and then quits, and the dripping goes on.

Underfoot the ground is new. You feel it in the edges. The rock has not learned roundness. Ribs of basalt, glassy where a flow cooled fast, still sharp enough to split a root that grew over them last season. The leaf mold is thick but young: a felt of this year’s falls, not a century of soil. Roots run on the surface because they have not had time to go down. Between the ribs the stone is porous and drinks. That is why the place is damp rather than flooded. Water arrives, shouts, and vanishes into the rock, leaving only the hollows, the hoof-prints, the cupped leaves, and a thread of stream that runs a hundred paces where the flow meets an uncracked sill and then sinks. Kneel—though kneeling is a mistake, because contact gets investigated—and the rock against the skin is faintly hotter than the air. Warmth pools here. The basin is a new low, and the tear is not finished cooling.

Light fails for ordinary reasons stacked on one another. The trees are not old-forest tall. They are fast, soft-wooded, crowded, many of them still thin, but there are so many layers that the day cannot get through. What light there is has the color of the inside of a closed hand. Now and then something large moves and the canopy tears for a second. A dull rust-gray shows, low cloud or fine ash or both, a sky that seems to have weather in it permanently. Then the gap seals with leaves and wings. Time is not told by brightness. The animals tell it, in surges.

The vegetation is almost rude. Lush, here, does not mean a garden. It means you cannot choose a path. The path is whatever last crashed through, and it is already closing. Vines with skins like wet leather. Ferns the size of doors growing out of cracks that still smell faintly of sulfur if you put your face near the rock. Moss in shelves. Flowers that open and shut on the timescale of the squalls, not the day: one unfurls while you stand, cream and dull red, and by the next rain it has set seed or been eaten. Everything is edible to something, and something is always eating. New shoots come up in torn ground before the drip from the last collision has slowed.

The fauna is the medium, not a feature of it. Density here means touching. A column of small dark fliers—feathered arms, naked faces, not quite birds—pours along a game-trail at knee height, and for three or four heartbeats the ones in the middle are walking on the backs of the ones below, until the column kinks around a rock spine and thins. Insects move in sheets. Something centipede-long and the color of wet bark flows over a boot without seeming to notice that a boot is different from a log. The larger bodies you hear more than see. A coughing call travels, answered from three directions at once, so you cannot tell which is the animal and which is the scarp giving the sound back.

The scarp is how you learn the shape of the place. A young fault-face, still bright with fracture, runs the length of the basin. Every call that hits it returns sharper. The valley argues with itself continuously. Deafening stops being a metaphor after the first hour. The ears give up sorting. What remains is pressure: a sudden drop means a clearing of wings overhead; a rise in the low register means the seep-field, where warm water beads out of the young rock and a thousand small throats drink and dispute the same puddles. You never see the far side of the seep-field. You hold it anyway, as a region of sound.

Other features declare themselves the same way. A cane-brake, hollow stems knocking when the gusts come down off the scarp—the only wind, and it comes in punches, not as a prevailing breeze. A sink line, a thread of lesser noise where the brief streams go under and the calling animals avoid it, so that quiet, relative quiet, reads as a road. Low ridges of flow-rock, ankle-high to shoulder-high, still roped with the patterns of cooling. Each ridge carries a slightly different leaf and a slightly different argument. You walk, and the basin does not end. The scarp stays on the left, learned by echo. You understand you are in something the size of a district, a town’s worth of ground if anyone had stayed to town it. No one has.

Nothing is built. Nothing remains as a structure long enough to be called one. The only technology is what can be carried in a mouth or a foot and dropped when the need passes. A heavy low animal, hide like cracked mud, too many legs or the right number moving too fast to count, pauses and strips a thorn-branch. It carries the branch sideways, using it to part vines rather than eating it. Others do the same. No lesson is visible. The behavior moves through the traffic the way sound moves, by proximity. Stones get turned—not stacked. Turned, so the sharp side is down and the flatter side is a brief standing-place in the mud. By whatever stands in for morning in this hand-closed light, growth has edged the stones again and the flats are gone. The basin does not keep tools. It keeps the habit of using them, distributed through too many bodies to count.

Turbulence is the rule, and it is not seasonal. A thicket that was a wall becomes a lane when a herd goes through at a trot that is almost panic and is not; it is only how they travel, shoulder to shoulder, because there is no room to travel otherwise. Behind them the thicket is wrecked. Smaller animals are already in the wreckage, eating what the herd shook loose, and the shoots are already up. Creation and destruction are the same minute. A branch that was never old enough to bear what landed on it fails, and the failure is absorbed into the noise before you have finished turning toward it. The canopy is the only cover, and it is alive, and it fails locally whenever the turbulence peaks.

The unusual thing, once you have stood in it long enough, is the age. The rock is younger than the habits on it. The land has not finished becoming land. The edges still cut. The stone still remembers fire. The life did not wait. It arrived at full density on a surface with no soil-memory, and it runs at a speed that keeps a soil from ever quite forming—everything eaten, dropped, rained on, and grown again before a profile can develop. Young ground. Noise with no season in it. All of it first season, sustained.

There are no fires, except the smell of hot stone where a seep runs warmer than the air, and the animals crowd that smell the way they crowd everything, and the crowding makes more heat. There are no roofs. If a wall were raised, the next herd or the next root-rush or the next warm rain would take it as food or footing or both. Stillness does not last long enough to put a wall up.

The far end of the basin, if there is a far end, is a lower roar under the near roar: the same arguments happening out of sight, slightly delayed, like a second congregation. The place is open in the sense that it continues. It is closed in the sense that you cannot see the continuation. You hear it.

If you stop moving, you are claimed as surface. Vines test you. A line of the small feathered things runs up an arm because an arm is the tallest available contour in a flat of fern. The sound does not exclude you. It indexes you. Breathing becomes one more rhythm in a field of rhythms, and within minutes you cannot hear your own breath as separate. That is the real darkness—not the failed light, but the moment your presence stops being a signal you can pick out of the basin’s continuous shout.
