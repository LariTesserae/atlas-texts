The bowl is smaller than it sounds from outside. You come up through a cut in the ridge—cool air already, and the stone underfoot worn smooth in the middle of the path, lipped with moss at the edges—and the land simply stops opening. A rim of weathered rock, no higher than a house, closes the place on three sides. On the fourth the ground falls away, not as a cliff you can see all at once but as a green lip where the last gardens lean over a drop that keeps its own weather. Inside that rim there is room for a town, and not much more. A cathedral’s worth of ground. A market’s worth. You can walk the long way around in the time it takes a pot to boil, if the lanes will let you.

They mostly will not.

Water is the first fact and the last. It does not arrive as a river so much as it refuses to leave. Seams in the old rock weep without drama, a steady dark shine on the cut faces, and every joint in the stone has been carrying that seep long enough that the channels have names. The soil never quite decides to be soil. Dig with a planting stick and the hole fills from the sides before your hand is out. Boards laid for a morning are silver by noon. The lanes are paved with split slabs, set edge-up in the old way so water can run between them, but the slabs have settled into the give of the ground and the runs have become a braid: here a rill clear enough to drink, here a black thread under a mat of leaves, here a pool no wider than a door that the children treat as a street. After rain—and rain is not an event so much as a change in volume—the whole floor of the bowl lifts a finger’s depth and the lowest houses stand in it like cattle, unbothered, their thresholds already built a step high for that reason.

Nothing here is only stone, and almost nothing is only timber. The plants took the substrate and then took the work. What you walk on is root-mat over peat over rock, springy, smelling of cold iron and crushed green. What you live inside was planted, bent, and kept. Willow and a tougher upland cane, jointed like bamboo but finer, have been trained for generations into walls: living poles set a hand’s breadth apart, woven while green, thickened by their own growth until a house-side is a hedge you can lean a ladder on. Thatch is not a roof laid on; it is the last year’s growth of the same stands, cut, bundled, and pegged back onto the living frame, so a street reads as a single organism with doors cut into it. Gutters are split cane. Fences are the uncleared edge of someone’s plot, told from the wild only by the way the stems have been turned to run parallel. Bridges over the deeper rills are roots that were guided across when they were thumb-thick and have since swollen into railings with a shine on them from hands. The oldest of these have swallowed the stone posts that first held them. You can still see a corner of dressed rock in one, gray and rounded, like a tooth in a gum.

The light is ordinary daylight, and that is almost the strange part, because so little of it arrives unedited. It comes over the rim in a clean cool sheet and then spends itself in layers. Upper leaves take the white of it. Under them the lanes run in a green dusk even at noon, broken where a household has pollarded a stand to let a work-yard through, or where the market opening was kept clear by the simple method of cutting anything that tried to close it. Steam from wet thatch, vapor off the seep-faces, breath of animals: the air is thick enough to see in shafts, not fog, just the constant exhalation of a place that is mostly alive and mostly damp. It is cool enough that metal, where anyone has it, beads. Cool enough that people work in wool even when the sun is on the rim. Not cold. The water does not ice. Hands go red and stay useful.

The town is a town. That is the other fact that takes a while to believe, because the material is all grove. There are wards. The upper terrace, drier by a finger’s depth and no more, holds the drying-racks and the looms, where cane splits pale and where wool is beaten outside because inside it would never finish. The middle band is houses, packed until eaves touch and until the gap between two living walls is a slot you turn sideways to pass, each slot worn shiny at shoulder height. The lower band, where the bowl’s water gathers before it finds the lip and goes over in a fan of small falls, is the wet market and the animal ground, and it is there that the place stops being a picture and becomes a noise.

Fauna is not a border here. It is the occupancy. The cane holds nest-hollows at every height a bird can argue for, and they do argue: a racketing that starts before the light and does not so much end as change register. Finches in the thatch, larger darker birds on the rim-rocks, waders in the lower rills that stalk through people’s ankles as if the ankles were reed. Insects in clouds that move like weather of their own—midges over the black water, larger flies at the fish-drying lines, a high thin whine in the shade that you stop hearing only when you leave. Frogs own the night and a good part of the day; their calling is structural, a floor under the other sounds, and when something startles them the whole lower town goes silent for one breath and then resumes as if embarrassed. Livestock is folded into the plan rather than pastured beyond it. There is no beyond, not within the rim. Goats stand on the living roofs because the roofs bear them and because the upper leaves are fodder; their bells are the town’s clock, tuned by accident into a clang that never quite agrees with itself. Ducks patrol the paved rills with the entitlement of officials. A few thick-haired cattle are kept in byres whose walls are the same trained cane, and their shifting, their coughs, their long splashing drinks travel through the water under the lanes so that a house three streets away feels them in the floor. Dogs, rats, a small deer that comes over the rim at dusk and is shouted at and sometimes caught and sometimes not. The air has a protein smell under the green: wet wool, guano, fish, milk, the sweet rot of leaves that never dry.

Sound is not an atmosphere. It is traffic. The falls at the lip run all day and thicken when the upper seams are giving hard, a white rush you can navigate by—louder on the west lanes, softer where a house-row baffles it. Over that, the market: wood on wood, cane on stone, voices pitched to clear both, the slap of fish, the argument of goats, a mallet somewhere splitting material for a new wall-pole. Children run the slots at full speed because the slots are short and because everyone else is carrying something. Work-songs start and lose their place and start again. When rain increases, the thatch takes the drops and multiplies them into a hiss that covers speech, and people move closer and shout, and the birds shout back, and for a while the town is only volume. Then the rain eases by a degree—not stops; eases—and individual sounds return one by one, as if remembered: a bell, a frog, a woman calling a name that is also the name of a lane.

None of this is coming apart. That is the balance under the turbulence. The rock has been this shape longer than the town, and the town has been this shape long enough that the rock has accepted it. Paths are not invented each morning; they are ruts in the root-mat, polished, with puddles in the same places, and the puddles have their moss. The seep-channels were cut once, by hand, with stone and fire and later with a little iron, and since then they have only been cleared, never redesigned. A house that fails is not demolished so much as harvested: the living poles are cut back, the thatch is stripped for mulch and for new roofs, and the plot is replanted to the same plan, because the plan is the water’s plan and the water does not negotiate. Tools are what hands can keep sharp in this damp—bone, hardened wood, a few iron blades passed around a ward and complained about. Joints are lashing and pegs. Nothing is machined. Everything is mended in public, in the lanes, while you are trying to pass, which is why passage is a negotiation and why the town feels larger than its measure.

By late day the rim takes the sun and the bowl does not. The upper leaves go briefly gold. Below them the lanes are already evening, loud as ever, wet as ever, the animals settling by layers: goats down off the roofs, ducks into the lower pools, birds into the cane with a last indignant surge, frogs rising to fill whatever quiet the birds leave. Cooking smoke tries to climb and mostly fails, wandering the slots at head height, caught in the damp. On the lip, if you can reach it through the last gardens—leaf-houses, those, too, beans and a bitter green trained on the same cane, glistening, dripping onto the path—you can look out and see the drop take the town’s water in a dozen threads and lose it in trees far below. Behind you the bowl roars softly, a contained weather. The boundary is the rim and the rooted walls and the habits of clearance. Inside it, nothing holds still, and nothing has needed to, for a very long time.
