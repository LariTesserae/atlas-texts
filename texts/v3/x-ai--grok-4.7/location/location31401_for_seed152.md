The ground that leads to it is new enough to still remember water. A low warm plain, barely lifted above the river that laid it down, holds the last signatures of that laying: ripple-marks pressed into silt, little curved seams like the inside of a shell, grains not yet sorted by anything but the current that dropped them. Nothing has cut a rill. Rain, when it comes, finds the surface too young to know how to fail. It beads, films, and leaves. Between rains the plain goes pale and firm, warm under a dim sky, with long reaches of almost nothing—scattered tussocks, a shrub with its own circle of bare crust, the wide polite distances of a place that has not had time to fill in.

You would miss the pocket if you were not already looking down. It sits inside a bend the river abandoned in the same season, a single hollow no larger than a courtyard, sunk a man’s height below the new terrace and closed on three sides by a loose screen of broad leaves. The fourth side is only air and a lip of crust. From the plain it reads as a darker green crease. Step through the leaves and the scale changes. The world becomes a room.

Light arrives already used. The canopy is not closed—there is space between every stem, sky in pieces—but the leaves are large and waxy, and the air under them is thick enough to soften edges. What light gets in is green-gold and low, more a condition than a source. Shadows do not sharpen. Heat holds without glare. The temperature of the hollow is the temperature of breath in a shut mouth: warm, even, a little wet on the next inhale.

Water lives here the way a guest lives, not the way a resident does. No stream crosses the floor. Instead there are pockets: a lens the color of tea in a depression shaped like a heel, a film shining along the base of the earthen wall, droplets that gather under each leaf and fall once, hours apart, into the same small cups of moss. At dawn the silt skins over with condensation, a cool glaze that is gone by the time the dim light has fully entered. After a rain—and rain comes in short warm spells, then stays away—the hollow drinks at the edges and remains damp in its seams while the plain outside whitens. The smell is clay, crushed leaf, and a faint sweetness from somewhere inside the walls, like grain kept too long in a jar.

The plants are lush and still leave room. A few trees with pale trunks and leaves big enough to roof a shoulder. Ferns in clumps rather than carpets, each clump an island. Creepers that have begun their work and not finished it, so a stem crosses open air and stops. Between them the floor is visible: new silt, litter thin as a single season, mica flecks that catch the dimness and give it back as quiet sparks. Nothing crowds. The lavishness is in the leaf, the gloss, the speed of growth, not in the packing of bodies. You can walk the hollow—if walking is the word for so small a place—without brushing more than you choose.

Off center, where the floor rises into a low platform of the same silt, the hollow has begun to build.

It does not look, at first, like a town. It looks like a pale stump, unfinished, the color of dry bone though it is only clay and the spit that binds clay when small mouths work it. The surface is pellet on pellet, a masonry with no tool-mark, each grain set and not yet weathered, the corners soft because weather has not been introduced to them. Shafts open in the flanks, dark and exact. Warm air comes out of them in a thin continuous breath, the only wind the room reliably has. Around the main mass, lesser domes and low ridges mark out lanes of cleared crust. The plan is already a plan: approaches, thresholds, a layered bulk that is higher on one side where new courses are being raised, raw and darker, still damp from the making. It is a settlement in the first season of itself. Infrastructure before façades. Rooms before the habit of rooms.

There are no devices. Nothing extracted, smelted, fired, or geared. What stands was gathered—silt, cellulose, water, the cement of saliva—and set down again in order. Inside the openings the order continues, though a person sees only the lips of it: stacked chambers, one above another, their floors of combed material pale as bread and breathing their own wet heat; narrow roads packed harder than the surrounding crust; nurseries of a different, finer grain; vents aligned so that the hollow’s dim warmth is drawn through and tempered. The city is embryonic and already civic. It adds. It does not cut. Erosion, elsewhere the editor of land, has not been hired. The plain outside still wears its ripple-marks. The mound still wears its freshest pellets. Subtraction has not begun. This is a landscape in the tense of becoming, and the settlement is the part of that becoming that has learned repetition.

Life is everywhere and does not press. The builders move as a sparse glittering, a line along one cleared lane, a scatter of bodies on the new courses, never a mass that reads as swarm unless you kneel and insist. They are the town’s traffic and its weather and its masonry in the same gesture. Above them, in the loose canopy, other lives use the room without owning it. A skink on warm crust, then a long wait, then the same skink a body’s length on. Frogs in the tea-colored lenses, throats working, silent or near it. A snake in a loose coil under a fern, occupying less space than the fern. Moths at the shaft mouths when the light drops further, drinking the faint sweetness. Higher, a heron sometimes stands on the lip of the terrace and does not call; it watches the lenses, shifts its weight, and is a shape rather than an event. At the warm edge of night, bats stitch the open side of the hollow and are gone before their wings have finished the thought. Abundance here is a census, not a crowd. Many kinds, many small errands, and between each errand the room’s real acreage: air, bare silt, the distance a leaf keeps from the next leaf.

Sound has almost opted out. The plain outside can hold a far insect tick or the soft collapse of a drying crust; inside, even that is muffled by leaves and by the habit of the place. No chorus. The builders’ jaws work below the threshold of hearing. Wings pass and do not clap. The heron is a discipline. What remains is structural: the breath from the shafts, steady enough that after a while you stop calling it sound and start calling it the room; a single drip from a leaf into a lens, bright as a pin, then the long repair of quiet around it; sometimes, when rain begins, the first drops on waxy leaves—separate, countable—before the shower thickens into a hush of its own. The hollow can be active for hours and register, to an ear, as silence with a schedule.

The processes do not wait on anyone’s attention. While the light holds its dim green, a course of pellets rises by the width of a finger. A lens shrinks, its edge writing a tideline in darker silt. A fern crozier unrolls and changes the shadow under itself. The sweet air from the fungus combs inside the mound—those pale stacked gardens, humid, tended, the city’s stored summer—thickens or thins as the vents trade the hollow’s air for the chamber’s. When rain arrives it does not yet have the authority to gully. It darkens the mound, runs in films down slopes still too sealed and too new to give way, pools where the builders have not raised a lip, and is drawn off into the plain’s thirst or back into the shafts’ damp. By evening the films have pulled into droplets again. Through the warm night the work continues without lanterns, a town under construction by touch, and at dawn the condensation returns to the ripple-marks as if the river had leaned close and thought better of it.

The rules are simple because the place is young, and they may not last, but they hold now.

The land is added to before it is taken from. What arrives—silt, rain, leaf, the bodies of the builders—stays in new shapes rather than being carved into old ones. Water is intermittent and local: a guest of seams and lenses, never a master. Space is part of the design; lushness is not permission to close the gaps. Sound is what the processes can spare, which is almost nothing, so motion carries the news instead. Building is biological or it is not done. No blade, no fire, no wheel. The settlement grows by the same law as the terrace: gathering, binding, repeating, not yet wearing down. And the whole of it remains hidden—one room embedded in a larger dryness, easy to pass, already organized, still unfinished.

Stand on the lip at the dim end of the day and the plain stretches away, warm, low, barely scratched by shadow, its ripple-marks intact under a sky the color of tin. Behind you the leaves close the crease. Inside, the shafts breathe. A pellet is set. A lens holds the last of the light like a coin someone has not decided to spend. The town is not ancient. The land is not old. They are the same event, still in its first quiet tense, and the hollow keeps them as a room keeps a lamp: contained, dim, and working.
