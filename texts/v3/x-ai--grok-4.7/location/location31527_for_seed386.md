The heat arrives before the height does. You know you are high because the air is thin in the lungs and the horizon has that hard, lifted rim, but the thinness does not cool anything. It only makes the heat more personal. It sits against the skin the way a hand sits, close, slightly damp, and does not leave. Midday light is ordinary daylight, white-gold and unmysterious, and under it the whole shoulder of the plateau shines as if it had just been rained on, which it has, and will be again before the light changes.

The land is a terrace system the size of a valley you could walk across in a long day and still not finish. Old. Nothing here looks newly broken. The ridges have been rounded down to the shapes water prefers, and the water has agreed to stay. Rock weeps. Not in falls, mostly, but in sheets and threads: dark stone with a constant skin of moisture, pale travertine lips where mineral water has been laying itself down for so long the lips have become the landform. Between them the ground never quite decides to be soil. It is a sponge of root, peat, and fine silt, warm underfoot, giving a little when you step, then sealing over the print with a slow suck.

Vegetation takes every surface that will hold it and several that should not. Ferns crowd the seep-lines until the rock is only a rumor of gray between fronds. Cushions of moss the color of old brass swell over the gentler slopes, and out of them rise stunted trees with pale, smooth trunks, their crowns flattened by wind that is not blowing now but clearly has a habit. Leaves are large for the altitude, glossy, always beaded. Where a branch overhangs a drip, the drip has worn a dark streak down the bark, and tiny plants have colonized the streak as if it were a road. Bromeliads and their cousins cup water in the crotches of everything. The air between the plants is not empty. It is layered: flower, leaf-shadow, hanging root, a second canopy of epiphytes lower than the first, then the wet floor. You do not see far into it. You see into the next thickness, and then another.

The water is not a river so much as a condition. Pools the size of rooms sit in the old drainage, tea-colored, still enough to hold the sky, disturbed only when something below the surface adjusts. Channels no wider than a forearm thread between the cushions and disappear under mats of vegetation, reappearing ten paces on with the same unhurried voice. In the hottest hours the pools do not steam so much as the air above them thickens. Condensation films the undersides of leaves and falls again, a private rain that never needs a cloud. Higher on the shoulder, where the rock is more honest, the weeping becomes audible: a soft, continuous patter, as if the cliff were a large animal shaking itself dry and never finishing.

What passes for industry here has no builder. Along one flank the seeps have sorted themselves by chemistry. An iron apron stains the slope the color of dried blood and flakes into metallic curls that the rain rearranges but does not remove. Beside it a paler terrace steps down in shallow pans, each pan rimmed with a crust that grows a little each season, the way a pot grows scale. The crust is warm. Stand near it and you hear a faint ticking, gas freeing itself from the water in small regular sighs, and under that a lower sound, more felt than heard, of water moving through stone that has been doing this since before the trees learned the slope. Yellow-green films coat the warmest pans, slick and alive, working the minerals with the patience of a mill that does not stop for night. Nothing is constructed. The processes simply have the scale and the rhythm of manufacture: intake, separation, deposit, a product left in place because there is no one to haul it away. In certain lights the iron curls flash like offcuts on a shop floor. Then the cloud thins and they are only rock again.

The quiet is real, and it is not emptiness. Wind is occasional. When it comes it moves the canopy in one long body and then quits, and the drip resumes as the dominant instrument. Insects keep up a low, close stitching, never a roar. Birds exist in numbers but call sparingly, single notes dropped into the wet and absorbed. Frogs answer the pools at a distance from one another, each voice small, the chorus never quite assembling. Something heavier moves in the fern, stops, moves again. You do not see it. The mud at the pool margins is written over with tracks that cross and soften and are written again: narrow toes, broader pads, the drag of a belly, the puncture rows of wading birds that come to the open water and leave before you arrive. Life is abundant the way moisture is abundant, distributed through the volume of the place rather than gathered into spectacle.

The day has a pace. Cloud builds against the rim by early afternoon, not a storm so much as the plateau inhaling. Light goes from clear to pearled without ever becoming dim. Rain arrives as a thickening of what is already happening, a few minutes of heavier fall that the leaves take personally, then the return to drip and shine. Between showers, dragonflies work the pool edges, and small fish or something like them dimple the tea-colored surface from underneath, rising to whatever the rain knocked down. On the mineral terraces the films brighten when the sun returns, as if the work had been waiting for the light to judge it. By late day the heat has not broken. It has only changed its alliance, from the sun to the ground, which gives back what it stored. The pools hold the color of the sky a long time after the sky has gone copper, then gray.

Nothing marks a boundary except the land’s own. To the west the terrace drops, not in a cliff you could romanticize but in a long forested steepening that disappears into its own weather. To the east the shoulder continues, more pools, more weeping stone, a second iron stain smaller than the first, a stand of the pale trees so dense their trunks touch. You could walk that way until dark and still be in the same kind of place, only deeper in its habits. There are no paths that were made. There are only the places where the water has already decided the walking will be easier, and the places where the moss has decided it will not.

At full dark the industry does not cease. The ticking in the warm pans goes on. Leaves unload their last drops at irregular intervals, loud because everything else has stepped back. Now and then a seep-vent exhales a breath that smells of metal and crushed green, and the air around it pricks faintly, a static that gathers in the wet and spends itself without lightning, without spectacle, a small electrical fact of hot water and thin air. Fireflies, or what pass for them, stitch short paths above the pools, ordinary enough until you notice they prefer the mineral margins, as if the crust itself were a landmark. The heat remains. High above the terrace, if the cloud breaks, the stars are sharp in the thin air, and they look down on a landscape that should, by every ordinary rule of height, be cold and spare, and is instead wet, crowded, and steadily at its work, exactly as weathered and exactly as unfinished as it has been for a very long time.
