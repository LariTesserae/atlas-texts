The place is smaller than it sounds. You can walk its whole length in the time it takes a valve to finish one cycle and begin the next, and you always know when you have reached the edge, because the edge is built: a low embankment of dressed stone, darkened to the color of wet tea, with a coping of iron that has been warm for so long the warmth feels like a property of the metal. Beyond the coping the ground drops a little and then simply stops being this place. Inside the ring, everything belongs to the works.

It sits in a basin that was never high to begin with. The stone underfoot is not bedrock so much as a settled skin — flagging, channel-cuts, and the original mudstone all worn down to the same patience. Nothing here is collapsing. The erosion finished its argument generations ago and now only maintains the verdict: edges rounded, treads dished, corners eased, every surface carrying the memory of water that has passed the same way ten thousand times and intends to pass the same way again. Rust, where it exists, is a glaze rather than a wound. Moss does not overrun the joints; it occupies exactly the joints, a dark green caulk that has learned the width of the masons' gaps and does not ask for more.

The air is hot before you understand why. Heat comes up through the soles and also hangs in the atmosphere, which is never quite air. It is a condensate. Walk twenty paces and your sleeves are heavy. The light never clears. There is no sky you can point to — only a low, amber-green thickness, as if the sun had been strained through leaves and then through steam and then through more leaves, and had given up. Shadows do not fall so much as pool. You can read a gauge if you stand close and angle the glass; you cannot read a face across a courtyard.

Water is everywhere and almost never in a single dramatic body. It beads on the undersides of lintels and lets go in slow strings. It sheets across courtyard stones that have been polished by that sheeting into a dull mirror. It stands in the joints. It rises in the channels cut between the buildings — not a flood, a level, kept there by gates you can hear working even when you cannot see them. Some walls weep on a schedule. You learn this if you stay. A limestone pier will be dry, then suddenly dark from a seam near its capital, then dry again, and the interval is regular enough that the plants have arranged themselves around it.

The vegetation is lush and oddly disciplined. Broad leaves, some as wide as a hatch cover, grow from planters that were once machine-beds and from the crotches of pipe-stands and from the open tops of disused cisterns. Ferns colonize the shaded side of every vertical. Vines run the pipe-runs as if the pipes had been laid as trellises, which perhaps, by now, they have. Nothing looks wild in the way of a jungle. The growth is thick, but it respects the clearances. A walkway remains a walkway. A gear housing grows a collar of leaves and not a burial. The green is deep, almost black in the dimness, with sudden pale veins where new growth pushes out — and the new growth is constant. You can watch a frond unfurl over the course of an hour if you have the patience, which the heat will try to take from you.

There are no animals. Not a skim of insects over the standing water. Not a bird in the canopy the vines have made of the roof-trusses. Not a lizard on the hot iron, where a lizard would have every reason to be. The channels are clear to the bottom, and the bottom is mineral crust and leaf-litter and nothing that moves of its own accord. The absence is not a silence. It is the opposite. The place is loud, and the loudness has nowhere to hide inside a living throat, so it stays mechanical and hydraulic and meteorological, and that is all it is.

The sound is the true density of the hamlet. Spatially the buildings stand apart — six halls, a pump house, a row of condenser sheds, a tower no taller than the trees that have taken its staging, courtyards wide enough to turn a cart in, if carts still came. You could call it sparse and not be wrong. But the noise fills every gap the architecture leaves open. Sluice-gates drop and lift with a boom that rolls around the embankment and comes back smaller, then again. A reciprocating engine in the pump house keeps a two-stroke patience, never hurrying, never resting; its exhaust is a wet cough into a stack that disappears into the haze. Water strikes water in the spill-bays at the southern edge, a steady roar with a higher thread in it where a broken lip makes the stream fan. Rain begins without warning and without thunder — a sudden intensification of the dripping already underway — and ends the same way, and the roofs, which are iron and patched and pitched just enough, translate both events into drumming. Metal ticks as it expands. Somewhere a belt slaps. Somewhere a float rises and a valve shuts with a clap you feel in your teeth.

The industry is not abandoned and not quite inhabited. It runs. Belts are not slack. Oil shines at the right joints and not at the wrong ones. Gauges have needles that travel. Lamps — few, caged, amber — come on when the haze thickens past a certain point and go out when it thins, which is a distinction only the lamps seem able to make. There are no workers in evidence, and no sign that any are expected before the next cycle or after it. Tools hang on painted boards whose paint has outlasted the names. A logbook lies open on a bench in the pump house, its pages warped by damp, the last entries in a hand that pressed hard and dated nothing you can parse. The machinery does not need the hand anymore. Or the hand comes when you are not there. Both explanations sit equally well, and the place does not care which you prefer.

What is strange announces itself slowly, which is how you know it is structural and not a trick of the heat.

The plants drink the condensate, and the condensate is not only water. Where a vent drips onto a cistern garden, the leaves grow in ranks that echo the spacing of the rivets on the vent-hood above them. Not approximately. Rank for rivet, as if the drip carried a pattern and the growth obeyed it. Move to the next shed and the obedience changes: leaves arranged on the bias, matching a run of diagonal bracing you can only see if you look up. The vegetation is lush because it is fed, and it is orderly because it is instructed, and whatever does the instructing has been at it long enough that the instruction and the habit are the same thing.

The water sometimes climbs. On certain stones — not all, and not always the same ones — a film will travel upward against the grade for the length of a hand before it loses courage and sheets back down. It happens near the active gates more often than near the still channels. If you wet your fingers in a climbing film it feels no different from the rest: hot, faintly sweet, mineral. The stone beneath it is the same settled stone as everywhere else. Only the water has, for a moment, remembered a direction that is not down.

And the cycles are nested in a way no shift-schedule would bother with. The weeping pier, the rain's onset, the float-valve's clap, the engine's cough, the lamps — they coincide, drift apart, coincide again, on an interval longer than an hour and shorter than a day. When they coincide, the whole hamlet seems to inhale. The haze lifts a finger's width. The leaves turn their pale undersides as if a wind had passed, though no wind passes here; the embankment keeps it out, or the heat refuses it. For that brief alignment you can see, across the widest courtyard, the far iron coping, and beyond it the drop, and beyond the drop a darkness that is not the haze. Then the valve claps, the rain resumes or ceases, the pier goes dry, and the place closes back into its own small weather.

Nothing hunts. Nothing is hunted. The motion is all process: water finding its old paths, heat driving vapor into the leaves and the leaves breathing it back, iron expanding and contracting inside tolerances worn smooth by use, gates keeping a level that the embankment was built to hold and still holds. The balance is real. Stand in a courtyard and you are not in a ruin and not in a factory at shift-change. You are in a machine that has become a climate, tended by growth that has become a kind of recording, loud enough to fill a space that would otherwise be only dripping and heat.

If you stay through one full coincidence of the cycles, your clothes will be soaked and your skin salt-filmed and you will have heard every sound the place makes, twice. You will not have heard anything living answer. When you leave — over the coping, down the little drop, out of the ring — the noise cuts off more sharply than distance can explain, as if the embankment were also a door. Behind you the amber thickness closes. Ahead, whatever country surrounds the works is ordinary, and quiet, and already less exact.
