The light arrives before any sense of a sky. It is already everywhere when you notice you are underground: a cool, even brightness, the color of chalk held up to noon, with no disk to look away from and no shaft you can blame. The ceiling is far enough overhead that it reads as weather rather than roof—a long vault of pale stone, planed by age until its ribs are only suggestions, giving the light back in a soft scatter. The air is cool enough that your breath does not quite show, and dry enough in the open that the brightness feels clean, until you step into a pocket where it isn’t.

The floor is a city that has forgotten how to stand up.

What remains of it spreads for a distance you would call a valley if you could trust the word. From any rise—and the rises are modest, old walls worn to ankle and knee—you see several districts at once, separated by stretches of bare mineral flat where nothing has been built, or where building has been erased down to a discoloration. The plan is still legible if you walk it. Streets are shallow grooves. Plazas are broader polish. A grid of cisterns survives as shallow pans, some holding a finger of water, most holding only a darker damp and a rind of pale crust. Foundations outline rooms whose walls are gone. In a few places a course of fitted stone still reaches a person’s shoulder, dressed with tools no more ambitious than mauls and bronze that has itself gone thin and green and, in the oldest courses, nearly back to ore. The joints are tight. Whoever laid them understood load and drainage and the long patience of a street. They did not understand, or could not refuse, the flattening that came after.

Nothing green grows. There is no soil in the living sense, only meal: stone ground against stone for a length of time that has outlasted whatever once held it together as dirt. Color is mineral and animal. Chalk, bone-yellow, the blue-gray of old reduction, a rust that is only iron remembering weather, and here and there a lane paved in black glass worn so smooth it holds the ceiling’s light like still oil. The glass is not being made. It is a leftover, older than the streets, older than the idea of streets, and the city learned to walk on it. Where moisture lingers, a film forms—slick, mineral, sometimes faintly iridescent, sometimes the color of a bruise. It is not a plant. It does not leaf or reach. It precipitates when the damp is right and powders away when the damp moves on. People scrape it. Animals lick it. It comes back somewhere else.

Water is a visitor with no schedule. Most of the basin is dry underfoot, cool and faintly dusty, the dust so fine it does not rise so much as lean. Then a dark patch: a cistern that caught last night’s drip from the vault, a thread occupying an old channel for an hour and gone by what passes for afternoon, a wall that beads and weeps onto a single flagstone until the flagstone shines and the wall goes matte again. Rain, when it happens, is condensation losing its grip. It falls on one plaza and not the next. You can stand at the edge of it and stay dry, watching a brief, soundless stippling darken the stone, and walk around. The larger dryness is the rule. The moisture is the exception that keeps redrawing where the exception is.

The quiet is the first thing that feels like a rule rather than an accident. The basin swallows noise. A footfall dies inside a step. A voice, raised, carries farther as shape than as sound—you see a mouth working across a flat before you hear a thin remainder of the word. The surfaces are wrong for echo: too worn, too meal-soft at their edges, too vast. So the turbulence registers in the eye.

It does not stop. Herds cross the open ground between districts in loose, constant streams—pale animals, long-legged, numerous enough that the far flats seem to crawl, sparse enough that you can walk among them without being in a crowd. They do not call. They turn together when the damp-line shifts, which it does, a boundary of darker stone migrating with no wind you can name, and the herds revise their path as if the boundary were a fence. Smaller things occupy the cistern rims and the glass lanes: many, quick, also quiet, flashing only when they cross from matte to shine. Higher up, in the bright volume under the vault, flyers turn in wide silent wheels. Their shadows move faster than they seem to, sliding over walls that are no longer walls. Nothing in the air roars. A front of pale motes will still come off the far rise and cross a district in the time it takes to cross a plaza, dimming the light to a cooler white, powdering every horizontal surface, and pass, leaving the stone a shade older and the cisterns a shade wetter. People do not run from it. They cover what they mean to keep and keep working.

They are not many, relative to the ground. The settlement is urban in the old sense: organized, infrastructural, meant to hold more life than a camp. You can read markets in the postholes, water law in the way every groove eventually finds a pan, quarters in the way foundations cluster and then stop, leaving the long empties on purpose or by attrition. What stands now above that plan is sparing. Hides on bone frames. Low walls of undressed stone stacked in a day and restacked the next, because the meal shifts and a wall that was true in the morning leans by the time the motes pass. Fire in shallow pits, fed with fat and with scrapings of the crust, never with wood. Cord of sinew. Blades of the black glass, flaked at the edge of the lanes and abandoned when they dull, which is soon; the glass is ancient and contrary, and it does not love being a tool. A few copper pieces, treasured, green, used for the work stone cannot do cleanly. No wheels that have survived as circles—only the memory of a track worn deeper than a footpath, and hand-barrows that complain, almost, if you put your ear to the frame.

The people move as the herds do: always, and with very little voice. Porters cross the empties between clusters because the clusters will not come to each other. A cistern that held water at the start of a crossing may be a dry pan at the end, and the porter’s route bends, and someone behind them is already taking down a hide roof because the damp has arrived on a street that was dry, and someone else is laying a roof where it has left. The city is busy the way a shore is busy between tides, except the tide is local and multiple and does not keep one clock. Districts brighten and powder and darken on different schedules. You can watch three weathers at once and hear none of them.

There are animals that do not travel. They keep to the glass, or to the warm-cool seam where a hide wall leaks body heat into the mineral air, or to the rims where the crust is thickest after a weeping. Some are large enough to matter and rare enough to be given room; the people do not fence them so much as decline to build where they lie. Eyes are ordinary here. The light is too complete for the old underground bargains. What is unusual is the hush they share with everything else, and the way a flock changing direction can look, from a worn foundation, like the basin itself deciding to lean.

Nothing marks an hour. The brightness does not travel. Sleep is arranged by custom and by the passage of the mote-fronts, which are at least an event. Eat the crust, eat what grazes the crust, drink where the vault has recently forgotten itself. Do not store water in a vessel you cannot carry; the basin will offer it again, but not where you left the vessel. Do not trust a wall you did not touch this morning. Do not expect the street you memorized. The grooves remain—the city is too old to be erased in the ways that matter to a map—but the life on top of the grooves is a turbulence of small decisions, hide and stone and herd and damp, all of it ceaseless, all of it nearly inaudible.

If you stand long enough on the glass, the light seems to deepen rather than change, and the far districts look closer, as if the basin were gently drawing its edges in. It isn’t. The horizon stays where the vault finally comes down to the floor in a haze of meal, a boundary you could walk to and still not be out. Beyond that haze there are only more pans, more grooves, more brief rains falling on no one. The city has already become its last shape. It keeps moving anyway, quietly, because nothing left in it knows how to be finished.
