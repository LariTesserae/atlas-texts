The valley does not sit in rock. Rock has not had time to become the floor. What holds the height is a crust of living tissue, still pale at the breaks, still wet where it was torn upward, and the whole region rides on that crust the way a raft rides a flood—except the raft is the flood’s own body, stiffened, braided, and already failing in places into new shapes.

It is high enough that the air should be thin and cold. It is not. Heat comes up through the weave as if the making of the place never finished cooling. By late morning the light is ordinary—white, a little hard, no drama in the sky—and yet every surface throws back a wet shine, and the shine trembles. The heat does not bake the wetness away. It keeps it moving. Water beads on the undersides of spans, runs in sheets down walls that have no masonry joint, collects in bowls that were never carved, and then, somewhere lower and out of sight, flashes back into vapor with a sound like cloth being ripped in a long continuous tear.

Nothing here was quarried or fitted. There is no substrate that is not plant. The ground underfoot, where there is ground, is a mat of fused sheaths, finger-thick, locked edge to edge the way scales lock, and if you press it the mat gives, then springs, then weeps a clear sap that smells of cut green and hot metal. Walk twenty paces and the mat ends at a lip. Beyond the lip is air, and across the air, far enough that a shout would thin out before it arrived, another lip, another mat, another standing form. The forms are sparse. The material is total. Between them the valley is mostly weather.

The forms themselves are young and still sharp. Edges have not rounded. A tower—if tower is the word—rises as a bundle of hollow columns, each column a single internode swollen past any ordinary stem, the walls thin enough that light comes through them green. Their tops are not finished. They are splitting. You can watch a crown open over the course of an hour: a seam appears, dark and wet, then the halves lever apart with a crack that carries, and from the split a new rib unfurls already rigid, already load-bearing, throwing a shadow across empty space toward the next structure, which may be a league off and may answer, hours later, with a rib of its own. They are building toward each other without having met. Most of them never will. The spans that do complete are few, and they sing.

The sound is not animal. There is no animal. No insect ticks the heat. No bird cuts the light. What is loud is the hydraulics of growth and the weather running through it. Sap under pressure knocks inside the columns—irregular, like someone shifting furniture in a tall empty house. Rain, when it comes, does not fall from a decided cloud so much as condense out of the column of hot air and then fall all at once, a vertical river that lasts minutes and leaves the hollow trunks roaring. Wind finds every unfinished mouth and turns it into a pipe. Some pipes whistle. Some boom. A low one, down in a pocket where three mats have slumped together into a cistern, produces a note so deep it is felt in the teeth more than heard, and the note changes key when the water level drops by a hand’s breadth, which it does, and rises, and drops again, because nothing here holds a level.

Call the larger arrangement a town and you will be wrong in every civic sense and right in scale. The structures scatter across a basin you cannot see the end of from any one mat. Some stand alone: a single vaulted hood, big as a hall, its floor a shallow pool, its walls sweating so steadily that the pool never quite empts and never quite overflows—except when a seam above it ruptures and dumps a sudden ton of warm water, after which the hood sags, reforms, and begins again the slow work of holding. Some cluster in threes and fours with gaps wide enough to lose a road in. A few have thrown partial bridges that stop in midair, frayed, dripping, the broken end already healing into a bulb that will either fall or reach. The reaching is the rule. The falling is the rule. Both happen in the same afternoon.

Heat makes a second architecture in the air. Where water strikes a hot sheath it flashes, and the steam rises in ropes rather than clouds, ropes that lean with the wind and sometimes braid, and where two ropes cross they leave, for a little while, a pale scar of condensed droplets hanging with nothing to hang from. People would call that a trick of light. There are no people. The scar lasts until the next gust, which is never long. Down in the cistern pockets the air is breathless and the light goes green, and the loudness becomes a closed loudness, knock and drip and the occasional gunshot of a sheath splitting under its own pressure. Up on the open mats the light is plain daylight and the loudness is distance: booms arriving late, whistles overlapping, the tear of vapor, the soft continuous fry of water turning to air on every sunlit face.

The ecology, if it can be called that, is one body arguing with itself. There are no grazers to prune it, no burrowers to aerate it, no seed-eaters to carry it elsewhere. What spreads, spreads by running. A rib touches a neighboring mat and the touch welds; within a day the weld has a vein, and the vein pulls water sideways, and the donor tower pales along one flank while the recipient darkens and swells and throws a new sharp eave that did not exist at dawn. Death is local and structural. A column that has given too much water goes chalky, then corky, then folds along a crease as clean as a cut, and the fold becomes, immediately, a channel. Rain prefers the dead. The dead become gutters, then soil for the next mat, except there is no soil—only the flattened corpse of the last construction, already being quilted over by living sheath. Young places keep their dead visible. This one quilts fast, but not faster than you can see the bones of the last hour lying under the skin of this one.

At the scale of the valley the pattern is a scatter of these arguments, none of them settled. From a high rib—if you trust the rib, and the rib is hot, and it flexes when the wind booms through its hollow—you look across a region of green knives and green bowls and green unfinished bridges, each separated by more air than substance, each throwing steam, each adding or losing a storey while you watch. The far rim is not a cliff of stone. It is a brighter haze where the mats thin out and the heat-column frays into ordinary sky. Somewhere under all of it, too deep to see and too new to have a name, whatever forced the crust up is still forcing. You feel it as a slow shove in the soles, out of rhythm with the knocks, and now and then a whole mat tilts a degree and every pool on it slops to one side and the pipes change key at once, a chord, ugly and enormous, and then the tilt stops and the place resumes its smaller violences.

Nothing hunts. Nothing is hunted. The strangeness is not a monster in the steam. It is that construction and weather and body are the same event, running hot, running wet, too new for edges to soften, too restless to become a ruin or a forest or a city, loud with the work of becoming all three and failing, continuously, in the open light.
