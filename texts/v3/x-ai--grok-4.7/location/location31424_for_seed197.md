The place is a single chamber, low in the world, and it has been one chamber for so long that the idea of an outside has thinned to a rumor in the damp. You come into it the way water comes into a root: not through a door, but by being drawn. The floor is not stone. It is a braided plane of old stems, pressed flat by centuries of their own weight, warm underfoot, giving a little, the way a sleeping animal gives. Between the braids the moisture never quite decides to be a stream. It beads, it films, it gathers in the long thumbs of hollows and then pulls back into the fiber, leaving a darker write of itself that fades before you can call it a mark.

Above, the ceiling is far. Not mountain-far — the whole of the place would fit inside a modest town’s worth of ground, if the ground were willing to be measured that way — but far in the manner of a nave whose builders refused to stop lifting. The lift is not masonry. It is growth that learned, long ago, the habit of architecture and then forgot it was a habit. Ribs of living wood rise, branch, reconcile, and rise again, and between them the volume is almost nothing: air, warmth, a pale ordinary daylight that does not seem to enter from anywhere you can point to. It is simply present, as if the chamber had been assigned a brightness and accepted it. In that brightness the ribs look bronze at the edges and green in the grain, and where two of them have grown into each other the join is not a scar. It is a street.

That is the first way the place lies to you, and then tells a truer thing. The city is not arranged on the floor. The floor is only the oldest street, the one that lay down. The rest of the settlement climbs the walls and lives in the thickness of the ribs. You can see the layering if you stand still long enough for your eyes to stop looking for stone. Courses of compressed leaf, pale as plaster. Arches of vascular bundle, hollowed by their own patience into galleries a body could walk. Balconies that are bracts, still faintly patterned with the ghost of whatever flower they once were, now load-bearing. Drains that are not cut but persuaded: channels where the tissue learned to die in a line so water would have somewhere to go, and then, having died usefully, stayed dead in exactly that shape while everything around it went on living. No tool touched any of this. There is no metal, no fired clay, no knot that a hand tied. The infrastructure is the organism’s memory of its own needs, repeated until the repetition became civic.

And yet the volume is empty. That is the second lie, or the second truth. A town’s worth of construction, and almost no one standing in it. The galleries open onto nothing. The plazas — there are plazas, cupped where several ribs decided, at the same century, to make a flat — hold only light and the brown-green dust of shed cuticle. You can walk a long while and meet no other walker. The distances between presences are the point. The city was not built to be filled. It was built to be heard.

Because the sound is the population.

It begins, if anything here can be said to begin, as a pressure behind the teeth. Warm air moving through a thousand living flutes. The ribs are not solid. They are chambered, and the chambers are tuned, not by design in any mind, but by the long argument between growth and weather: a wet season swells a throat, a dry week shrinks it, and over an age the swelling and shrinking settled into a chord the place could bear to repeat. Now it repeats without rest. A low note lives in the floor and does not so much rise as occupy you. Higher notes thread the galleries, bright, almost verbal, arriving from a rib you are not looking at and then, impossibly, from the rib you are touching, as if the sound had crossed the space by a shorter path than the space allows. When rain comes — and rain comes as a decision of the ceiling, a brief silver opinion, never a storm — every surface answers. The answering is deafening in the old sense: it makes the idea of hearing something else feel provincial. You do not cover your ears. The sound is also coming up through the soles, through the long bones, through the little empty rooms of the skull. Covering the ears only clarifies it.

The fauna are abundant, and they are not where you expect animals to be. They are in the walls, and they are the reason the walls keep their names. A pale many-legged thing the length of a hand travels the inside of a gallery, and where it travels the tissue brightens, conducting; it is a maintenance caste, or a nerve, or both, and it does not regard you because regard is not among its offices. In the cupped plazas, what you took for dust sometimes stands up. It is a moth-weight collective, thousands of bodies the color of the light, and when they stand they sing a note that was missing and the chord completes and for a moment the chamber’s geometry flinches. Birds — if they are birds; they have the discipline of birds and the materials of seed-coats — nest in the dead-drain channels and fly the empty volume in long ellipses that do not close. You watch one ellipse fail to meet itself and understand that the failure is patrol, not error. Something in the middle of the volume is being kept empty on purpose. The flyers spend their lives insisting on the hole.

There is a predator, because a place this active cannot be all chorus. You will not see it as a body. You will notice a gallery go quiet in a shape, a moving absence the size of a doorway, and the maintenance-legs will have already fled the brightness ahead of it, and the seed-birds will have banked their ellipse into a tighter insistence. The absence feeds on conduction. Where it passes, the tissue forgets a phrase of its song for an hour, and the city routes around the forgetting the way a living town routes around a fire: not with panic, with habit. By the time the phrase returns, thinner, the absence is elsewhere, and the floor-note has never stopped. The floor-note is older than predation. It does not negotiate.

Water’s part in this is modest and constant, which is how it keeps its power. No river owns the chamber. Moisture arrives as breath on the high ribs, as the ceiling’s occasional opinion, as a seep that finds the persuaded drains and travels them with a sound like a fingertip on a cup. In the lowest hollows it stands for a day as a dark lens, and the lens shows you the ribs upside down and slightly delayed, so that a bird crossing above crosses below a heartbeat later, and sometimes the later bird is not the same bird. People who stay long enough learn not to drink from the lenses. Not because the water is foul — it is only plant-water, green at the edge of tasting, warm as the air — but because drinking is a kind of agreement, and the lens has already proposed a sequence of events that has not happened yet to the mouth that would swallow it. The established balance here includes that proposal. Erosion, such as it is, is not a violence against form. It is form’s way of remaining. Tissue sloughs, compresses, becomes the next street. Nothing is ruined. Everything is in its own state, and has been, and the state includes a small, steady unmaking that the song accounts for, the way a choir accounts for breath.

The weirdness is not an intrusion. It is the method.

You walk what you believe is a gallery along a rib, counting your steps because counting feels like a tool and you have no tools, and at step three hundred the gallery is the floor, and you are standing on what you can see, from here, is the same braided plain, except the plain is now also above you, still braided, still warm, and the daylight has not changed its mind. You did not climb. The city reassigned the direction your weight meant, politely, without a seam. If you turn around, the way back is a different century of growth: younger tissue, louder, the song in it not yet worn smooth, and a maintenance-creature brighter than the ones you passed, as if you had walked into the hour it was born. Wait, and the hour thickens into masonry. Leave, and you will not find that young street again, though you will hear it sometimes inside the floor-note, a high thread that does not belong to the present tuning and is kept anyway, the way a town keeps a bell it no longer rings.

Scale misbehaves in the same grammar. The chamber is small. You can know this. There is a far wall — there is always, eventually, a far wall, a place where the ribs decline to continue and turn inward like the margin of a leaf — and a determined walker, granting the reassignments, can reach it between one rain and the next. But rooms open off the galleries that are larger than the chamber that contains them. You step into a bract-balcony and the balcony is a hall, and the hall has its own empty plazas, its own absent predator, its own lenses showing delayed birds, and when you look back the doorway is a pore in a leaf you could cover with your hand. The city is a settlement and a single organ and a diocese of one parish. All three descriptions are locally true. None of them survives being carried across a threshold.

At the margin, if you reach it, the ribs turn in and the song becomes briefly intelligible, which is worse than the roar. For a few paces the chord sorts itself into something like syllables, plant-syllables, a civic announcement older than any mouth: here is the edge, here is where growing stopped agreeing to be a city, beyond this the world is only weather and you will not be tuned to it. People who have gone beyond — there are stories the lenses tell, in the wrong order — do not return as sound. They return as moisture. A fine warm rain with no ceiling deciding it. The chamber accepts the rain without recognition. Recognition is not among its offices.

Light stays ordinary through all of this, which is the kindness and the cruelty. You can see everything. There is no merciful dark, no sacred dim in which the reassignment of down could pass as dream. Daylight, plain as a courtyard’s, lies on the living courses and shows you the joins, the drains, the dust that stands up to sing, the ellipse that refuses closure, the doorway of absence eating a phrase out of a wall. You see the city being a city. You hear it being a multitude. You stand in its vast sparse middle, warm, damp at the ankles, deafened, and the floor-note takes your pulse and spends it, and for as long as you let it, you are infrastructure too: another hollow in the chord, briefly load-bearing, already being memorized into the next layer of street.
