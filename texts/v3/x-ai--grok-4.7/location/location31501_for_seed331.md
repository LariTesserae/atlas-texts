The place is smaller than it first seems, and older than the smallness can explain.

You come to it by a descent that has forgotten it is a descent. The land around has been planed so long that slope survives only as a change in the color of the light, a paling, and then a lip no higher than a knee: stone worn past grit, past bedding, past any memory of having been a cliff. Beyond the lip the floor drops the depth of a room and stops. That is the whole of the relief. A basin you could walk across before the warmth made you want to stop, walled by a rim the weather has nearly argued out of existence, and yet the rim holds. Water does not leave. Silt does not arrive. The shape is what is left when a landscape has finished every other shape it was willing to be.

The floor is water, but not as a lake is water. It is a sheet, finger-deep in the open stretches, thigh-deep where the stone still remembers a hollow, and in places only a brightness moving over rock, a film that never quite decides to dry. The stone under it has no grain you can find with a hand. It has been rubbed by water and by the small feet of living things for so long that it has the nap of old soap, the temperature of something that has been warm for longer than warmth usually lasts. You can see through the shallows to that floor, and the floor gives the light back doubled, so the brightness of the place is not only from above. The sky is a high white, shadowless, and the water answers it. There is no hour here that could be called dim. Even what passes for weather arrives as more light or less, never as dark.

Warmth sits on the skin before you notice the air. It is not the heat of a desert noon and not the closeness of a jungle shade; it is the steady warmth of water that has nowhere colder to go, rising into an atmosphere already full of itself. Moisture beads on the rim, on the undersides of leaves, on any surface that offers a pause. Stones weep without having been rained on. The air condenses and clears and condenses again on a cycle shorter than a day, though days are hard to count. A mist lifts off the sheet in thin scarves, drifts the length of the basin, and lies back down as if the rising had been a courtesy rather than a change. After one of these, every surface is newly wet and the light is briefly harder, as if the place had been wiped.

The plants are few, and each one is extravagant.

They do not make a thicket. Between them the water runs open, bright, unobstructed, so that from the rim the basin can look almost empty: a pale floor, a scattering of green forms set down with the deliberation of furniture in a large room. Come closer and the lavishness of each form corrects you. One is a low dome of leaves the size of shields, overlapping so tightly that rain — if rain is the word for what falls out of this air — runs to the dome’s edge and drops in a single repeating place, wearing a shallow cup in the stone that has not deepened in any time you could measure. Another stands on a trunk no thicker than a wrist and opens, high, into a crown that sheds water in threads. A third has given up the idea of a trunk and lies as a braid of succulent stems across the film, flowering where the film is thinnest, roots that are also leaves drinking from both sides. Moss, where it occurs, occurs as whole islands, velvet thick enough to hold a footprint until the footprint becomes a pool and the pool becomes moss again. Nothing here competes by crowding. Each plant seems to have been granted a territory of empty water and bright air, and to have spent all its force inside that grant.

The quiet is not the quiet of absence. It is the quiet of a place that has learned how not to report itself.

Stand on the rim and you hear, at first, nothing you would call a sound: a soft ticking where water falls from the shield-leaves into its cup, a faint tick of condensation letting go of the rim, and under those, a hush so continuous it reads as silence. The hush is the sheet moving. It does move. Imperceptibly, toward no outlet, circling or settling or being turned over by something you cannot see, the whole skin of the basin shifts the way a sleeping animal shifts, and the sound of that is below the threshold where sound becomes news. Wind, when it comes, comes as a change in the temperature on your wet skin, not as a voice in the leaves. The leaves are heavy with water and do not clatter.

Then you understand that the quiet is crowded.

The fauna do not announce themselves because they have had geological time to stop announcing. They are everywhere the water is, and the water is everywhere, and they have filled the vertical as well as the floor. In the film itself the life is a density that defeats counting: gray-glass shrimp no longer than a nail, moving in schools that look like shivering light; flatworms the color of the stone, visible only when they cross a darker vein of algal crust; larvae hanging in the deeper pockets, transparent except for a dark gut and a pulse. Turn a shield-leaf and the underside is a city of scale and silk and slow legs, none of which rustle. The moss-islands breathe — you can see the minute rise — because what lives in them is adjusting the water they hold. Above the sheet, in the bright air, the abundance continues at a larger scale and still without noise. Moth-bodied things the size of your two hands drift between the spaced trees on wings that do not clap. They feed on the condensation, or on whatever the condensation carries, and when they pass close their wingbeats are a pressure, not a sound. Long-legged waders stand one to a territory, far enough apart that each seems alone, and strike into the film without a splash that lasts. Their necks are wrong by a small degree, or their patience is: they hold still longer than a bird should, and when they move it is as if the decision and the motion were the same instant, with nothing wasted on display.

There are more of them than the openness suggests. That is the trick of the spacing. The large animals honor distance the way the plants do — one wader, one low browser with a hide like wet slate, one thing in the deepest pocket that surfaces only as a slow lens of displaced light — while the small life saturates every remaining surface until saturation itself becomes the climate. You cannot find a dry neutral place to put your eye. Every brightness is a body or the sign of one. Every softness is inhabited. The emptiness between the trees is not empty; it is only unobstructed, a corridor through a biomass that has agreed, for reasons of its own, not to shout and not to clump.

What hunts, hunts on that agreement. You see it as sequences rather than as violence. A drift of the glass-shrimp parts around a stillness you had taken for a pebble. The stillness becomes a mouth. The drift closes. A moth-thing settles on a crown and is there, and then is a fold of leaf that was not a leaf, and then the crown is only a crown again, dripping on its same schedule. No cry goes up. No flock bursts. The basin registers the exchange as it registers condensation: a brief local change in brightness, then the long quiet reasserted. Predator and prey here are not a drama. They are one of the processes by which the water stays clear and the plants stay few and the stone stays smooth. Surplus is taken before it can become clutter. The ancientness of the place includes this. It has reached a form in which appetite maintains the emptiness rather than wrecking it.

The built things are easy to miss, and then impossible to set back into the category of ruin.

There are four of them, or four that declare themselves; others may have subsided all the way into the floor’s nap and now function only as slight alterations in how the film drains. One stands near the center, no taller than the shield-leaf domes: a spindle of material that is not metal and not stone, the color of the white sky, narrow enough that you can see past it without trying. Water does not bead on it. Water climbs it, in a skin too thin to drip, and vanishes at a height where nothing visible receives it. The air immediately around the spindle is a degree cooler and a degree clearer, as if moisture were being sorted rather than merely lifted. Stand close and the hairs on your arm lift, not with static exactly, but with the sensation of a rule being applied to you and then politely declining to finish. The spindle has no seam, no joint, no face. It is doing something the basin needs and has been doing it long enough that the stone at its foot is not more worn than the stone elsewhere — which is to say the wearing has been equalized, or the spindle arrived after the wearing was already complete and has refused to participate in further change.

The second is a pavement of three flags set into the rim, flush, almost the rim’s own color, distinguishable because they do not weep. Rain-that-is-not-rain runs to their edges and stops. On their surface, in certain angles of the doubled light, a pattern shows that is not carving: a shifting mesh finer than any tool, present for a moment when condensation fails to settle in a geometry, absent when you lean to see it directly. It is not writing. It behaves like a calibration mark that the air itself is still obeying.

The third has almost finished becoming geology. A low arch, broken, half a stride wide, made of the same soap-smooth stone as the floor, except that the break is not a break. The two ends of the arch approach each other across a gap and do not quite touch, and in the gap a thread of the white material spins slowly, or seems to, without winding down. Small life will not cross it. The glass-shrimp schools divide. The flatworms turn aside. Even the moss stops a hand’s width short and grows dense along that invisible fence, a hedge of refusal. Whatever the arch was — gate, instrument, sample of a larger work — it still separates one kind of water from another, though both sides look the same and are the same temperature and hold, as far as you can tell, the same shrimp. The difference is in what the shrimp will risk.

The fourth is not an object so much as a persistence. On the inner face of the rim, at the point where the land outside is highest and the lip is lowest, there is a scar of fused material no larger than a plate, cracked, ordinary-looking, colonized at its margins by the velvet moss. Touch it and it is warm in a way the stone is not: warm from within, a small private climate, and the moss there is a different green, almost luminous in the general brightness, growing in a tight spiral that reasserts itself if you brush it flat. Leave it. By the time the next condensation cycle has lifted and lain down, the spiral is back, and the fused plate has not cooled. This is the youngest-looking thing in the basin, and it may be the oldest act: something left to keep a point on the rim from eroding the last thumb’s width that would let the sheet escape. The rest of the leveling was done by time. This one correction was done by a decision, and the decision is still being enforced by a mechanism that no longer has a housing.

None of this is technology in the sense of devices you could open. It is maintenance that has outlived its makers and been simplified by survival into functions without interfaces. The basin should have filled with silt, or drained, or grown into a single choking mat, or lost its warmth to the long flattening of the country around it. It has done none of these. It holds its terminal shape the way a held note holds, and the holding is active. You can feel the activeness as processes rather than as events. The sheet turns over. The mist rises and lies down. The shrimp-schools migrate from the bright center to the shade of a dome and back, following a brightness gradient too subtle to see until you see them follow it. The waders change territories on a schedule longer than an afternoon and shorter than a season, walking the boundaries without display. The shield-leaf cups fill and drop and fill. Somewhere under the film, in the deeper pockets, something large circulates nutrients by the mere fact of its slow patrol, and the water over its path is briefly clearer, a moving window, and then the window closes and the patrol goes on without a wake that lasts.

There is weather, of a kind. Not storms. Pulses. A period in which the condensation thickens until the far rim softens and the moth-things fly lower, their wings collecting a shine. A period in which the sheet draws down by a finger’s depth and the stone’s nap shows through everywhere, darkening as it drinks, and the fauna compress into the remaining hollows in a density that is briefly, visibly appalling — a seethe of glass and pulse and flat pale bodies — and then the sheet returns, from no inlet, condensing out of the air or being yielded back by the spindle, and the compression eases into the old spacious crowding. These are not catastrophes. They are the basin breathing on a period you could learn if you stayed, and nothing in the staying would teach you a word for it. The animals do not panic when the water thins. They have been thinned before. The place has been doing this since before panic would have been useful.

The strangeness does not break the surface. It revises the rules underneath.

Sound does not carry. You can clap — you will, once, because the quiet invites a test — and the clap dies inside a few strides, absorbed as if the wet air and the wet stone had been built to eat reports of impact. Speak, and your voice comes back smaller than it left, from no particular wall. The animals do not startle at the clap. Either they do not hear in the range you occupy, or they have categorized sudden air-pressure as weather, irrelevant, already accounted for in the long calibration of their nerves.

Light behaves with a courtesy that ordinary light does not. Shadows exist, but they are shallow and they do not cool. Stand so that your body blocks the sky and the water still shines on the shaded side of you, returning brightness from the floor, so that you cannot make a dark place simply by standing in the way. The moth-things, when they pass between you and the white sky, do not dim it. They add a brief prism at the wing’s edge and are gone. Color is present and restrained: the green of the granted plants, the slate of the browser, the glass of the shrimp, the white of the sky and the spindle, the warm gray of the stone. Against that economy the spiral moss on the fused plate is almost indecent in its green, a small excess the maintenance allows itself.

Time pools rather than passes evenly. This is the part that takes longest to trust, because nothing fantastical happens to prove it. It is only that tasks done near the spindle take less of you, and tasks done near the broken arch take more, and a circuit of the rim can feel longer in one direction than the other though the distance is the same. The condensation cycle is the clock you can actually use. Everything else — the waders’ exchange of territories, the patrol under the film, the reassertion of the spiral — locks to that cycle or to some multiple of it, and if you try to count by your own hunger or fatigue you will find those have become unreliable narrators. The warmth does not exhaust. The brightness does not tire the eyes so much as empty them, the way a long look at snow does, except that here the emptying fills again with detail: another school, another thread of falling water, another correction in the way a leaf holds its drop until the appointed moment.

The rules, then, as the place itself seems to keep them:

Nothing is permitted to accumulate enough to change the shape. Silt is taken up by the small life and moved until it is no longer silt. Leaves that fall are recruited into the moss-islands or drawn under by the browsers before they can stain the sheet. Bodies do not linger. Whatever dies is in the water and then is the water’s clarity, on a schedule so even that you will not see a carcass if you wait a cycle. The overwhelming life is overwhelming precisely because it is spent as fast as it is made, a standing wave of organisms whose number is huge and whose remainder is near zero.

Distance is maintained by agreement and, where agreement might fail, by the remnants of a fence. The large forms do not cluster. The small forms may seethe, but only inside the volume already granted to them — under a leaf, inside a moss-island, in the film — and when the water thins they compress without crossing the arch-gap or climbing the spindle or leaving the basin over the rim. The plate on the low lip is doing that last work still, warm under your hand, a refusal of escape maintained at one point because every other point no longer needs it. The country outside is lower than it used to be, or the basin has been held up while the country wore down around it. Either way the lip is a decision.

Sound is not a resource here. Communication, if the word still fits, moves by light-edge and by chemistry in the film and by the pressure of a wingbeat that does not clap. The quiet is structural. It is part of how the maintenance keeps its measurements clean. A noisy place would be a place whose data were corrupted by its own inhabitants, and this place has not allowed that corruption for a time long enough that the inhabitants no longer produce the corruption even as a possibility. The moth-things were perhaps never moths. The waders were perhaps never birds that called. Or they were, and the calling was edited out by the same slow preference that smoothed the stone: whatever made a report of itself did not remain.

You can leave. The rim is only a knee high, and outside, the planed country continues, bright and warm and much less wet, with ordinary wind and ordinary small sounds and a faint sense of slope you had forgotten. Nothing prevents the step. What the basin keeps, it keeps by functions older and finer than a wall. Step back in and the hush closes over the ordinary sounds you brought with you from outside, not dramatically, the way warm water closes over an ankle. Within a condensation cycle you will stop listening for birdsong that is not going to come. Within two, you will start to see the schools as weather and the weather as a kind of counting. The spindle will be turning moisture over in its private way. The arch-gap will be refusing something you cannot identify. The plate will be warm.

It does not need you to understand the engineering. The engineering has already finished needing to be understood. It remains as a few scattered insistences — a climb of water into nothing, a gap nothing small will cross, a warmth with no fuel you can name, a mesh the dew declines to cover — and around those insistences an ecology has settled into what looks like its last stable kindness: lush, but only in the places lushness was allowed; wet without flood; bright without a sun you can locate; full of animals to the point of saturation, and quiet to the point of peace, if peace is the right word for a system that eats its dead so cleanly and keeps its distances so well.

The oldest thing about it is not the stone. The stone is only what is left. The oldest thing is the decision, still running, that this small floor of water should not be allowed to become any other kind of place.
