The chamber has no floor. What you stand on is a living rind, warm as a flank, ribbed with vascular bundles the width of your wrist that pulse in slow, irregular waves. It is not soil and it is not stone. It is tissue, still unfinished, still deciding what it will be, and it gives a little under your weight the way a muscle does when it has not yet learned its own tension. Dust rises from it when the pulse flexes — a fine mineral flour, dry as flour, glittering, with no moisture in it at all. You can taste the dryness before you notice thirst. Your mouth goes to salt and chalk.

You are under. How far under is hard to say, because the ceiling is not a ceiling. It is a roof of the same tissue, hung with hanging ropes of it, and above that another shelf, and another, so that the place reads as stacked hollows rather than a single cave: a regional underside, a valley turned inside out and still being excavated from within. The rock that once closed over it is visible only in shards — pale plates of unweathered mineral, edges sharp, not yet rounded by anything. They jut through the living mass like bones through skin that has not finished covering them. New fissures open while you watch. A seam in the far wall splits with a dry crack, and pale tissue pours into the gap the way dough fills a tear, swelling, branching, laying down a lattice before the crack has finished sounding. The geology is embryonic. The plants are racing it.

There is no water. Not a seep, not a film, not the dark gleam that caves usually keep in their lowest places. The chemistry here runs on something else. The tissue drinks mineral dust and warm gas that leaks from the splitting rock — a faint sweet-metal breath, not quite sulfur, not quite solvent — and it builds itself from that. Cut a bundle and it does not bleed. It exhales a dry white powder and a thread of that gas, and the cut edge seals over with a papery callus in the time it takes you to step back. Leaves, where there are leaves, are thick and waxy and almost ceramic, pale green going to the color of old bone, arranged in overlapping scales so tight that light skates across them. Where there are no leaves there are plates, tubes, spongy vaults, and long tensile cables that thrum when the chamber flexes. The vegetation is the substrate, the architecture, the weather, and most of the horizon. You cannot see where it ends. Passages open in every direction at the scale of a town, then a valley, then a further valley whose far wall is only a warmer dimness, all of it grown rather than dug, all of it still growing, none of it wet.

The light is ordinary, which is the strangest fact in the room. It is not the green foxfire of rotting wood and not the blue of deep-sea lamps. It is the color of an overcast afternoon, even and sufficient, and it comes from the tissue itself — from a pale rind just under the surface of every plate and cable, a layer that metabolizes the leaking gas into radiance the way a leaf metabolizes sun into sugar, except reversed, and brighter than it has any right to be. Shadows fall correctly. You can read a face by it. You can see, across a hundred paces of interlocking growth, the small wrongnesses that mean someone else has been here.

Those are few. A platform of lashed cable, not grown but tied, the knots clumsy and human-handed, or something near to human-handed: fiber twisted with fiber, pulled tight, anchored to a jut of unfinished rock with wooden pegs that have already begun to be swallowed by the rind. A ladder of the same work, three rungs remaining, the rest digested. A scatter of stone flakes struck from the raw mineral plates — old toolmaking, edges still keen because nothing here weathers them. A low wall of stacked shards, waist-high, already half-engulfed, with a narrow slot in it as if someone once meant to watch the passage and then left before the wall was finished. No metal. No mortar. The marks are scattered at long intervals, the way camps are scattered in a country too loud and too alive to settle. Whoever made them worked by hand, quickly, and did not stay.

They did not stay because the place will not hold still, and because it is not empty.

The fauna is the noise, and the noise is total. It begins as a pressure in the teeth before it resolves into sound. Every surface is occupied. Things the size of locusts, armored in the same pale ceramic as the leaves, clatter in sheets across the cables, thousands at a time, changing direction as one body so that the tissue rings like a struck beam. Larger animals move in the underlayer — you see the rind bulge and subside, bulge and subside, a traveling swell the length of a boat, and then a mouth breaks the surface, lipless, ringed with grinding plates, and takes a mouthful of its own world and pulls back under. The swallowing is audible. The regrowth is audible too, a wet-sounding crackle that is not wet, the tissue racing to close the wound before the next mouth arrives. Between these, a middle kingdom: jointed climbers with too many legs, each leg ending in a hook that ticks against ceramic leaf; gliding membranes that are not wings so much as extensions of the same tissue, launched from high cables and steering by flexing; clusters of small warm bodies packed into every crotch of the growth, breathing in unison so that patches of the wall seem to pant. None of them look like surface animals except in the way a tool looks like the hand that needs it. Eyes, where there are eyes, are numerous and small and set in rings. Many have no eyes. They navigate by the thrum of the cables and by the light, which flickers when a great flex passes, and they treat that flicker as weather.

The sound does not layer so much as collide. The clatter of the armored sheets. The grind of the subsurface mouths. The tick-tick-tick of hooks. A deep tonal groan from the cables when a new fissure opens and the whole regional span of tissue rebalances its tension — a groan you feel in the sternum, long, and then cut off by the crack of rock and the papery rush of new growth. Above that, thinner cries, rhythmic, almost like speech and not speech, coming from high in the stacked hollows where the gliders roost and quarrel and launch. Nothing here is quiet enough to be background. Conversation, if you tried it, would be shouting, and shouting would be lost inside a minute. The air itself seems thick with vibration. Dust lifts and hangs in visible sheets, shaken free, settling, shaken free again.

And it does not settle into a pattern you can trust. A passage that was open narrows as cables on either side decide, simultaneously, to throw laterals across it; you watch a corridor become a thicket in the space of a slow breath, the new growth pale and soft at the tips, hardening to ceramic as it meets in the middle. Elsewhere a shelf of tissue, overloaded with the clattering swarms, tears free of its anchors and slumps into the hollow below, and the animals ride it down without seeming surprised, and the hollow receives the mass and begins, immediately, to digest and rebuild it into a different shape. Creation and wreck run on the same clock. The emerging rock does not get to finish being rock. The plants do not get to finish being a forest. The animals do not get to finish a migration before the country they are migrating through has become another country. From any high cable you can see this happening across the whole reachable span: bulges traveling, shelves collapsing, new vaults inflating, dust rising in discrete storms where a swarm has turned, light dimming and returning as the radiant rind is torn and replaced. It has the energy of a market and the energy of a landslide, and it does not pause between them.

Warmth comes up through everything. Not heat that burns — a constant, blood-warmth, stronger near the fresh fissures where the gas leaks, milder in the older vaults where the tissue has thickened and insulated itself. Sweat starts and does not cool you, because there is nothing for it to evaporate into that feels like air you know. The air is dry, mineral, faintly sweet, moving in gusts driven by the flexing of the great bundles rather than by any wind from outside. When a regional-scale tension releases, the gust becomes a shove. You brace. The dust blinds you for a moment. When it clears, the ladder you noted is gone, or the passage you came by is a wall, or a new mouth in the rind has opened three paces from your feet and is already closing, the grinding plates withdrawing, the callus racing inward, the clatter of the small armored things flooding over the scar as if it had always been there.

If there is a rule, it is only this: nothing in the underside is prior to anything else. Rock, tissue, animal, the few lashed platforms, the light, the noise — all of them are arriving at once, in a dry warmth, under a surface you cannot see, across a country of hollows that keeps exceeding the distance you can shout across. You do not walk through it so much as keep being offered a new version of it, loud enough that your own pulse disappears into the general thrum, dense enough that every reach of the hand meets a body or a cable or a hook, and unfinished enough that the meeting itself becomes part of what the place is still becoming.
