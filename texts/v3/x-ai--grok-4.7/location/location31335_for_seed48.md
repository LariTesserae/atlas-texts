The basin sits below the weather. Heat arrives from underneath as much as from above, and the sky never quite clears: a low lid of steam and sulfate haze, the color of old tin, so that noon is only a brighter species of dusk. The ground is not high enough to make a horizon that feels distant. The horizon is simply where the flats stop being countable.

Water is never a single thing here. It beads on pipework. It skins the concrete in a film that never quite dries. It stands in geometric ponds the color of weak tea, copper, and bottle-glass, divided by dikes of crushed mineral that have slumped and been rebuilt until the repairs are older than the originals. Between the ponds, the crust is damp salt and sinter, patterned with the small geometry of evaporation—polygons, then smaller polygons—established so long that walking on it is like walking on a decision the land made and then forgot. Rain is not rare. It falls warm, out of the haze, and does not seem to change anything except the smell, which goes briefly from metal to wet stone and then back.

Nothing green grows. The chemistry will not have it. What lives on the surfaces that look biological is a dark slick of bacteria and mineral stain, a varnish rather than a growth, thickening where warm seepage weeps from the dikes and thinning where the wind has polished the crust to a pale rind. No trees. No reeds. No shadow that is not thrown by a structure or by a body.

The structures are a city in the technical sense and almost not in any other. Pump houses, gantries, evaporator halls, rail spurs, a grid of power pylons walking off toward a low ridge that is only a wrinkle in the haze. They are spaced as if the builders were afraid of crowding the heat. Between one engine house and the next there is enough flat ground to lose a truck in the shimmer and find it again only by the tick of its cooling fan. Roofs are broad and low. Walls are stained to the same palette as the ponds. Concrete has spalled in the old places and been patched with plates; the patches have themselves gone soft at the edges, rounded by decades of warm wet air and salt. Nothing looks new. Nothing looks abandoned. The machines run. Most of them are far enough away that they register as a pressure in the ears rather than a noise.

People are scarce in the way spare parts are scarce: present, necessary, usually indoors. A figure on a catwalk. A lamp in a control room. The settlement is infrastructure wearing the outline of streets. Where housing exists it is bunkered against the glare that is not really glare, rows of small rooms with wet towels on the sills. Laundry never quite dries. Metal handrails are always warm to the touch, sometimes too warm.

The life is animal, and it is everywhere the water is shallow enough to be a skin.

Brine organisms cloud the ponds in pale billions, a living turbidity that turns with the slow pumps and piles in windrows along the lee dikes, pinkish, smelling of warm ocean and old metal. On every damp margin the flies rise in sheets when a boot disturbs them and settle again within a breath, so many that a dike can look furred, then bare, then furred. They do not sing. Up close there is a dry ticking, like seeds poured on tin, and then nothing. Flies walk the stains. Flies stitch the air a hand’s width above the crust and no higher, as if the haze itself were a ceiling they respect. In the culverts and warm sumps, fish the color of concrete hold in slow schools, mouths working, indifferent to light. Crabs the size of a palm work the outflow grates, precise, numerous, audible only as a faint china-click when they meet.

Above them the birds. Not a spectacle in the theatrical sense—no sudden cloud that darkens the dimness further—but a constant occupation of the middle distance. Waders on every dike-crest. Gulls loafing on transformer yards and on the pale crust between, spaced with the same economy as the buildings. They lift, settle, lift. Their cries are small and infrequent, lost in the size of the air. At times a whole pond’s surface shivers as something below turns as one body, and the birds adjust their feet and do not bother to call.

The volume of the place is still mostly empty. You can stand in the middle of a service road and see, in one turn, five engines, a million flies, a rack of insulators, and nothing that crowds you. The fauna occupies surfaces and skins of water. The air above shoulder height belongs to heat, haze, and the slow freight of sound: a pump cycling on three kilometers off, a rail car coupling once, the tick of cooling metal, then a long quiet in which you can hear water finding its level in a pipe.

The processes do not pause for the quiet. Pumps lift. Ponds concentrate. Warm seeps keep the crust from locking into true desert. Condensation crawls down the undersides of gantries and drips, all day, into stains that have had years to learn their shape. Birds trade places. Flies rise and lay and die in the same windrow, and the windrow is raked by machine on a schedule, and the schedule has been kept long enough that the piles have a preferred lee, a preferred height, a preferred smell. Weather moves through—sheets of warm rain, a heavier pall when the geothermal field to the east vents, a brief thinning of the haze near what must be evening, though the light does not so much fail as exhaust itself.

By the time the shift lamps come on they do not defeat the dimness. They make small accurate pools on catwalks and leave the ponds to their own pale burning, which is not light so much as the memory of light held in mineral water. The city continues its slow work. The flies settle. Somewhere a valve opens with a sound like a swallowed word, and the basin, already full of water and almost empty of everything else that is not alive, receives a little more.
