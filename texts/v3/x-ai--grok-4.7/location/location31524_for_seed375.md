The place is a floor, not a country. You come to its edge the way you come to the lip of a cistern: a pale rim of unbroken mineral, sharp enough that the palm hesitates, and beyond it a drop of only a few body-lengths into a basin no wider than a hamlet and no deeper than a church is tall. The rim is new. Its crest has not been rounded. Flakes still stand along it like the teeth of a saw that has never bitten, and where a flake has fallen it lies below exactly where it broke, bright-edged, not yet buried by anything.

Inside, the air is cool and does nothing. It does not move against the face. It does not carry scent far. It holds a dimness that is not dusk and not a roof — a gray milk, even, as if light arrives already tired and then simply stays. There is no sky in the ordinary sense, only the upper dark of the basin’s own walls closing over, and through that dark a slow pulse of paler and dimmer that has the period of weather without any of weather’s water. When the pulse brightens, every edge in the city throws a hairline shadow. When it dims, the shadows do not soften. They withdraw, intact, into the faces that cast them.

There is no water. Not in cracks, not as film, not as the memory of a stain. The cold here is the cold of stone that has never been wet. What would be dew in another country arrives instead as a faint rime of salts that do not melt; they flower. Overnight a wall grows a nap of crystals no longer than an eyelash, each one a little pipe or a little blade, and by the next dimming half of them have detached and lie in a glittering sift at the footings. The sift does not drift. Nothing drifts. It sits where it fell until something — not wind — takes it up again into the joints.

The city is built, and it is empty in the same breath. Lanes run true. Thresholds are cut square. Walls meet at angles a mason would trust, courses stacked in a rhythm that repeats and then, without announcement, inverts, as if the plan had been folded once through itself. There are stairs that climb three turns and open onto platforms with no further purpose than the view back down the stair. There are rooms the size of closets nested inside rooms the size of naves, and between them distances that feel wrong: a street you can cross in twenty paces, then a court so vacant the far wall seems to recede while you watch it. Infrastructure without inhabitants. Layer on layer — foundations visible where a newer wall has risen against an older one and not yet married to it, lintels seated on piers that are still growing their own capitals out of the pier-stone, as if the building were being remembered into place rather than assembled.

No tool marks. No devices. The joints are not mortared; they are continuous, the grain of the mineral turning the corner without a seam, the way a bone turns. And yet the plan is urban. Someone, or something that is not a someone, keeps a civic order: alignments held across the whole basin, a preference for right angles that occasionally fails into a shallow spiral, drains that are drains of nothing — channels cut to carry a flow that never comes, their beds sharp, unworn, already filling with the crystal-sift they were perhaps meant to refuse.

Vegetation is an argument the stone mostly wins. In three places, maybe four, a growth has taken a joint and treated it as soil. It is not green. It is the color of old paper and of tarnish, a low crust that lifts into spars no taller than a hand, brittle, branching once and then stopping as if the instruction ended. They do not sway. When the light-pulse passes over them they throw shadows as hard as the architecture’s. One of them, in the western court, has split a paving slab by the simple insistence of its base. The crack is new, bright inside, and the slab’s two halves have not yet settled apart. You can see the decision still happening.

Fauna is rarer than that. For a long time there is none, and the silence is so complete that the eye begins to invent motion in the crystal-nap and then correctly discards it. What lives here does not announce itself by sound, because nothing does. The basin has no echo worth the name. A fallen flake, if you were cruel enough to push one, would meet the floor and contribute nothing you could hear — or so the quiet insists, and the quiet has not been wrong.

Then a process declares itself by rearrangement alone.

Along the inner face of the northern wall, a band of stone the width of a road is darker than the rest, not stained but occupied. It migrates. Over the course of a light-pulse it advances a finger’s breadth toward the east, and the wall it leaves behind is smoother, younger, the nap not yet started. The band itself is a congregation: thousands of small bodies, each no larger than a seed, pressed flat, the color of the stone they eat and of the stone they become. They do not crawl in any way the eye can separate from the general dim. They are the dim, locally thicker. Where they have passed, the sharp newness of the masonry is reset — edges refreshed, a course re-cut by appetite, a lintel straightened by the subtraction of whatever had begun to bulge. They are the city’s only evident citizens, and they practice maintenance without rest and without noise. Sometimes a ribbon of them detaches and crosses a lane in a slow front, and the lane is clean behind them, sift gone, crystals harvested back into whatever chemistry they are. They do not drink. There is nothing to drink. They mine the dry and lay it down elsewhere as wall.

Other motions keep their own calendars, and none of them consult the ear.

A tower near the center — if tower is fair; it is a pier that decided, three stories up, to become a room with no door — sheds a scale of facing every so often. The scale does not fall. It peels, pauses at a lean, and is drawn back into the pier along a seam that opens to receive it, the way a lid accepts a page. Each time, the room’s plan is slightly otherwise: a corner migrated, a bench of stone that was not there before, or a bench gone. The city edits itself. Young features stay young because age is being eaten as fast as it forms. Erosion here is not the long work of weather. It is a countersignature. The basin is early, still in the hour of its first masonry, and it refuses to leave that hour.

In the lowest court, which is only a step lower than the rest and already feels like a cellar, a circle of paving rises and subsides. Not much. A palm’s thickness. The period is quicker than the light-pulse, slower than a breath you would bother to count. When it rises, the joints between slabs seal for a moment into a single dish; when it falls, the joints reassert, sharp, and a little air — or whatever stands in for air — is exchanged with the dark under the floor. Nothing visible comes up. Nothing goes down that you can see. But the crystal-sift never accumulates inside that circle. The city’s under-work takes it. Somewhere beneath the hamlet-scale plan, a second plan is turning, mineral into mineral, pressure into shape, and the dish is its only window, opening and closing without a click.

Rules, if they are rules:

Nothing here is wet, and nothing here is old for long. Sharpness is the native state. A broken edge is a recent edge; a rounded one is a lie, or a place the seed-bodies have not reached, and those places are few and look ashamed of themselves.

Sound is not absent because the place is dead. Sound is absent because the place does not spend itself on air. Motion happens in contact: body against stone, stone against stone, crystal unseating crystal, the dish kissing its own underside. If there is a noise, it is private to the materials. A visitor’s boot is the loudest event the basin has hosted, and the basin does not answer it.

Light does not reveal distance honestly. In the bright phase of the pulse, the far wall of a court looks near enough to touch and is not. In the dim phase, lanes that are short look like corridors drilled through a larger dark. The city uses this. Alignments that are true by pacing are false by eye, and the seed-bodies, which do not use eyes, keep the true ones.

What grows, grows once and stops. The paper-colored spars do not thicken with seasons. There may not be seasons. There is only the pulse, the migration, the peel-and-return of the tower’s face, the breathing dish, and the slow civic hunger that keeps every corner as fresh as the hour the basin first closed over itself.

You can walk all of it before the light changes twice. That is the scale: a settlement you could memorize, lanes you could name, rooms you could assign to purposes no one has. And still, standing in the central vacancy — the one court with no spars, no band of bodies, no dish — you understand the density correctly. The city is organized to the point of obsession, and it is almost nothing per volume. Walls are thin. Streets are hollow. The rooms hold only the dim. Between one presence and the next — a spar, a migrating front, the tower’s indecisive room — there is space enough to believe you are alone in a finished work, until the paving under one foot is a grain higher than it was, and the grain is the work, continuing, in a silence that is not peace and not threat. It is the sound of a place that builds by other means than noise, and that has not yet, in all its newness, found a reason to be heard.
