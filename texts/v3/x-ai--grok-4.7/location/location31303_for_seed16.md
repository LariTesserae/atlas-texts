The chamber is smaller than a town and larger than a room, and it has been both for so long that the distinction has worn smooth. You come into it the way you come into a cellar: a stoop, a drop of the shoulders, heat already on the face before the eyes adjust. There is no water anywhere in it. Not a seep, not a film, not the ghost of a stain that once was wet. The air is dry the way kiln-brick is dry, and it tastes of iron and old ash and a sweetness that does not belong to fruit.

The floor sits low, almost a basin, though nothing ever pooled here. Generations of feet and the slow slump of the walls have planed it into a shallow dish of packed grit, dark as spent charcoal, polished in the paths and coarse where no one walks. Those paths are few. Between them the ground opens into hollows — not pits so much as absences, places the builders left and the heat kept. You can stand in the middle of one and feel the settlement withdraw: a wall at the edge of sight, a lintel, then nothing but warm dark and the tick of cooling stone.

What grows here drinks heat, not water. The vegetation is lush in the only sense the place allows — thick, layered, insistent — but it is a lushness of crust and filament. Mats of rust-orange scale creep up every vertical face that holds the day’s stored warmth, overlapping like shingles, lifting at the edges where a new season of mineral has pushed under the old. From the joints of the walls hang curtains of pale thread, dry as hair, that sway when the air moves and shed a fine dust when they do not. In the hollows, where the dark is deepest and the stone stays hottest, bladder-like growths the color of old brass swell against the floor. They are not soft. Press one and it rings, faintly, and the ringing travels.

The settlement is built of the same stuff it stands in. Low courses of cut block, no mortar that you can see, each stone worn at the arris until the corners have become suggestions. Lintels are single slabs, sagged a finger’s width in the middle from an age of bearing. Doorways are narrow because the people who cut them were saving labor, not because anyone needed to keep weather out; there is no weather in the ordinary sense, only the breath of the chamber itself. Roofs, where roofs exist, are skins of the orange scale grown thick enough to shed the constant fall of dust. Most structures have no roof. They are rooms open to the greater room, their purposes readable in the wear: a bench hollowed by sitting, a niche blackened past the black of the stone, a row of pegs worn shiny where cords once hung and do not hang now.

The tech is what hands can do with stone, fiber, and fire. Cord is braided from the pale threads, surprisingly strong once twisted, and it laces bundles of the brass bladders together along the paths like markers, or like a ledger no one has explained. Blades are flakes. Fire is a trench of embers kept in a wall-niche, fed with dried scale, which burns slow and almost without smoke and leaves a white ash that the growers — if that is the word — scatter back onto the mats. There is no metal worth the name. A few pieces of native wire, bent and re-bent until they snapped, lie in a bowl by the ember-trench, kept because they were found, not because they are used.

Light never quite arrives. It leaks from the scale itself when the heat is high: a dull copper glow along the shingle-edges, enough to tell a path from a hollow, not enough to read a face across a room. Shadows pool and do not lift. At the far end of the chamber, where the ceiling drops until you must crawl, the glow fails entirely and the growth continues by touch, a darkness that is occupied rather than empty.

Nothing with eyes lives here. No tracks that are not feet. No webs, no husks, no scatter of bones. The ecology is the growth and the stone and the people who tend the line between them, and the people are few enough that you can walk the length of the place and meet no one, only the evidence that someone passed: a cord retied, a mat trimmed back from a doorway so the lintel can breathe, ash raked into a thin bright line.

It is loud anyway. The chamber speaks in its own processes, and they do not rest. The bladders tick as they swell and ease, a scattered clock with no agreement among its parts. Scale sheets flex against the walls with a sound like paper being smoothed, then crack — small, dry reports — when a lower layer pushes them free. Heat moves. You hear it as a low rush in the hollows, air trading places with air, and as a higher note where a fissure in the back wall vents a breath from deeper stone, hot enough to tighten the skin if you hold a hand in it. Cords thrum when that breath finds them. Somewhere a slab, worn past its balance, settles by a hair and the sound goes through the floor into the soles. People add to it without meaning to: the scrape of a flake on stone, the pat of ash, a voice that does not carry far and does not need to, because in this dryness every word arrives already close.

The weirdness is small and structural. The growth prefers the worked stone to the native wall, as if cutting woke something the rough face never had. Paths that are abandoned for a season vanish under mat, and paths that are walked stay clear even where no one trims them; the scale will not root in ground that is stepped on daily, and it will colonize a threshold the morning after the stepping stops. The brass bladders, if left in a pile, migrate. Not quickly. Over days they ease toward the hottest hollow, rolling by a patient collapse and reinflation, and the people stack them anyway, and restack them, and do not seem surprised. In the crawl at the far end, the filaments have braided themselves into a rough copy of a cord-lacing, knot for knot, though no hand has been back there in a long while. It does not lead anywhere. It simply repeats the pattern it found, faithfully, in the dark.

Nothing here is arriving and nothing is leaving. The erosion is finished in the way a well-used tool is finished: edges gone, purpose intact, the shape now the shape of the use. Heat cycles. Mats thicken, shed, thicken. Embers are fed and sink and are fed. The hollows stay hollow. The settlement holds its thin net of paths across a volume that is mostly unoccupied air, hot and dry and ticking, and the air, having nowhere else to go, stays, and works, and sounds.
