The height arrives before the ground does. There is a pressure in the ears, a thinning, a coolness that has no weather in it—only the fact of being too far up. The shelf is still deciding how high it means to be. In some stretches the stone lifts under its own unfinished weight, a slow architectural intention, and the air above it has not yet learned the manners of an atmosphere. It is dim without being night. Light comes as a bruised, even glaze, as if the sky were a membrane stretched too tight and too low, and the sun—if the smear on that membrane is a sun—cannot choose an edge.

Nothing here has been used. The rock is raw in the geological sense: jointed, but the joints are new, pale, almost sugary where they have split, and they have not been taught by wind or by anything else to round themselves. Erosion is a rumor the land has heard and not believed. Gullies exist as faint scores, lines that look like the beginning of a drawing rather than the memory of a flow. There is no flow. There has never been water. No seeps, no pans, no rime, no darkening where a storm might once have stood. The cool is a dry cool. It takes heat from skin and gives nothing back—no damp, no breath-fog, only a fine mineral meal that hangs and does not settle, because settling would require a patience the air has not finished inventing.

Across that openness—valley-wide, ridge after raw ridge, a country you could walk in a long day if walking obeyed itself—the growth stands in islands. Lush is the wrong word and the only one. Between the islands there is a great deal of nothing: bare plates of stone, scattered blocks that have not yet fallen so much as leaned out of the parent mass and paused. Then a grove. Then another, far enough that the first becomes a dark clot on the dim. Inside a grove the mass is obscene. Trunks of braided fiber, not wood and not quite crystal, rise and divide until the canopy knits. The fiber is cool to the touch and dry as bone left in a high room. It branches with the greed of a true forest. It throws a shade darker than the already-dark sky. It sheds, continuously and slowly, a scale-dust that does not rot, because rot would need water and the company of small lives, and there are no small lives. Nothing moves of its own will. No track, no wing, no click in the litter. The litter itself does not compact. It accumulates in soft, ankle-deep reefs of the same material as the living stands, and if you disturb it the disturbance remains, a pale scar, for longer than seems decent.

What feeds them is not moisture. Kneel—though kneeling is an act the sound will argue with—and the bases of the trunks are rooted in hairline vents, places where the unfinished crust exhales a thin, cold, non-aqueous breath: a sharpness, a metal-sweetness, a chemistry that beads as frost and is not frost, that wets nothing, that the fiber drinks by some surface hunger. The groves follow the vents the way ordinary forests follow seeps. Where the breath is stronger the canopy is tighter, almost woven, and the light inside goes from dim to a private dusk. Growth is measurable only if you are willing to stay through a season that does not announce itself. A new wrist of fiber. A pale ring. The shedding a little deeper around the bole. The land is embryonic, and so is its garden: lush already, and still arriving.

The sound does not arrive. It is the condition. At the rim of hearing—which is everywhere; there is no rim—you understand that the shelf is not quiet underneath a noise. The noise is the shelf being made. A roar, yes, but roar is a word for water and for crowds, and this has neither. It is the acoustic body of rock taking a shape it has not yet committed to: a broad, low, physical tone that sits in the chest and the teeth and the long bones, and over it a mesh of higher tones, some of them almost tidy, like struck plates, renewing themselves so gradually that a change in pitch might be the work of a year. It does not pulse. It does not rest. Close your eyes and the dark is the same volume as the light. Open them and the groves do not soften it; fiber drinks chemistry, not sound. The sound uses you as one more surface. After a while—minutes, or what you are calling minutes—you stop trying to locate a source. The source is the fact of elevation still occurring. Extreme height, here, is not a view. It is a process you are standing inside, and the process is deafening in the plain sense: it occupies the place where other sounds would live, and then the place where thought would speak to itself, and it does not negotiate.

Because of that, the built things are small and far apart, and they are all, in one way or another, about refusal.

You find the first as a change in the roar’s texture before you find it as an object: a pocket where one of the higher tones cancels, not into silence—silence is not available—but into a thicker, duller pressure, as if a bell had been wrapped in cloth. Inside that pocket, a wall. Low, dry-laid, courses of the local plates stacked with the care of someone who had only their hands and a great deal of time. No mortar. The stones are chosen for the way they break the air, not for beauty, though some of them have split along faces so new they flash even in this light. A second wall meets the first at an angle that is not quite a corner. A lintel, a single slab, too heavy for one person and yet clearly moved by persons, or by something with only the leverage of poles and patience. Under it, the dull pocket deepens. You can hear your own blood again, which is a kind of news. There are no tools left in this one. In the next, half a day’s walk if the walk holds, a scatter of hand-stone: a pounder, a crude pick bitten from a darker vein, edges bruised rather than sharpened, the kit of a people who had not come into metal or who had come into it and found metal shrieked in this air and gave it up. The work looks interrupted rather than ruined. A course stopped mid-wall. A socket started and not finished. As if the makers stepped out to listen for a better node and the listening took them.

Nothing occupies the shelters now. No bones—bone would imply the animals that are absent, or the dead of a species that might have been animal once. No ash. No midden. The fiber-dust has drifted in and made a clean bedding no one sleeps in. If there were inhabitants, they were thin on the ground, as thin as the groves, and they built only where the unfinished mountain accidentally argued with itself and made a little quiet. Most of those arguments have since drifted. You can walk the line of a wall and feel the pocket slide off it, the roar climbing back into your mouth, the wall suddenly just a pile, its purpose a few strides to the left of where the stones are. The makers, if they return, return on a schedule slower than weather. Nothing here hurries them, or anything.

That is the pace of the whole shelf: slow, and not peaceful. A block lean-out from a ridge may complete its fall in a decade, and the fall, when it comes, will be soundless only in the sense that it cannot be heard over the roar it joins. The fiber adds its rings. The vents breathe their cold un-water at a rate you would call steady if you had another season to compare. The dim does not lift toward evening or sink toward morning; it modulates, over long durations, as the membrane of the sky thickens or thins by a shade. You can watch a ridge for an hour and learn nothing. You can watch it for a year and learn that it has become slightly more itself.

And then the shelf misremembers.

This is not a haunting laid over a solid place. The place is the misremembering. Walk toward the second grove and the first is suddenly beside you, its canopy at your shoulder, though you have not turned and the pale litter underfoot is the litter you left behind. The horizon, which ought to fall away from an extreme height, sometimes stands up—becomes a wall of the same raw stone, close, as if the valley were the inside of a vessel and you had been on the inner curve all along. Shadows do not agree. In the groves they pool toward the smear of light; on the open plates they pool away from it; in the shelters they do not pool at all, but hang as flat dark shapes that do not belong to the walls. A ridge you used as a landmark is present twice, once ahead and once, with the same unweathered fracture, under your boots, and both are load-bearing. Distances between the shelters are not commutative. The walk back is not the walk out. It may be shorter. It may include a third shelter you did not build a memory of, its lintel already dusted, its pocket of dull sound waiting as if it had been tuned for you and then forgotten by the rock.

The weirdness is not spectacle. It does not flare. It is structural, and it keeps the same slow tempo as the growth and the lift. You notice it the way you notice the new ring on a trunk: by having been here long enough to know that a fact has edited itself. Causality frays at the edges. A plate you set upright in the litter is lying down, and the dust has already begun its long, indifferent covering, and your hands are still in the posture of setting it up. The roar develops a harmonic that is the exact pitch of a word you were about to think, and the word is spent, used up, unavailable. For a little while the fiber in the nearest grove is visibly lengthening—no, not visibly; you simply understand that lushness has increased by a small, real amount while you were looking at a stone—and then the understanding is older than the looking. The land is coming into being, and being is not required to happen in one order.

There is a rule, if the shelf can be said to have rules rather than habits it has not yet outgrown. Dryness is absolute. Nothing drinks, sweats, condenses, or weeps. The lushness is a mineral and fibrous abundance, a forest of intake without sap. Life, if the groves are life, is sessile and solitary in its kingdom: no pollinators, no grazers, no decay-fauna, no hunters of the things that are not there. What looks like ecology is chemistry plus time plus the accident of vents. Animal absence is not a silence—silence is impossible—it is a vacancy in the scheme. No eye has evolved to use the dim. No ear has evolved to parse the roar, which may be why the roar is free to be total.

Another habit: height is not stable, but it is always extreme. You do not come down. Paths that seem to descend deliver you to another reach of the same shelf, higher in the body if not on any instrument, the air thinner, the cool a degree more earnest, the sky-membrane closer. The openness remains. You can see a long way, across multiple ridges and the dark islands of canopy, and the seeing is not trustworthy and is also not a lie. Both groves are there. Both shelters. The unfinished gullies, still only drawings. The scattered walls, each one a small, primitive argument with a deafening that does not notice it has been argued with.

Stay, and the place continues its one slow act, which is becoming. The fiber thickens. The tone drops by a fraction no living interval could measure twice. A new vent opens with a sigh of that sharp un-water breath, and around it, not yet but soon—soon on the order of this country—another island of lushness will knot itself out of nothing wet. The light will not improve. Nothing with legs will cross the litter. In a pocket of dulled roar, a wall will be one course higher than it was, or one course lower, or standing in a shelter that was not here and has always been the one you meant to reach. The stone underfoot is new. It will be new for a long time. It is loud with the work of that.
