The ground is already warm before anything else registers. It gives heat up through the soles, a low steady return, as if the basin has been holding the day for so long that night no longer clears it. There is no high ground to climb toward cooler air. The land sits flat and old, a broad shallow bowl whose edges are only a change in the weight of the vegetation, not a horizon you can use. Whatever ridge once walled it has been worn down to a rumor. The rivers that remain have stopped arguing with their banks. They occupy the same lazy bends they have occupied for a very long time, brown and slow, more like trenches of warm water cut into leaf-mold than like streams with an ambition to reach the sea.

Moisture is not a climate so much as a series of agreements. Rain comes and goes without ceremony. Between rains the air stays thick enough to film the skin, and water gathers where it can: in the cupped leaves of plants that have learned to hold it, in the shade under roots, in hoof-prints and the collapsed mouths of old burrows, in a chain of oxbows that no longer connect to the channel except when the river, very occasionally, remembers them. Condensation beads on anything that stays still. Bark glistens. A stone left in the open grows a dark collar of damp and never quite dries. You can walk a long way and not find a pool you would call a lake, and still never find a place that is properly dry.

Under that regime the growth is rank and competitive and very old. It is not a garden. Stems pack against stems until the idea of a gap becomes theoretical. Canopy closes early and stays closed, so that even at the hour that ought to be noon the floor lives in a green-black dimness, a darkness with texture rather than a darkness with an edge. Leaves overlap in layers. Vines use whatever is already standing. Where a tree has finally given up, the gap is claimed before the wood is cold, filled from the sides and from below by things that were waiting in the shade for exactly that permission. The erosion here is finished work. Banks are root-bound. Slopes, such as they are, have settled into the angle the soil and the mat of roots will bear. Nothing is freshly cut by water. The place has arrived at its own shape and is busy maintaining it.

What passes for industry is the maintenance. Nothing was built. There are no walls, no roads, no roofs, no machines left by anyone who had hands. And yet the basin runs like a works that never shuts. Heat is the engine. Wet litter is the feedstock. Everywhere underfoot the ground is a slow furnace of decay, yards deep in places, giving off a sweetness that tips, if you breathe it too long, toward the smell of heated metal and turned earth. Fungi vein the fallen trunks in sheets and shelves, processing wood at a rate that would shame a mill if anyone were timing it. Insect galleries honeycomb the standing dead until a trunk is more tunnel than timber, a factory of chewing and carriage and brood, audible only if you put an ear to the bark. Beetles move burdens many times their size along routes that do not change from season to season. Termite soil is raised, used, abandoned, raised again, a mineral industry without a plan and without a pause. In the warm pools, mats of algae and bacteria crust and uncrust, fixing and unfixing, leaving mineral stains on every stick that trails in the water. On still afternoons the air above the seeps wavers, and a hand held near the mud can feel the faint, stupid heat of chemistry that has been running since before any of this growth had a name.

The dark is full. That is the first correction the eye has to make. It is not empty dark. It is occupied dark. Bodies are packed into it at every height: in the litter, in the root gaps, along the branches, against the trunks, in the warm film of the oxbows, in the air between leaves where there seemed to be only air. Most of them are not moving, or not moving in any way that spends energy. The heat punishes hurry. A thing that can wait, waits. Eyes take the light that there is and give back only a small coin of it. Flanks rise and fall. A hide shifts against bark and is still again. Something large stands in the water up to its belly and does not flick an ear for a long time. Something else is a weight on a limb that the limb has grown to accept. The basin is at carrying capacity in the plain sense: there is no room that is not already somebody's room. Territories overlap by necessity and are kept by presence more than by display. You do not so much walk through animals as through the intervals they have agreed, for the moment, to leave.

And it is quiet. The quiet is not peace and not absence. Wet air and layered leaves eat sound before it can travel. A call, when one comes, dies within a few trunks. Wings pass with a soft mechanical stroke and are gone. Water ticks from leaf to leaf on a schedule that has nothing to do with anyone listening. Now and then a branch complains under a relocated weight, a single low note, and the dark rearranges itself by a fraction and settles. The overwhelming fact of the life here is mass and attention and breath, not noise. If you stop, you begin to hear the smaller registers: the minute tick of mandibles in wood, the sip of a tongue at a pocket of water, the slow oral sound of something ruminating where it stands. Under all of it, always, the faint industrial hum of the ground itself, decay and seepage and heat, which is not a sound so much as the feeling that the place is working.

Nothing in it is in a rush to become something else. The river will cut a new bend, but not this year, and not so that you would notice if you came back after one. A tree falls on the time scale of its own rotting, and the gap it leaves is a rumor by the next wet season. Animals are born into a census that is already full, and the full census continues, patient, hot, dim, and exactingly occupied. The rules are ordinary. Water stays where the ground lets it. Heat does not leave at night. What can eat, eats; what can wait, waits; what dies is taken up into the works before it has finished cooling. There is no sign that anyone has ever tried to improve it, and no sign that it is waiting to be improved. It is simply, and at a scale you can walk across without ever coming to the end of the same conditions, completely itself.
