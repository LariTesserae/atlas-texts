The place is small enough to walk around before the light changes much, and dense enough that the walk never opens into a view. It sits low, in a bend where a slow river has spent a long time deciding not to go anywhere in particular. The ground is not soil in the ordinary sense. It is a mattress of roots, peat, leaf-mold, and the compacted trash of old cuttings, and it gives a little underfoot even where planks have been laid over it for decades. Water is never far. It stands in the ditches, beads on iron, darkens the lower courses of brick, and comes up through the root-mat in thin sheets after the midday heat has passed. Nothing here is flooded in a dramatic way. It is simply wet as a condition, the way a lung is wet.

What grows is nearly everything. Cane and a coarser reed fill the margins until the settlement’s edge is a wall you part with your hands. Figs and a few heavy, small-leaved trees have been left where their trunks serve as posts, and their roots have taken the pilings into themselves so that timber and living wood share the load. Creepers use pipe runs the way they use branches. On the drying racks, cut fiber is only a paler version of the same plant still standing in the water. Roofs are tile where the works required it, and elsewhere a thick, replaced thatch that has gone the color of old rope. Between buildings the gaps are narrower than a cart and are already half-filled: stacked bales, coiled hose, a ladder left where someone will need it again, leaves pressed flat against warm brick. You do not see sky in pieces larger than a handkerchief unless you are on the short wharf or inside the main shed, where the ridge lights are open to ordinary day.

The settlement is a works, not a ruin and not a new town. A fiber mill, a boiler house, a pump room, a dye shed, two rows of quarters, a store that also keeps the tally, a small clinic with a tin sink, and the yards between them. Brick is softened at the arrises. Iron has taken its rust and then stopped, painted where hands reach and left alone higher up. The piles under the walkways are grooved by years of the same waterline, not eaten through. Banks have found their angle. Nothing is being reclaimed by wilderness, because the cutting never stopped, and nothing is being cleared for a larger plan, because there is nowhere to expand that the river bend and the cane wall have not already refused. The balance is maintenance. People cut on a schedule measured in months. They replace a board. They clear a sluice. The plants answer on their own, slower clock.

It is warm without being punishing, a heat held in by leaves and given back by brick in the late afternoon. Steam from the plant vats thickens the air in the yards and then thins. Condensation gathers under the shed eaves and falls in single drops onto tin, a small irregular ticking under the larger noise. The light is plain daylight, not green gloom and not glare. It comes down through the ridge glass, off the canal, and through gaps in the cane, and it is enough to read a gauge by and to see the grain of a cut stalk. Shadows are short-lived. Leaves move them.

Animals are scarce. A few insects attend the wet fiber and the dye vats; something small sometimes crosses a plank at dusk and is gone. There is no chorus. The loudness is the works. Beaters turn in the long shed with a heavy, repeating concussion you feel in the sternum before you sort it from the other sounds. Pumps lift brown water and let it down again over a weir at the lower end, a steady pour that never hurries. Valves spit. A belt slaps. Hand-carts knock on the plank walks. People call short distances because a longer call would be lost, and their voices sit inside the machine noise rather than above it. At night the beaters stop and the pumps do not. The place gets no quieter than a weir.

Change is patient. The river lifts a hand’s width in the wet months and settles back. A fig root thickens around a bolt. A course of brick is repointed and looks, within a season, as if it had always been the softer one. Bales leave on the shallow boats that nose in at the wharf, and new cane is cut from the same beds that supplied the last load. No one is in a rush that would show. The engines run because the fiber is there, and the fiber is there because the engines, the cutting, and the wet low ground have been keeping one another’s hours for longer than the paint on the boiler-house door. You could cross it in twenty minutes if the walks were clear. They are not clear. You go by inches, between leaf and pipe and warm brick, with water sounding under the planks the whole way.
