The shelf is small enough that its edge is always a fact. From the middle of the grown lanes you cannot see the drop, but you can feel the place ending: the warmth in the floor thins toward the rim, the press of bodies loosens by a hand’s width, and the canopy, which everywhere else knits shut, leaves a pale seam of ordinary sky. Walk the length of it and you are not gone an hour. It is a hamlet of stone and resin, a cathedral of rooms that were never cut, a single feature with a boundary you could trip over.

The stone is new. Nothing here has had time to be rounded. The rim is a decision—glass-bright fractures, edges that would open a palm, corners still arguing with the air. There is almost no talus. What has broken off has not yet learned to be gravel; it lies in plates, still fitting the scars they left, as if the shelf only recently finished arriving and has not agreed to shed. The color of the rock is the color of something that cooled last, not last era: pale, faintly lilac in the grain, warm if you keep your hand on it. Formation heat is still in it. That is why the height does not make a cold. The shelf sits up above a dun haze that never shines. Down there is not a valley you could name. No thread of water catches the day. The haze is dry as the stone, and the day itself is a normal day—sun, cloud-shadow, a blue that does not perform. Only the light is ordinary. Everything it falls on has had to invent another way to be alive.

Nothing drinks. That is the rule you learn by absence, and then by touch. Leaves are lush and they are dry. They crowd, overlap, fail to leave a gap, and they do not glisten. No dew. No film that would wet a fingertip and vanish. The green is real chlorophyll green in the upper blades, but it is a waxy, meaty green, thick as a thumb, matte, slightly tacky with a resin that is not sap in any watery sense. Break a leaf and it does not weep. It exhales a slow, warm smell—mineral, sweet-fat, a little like warm stone and crushed seed—and the wound skins over with the same tallow the plant runs on. Lower down, the growth stops pretending to be leaves. In the cracks, where the young stone is still split sharp, a denser garden stands: sponge-forms, fan-forms, things like coral that have never known a tide, russet and olive and a dull gold, packed until they touch. They face the cracks, not the sun. You can watch it if you are patient, which is the only speed the shelf allows. Over a long afternoon a fan may tilt a finger’s width toward a seam that is warmer than its neighbors. The light is for making. The meal comes up from the floor.

What comes up is not water and not steam. In the freshest fractures a clear gel gathers, slow as an hour hand, neither wet nor dry in the way a visitor means those words. It is a mineral exudate—silica-fat, salt-sweet, warm as skin—and the gardens feed with their undersides clamped to it. The gel never pools. It is taken as it forms. Where a crack is too new even for that, the stone simply smells of it, and the finest growth, a velvet of mineral lichen that is somehow lush, drinks the smell. Carbon comes from the air. Electrons come from the rock. Thirst is not a category here. Animals do not seek a stream because there is no stream and no word for what a stream would fix. Their flesh runs on the same solvent as the plants. Cut one, if you could bear to, and you would not find the bright water of blood.

They are many, and they are quiet, and they are the reason the shelf is a city.

They pack the volume the way the leaves pack the light. Near-touching is the ordinary condition: a lane is a gap two bodies wide, maintained because bodies keep using it, not because anyone cleared it. Above the lanes the canopy locks. Below them, root-vaults and resin shelves make a second story that is really a cellar, and in places a third, where the sponge-forms have been grown, or have grown themselves, into partitions. You move through layers. A shoulder brushes a flank. A flank brushes a wall that is also a flank—some of the architecture is animal, sessile, still alive, pulsing on a cycle so slow you only notice when you come back and a doorway has narrowed overnight by the thickness of a nail.

No one built this with a tool. There is nothing to build with that is not already growing or already a body. No metal, no fired clay, no wheel, no cord drawn from a twisted fiber and then left as cord. What looks like infrastructure is agreement. A threshold is a stem that learned to pinch. A roof is a thatch of blades that interlaced and then resin-welded where they crossed. A stair is a stack of plates the stone itself shed, seated into place by weight and by the habit of feet, not by a hand that planned a riser. Chambers exist where a cluster of the sessile masons died in a ring and the living ones closed over the absence, or where enough of the walking kind slept in one posture for enough seasons that the tallow shelled them a room and they kept the room after the shell hardened. It is organized all the same. The shelf has wards.

On the warmer east cracks the sponge-forms dominate, and the walking kind there are low, many-padded, broad as bread-loaves, their hides the color of the gel they lap from the seams. They do not lap noisily. A mouth meets the crack and the gel is gone and the only sound is a small tick as the stone cools where it was touched. Their ward smells sweetest. Lanes there are floored in shed skin, laid and laid until it is a carpet, springy, silent. In the mid-shelf, where the canopy is oldest—and oldest here means a few long seasons, not an age—the blades have made halls. Light comes down ordinary and arrives amber, not because the sun is strange but because wax filters it. In that ward the animals are climbers, slender, with hands that are only hands, no hook or knife. They comb the blades. Combing is how a lane stays a lane. A parasite-moss, dry as the rest, will felt a passage shut if it is not combed, and the combing is done with fingers, hour after hour, a civic act that looks like grooming. They do not speak while they do it. They do not speak.

Sound has almost nowhere to happen. The air is still. What little flux there is moves the way the fans move: across long durations, a weak lean of warmth from the sunlit rim toward the shaded, a breath you would not call wind. No water converses with stone. No wing claps; the fliers, and there are fliers, abundant in the upper lace of the canopy, are light-bodied and fringed, and they pass through with a hush that is mostly the sound of your own attention. Calls would be a waste. Everyone is already touching, or about to be. What must be said is said by a flush of color under thin hide—an arm going dusk-colored, a throat briefly gold—or by where a body stops. A lane blocked by three loaf-bodied sitters is a closed market, or a grief, or a vote. You learn the difference by the smell they lay down, a grammar of resins dabbed on the carpet of skin. The shelf is loud, if you are the kind of creature who reads that. To an ear it is a hush with accidents: a resin pod ticking as it tightens in the afternoon cool; a plate of rim-stone answering the sun with a single high note so faint it might be inside your head; once in a long while, a young fracture giving, not as a crack but as a sigh, the shelf still finishing the gesture that raised it.

The west ward is the newest and the strangest, and it is strange only by a little—the whole place is unusual the way a rule is unusual when you have lived by another. There the stone is sharpest, the gel scarcest, the growth more velvet than hall. The animals of that ward are small and numerous past counting, a glittering of quiet bodies in the lichen, and they are the builders of the finest work: granules carried one by one, cemented with a dab of body-fat, making walls so thin the ordinary light comes through them rose. It is urban at the scale of a thumb. Courts, alleys, a plaza the size of a palm where they exchange nothing you would call goods—only position, only the right to stand where the evening warmth pools. Zoom out and their city is a crust on the crust of the larger city. Zoom out again and the larger city is a crust on the shelf. Density all the way down.

Nothing is in a hurry, and nothing is finished. The shelf is still rising. You will not see it rise. Sit from first light to last and the rim is where you left it, the haze the same dun, the sun doing only what suns do. But a mark you press into a resin lintel will, across a span of seasons, sit differently against the far haze, a finger higher, and the crack you chose for its warmth will have opened enough to feed a new fan, which will have turned toward it, which will have pinched a new threshold, which some climber will have begun to comb. Erosion, such as it is, has barely started. The sharpness is not a ruin’s sharpness. It is the sharpness of a thing just born, colonized immediately because there is nowhere else this solvent, this warmth, this mutual touching can occur. Below the haze, if there is a below, it does not offer this. The shelf is the whole habitable world at this height, and it is full.

At the rim, if you go there in the middle of the day, the ordinary light is almost harsh. The stone burns warm, not hot. A loaf-bodied sitter may be there, broadside to the drop, not looking down—there is nothing down worth a color-flush—but warming the gel in its belly, being a slow engine. Beside it a blade leans over the edge and stops, because past the edge there is no crack to face. The boundary is that clean. You can hear, in that place, the near-silence at its purest: your breath, the sitter’s breath, neither of you thirsty, the pod-ticks far behind in the canopy, and under your feet a patience so large it feels like stillness. The city at your back is dense enough that if you turn you step at once into bodies and leaves and the amber of filtered day. Ahead is height, and young air, and a dryness without desert’s theater—no dunes, no bones, no wind to arrange them. Just the shelf, recently emerged, already lived in to its last sharp inch, changing at the rate of resin and heat, saying nothing, because everyone who needs to know is already close enough to touch.
