The plain has no edge you can walk to. It has weather instead of a border, and the weather does not end.

From any standing place the ground runs out in a color that is not quite a color—warm gray, wet clay, the dull tin of water lying in a film so thin it is only a different way for light to fail. The sky sits low and does not lift. Between them there is almost no vertical room, and what room there is is already full: mist that will not decide whether it is air or rain, silt lifted and not yet willing to settle, a warmth that does not come from sun so much as from the long work of water moving through stone that has forgotten how to be high. You are warm. Your clothes never dry. The light is the color of the inside of a closed hand.

There used to be mountains. The plain still knows their names the way a mouth knows a word after the teeth are gone. What remains is grain. A darker streak in the mud that was a range. A sweetness in the mineral taste of the water where a peak stood long enough to be rained into nothing and then rained on after that, for an age that had no one in it to count. The land has reached the shape it was always practicing: nearly level, nearly finished, still not allowed to stop. Relief is measured in the height of a ripple, the lip of a pool, the small courage of a stone that has not yet lain down. Stand on the tallest thing for a day's walk and the horizon does not drop. It thickens.

Water is not a feature here. It is the condition under the other conditions. It lies in sheets no deeper than a wrist over hardpan polished by its own patience. It beads on every surface that offers a face, and most surfaces do, because the air is always on the point of giving itself up. Rocks weep without having been wet by any fall you saw; the fall is happening inside the temperature, a fraction of a degree, endlessly. After rain—and there is always an after, and always a before braided through it—the plain looks the same as before the rain, only louder. Marsh takes the slight depressions and holds them as dark lace, warm, smelling of metal and leaf-rot from plants that are mostly elsewhere. In the shallows the bottom is visible and also not: a skin of clay breathes, lifts, settles, and the mark of your boot fills before you have finished the step.

The vegetation is an apology. Mats of something like salt-crust and something like liver, green only when the dim finds a seam in the cloud and then only for a minute. A reed-island, rare enough to be a destination, rooted in a raft of its own dead and drifting when the sheet-water decides to have a current. No forests. No shade except the shade of weather. What grows grows low and bets on being missed by whatever is moving. Most of the occupation of this place is not alive. The density is physical. There is no empty cubic meter. Vapor, grit, spores of mineral, the foam-line of one wind dying into the start of another, droplets too small to fall and too heavy to be air—these stand shoulder to shoulder from the mud to the cloud deck, and the cloud deck is not a ceiling so much as the next density, packed until light arrives already tired. You do not see far. You see deeply into a short distance, and the short distance is crowded with the suspended wreckage of the last hour's wind.

Sound has the whole fetch of the continent and nothing to break against. It does not echo; there is nowhere for an echo to turn around. It arrives already multiple, already late and early, a pressure more than a set of notes. Rain on water is the ground-tone, a hiss raised to the size of a country. Over it, wind with a thousand kilometers of open warm flat to organize itself, shearing, folding, dropping in slabs you feel in the sternum before you hear them as weather. Thunder does not punctuate. It inhabits. Storms here are not events passing over a landscape; they are provinces, slow-turning, grinding their edges against neighboring provinces, and where two of them meet the sound becomes a wall you can lean on and not fall through. Under all of it, rarer, a low hydraulic creak—the plain adjusting, water finding a new film-path through clay that has been sorted for so long it is nearly a single thought. Speak and you do not hear yourself. The place speaks in your mouth.

Animals are almost a rumor, and the rumor is hard to credit because nothing here rewards a body that must listen in order to live. What persists is small, low, and largely deaf to the meanings other worlds put in sound: a armored thing in the reed-raft that eats the mat and is eaten by the water's acidity when it is careless; pale crawlers in the warm film, visible as a brief disorder in the silt; high up, sometimes, a single shape crossing from one storm into another, too far to name, using the wind the way a stone uses a slope. You can walk for days and count the lives you meet on one hand, and still never feel alone. The plain is occupied by processes. They do not look at you. They also do not leave a gap for you to stand in unbothered. Every surface is being worked. Every silence you think you have found is only a frequency your ears have given up.

Change does not visit. It is the resident. The form of the land is ancient—the end-state, the flattened argument, the oldest stable lie—but the skin will not accept stability as a resting place. Pools open where no pool was at dawn, not by flood but by the clay sighing down a finger's depth and calling it a basin. Wind builds dunes of wet silt the height of a person and unbuilds them before they can dry enough to remember being dunes. Foam-streets form along shear-lines and march, luminous in the dim, and where they pass the ground is rearranged into ribs that will not survive the next street. Creation and destruction are not alternating seasons. They are the same gesture seen from two sides of the mist, and the mist does not take sides. Walk a line you believe is straight and by evening the straightness has been distributed into other people's weather. The map, if anyone had carried one, would describe a place that existed for an afternoon.

The rules are local and they do not hold still long enough to be written down, but some of them recur, the way a scar recurs.

Down is a courtesy, not a law. In the oldest flats, where the erosion has gone past shape into habit, water sometimes climbs a lip it should not climb, not as magic announced, but as a tiredness in the grain: the stone was laid down under a gravity that has since been averaged with other gravities, and in certain seams it still answers the older one. You learn not to trust a slope by its look. You learn by whether your feet agree with your eyes, and they often do not, and the disagreement is small and nauseating and gone when you step back.

Distance is packed the way matter is packed. A storm that is a day's walk off can put its rain on your shoulders while its body stays on the horizon, as if the space between had been folded to save room. Conversely, a reed-island you can see—dark comma, the only vertical mercy—may take three days to reach, and when you reach it, it is not the one you saw. It is a cousin. The one you saw is still there, smaller, in a direction your sense of behind no longer believes in. People who stay long enough stop pointing. Pointing is a claim about space the plain does not co-sign.

Sound outruns cause. You will hear a sheet of wind cross you, lay you half down, and only afterward see the dark of it arrive, as if the noise had been sent ahead as a scout and the air were following out of obligation. Sometimes the reverse: the dark arrives in silence, and the roar comes up out of the ground minutes later, belonging to a passage already gone, dutiful, late, deafening anyway. No one who lives with this—there are not many—trusts an alarm. The alarm is part of the turbulence. It does not refer reliably to what is about to happen to a body.

Light leaks. Not brighter, never brighter for long, but wrongly. A patch of amber on the water with no seam in the cloud to justify it, showing a bottom that is not the bottom under your feet: clearer, older, with the ghost-relief of hills that finished their dying before this sky was this sky. Step toward it and it stays the same size, which is how you know it is not near. Look away and it is on your other side. Reflections are not bound to their objects. A pool will hold, for an hour, the vertical shadow of something standing, and there is nothing standing, and the shadow does not ripple when the pool does. The plain is so worn that separate times no longer have ridges between them to keep them apart. They ooze. You walk in the present and occasionally put your foot through into a minute that has not agreed to be over.

The built things are few, and it is a kindness to call them built. Tech, if the word means devices, is absent; nothing hums that did not learn to hum from wind and water. What remains are marks that might have been intention and might have been the plain imitating intention, which it does, in its density, the way a turbulent surface will momentarily counterfeit a pattern.

A ring of stones, none taller than a knee, so flush with the mud that you find them with your shin. They are warmer than the ground. They have been warmer for longer than warmth has a reason. Inside the ring the film of water is still, which is so contrary to the local law that people—when there are people—leave offerings they do not name and do not stay to see taken. The stillness lasts until it doesn't. Then the ring is only stones again, and by the next storm two of them have lain down and the circle is an arc, and an arc is just weather that hasn't finished.

Farther, and rarely, a single wall. Not a ruin in the romantic sense: no height to mourn, no rooms. A course of fitted blocks sunk to their tops, running for a hundred paces and then ending in a place where the fitting gives up and the blocks become lumps and the lumps become the plain. The joints are tighter than any hand working without tools should have managed, and there are no tools, and no chips, and no quarry, because the whole continent is the quarry and it has already been spent. The wall does not divide anything. Water crosses it without noticing. Its only function now is to be a discrepancy: a straightness in a world that has sanded straightness down to rumor. Stand with your palm on it and you feel, under the deafening, a finer vibration, regular, like a word repeated until it is only rhythm. It is not a machine. It is the memory of a joint that once held, still holding in a frequency the air is too thick to let die.

Some of the marks are not stone. On certain mats, after a particular wind, you find lines pressed into the living crust—parallel, then branching, then parallel again—as if something had written while the mat was soft and the mat had chosen, narrowly, to keep the writing as scar. The script, if it is script, is not repeatable. Copy it and the copy is only grooves. The original, while it lasts, has the property that looking along it changes the order of the sounds in your head: the roar sorts, for three breaths, into something like syllables, and then the next gust unscatifies it and you are deaf again in the ordinary way. No one has agreed whether this is address or accident. The plain produces so many almost-patterns that meaning would be the stranger claim.

There are no roads. Passage is a negotiation with the sheet and the shear. You walk the slight rises where the clay has armored itself, and you wade where you must, and you do not camp in a hollow, because hollows are where the plain practices being a mouth. Shelter is a mathematical error. The best you get is a lee that lasts as long as the storm that makes it, which is to say you are hiding inside the thing you are hiding from, in the pocket where two densities argue and neither has won. Fires are difficult. Smoke has nowhere to go; it joins the packed air and comes back down as a warm foul rain. Food is the mat, the crawler, the occasional mineral crust that will not poison you if you learned the color from someone who is no longer here to be asked. Most who cross are crossing toward the idea of an edge. The idea is the last tall thing, and it erodes like the others.

At night—which is not dark, only a dimmer dim, the cloud deck bruising into a brown that eats the amber—the turbulence does not sleep. It changes instrument. The wind drops half a tone and seems, falsely, kinder. Phosphor wakes in the film, not the sea's polite glitter but plates of slow light under the surface, large as doors, drifting with currents that do not match the currents you can feel against your ankles. Where a plate passes under a weeping rock, the rock's wet face answers with a brief internal shine, as if light were a liquid that had found a path through both of them. You can navigate by this if you are desperate and wrong. The plates are not going anywhere you are going. They are the plain thinking about other arrangements of itself, visibly, and discarding them.

What emerges, if you stay long enough for the first images to wear off the way the mountains wore off, is not a scene. It is a condition of matter that has used up its shapes and kept its energy. Ancient in section, violent in plan. Vast enough to have a climate with no outside, packed enough that vastness never becomes emptiness. Warm, wet, low, loud. Life thinned to a few specialists and a scattering of scars that might once have been decisions. The rest is process, continuous, deafening, and slightly unfaithful to the world that taught it physics.

If there is a center, it is only the place where the wearing-through is worst: a wide stillness inside the noise, not silent—nothing is silent—but simpler, one note instead of the chord, and in that simplification the ground is transparent to other grounds. You see, under the film, the same plain, and under that another, each a little less eroded, each a little more willing to have been mountains, stacking down into a depth the flatness should not be able to contain. They move at different speeds. A hill in the deepest one is still deciding to fall. It has been deciding for an age. On the surface, its decision arrives as a shudder in the clay, a new weep in the rocks, a roar with no cloud attached to it, and a smell of wet stone from a quarry that no longer exists at any height a body could stand on.

You step back. The shudder passes into the general turbulence and is spent. The film closes over the view. Warm rain that might not be rain settles on your hands. Somewhere a reed-island is either closer or further, and the plain, packed to its edges with its own unfinished ending, continues the work of being flat and the work of never being finished, both at full volume, with no room between them for anything to be spared.
