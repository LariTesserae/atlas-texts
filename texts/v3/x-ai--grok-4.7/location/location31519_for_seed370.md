The chamber is smaller than a chapel and fuller than a storehouse, and it has no outside left to speak of. What was once a high shelf of work—raised by hand, course on course, until the makers stood above whatever country they had left—has closed over itself. The last opening was bricked from the inside. You do not see sky. You feel height only as a dryness in the throat and a thinness in the breath, as if the air had been lifted with the walls and then forgotten there.

Everything you can touch was made. There is no bedrock showing, no seam of native stone, no pocket of soil. Floor, spring of the vault, the vault itself, the fill that has swollen into the vault: fired clay, lime-tempered brick, river-cobble set in mortar, slabs dressed with stone hammers and then set edge to edge until edge stopped meaning anything. The work is primitive in the old sense—no metal left in it, or none that still reads as metal. A few green crumbs in the joints might once have been pins. They have gone to dust and the dust has gone into the lime. What remains is hand-knowledge: thumb-hollows, cord-marks, the drag of a wooden float, the place where a wrist tired and the course went a finger out of true. Age has not erased the making. It has finished it. Joints have sintered. Arrises have rounded until a corner is only a change in the light. The whole interior has settled into one warm mineral body, the way a kiln settles after the last firing is allowed to die down and then, somehow, does not.

It is warm. Not hot—nothing glows, nothing smells of scorch—but warm the way a wall is warm on the side that faced a day’s sun, except there is no day to face. The heat is even. Palm to brick, palm to the lip of a buried jar, palm to the plaster that has become indistinguishable from brick: the same mild fever. It does not rise toward the crown or pool at the floor. Equilibrium has eaten the gradients. If you stand long enough for your own skin to match it, you stop being sure which warmth is yours.

There is no water. Not a seep, not a salt crust, not the dark map of an old drip. The clay was wetted once, in the making, and that wetting is a fossil now—pores collapsed, surfaces tight, a dryness that feels older than thirst. Nothing grows on it. Nothing could. No film, no fiber, no pale fan in a crack. The cracks themselves have closed. What looks, in the dim, like a root-trace is a trowel scar filled with finer slip and then worn flush.

Light comes from nowhere you can point to. A low amber, grainy, as if the clay itself were remembering fire at a great distance. You can see your hands. You cannot see far, and far is not available anyway. The room is packed. Vessel against vessel, flue against flue, the hollow bodies of hand-built resonators stacked and mortared until the negative space is only the space inside them—and even that is shared, mouth to mouth, throat to throat, a continuous interior no wider in most places than a wrist. Where a person might once have walked, the walk has been filled. Shoulders of brick press your shoulders. A course of pots, their rims ground flat by centuries of contact, occupies the volume where your chest wants to be, so you turn sideways and even then the place does not make room; it lends you a crevice and takes it back in the next breath. Density here is not clutter. Clutter implies neglect, gaps, the possibility of rearrangement. This is saturation. Every presence touches every other presence. There is no interval left for a change to pass through.

The sound is the interval that remains.

It does not arrive and it does not leave. It is not wind—there is no wind—and it is not an animal, and it is not weather. It is the voice of the packed hollows driven by the warmth itself: a pressure so steady, so perfectly matched to the network of throats, that the air inside them holds one chord and cannot fall out of it. The note is low enough to live in the sternum and high enough, in its partials, to needle the teeth. It does not swell. It does not thin when you cover your ears; covering your ears only moves the hearing into bone. Near a wall it is the wall. In the crevice it is the crevice. There is no quiet corner because there is no corner the chord does not already occupy. Deafening, then, not as a blow but as a climate. You stop waiting for it to resolve. Resolution would be a change, and the chamber has finished with change.

Stand in it—if standing is the word for being slotted among the pots—and the first impression is violence: too loud, too close, too warm, too full. That impression does not develop. Nothing develops. The handprints do not become clearer. The chord does not find a melody hiding inside it. A hairline you were sure was a crack is only a shadow of an old cord-mark, and it will be that shadow for as long as the mass holds its heat, which is as long as the mass holds. There is no drip to count. No insect to track across a rim—there are no insects. No dust motes riding a draft; the air is either inside the sounding throats or pressed flat against fired skin, and in neither place does it wander.

What is slightly strange is not a apparition. It is the competence of the stillness. Primitive hands built a thing that could have cracked, slumped, gone cold, gone silent. Instead every fault has already occurred and been absorbed. The chamber has reached the shape it was always going to rest in, and the sound is not an event happening inside that shape. It is the shape, heard. A single small room, high above a world it no longer admits, warm, dry, blind of life, packed to the last finger-width with work, and loud with a note that has become, like the brick, a final surface—smooth, continuous, and no longer in the business of becoming anything else.
