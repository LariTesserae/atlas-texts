You hear it before there is anything to see. The sound comes up through the turf as a pressure in the feet, then as a dulling of the other noises—the wind in the grass goes thin, your own breath loses its edges—and only after that as a sound you could point to. It sits low in the headland, inland of the cliff by less than a shout, under a sag in the ground where the grass grows longer and lies one way, combed by air that is always leaving a crack.

The crack is not a cave mouth. It is a split in the lip of the sag, shoulder-wide, with a lip of stone worn to a dull polish by hands and by weather in proportions you cannot separate. Cold comes out of it steadily. Not a wind with a direction so much as the overflow of air that has been sitting on stone. If you lie on the turf and put your face to the dark, the roar arrives whole. Speech is already useless there. A shout becomes a shape in the mouth and nothing in the ear.

Down inside, the place is a room that has forgotten it was ever meant to be entered. Low, in the way a cellar is low: the land above it is only a skin of soil and root over rock, and the rock itself barely clears the reach of the highest tides on the outer coast. You are not up anywhere. You are under a fold. The chamber opens after a short twist—three body-lengths of stooping, one place where the passage pinches and the sound changes register, a higher note bored into the bone—and then a hollow the size of a small barn, taller than it first seems because the floor drops away into shadow and the ceiling holds a shallow dome. The volume is almost empty. That is the first correction the eyes make, once they accept how little light there is. You expected wreckage, water to the knees, a throat full of sea. What you get is air, a great patient quantity of it, and the sound occupying the air the way smoke occupies a closed kitchen, evenly, with nowhere left to go.

Light enters as a grey thread from the crack behind you and as a second, meaner seam high on the seaward wall, where the dome has worn thin and a blade of day lies across one rib of stone. Neither is enough to read by. The palette is what darkness allows: wet black, a brown that is only black with water on it, and, where the drip has fed the same patch for longer than any tool has existed here, a green so dark it reads as a stain until you are close enough to see that it is living and thick. The green is the lushness of the place, and it does not fill the room. It coats. On the upper ledges, where condensation gathers and the seam-light lasts an hour past the rest, the moss stands in cushions deep enough to take a fingerprint and return it slowly. Ferns, small and strict, grow in the cracks that stay damp without staying drowned. A skin of algae marks the line where spray, on the few days the outer sea is angry enough to find the seam, has salted the stone and then been rinsed by seep. Lower down the walls go bare again. The floor is mostly naked rock, scalloped, with pockets of sand no larger than a basin, and one bowl of water that never quite dries and never quite joins anything else.

That bowl is the water of the room. Everything else is intermittent: beads on the ceiling that fatten, let go, and tap the floor in a pattern you could map if the roar let you think in patterns; a thread down the landward wall after rain, bright for a day, then a darkening; damp that wicks into cloth and into the moss and into the little sand-pockets and stays there, cold. The sea is not in the chamber. It is in the wall. Two arm-lengths of rock, maybe three, separate the hollow from the cliff face, and in that thickness the waves do their old work—compressing air in some outer notch, driving it through flaws, striking the same planes they have struck since the headland was this height. What arrives inside is the consequence. A boom you feel in the teeth when a larger wave lands. A hiss that is spray becoming mist in a passage you cannot see. A ringing, high and constant, which is the room itself, its particular length and its particular dome, answering. The three do not take turns. They stack. After a short while the ears hurt in a flat way, without drama, and the hurt is the local weather.

The stone has the look of a thing that finished arguing with the water a long time ago and has since only been maintaining the agreement. Edges that once might have been sharp are fluted. The floor’s scallops are even, not fresh gouges; sand in the pockets carries a film of silt that says nothing has rolled here this season, or the season before. A fall of blocks on the far side has settled into the wall so completely that the joints are darker with moss than the faces, as if the collapse were a feature the room grew rather than an accident it suffered. Change is still happening—every bead that falls is a change—but it is the kind you would have to sit a year to witness as difference rather than as repetition. The tide, outside, shifts which of the stacked notes sits on top. That is the fast end of the local clock. The slow end is the moss adding the thickness of a fingernail, and the bowl’s water freshening by seep and thinning by whatever salt mist the seam admits, layering and unlayering without ever becoming a stream.

Almost nothing else lives here in any way you can count. The roar is a poor neighbor. At the crack, on the turf, a bird sometimes crosses, small and uninterested, and does not come down. In the bowl, if you cup the water and wait for your eyes to use the seam-light, there are a few pale specks that move when the water is disturbed and otherwise might be grit. High on one dry rib there is an old streak of white, guano from a roost abandoned long enough that the streak is only a mineral fact, not a sign of tenants. No tracks on the sand-film. No webs worth the name; the damp and the air-shift defeat them. The emptiness is not a clearing where animals have gone elsewhere for the hour. It is the ordinary population of a room that offers cold, dark, wet stone, and a noise like a mill that never stops.

People have been here anyway, and left the kind of marks that do not accumulate into a house. The footholds in the entrance twist are cut, not weathered: a rhythm of scoops at the spacing of a careful descent, edges softened until they are almost natural again, but still too regular, still facing the same way a right-handed cutter would face. On the landward wall, at the height of a seated shoulder, someone pecked a short shelf and never finished the second one beside it. The unfinished one is three blows and a false start. In a sand-pocket near the bowl lies a scatter of worked splinters—stone, not metal—and a knot of root-fiber that was rope once and is now a stain that holds the shape of a knot if you do not touch it. The bowl itself may have been deepened; its rim is rounder than the other pockets, and a groove leads into it from the drip-thread, too straight for a crack. None of it is metal except, if you look a long time at the shelf, a red-brown freckle the size of a seed, a nail or a pin lost and eaten down to a color. Whoever came, came with a hand-tool and a reason that fit in a pocket: fresh water, if the bowl was the reason; or the shelf, if someone meant to wait out weather and learned what the sound does to waiting. They did not stay. The marks are scattered the way initials are scattered in a place that is visited, not occupied. The room has had more than enough time to erase anything less stubborn than a cut in stone, and these remain, which means the visits were either recent in the life of the moss or else were repeated, lightly, across a span the moss has already swallowed.

The rules are simple and do not care that you have learned them. Cold air pools; the floor is colder than the dome, and wet cloth loses heat faster than you expect, so that an hour is a reasonable visit and a second hour is a decision you will feel in the hands afterward. Sound does not cancel in here. It adds. You will not hear a footstep, or a warning, or your name. Water stays where the stone has agreed to hold it and does not rise to meet the noise that suggests it should. The green grows only where drip and the thin light overlap, and it grows slowly enough that bruising it is a longer vandalism than it looks. Nothing in the room is hiding. The darkness is only thickness of stone and a small sky. If you climb out while you can still feel your fingers, the turf and the combed grass and the ordinary wind seem, for a minute, elaborately quiet—not silent, just furnished with sounds that have edges again—and the sag in the ground behind you is only a sag, until you put a hand on it and the pressure is still there, patient, keeping the agreement.
