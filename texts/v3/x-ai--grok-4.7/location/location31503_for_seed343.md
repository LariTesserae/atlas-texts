The plateau does not announce itself as high. It has been high so long that height has gone out of it as a sensation and remained only as a fact of the air: thinner than it ought to be for the warmth, dry in the throat by afternoon, and carrying sound farther than the eye trusts. Whatever range once stood here was planed down before anything now living had a name for stone. What is left is a broad, warm table, regional in extent, its horizon a slow crease rather than a wall, and underfoot not rock at all but a pavement of living matter packed, felted, and grown into itself until it has forgotten it was ever separate plants.

Walk it and the ground gives, very slightly, like a hide stretched over a drum. In places the hide is finger-thick and dark, a peat of stems and root-leather gone nearly mineral. In others it rises into ribs and vaults: great pale culms, jointed like cane, leaning into one another until they make halls with no carpenter. The halls are mostly empty. That is the first surprise and then the permanent one. The vegetation is total — there is no soil that is not plant, no wall that is not plant, no shade that is not the shade of something still exchanging gases — and yet the volume inside that living mass is vacant. You can stand in a gallery tall enough for weather and see, through a gap in the culms, another gallery, and beyond that open plateau again, and nothing occupies the space between except light and whatever is crossing it.

Moisture comes the way it must, this far up and this old. Not as rivers. The plateau shed its rivers ages ago, when the last gradients worth following were worn out of it. What remains is dampness with a memory of weather: condensation beading along the inner curves of the culms at dawn, a rain that arrives as a brief, warm sheet and is gone before the mat has darkened all the way through, pockets of standing water in the shallowest dishes of the living floor, tea-colored and still, ringed with a finer grass that never quite decides to be separate from the pavement. By midday most of those pockets have sunk back into the felt. The air keeps a little of them. Breath tastes of green water and sun-warmed fiber.

The light is ordinary daylight, which here feels like a decision. Nothing filters it into jewel-tones. It falls straight onto the pale culms and turns them the color of old bone and new straw at once, and it falls into the empty halls and stops, because there is nothing in them to scatter it further. Shadows are hard-edged and few. You can see a long way. What you see, most hours, is the repetition of the same three facts: mat, vault, open distance.

Then the animals arrive, or rather you notice that they have not left.

They do not fill the ground. The ground could not hold them in any case; it is a skin over hollows, and anything heavy learns quickly where the ribs are. They fill the air and the sound of the air. Flocks move in sheets across the open reaches, low enough that their shadows write and erase sentences on the mat, high enough that a single turn of the mass darkens a whole gallery mouth. Between passages there are the residents: things that nest in the joints of the culms, things that run the inner walls on pads that do not puncture the living tube, things that hunt the runners and are hunted in turn by whatever drops out of the sheets when the sheets tilt. None of them are many in any one place. Step into a hall and it can seem abandoned — a feather caught on a seam, a track in the damp film on the floor, the smell of a body that was here at first light. Step back out and the plateau is roaring.

The roar is not weather. Weather here is a softer instrument: the tick of condensation giving up, the occasional rain, a wind that combs the mat and makes the vaults knock, very deep, like boats. The roar is animal and it does not localize. Calls overlap until individual voices stop being separable; wingbeat becomes a surf; somewhere a colony in a culm-joint answers a colony three valleys of open ground away, and the answer arrives already mixed with the next question. At the margins of hearing there is a lower register, almost muscular, the sound of many light bodies landing at once on a surface that is itself alive and slightly taut. Conversation is possible only in the lulls, and the lulls are short and feel accidental. People who stay learn to speak between impacts.

There are people, or were, in the scattered way of a place that offers endless material and almost no privacy from noise. The structures are not towns. A ring of stones — true stones, carried, because the plateau does not offer them anymore — set into the mat to pin a shelter of woven culm. A raised platform where the living floor was cut back and encouraged to scar into a harder callus, so that sleep would not mean sinking. A line of peeled stakes marching toward a water-pocket, not a road so much as a reminder that the pocket moves its banks with the season and the stakes were an argument with that fact. Tool-marks are simple: edge, twist, bind. No metal worth the name, or if there was, it has gone back into the damp and the green and left only the shapes it taught. Some of the rings are empty. Some have been grown through, a culm rising politely through the middle of a floor as if the floor had been a suggestion. The oldest marks are nearly flush with the mat, legible only when the condensation is right and the low light picks out a groove that hands made and roots have been revising ever since.

Nothing here is finished, and nothing here is young. That is the rule the erosion wrote and the plants accepted. The plateau is at the end of its mineral life — flattened, settled, no longer making mountains or tearing them down in any way a person could watch — and at the same time the surface is all process. Culms put on a joint and abandon another. The mat thickens where feet and flocks compact it and thins where a gallery collapses inward and the living roof becomes, overnight, more floor. Predators follow the sheets of birds along routes that shift with the rain-pockets. The sheets themselves wheel when a shadow that is not theirs crosses the mat, and the wheeling is loud enough to stop a person mid-step, hand half-raised, waiting for the air to become usable again.

By late day the warmth, which never became heat and never forgot itself, sits in the fiber of the culms. Touch a wall and it is blood-warm, as if the plant had been in the sun on your behalf. The water-pockets that survived the afternoon hold a skin of insects, and the insects hold a second, finer sound under the roar, a seethe that you only hear if you kneel and the flocks have banked away toward the far crease of the land. Kneeling, you see the pavement for what it is: not ground, but a crowd lying down, stems and roots and the ghosts of stems, interleaved until individuality is a courtesy. Stand again and the courtesy is withdrawn. The hall in front of you is empty. The plateau beyond it is empty. The sky is not, and will not be, and the deafening thing about the place is not that it is loud when you arrive. It is that the loudness is the stable state, the ancient weather of a living flatness, and the silence, when it comes, is the visitor.
