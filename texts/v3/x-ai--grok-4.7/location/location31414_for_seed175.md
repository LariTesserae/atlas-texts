The basin is small enough that its far wall is always in the same glance as its near one. You stand on a lip of stone no wider than a path and the whole place is already contained: a shallow bowl of warm rock, open to the sky, closed on three sides by cliffs that have long since stopped falling and only lean.

The height is real. Air thins a little in the lungs, and beyond the rim the country drops away into a pale distance that does not belong to this room. But the bowl itself holds heat the way a cupped hand holds water. The stone underfoot is warm even where no sun has sat on it. Seams in the walls sweat. Water does not arrive as weather so much as it arrives as a fact of the rock—beading from hairline cracks, gathering in the low places, never quite drying. After rain the difference is only one of degree: the lenses of standing water deepen by a finger, and the cliffs darken as if bruised.

Nothing here is new. The walls have been rubbed into the same curves for longer than any single wet season could explain. Ledges are rounded. The floor is a pavement of slabs whose edges have forgotten they were edges, fitted by patience rather than by any hand. Mineral stains run in slow curtains down the cliff faces—rust, a green that is not quite moss, a chalky white that builds and sheds in flakes so thin they do not sound when they fall. Balance, not ruin. The place has finished becoming what it is and now only maintains it.

What grows does so without crowding. A single tree stands near the middle, trunk the color of wet clay, its crown broad and heavy and almost too lush for the space it occupies—leaves large, overlapping, dripping from their tips long after the rest of the basin has gone quiet. Around it, nothing for several paces. Then a mound of moss thick enough to take a print and hold it until the next slow seep erases the mark. Then bare warm stone again. Ferns occur as individuals, not as stands: one in a crack, one at the foot of the far wall, each frond fully made, none of them jostling. The vegetation is extravagant where it exists and absent where it does not, as if the basin permits only a few presences and requires each of them to be complete.

There are no animals. No midges over the water, no lizards on the warm rock, no birds in the tree. The leaves move only when water leaves them. Tracks do not appear. The silence is not the silence of something hiding. It is the silence of a room that was never occupied in that way.

Sound, when it comes, is small and close: a tick as a drop lets go of a fern-tip and meets a lens of water; a faint, continuous whisper where a seam weeps down the cliff and spreads into a film too thin to ripple. Wind, if it enters, does not cross the bowl. It stalls at the rim and the air inside stays warm and slightly thick, smelling of wet stone and the sweet, green edge of leaves that never quite dry.

The strangeness is not a spectacle. It accumulates if you stay. The water in the shallow pools does not sit level with the sky in the way water elsewhere does; its surface tilts a fraction toward the tree, as if the basin had a private downhill that the eye cannot find in the stone. Drops on the undersides of the leaves bead, swell, and sometimes climb back along the rib of the leaf instead of falling. Moss on the shaded side of a boulder is damp; moss on the sunlit side is damper. The warmth does not come from the sun alone. Press a palm to the cliff in full light and then to the same cliff in the overhang, and the overhang is warmer—slow heat working outward from inside the ridge, older than the afternoon.

Light itself is ordinary. Morning finds the east wall first and walks across the floor at a human pace. At midday the tree throws a clean shadow. Late day the west cliff goes amber and the pools take that color without changing it. Nothing flickers that should not. The weirdness prefers to live in the water and the heat and the rules of what is allowed to grow, not in the light.

Change, here, is a matter of seasons you would have to inhabit. The mineral curtains lengthen by a line you could not measure in a day. A new fern may open in a crack that was empty last year, and an old one may brown at the edges and remain, still occupying its place, still unrushed. The lenses of water rise and fall by less than a hand across a whole wet season. Nothing startles. Nothing is built. No tool has bitten this stone; no path has been cut. If anything like a craft exists, it is the basin's own: the slow fitting of slab to slab, the way a leaf learns to return its water upward, the refusal of this small warm room to admit a single living noise.

Stand long enough and the far wall does not come closer. The place does not expand to meet you. It remains the size it decided on—grove, chamber, a single feature with a rim—and inside that size it keeps its wet, its few lush things set far apart, its warmth rising from stone that has already finished weathering into the shape it means to keep.
