The plateau holds its heat the way a kiln holds it: not as weather passing through, but as a condition of the stone. You feel it first in the soles, then in the air against the teeth. The light is hard and white and almost without shadow at midday, poured down from a sky that never quite clears and never quite clouds—only thickens, a bright vapor that condenses on every upward face and runs off in threads no wider than a finger.

There is no green. There has not been green here in any memory the stone keeps. What grows is crust: pale mineral skins, sulfur-yellow rinds, black glassy blooms where old flows met old water and both decided to stay. The crusts flake underfoot like dry bread and reform behind you if you wait, fed by whatever is always seeping. Water is not a feature of the place so much as its habit. It beads on the undersides of ledges. It stands in shallow pans the color of weak tea, too hot to drink and too mineral to clear. It weeps from joints in the rock in slow, continuous sheets, polishing channels that have had centuries to find their exact shape and no longer change them. After the brief hard rains—which come as steam more than as weather—the whole slope glistens and then, within an hour, looks as it did before. The water was already here. The rain only made it visible.

The elevation is enough that the horizon sits lower than you expect, a long rim of darker rock boxing a basin the size of a small country district. Inside that rim the land is open and mostly empty: long benches of sintered stone, a few standing fins of volcanic glass gone dull with age, wide flats where the crust has settled into a pavement of hexagons. Things are far apart. A tower does not crowd the next tower. A flue does not lean on a wall. The settlement is urban in the way a string of works along a rail is urban—organized, infrastructural, meant to be lived in by people who service machines—but it never densifies. You can walk twenty minutes between compounds and meet only the heat, the seep-lines, and whatever is moving on the flats.

The works themselves are old enough to have weathered into the same grammar as the rock. Brick and cast plate, stained the colors of the water that runs over them. Roofs pitched just enough to shed condensate into gutters that feed cisterns. Chimneys that do not smoke so much as breathe: a pale exhalation, almost the same temperature as the air, visible only where it bends the light. Engines turn somewhere under the floors. You know this because the rail sheds vibrate, faintly, through the boot. You do not know it by ear. Sound here does not carry. The vapor eats it. A hammer struck on plate arrives as a soft click, already tired. Voices stay in the mouth. Even the wind, when it moves, moves as pressure against the cheek and not as noise. The quiet is not peace and not absence. It is a property of the air, the way heat is a property of the stone. People who live on the plateau learn to watch hands.

What is abundant is not silent in the way the people are. The flats crawl. Low, many-legged things the color of wet ash move in loose scatterings between the pans, never bunching, never still for long. They graze the crusts—actual grazing, mouths working, leaving pale tracks that the seep refills by evening. Larger animals, shoulder-high, stand at the edges of the hotter pools with their heads lowered into the steam, and what they are doing there is not obvious until you see the filaments: pale threads they draw up and swallow, grown in the water itself, neither plant nor quite animal, a chemistry that found a way to eat light and mineral and heat without ever becoming a leaf. Birds—or what occupy the office of birds—circle high and almost invisible against the white sky, their wings narrow, their calls so thin the vapor takes them before they reach the ground. At dusk, which is only a softening of the brightness rather than a dark, the grazers drift toward the compounds. They are not tame. They have simply learned that the outflow pipes shed a richer crust, and the people have learned not to stop them. A balance, long settled. The fences, where fences exist, are for machines, not for beasts.

There is a strangeness, but it is the strangeness of a rule you have not been told, not of a world coming apart. Shadows fall the wrong length in the vapor, shorter than the sun should allow, as if the light were arriving from more than one height at once. The hexagon pavement sometimes shows a new crack by morning that matches, exactly, a crack on a bench half a kilometer away, as if the basin were still one skin and still remembering how to split. Clocks in the engine houses lose minutes and then, without adjustment, find them again. None of this alarms the people who keep the flues. They say the plateau has always done a little more than stone should do, and that the works were built to that fact rather than against it. The pipes follow the seep-lines. The cisterns are sized to a condensation that never fails. The rail runs where the crust is oldest and least likely to heave.

By late day the light goes brass, still bright, still hot, and the weeping faces of the rock turn the color of honey. You can stand on a gantry above one of the mid-basin compounds and see the whole arrangement: sparse towers, a thread of rail, the glint of pans, the slow drift of grazers, the rim far off and steady. Nothing hurries. Nothing is crowded. Under the floors the engines keep their muted turn. On the benches the water keeps its slow police of the stone. The place has been in this state longer than the settlement, and the settlement has been here long enough to match it—weathered, spaced, quiet, fed by what seeps and what crawls and what the bright vapor keeps condensing out of a sky that does not rest.
