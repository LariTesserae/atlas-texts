The place sits in a notch cut long ago by weather and then kept by hands. It is not a city seen from far off, and not a village spilling down a slope. It is a single enclosed ground, no larger than a market town’s oldest quarter, held between two shoulders of pale rock. Beyond the shoulders the land drops away into cooler air and empty distance. Inside the notch the world is complete, and has been complete for so long that nothing in it still looks like a decision.

The ground is high enough that breath comes a little short if you climb the last switch of the approach path, and cool enough that the cool never quite leaves the stone. Morning does not warm the lanes so much as light them. The light is ordinary: a high, even daylight when the cloud is thin, a flat gray when it is not. There is no drama in it. Shadows fall where walls make them fall, and they fall in the same places every day, because the walls do not move and the season, if there are seasons, does not rearrange the sky enough to matter.

Water is not a river and not a lake. It is a habit of the stone. Seams in the rock weep a little after a night of low cloud. A shallow cistern at the upper end of the settlement takes what the seep gives and holds it under a lid of fitted slabs, dark and still. Gutters run along the bases of the houses, worn into smooth channels by water that has used them for centuries and by feet that have learned not to step in them. Sometimes a brief rain comes, fine and without wind, and darkens the paving for an hour. Then it is gone, and only the damp remains: in the joints, under the eaves, on the north faces of walls, in the smell of the air. Pockets of it gather where a flagstone has settled a finger’s width lower than its neighbors. Moss does not take them. The stone is too clean, or the cold too steady, or the use too constant. The water sits, reflects a strip of sky, and slowly goes back into the rock.

The buildings are many for so small a ground, and yet the place never feels full. They stand in loose ranks along three lanes that meet at a worn open space no one would call a square with any pride. Houses of two stories, sometimes three where the rock steps up and a lower room is half cave. Walls of dressed stone, pale where the weather has bleached them, darker in the courses that stay damp. Lintels are single blocks, edges softened until the corners are only memories of corners. Roofs are flat or nearly so, of smaller slabs laid in overlapping courses and weighted with river-rounded stones that did not come from any river here. Chimneys are few and short. Smoke, when there is smoke, is a thin thread and does not stain the walls anymore than they are already stained.

Between the houses there is more space than use. Yards that hold nothing. A stretch of paving wide enough for two carts that has never, in living memory, needed to hold two carts. A stair that climbs to a terrace where three houses share a wall with the cliff and then simply stops, as if the plan had always included the stopping. The density of making is urban: drains that meet, corners that turn true, doorways sized to the same measure, a public cistern, a covered way along the western row where the drip from the rock is worst. But the density of presence is low. You can stand in the central open ground and see through gaps to the rock beyond. You can walk the length of the longest lane and pass more shut doors than open ones, more empty thresholds than occupied.

The work that keeps the place is old and simple. Iron exists, but sparingly: strap hinges, a few latches, the rims of buckets, a hook in a beam. Most of what is made is stone fitted to stone, wood pegged to wood, cord, hide, clay fired in a small kiln at the lower edge where the smoke can leave. Tools are hand tools. The marks of them are everywhere and nowhere particular — chisel tracks planed down by later hands, a door worn concave where shoulders have passed, a step dished in the middle until the dishing itself is the step. No one is building. No one is letting anything fall. The settlement is in the condition it has earned: weathered into balance, every surface already the surface it will be.

Vegetation keeps to the margins and does not press. A few tough tufts in cracks high on the cliff, silver-green, never spreading. A single small tree, or something like a tree, rooted in a pocket of soil behind the cistern house, its trunk no thicker than a wrist, its leaves few and held close. Someone has cleared around it for so long that the clearing is now just the ground. There are no gardens in the lanes. What food comes here comes in, or comes from stores cut into the rock: cool rooms where grain and dried things keep because the temperature does not swing. The smell of the place is stone, damp mineral, a faint sweetness of old wood, and sometimes, near the stores, the dry scent of what has been put away.

Animals are rare enough that a movement at the edge of sight is an event. Swifts, or birds like swifts, cut the air above the notch at certain hours and do not land. A lizard the color of the paving may hold still on a warm stone if the day has any warmth in it, and then is not there. No dogs in the lanes. No flocks. No insects in any number; a fly, now and then, and then not. The quiet is partly this. Sound has little to catch on. Footsteps, when they come, carry and then stop. A bucket set down on stone rings once and is done. Water in the gutters makes a small sound only when the seep is running harder than usual, a thread of noise you stop hearing as soon as you have noticed it. Voices exist. They do not travel. People speak in doorways and in the covered way, low, as if the place had taught them the size of a voice that fits. Most of the time there is no voice at all, and the loudest thing is your own breath and the faint tick of cooling stone after the light has moved.

The people, such as they are, do not crowd the description because they do not crowd the ground. A figure crossing the open space with a bundle. Another on a roof, resetting a weight-stone that has shifted a finger’s width in a year. Someone at the cistern, lifting the slab just enough to dip. They dress in dull cloth, layered, because the cool is constant and the damp finds skin if cloth does not. Their paths are the paths already worn. They do not cut new ones. A child, if there is a child, plays in a place where children have played long enough that the play has a groove in it: a certain step, a certain flat stone, a game of placing and replacing that leaves no mark the stone does not already bear.

Nothing here is strange. The rules are the rules of stone, water, and long use. What is set down stays. What is walked on deepens. What is jointed well does not leak more than it has always leaked. Night comes at the ordinary hour and is only darker; there are no lights but what a household keeps, a small flame behind a shutter, and most shutters stay dark because most rooms are not in use at once. Morning returns the same light to the same walls. Rain, when it comes, wets the same stones and leaves by the same channels. The seep at the head of the cistern gives what it gives. The tree does not grow in any way you could measure between one visit and the next.

If you stay, the place does not reveal a hidden life. It reveals its sameness more carefully. You begin to know which flagstone holds water longest, which doorway smells of the stores, which stretch of lane is quieter because it leads only to a blank wall and a stair that ends. You notice that the iron has the same thin rust in every hinge, not spreading, not cleaned, held at the point where air and damp and rare use have agreed to stop. You notice that the cliff face above the western row is stained in a fan where water has come down for longer than the houses, and that the houses were built to that stain, not against it. The stain does not lengthen.

There is a boundary, and it is obvious once you look for it. On the east, a low wall of the same stone, not defensive, only a line where the paving ends and the rock of the approach begins. On the west, the cliff itself. North and south, the shoulders close in until there is no lane left to walk. Inside that line the settlement is entire. Outside it, the high country continues, sparse and cool and empty, and does not concern the notch except as the source of weather and the reason the air is thin. No road widens here. No new course of stone waits to be laid. The last block that needed placing was placed so long ago that its edges match the rest.

Stand in the open ground at the middle hour, when the light is plain and the gutters are only damp and no one is crossing. The houses hold their positions. The cistern holds its dark. A bird cuts the air and is gone. Somewhere a hinge answers a door and then is silent. The place does not wait for you, and it does not continue without you in any way that would look like change. It is already what it is: a small, high, cool town of fitted stone, sparse of green and of life, wet in its joints, quiet in its lanes, worn to the exact shape of its own long continuance, and holding that shape without effort, the way a bowl holds the shape of having been used.
