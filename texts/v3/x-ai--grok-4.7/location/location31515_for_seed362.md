The plain has no horizon in the ordinary sense. What you take for distance is a brightening, a thinning of the same substance, until the air itself is only a paler grade of the growth. The ground is low—always low—never a ridge that could be called a hill, only a slow heave like a held breath under a carpet. You walk on it and feel, under the sole, the give of something that was poured, cured, and then allowed to keep growing after the mold was forgotten.

There is no water. Not a seep, not a fog that wets the lip, not the mineral smell of a dried lake. What moisture you imagine is a mistake of the eye: the surfaces are glossy because they are resinous, not wet. The chemistry here runs on oils that never evaporate in any weather you know, on salts drawn up as fine white wires through the living bulk, on light. The salts crust in hexagonal plates where a trunk has split its own bark to vent heat, and the plates do not dissolve. They ring if you tap them, a small glassy note that dies at once.

Everything is plant, and everything is made. There is no wild edge. The canopy is a roof of laminated fronds, each frond ribbed with metal that was never mined—metal the tissues precipitated, grain by grain, until a girder could be cut from a stem and still be alive at the cut. Walkways are not laid on the growth; they are the growth, planed flat by a process that still runs, a slow industrial smoothing, fibers aligning under your weight as if the floor were remembering a specification. Factories occupy the mid-story not as buildings set among trees but as swellings: bulbous engine-rooms where the xylem has been bred into piston-geometry, sap thickened into a hydraulic that is not water and does not freeze in the cool. You hear almost nothing. The engines turn inside sheaths of cork and felted fiber. What leaks out is a hush, a pressure on the ear, the sense that work is being done at a scale that has learned not to announce itself.

Light is excessive and directionless. It does not come from a sun you can find. The upper leaves are mirrors turned inward, and the brightness pools in the aisles as if the place were lit from its own inventory of reflections. Shadows, when they occur, are green-black and wrong: a shadow will sometimes lie across a surface and then continue onto a surface that is not in line with it, as if the light had agreed to a floor plan the mass no longer obeys. In those mismatches the air pricks. Perspective stutters. A corridor you have been following for what feels like an hour opens into the same corridor, except the salt-plates on the wall are a size larger, or the pistons in the swelling beside you complete their stroke in a rhythm that does not match the ones you passed. The place is vast enough to have weather, and the weather is this: slow rearrangements of brightness, drifts of cooler air sliding along the undersides of fronds, a continental respiration that never becomes wind loud enough to name.

Fauna is an rumor. Once, far off, something small crossed a bright aisle and was gone—too quick, too quiet, perhaps only a loosened scale of bark moving on a draft you could not feel. The ecology does not need animals. Pollination, if the word still applies, is mechanical: brushes of fiber sweep anthers that are also switches. Seed is not scattered; it is extruded as components, dropped into hoppers grown for the purpose, sorted by riffles of living tissue that know mass and alloy by touch. Predation, where it exists, is pruning. A limb that has drifted out of spec is girdled by a finer growth, starved of its oil, and folded back into the floor. You can watch it happen if you wait. It looks like patience.

The cool is steady, industrial cool, the cool of a plant that vents its own metabolism through acres of louvered leaf and refuses to let heat accumulate into climate. Your breath does not fog. It simply goes thin, as if the air had been strained of anything that could carry a cloud. Sound stays local. A tool dropped on a walkway taps and is swallowed. Far processes—continent-sized, you suspect—register only as a change in the pressure behind the eyes, a brightness that leans, a new alignment of salt-crust catching the stored light.

Rules, such as they are, are specifications half-remembered by the mass. Do not cut what is still in cycle; the sap that is not water will set in the wound as a plug of metal-salt and the plug will grow a flange that seals you to the cut. Do not trust a straight sightline longer than you can throw a stone; straightness here is a temporary courtesy. Do not look for a center. The plain has none, or it has too many, each engine-swelling convinced, for a shift of the internal day, that it is the one the rest of the growth is feeding.

If you stand still long enough the floor adjusts under you, fibers ticking into a denser mat, not hostile, only precise. The brightness does not dim. Somewhere beyond the range of any footstep, another weather of the same plant turns over—an entire district of fronds tilting, a factory-heart changing its stroke—and the hush deepens by a degree you feel in the teeth. The place continues. It has been continuing. It was built, and then it became the only ground, and the difference stopped mattering.
