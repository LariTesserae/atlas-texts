The basin sits high enough that the air above it has already thinned, and yet the place itself is water. A cirque no larger than a hamlet’s worth of ground holds a tarn the color of bottle glass, fed by a sheet of melt that never quite stops coming over the northern lip. The overflow leaves by a single notch to the south, a white thread you can hear before you see it. Between those two mouths the water does not hurry. It has been this deep, and this still in its middle, longer than the stone around it has kept its present face.

You do not stand on a shore so much as wade into a living floor. A hand’s breadth under the surface the bottom is not gravel. It is a mat of root and moss and pale jointed stems, woven so tightly that a foot sinks only to the ankle and then finds give, like a soaked rug that has forgotten how to rot. The mat is the street. It has been walked, or rather swum and poled across, for so long that pale tracks show where the weave has been pressed thinner, and those tracks do not close. They are the established roads. Everything else is plant grown into use.

The houses are few, and there is water between them. A dozen, perhaps, scattered through the bowl rather than packed against one edge: hollow columns of reed as thick as a person’s waist, lashed and grafted until they stand like organ pipes, their tops breaking the surface in crowns of living green. Inside each column the water is held back by a lining of overlapped leaves, pitch-dark and waxy, cobbled on with bone awls and cord. You enter by ducking under a root lintel and rising into a pocket of air that smells of wet hay and cold iron. The floor of such a room is a raft of bundled stems, and it rocks, very slightly, with the breathing of the tarn. Light comes in ordinary and high, fractured by the surface into moving nets across the inner walls. No glass. No metal worth the name. What holds is tension, graft, and the habit of the plant.

Between the columns the open water is the square, the lane, the unbuilt lot. It is wide enough that a shout carries cleanly and a swimmer has room to turn. Nothing crowds. A few narrower causeways of braided willow run just under the surface, invisible until a pole finds them; they sag where feet have trusted them for generations and have been mended by tying in new withes, not by replacing the old. The old stay. They are gray, slick, and still load-bearing. Weather has had its say and then stopped arguing.

The vegetation is not decoration laid over a town. It is the town’s substance and also the country around it. From the mat rise forests of hair-fine weed that never thicken into jungle; they stand in clumps, with clear corridors between, and in those corridors the fish move in schools so constant they read as weather. Trout the length of a forearm hold in the shade of a reed-tower and do not flee unless a hand breaks the surface. Newts the color of wet slate crawl the root-mat in numbers that make the floor seem to crawl with them when the light shifts. Above, on the crowns and on the stone rim, birds nest in a density the water does not match: dippers, wagtails, a colony of small dark swifts that pour off the northern lip every time the sheet of melt changes its note. Their cries, the fall, the notch-overflow, the knock of poles against reed, the slap of a raft when someone shifts their weight — the basin is loud in a way that does not peak and fade. It holds a volume, the way a millrace holds a volume, and has held it so long the ear stops treating it as event.

Cool all the way down. Even at noon the water takes the heat out of a wrist in a few breaths. The stone walls of the cirque are lichen-gray and dry above the splash line, wet and black below it, and that line has not moved in living memory. Moss beards hang where spray reaches and nowhere else. Nothing here is young in the sense of unfinished. The reed-columns have been recrowned, the leaf-linings restuck, the causeways rebraided, but the plan of the place — which towers, which open water, which path under the skin — is the plan it settled into when the mat first closed over the gravel and the people who pole these lanes decided the gravel was no longer the ground.

They use what the basin gives. Bone hooks. Cord of twisted stem. Poles of last year’s reed, cured on the stone rim until they pale. Baskets that are still half alive when they are woven and finish dying into stiffness. No wheel, no kiln worth the walk down the notch, no ore. A few pieces of cold-hammered copper turn up in the oldest rooms, green as the water, used as weights and as needles, not as a technology anyone is extending. Fire, when it is wanted, is a small thing on a stone sled rafted to the middle and fed with dried crowns. It does not last. The place does not need it to last.

What is unusual is quiet in the telling and obvious once you are in it: the air-pockets do not foul. The leaf-linings transpire; a faint sweetness moves through the rooms; sleepers wake clear-headed. The mat knits over a dropped basket before the season turns, but it does not knit over the pale roads. It has learned the difference, or the people have taught it by walking, and either way the lesson is old. Stand on the rim at the hour when the sun is simply there, not low and not fierce, and the town reads as a scattering of green crowns in glass, birds stitching the air, fish stitching the shade, and a sound that has been going since the notch first cut itself and does not intend, any time soon, to stop.
