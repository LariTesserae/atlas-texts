The bowl is newer than the water in it, and both of them are newer than the dark.

You come to it by the high cut. The rock there has not learned weather. Edges still carry the angle of the break that opened them: planes of gray-green stone, almost vitreous where the split ran fast, faceted like a thing struck once and then left alone. Nothing has rounded. Grit has not gathered in the joints. If you set a hand on the lip — and the lip will take a hand, cool, dry on the outside of the skin — you feel a sharpness that ordinary mountains spend centuries filing away. This one has not begun. The fracture is a fact that has not yet become a history.

Beyond the lip the world drops into a volume no larger than a town you could cross before hunger, and no smaller than a nave. It has a boundary you can find. Walk the rim and the rim returns you to your own tracks; the place is a circuit, not a coast. Outside that circuit the high air is thin and ordinary and moving. Inside it, the air has been dismissed.

What fills the bowl is water, if water is the word for a liquid that occupies every street and does not flow. It stands to the rim and no higher. It does not lap. It does not skin over with ice, though the cold in it is the cold of stone that has never held sun — a clear, unhurried cool, the temperature of a deep room in a building that has no furnace and does not need one. Put a wrist through the surface and the surface accepts the wrist without a ripple that lasts. The disturbance you make is local, polite, and then it is gone, not by fading but by being finished. The liquid returns to the condition it had before you, which is the only condition it has.

There is no plantside to it. No moss on the drowned steps, no hair of algae on the cables, no garden that failed and left its shapes. The ecology, if that is the word, is mineral and made. The walls of the bowl are the youngest stone. The floors of the town are older only by the time it took to lay them: plates, ribs, interrupted arcades, a few bridges that now bridge nothing because there is no air to span and no bank to reach. Everything is sharp. Corners meet as drawn. A stair descends and does not blur into the tread below it. Erosion has not been invited, and the water, having no current, cannot insist.

The town is real and it is sparse. You can see through it. Structures stand with the confidence of a civic plan — alignments, a grammar of thresholds, heights that answer each other — and then long stretches of unoccupied liquid where the plan simply elected not to build. A tower, then forty body-lengths of nothing, then a low hall whose roof is a sheet of something darker than the water and perfectly flat. A ring of piers. A narrow engineered cleft that might be a street and might be a conduit. Density never thickens into maze. You are never crowded by the city. You are accompanied by it at a distance, the way a single bell accompanies a square.

What the structures are made of does not sit cleanly in the list of materials. Some of it is stone cut from the bowl itself, still carrying the same unweathered faces. Some of it is metal only in the sense that it refuses the hand and returns a faint structured cold. Some of it is a decision the liquid has agreed to keep: a wall you can lean on that, if you look along it, is a standing discrepancy in the water — a plane where refraction fails, where the far side is postponed. Joints do not weep. Fastenings do not corrode, because corrosion would be a change, and change is not practiced here. The engineering exceeds the physics you arrived with. Pressure, at the depths the bowl actually has, should stack. It does not stack. However far you descend — and descent is possible; there is down, and it is deep relative to the smallness of the place, a vertical more serious than the horizontal — your chest is not asked to pay for it. Breathing is an arrangement, not a triumph. The liquid has been instructed, at a grain smaller than anything you can see, to be breathable in the volumes where bodies are expected and merely heavy where they are not. You do not see the instruction. You inhabit its consequence.

Light is almost absent and not quite banished. There is no sunshaft, no lamp-standard doing the work of day. What light exists is leftover and local: a dull mineral phosphor in certain panels, a rim-glow along one bridge where the material remembers a charge, a faint internal brightening in the water itself when you move your hand, as if the liquid kept a ledger of disturbance and displayed the most recent line before erasing it. Ten paces from a panel you are in true dark. The dark is not hostile. It is the default, and the lit places are the exceptions the builders bothered to write down.

Sound has the same economy. The bowl does not boom. There is no wave-slap, no drip, no wind inside the skin. Your own motion makes a soft thick noise, close to the skull, that dies within arm's reach — not muffled by distance so much as declined by the medium. Far off, if you hold still long enough to trust your ears, there is sometimes a single low tone, steady, without attack or decay, coming from no tower you can identify. It does not vary in pitch. It does not announce anything. It is present the way a foundation is present. Mostly, the place is quiet in the manner of a closed book.

Life is rare enough that meeting it feels like a miscount. No schools, no drift of small things, no scavengers working the corners. Once, between two piers, a shape the size of a curled hand holds position in the dark, not swimming, not attached. It is not a fish. It has no flutter. Its surface reads as the same family as the bowl's newest stone, except that it chose, at some point that is not repeating, to be in the water rather than of the wall. It does not approach. It does not flee. If you return later — though later is a courtesy the place extends to you, not a difference it keeps — it is either there or it is not, and neither state looks like the result of a decision made in time. Elsewhere, high under the skin of the surface where the leftover light is least weak, a second presence: a slow filament, pale, unbranching, with no root and no crown. It is not a plant. It does not grow toward anything. It hangs as if hung, and the water does not move it, and it does not move the water. Two is almost the whole census. The rest of the volume is uninhabited with a thoroughness that feels maintained.

Nothing is becoming. That is the rule that organizes the others, and it takes a while to believe. Sediment does not settle, because settling would sort the liquid into ages, and the liquid is one age. The sharp stone does not dull. The phosphor does not spend itself. The tone does not finish. If you leave a tool on a step and come back, the tool is where you left it, not filmed, not shifted, not claimed. The city is not ruined and not new in the way of a construction site. It is held at the moment after completion and before use, except that use has happened — there are thresholds worn nowhere, rails shaped to hands that are not here — and the moment has simply refused to advance into the next. Outside, on the rim, a wind may move. Cloud may form and unform below the elevation of the cut, a floor of weather you stand above. Inside the skin, gradients are not allowed to steepen. Heat does not travel. Salt, if there is salt, is the same in every mouthful. Up and down exist, and the civic plan respects them — taller buildings, deeper cuts, a lowest chamber whose floor is the bowl's own unbroken stone, still faceted, still young — but they do not accumulate consequence. You cannot wait the place into a different place. Waiting is something your body does. The bowl does not join you in it.

The strangeness is not a spectacle hung in the center for you to gasp at. It is in the agreements. Streets continue through liquid as if the absence of air were a zoning choice. Your reflection, when a panel gives you one, lags by a fraction and shows you from an angle you do not occupy. A doorway at the end of a conduit opens onto water that is the same water, and yet the conduit's far side is darker in a way the distance does not explain, as if the builders had partitioned night itself and assigned the thicker portion to a district. Scale misbehaves without becoming vast: a hall that from the entrance is the size of a chapel lengthens, as you cross it, only in the memory of crossing, so that you arrive having walked farther than the room contains, and when you look back the entrance is where the architecture says it should be. The place is small. You can reach its boundary. And inside the boundary, measurement is a guest, tolerated, not sovereign.

There is no sign of why the people — if people is the right category for whoever struck the agreements — are absent. No scatter of tools in panic, no sealed last room, no message cut into the young stone. The infrastructure simply continues: a conduit that still carries nothing because nothing was the cargo; a pier that still offers its flatness to a boat that would have no reason to float; a high walk along the inner rim, just under the skin, where someone could have gone to see the ordinary sky and did not need to, because the sky is not part of the town's work. The work was the holding. The holding is complete. It does not elaborate itself.

If you stay, the dark stays with you, and the cool, and the quiet that is not silence but the sound of a system with no moving part left to hear. The rare shape between the piers keeps its post or does not. The filament does not grow. Above you, on the other side of a surface that will not wrinkle, the high rim cuts a circle out of whatever day or night the rest of the mountain is having. That circle is the only clock, and it is outside. In here the water does not mark it. The town does not weather toward the circle or away from it. Young stone, unmoving liquid, a city with room enough between its buildings to be lonely in, and a dark that has been made exact — all of it balanced, and the balance not a pause.

The bowl will be this when you leave it. It was this before the cut in the mountain had a name. The newness of the rock and the stillness of the water are not in contradiction once you stand inside them. One is the freshness of a beginning that was not allowed to continue. The other is the form the continuation took instead: not sequence, but maintenance, perfect and unadvertised, in a town-sized dark above the clouds, where nothing grows, almost nothing lives, and the streets are full.
