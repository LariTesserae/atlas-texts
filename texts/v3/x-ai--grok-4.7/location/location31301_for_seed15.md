The chamber is small enough that its far wall can be found by a short reach, and it is full. There is no air left in it. Warm water stands from the dressed floor to the soffit of the roof, pressing into every joint, and the only open thing is the square shaft cut through the ceiling, no wider than a person’s shoulders, rising a little way to the surface of the yard above. Day comes down that shaft as a hard white column and does not fade much on the way. The water is clear enough, and new enough, that the light arrives almost whole.

Everything that holds the water was made by hand and set here recently. The walls are blocks of pale stone, cut square, bedded in a lime mortar that has not yet taken the color of the water. Tool marks remain on the faces: the ridged track of a chisel, the finer scrape where a surface was dressed flat. Corners meet at true right angles. Arrises are still sharp enough to catch light as a thin bright line. Nothing has rounded. The flood that fills the room has not had time to soften what contains it. Where a block was set a finger’s width proud of its neighbor, it is still proud. A drip of mortar, missed by the mason’s thumb, still hangs at one joint like a small pale beak, not yet dissolved.

The floor is the same stone, laid in courses that slope, very slightly, toward a circular drain at the far end. The drain is not a pipe in any later sense. It is a hole cut through a single slab, its lip left crisp, and beyond it a short channel of fitted blocks that must run somewhere under the yard. Water leaves by that hole in a steady pull. You can see the pull if you watch the shaft of light: threads of brightness bend, once, as they pass over the mouth, and a low throat-sound comes up from the channel and never stops. At the opposite wall a spout enters, also stone, a short trough cantilevered from a joint and locked with a wooden wedge that has swollen tight. The inflow is faster than the drain. The excess has nowhere to go but up the shaft, so a skin of water constantly lips the rim above and spills out across the yard paving. That spill is the loudest thing. It breaks on the lip, falls a hand’s breadth onto stone, and comes back down the shaft as a bright, continuous clatter, multiplied by the four walls until the chamber seems to be speaking in one hard syllable that does not end.

The heat is in the water itself. It arrives already warm, near the warmth of a wrist held under it, and it does not cool much in the short time it spends in the room. There is no fire here, no metal boiler, no flue. Somewhere farther back, beyond the spout, the same hands that built this box captured a warm seep and led it in a covered runnel of slab and clay. The runnel is new too. Its joints still show the fingerprints of whoever pressed the clay home. By the time the water reaches the trough it has traveled only far enough to lose its first silt and none of its heat. In the chamber it moves as a slow replacement: in at the spout, across the lit middle, down the slight slope, out the drain, and whatever cannot leave that way rises and leaves by the shaft. The whole volume turns over. It never sits.

Because it never sits, almost nothing has taken hold. A few pale green threads cling in one mortar joint just below the roof, where the light is strongest and the current softens against the wall. They are short, sparse, not yet a mat. Lower down, near the floor, two clay pots were set into circular recesses cut for them. The pots are coarse, coil-built, thumb-marked, and each holds a knot of root and a few narrow leaves that reach a little way into the water and then stop. The leaves move. They do not drift; they are tugged, steadily, toward the drain, and spring back a fraction when the inflow pulses. That is the only vegetation. No scum clouds the shaft. No film dulls the stone. The water is too young in this room, and too constantly changed, for much else to begin.

There are no animals. Nothing swims across the column of light. No larvae tick along the joints. The drain gives back only water-sound, not the click of a shell or the burrow of anything living in the channel. If something were carried in from the seep it has not stayed, or has not yet arrived. The room is occupied by stone, water, light, and those few leaves.

The loudness is not weather. It is the room doing its work. The spill at the lip keeps its bright shatter. Under that, the drain keeps its lower note, rounder, as if the channel beyond were a short throat. The inflow adds a third sound, thinner, where the water leaves the trough and meets the standing body: a small, continuous tear. When the pulse upstream changes—when whoever tends the seep upstream shifts a board, or when the warm source itself surges—the tear rises in pitch for a moment and the leaves in the pots lean harder, and the column of light wavers, and then the three sounds settle again into the same chord. Above, in the yard, the overflow runs off across new paving toward a gutter. That sound does not enter the chamber except as a faint extra hiss at the rim. Down here the walls keep what they keep.

The rules of the place are simple and already visible. Water enters warm and leaves by two mouths, one low and one high, and the high one only works because the low one cannot keep up. Light enters only by the shaft and is held in the water rather than stopped by it; at this hour it reaches the floor and lays a blurred square there, the edges of the square trembling where the surface at the rim is broken by the spill. Stone does not yet give way to the water. Mortar is still mortar. Edges are still edges. The pots stay because they were cut seats for them; nothing else has a place to root. Sound does not die in the corners. It stacks. The chamber is below the yard by the height of a person and a little more, and that is all the depth it has: a made box, recently closed, filled on purpose, and kept full.

If you put a hand in at the shaft you meet warmth at once, and the downward light, and the tug that is stronger near the floor than near the roof. The stone under the hand is gritty with its own fresh dressing, not slick. A joint, followed sideways, runs true until it meets the corner, and the corner is still a corner. The leaves, if the hand finds them, are few, cool only by comparison, and already bending the way the water bends. Above, the spill continues, loud and ordinary, wasting a little of the warmth onto the paving so that the room below can remain exactly full.
