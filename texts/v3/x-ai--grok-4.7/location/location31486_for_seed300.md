The place is a sunken yard no wider than a parish church is long, set in a shallow pan that the land has already decided to keep. Low embankments ring it—not hills, only the piled spoil of an older cut—and from their crests the whole floor is visible at once. You could cross it in a few minutes if the ground would hold. It will not. The boundary is a lip of settled clay and broken slag, greened in patches and gone gray where the last rain still stands. Beyond the lip the country is ordinary and cooler than it looks. Inside, the yard has its own weather, close to the skin and never quite dry.

Water is the material that does the most work here. It does not arrive as a river. It rises. A hand’s depth under the crust the ground is already wet, and in the low seams it has given up pretending to be land. Pools occupy the joints between old concrete pads, black where they are deep enough to hide their bottoms, tea-colored where iron has bled into them. After rain—which is often, and which does not need to be heavy—the pads become islands with edges sharp as curbs, and the walking routes shift by a shoe-width. Rocks at the western lip weep continuously. The seep is not dramatic. It is a darkening, then a thread, then a fan of mineral stain that has been writing the same sentence down the face for decades. The air itself condenses on anything colder than the morning: pipe, rail, the underside of a tipped hopper. Drops gather, hesitate, and fall into water that was already there. Nothing in the yard is ever fully between weathers.

The temperature stays cool even when the light is ordinary and full. Midday comes down straight and gray-white, no drama in it, and still the metal sweats. Breath shows only at the first hour. By noon the chill has retreated into the shade under flanges and into the water, which takes the heat and does not give it back. You can stand in sun on a dry pad and feel, at ankle height, a draft off the nearest pool as if the pool were a door left open onto a cellar.

Vegetation has not been invited and has not insisted. What grows is thin, local, and easily counted: a wire-grass in the cracks that never makes a sward, a few sedges ringing the more permanent pools, lichen the color of old brass on the north faces of concrete. No trees. No thicket. The green is a notation, not a covering. Where a person expects moss to soften the works, the works have stayed hard. Roots find the joints and stop. The yard’s abundance is not alive in that way.

What is dense is everything else. The floor is a packing of presences that nearly touch. Concrete pads from some small works—cooling, sorting, washing; the purpose has weathered out of the signage—sit at slight angles to one another, settled, not ruined into rubble. Between them, rails no longer continuous, a run of pipe on low saddles, chain that has become a mineral object, hopper cars without their trucks, a gantry whose legs have sunk to different depths so the beam across the top lists like a roofline after a slow quake. Slag, brick bats, coil ends, wooden sleepers gone silver, cable trays, a single enamel sink set into a plinth for no reason the plinth explains. None of it is heaped into a dump. Erosion and use together have sorted it into a settled form. Things rest where the water and the weight have agreed they should rest. The balance is old. A new arrival—a sheet of roofing, a glove, a length of rebar—does not stay proud of the surface for long. The yard takes it down to the common level.

The industry is still legible as industry, not as picturesque wreck. There are engines, in the modest sense: a pump house the size of a shed, brick and corrugated iron, one wall bowed; inside it a motor on a plinth, couplings, a flywheel stained the color of dried blood. Electrification remains in porcelain insulators on a short pole, in conduit that enters the ground and does not come out, in a switch cabinet whose door has seized half open. None of it is in full production. Some of it is not dead. On certain days a valve somewhere below the pad chatters, very small, and a pool at the eastern edge changes its level by a finger in the space of an hour, rising as if breathed, then slipping back through a crack the eye cannot find. A belt in the pump house sometimes turns a quarter revolution and stops, stirred by a shaft that still has a little play and a little water behind it. Scaled production has shrunk to this: a mechanism that no longer makes anything but continues, at long intervals, to move.

The turbulence is constant and almost entirely quiet. That is the rule that takes longest to believe. The yard is in a storm-state of rearrangement—edges slumping, films of water racing along grooves and then vanishing into joints, rust scales letting go, pads ticking as they warm and cool, the listed gantry working a fraction of a degree when the wind, which hardly registers as wind, leans on it. Pools exchange themselves. A skin of iron bacteria marbles, tears, reforms. Condensation maps and unmaps every metal surface on a cycle shorter than attention. From the embankment the floor looks stable, a diagram. From inside it, nothing holds its outline for the length of a slow look. Creation and destruction here are the same gesture, repeated at the scale of a drip. The anthill energy is hydraulic and chemical, not animal. There is no roar to accompany it. Sound has been taken down into the water and the clay. What remains is a low existence of noise: a tick, a single knock when a flake hits a pool, the faint tubular note of a pipe when its temperature changes, and under all of it a pressure more than a tone, as if the yard were full of held breath. Speech carries oddly. A voice seems louder than the place wants, and the place does not answer.

Animals are rare enough that a sighting reorganizes the hour. Tracks appear in the silt aprons—narrow, clawed, gone by afternoon when the apron has been rewritten. Once, a bird used the gantry as a pause between somewhere and somewhere, and left. The pools do not boil with life. A few water-striders occupy the blacker water and seem, in the quiet, loud. No chorus. No flies in any number. The ecology is mostly microbial and mineral: stains that are colonies, crusts that are both rust and life, a scum that forms by noon and is gone into the joint by dusk. Whatever larger creature comes here comes as a crossing, not a residence. The yard does not feed a population. It tolerates a visitor and then resumes.

The unusualness is modest and physical, not a wonder. It is the way the water finds level across pads that are not level, so that three separate pools will show the same hairline against different walls, as if consulting a single hidden surface. It is the sink, still glossy in its bowl, holding a permanent inch that never greens and never empties. It is the fact that the pump, which should be scrap, still answers to a head of water somewhere upslope of the lip—someone’s ditch, a leak in a larger works, a spring the excavation uncovered and then forgot. The yard is downstream of a decision it no longer remembers. That is as strange as it gets. No lights without sources. No voices in the pipes beyond the knock of temperature. The weirdness is that an industrial floor, left alone, has become a kind of instrument: dense, cool, wet, and playing itself below the threshold of noise.

Rules, as they emerge. You do not trust a surface that looks continuous; continuity here is a film. You do not expect the route you took in to be the route out—an island pad will have acquired a moat, or lost one. You do not listen for the activity. You watch for it: the darkening of a wall, the sudden ellipse of a ripple with no wind, the belt that has moved and will not admit it. Objects left on a pad migrate toward the joints by evening, not by magic but by the small collapses of wet grit under them. Names and marks do not last. Paint peels to the color of the water. The one readable plate on the pump, lettered in a plain industrial face, has lost its consonants to seep; the vowels remain as pits. Time in the yard is not ruined time. It is established time. The form is settled. The contents never stop exchanging places inside that form.

By late light—the same ordinary light, only lower—the weeping rock goes brighter where the water tracks, and the pools take on the color of the sky without becoming reflective in any generous way. They are too stained, too busy with their skins. The gantry’s list reads, for a moment, as intention. The pump house holds a warmer dark than the open pads. From the embankment the whole instrument is visible again, small enough to be one thought, packed enough that the eye keeps finding one more pipe, one more chain, one more pad it had folded into its neighbor. Nothing calls. The turbulence continues at the volume of a drip finding, at last, the pool it has been negotiating toward all afternoon. The yard accepts it without sound, and is already otherwise.
