The first thing the air does is refuse to be thin.

You are high—high enough that the horizon, when a horizon is permitted, bends with a tired honesty, high enough that breath ought to come short and cold. It does not. The warmth is metabolic, held in the mass the way a closed hand holds heat, and the wet is not weather arriving from elsewhere. It is the place sweating. Every surface you can put a palm to gives a little under the skin of your hand, not like mud, not like rot, but like something that has agreed, for a very long time, to remain a wall.

There is no stone under any of it. That is the fact that takes longest to believe, because the forms are so settled. Floors camber and drain. Galleries hold their spans. Joints weep in the same places they have wept since before anyone walking them could have counted the seasons. You look for bedrock the way you look for the back of a stage, and you do not find it. What you are standing on was compacted, layered, grafted, lashed, and then left to become what it already nearly was. Cord-scars run through the grain. Wedge-slots have healed around the wedges and carried them outward as the members thickened, so that a cut made at arm’s height is now a dark lens a day’s climb above the path, still holding, somewhere in its pith, the shape of a hand-tool that no longer exists as metal or as stone—only as a decision the plant kept.

The scale is wrong in the way a coastline is wrong when you have only ever seen ponds. A single chamber can brew its own wind. Not a draft: a circulation, a slow wheel of air that has a preferred direction and a season, fog forming in the lower third where the floor sags into a marsh of fiber and standing water, the fog lifting, striking the ceiling, and falling again as a rain so fine it never quite becomes sound. The ceiling in that chamber is a continent’s worth of living span, ribs of trained trunk, infill of woven cane gone rigid with age and secondary growth, and between the ribs a darkness that is only leaves, pressed to felt over centuries. Light comes through it as rumor. Where a designed opening still functions—an oculus no wider than a cart, kept clear by something that brows the rim and will not let it close—the day arrives as a coin on the floor, and everything that can move moves toward it or away from it with the seriousness of tide. Everywhere else the light is the light of the inside of a closed fruit: green at the edges of vision, brown where your eyes rest, warm, and insufficient for shadows to have sharp opinions.

Between these presences there is almost nothing.

That is the other fact, and it does not contradict the first. The architecture is total. There is no natural substrate left to find, no unmanaged slope, no original ground. And yet the volumes are empty in the way a lung is empty: the walls are the event; the air they enclose is most of the world. You can walk a hollow broad enough to have weather and not meet a partition for longer than thirst allows. Paths exist—raised cords of living root, worn glossy, repaired by the same slow graft-work that made them—but they are rare relative to the volume, threads in a held breath. Sound dies in the fiber. A call you make at the mouth of a gallery arrives somewhere else as a change in pressure, not as a word. The abundant life of the place is mostly known by what it has done to the structure, and by the small, close noises that never become a chorus: the tick of condensate freeing itself from a rib and falling into water that is already there; the shift of a grazer’s weight on a floor that answers by drinking the print and, over weeks, forgetting it; the dry kiss of wing against wing where, high in a dark that is not night, something innumerable turns as one and then does not, because there is no reason to.

The animals are many, and they are far apart, and both statements are true for the same reason the chambers can rain on themselves. In a volume this large, abundance does not mean crowd. Herds of slow, low beasts crop the inner meadows—those stretches of floor where a chamber has sagged into permanent wet and thrown up a lawn of the only plants that are not also the building. They are not wild in the old sense and not tame. They keep the lawn from becoming wall. Something larger follows them at a distance measured in silence, hunting by waiting, because pursuit would announce itself as a vibration in the joins and the joins remember. In the condensate there are lives too small to see except as a glittering when an oculus-coin slides across a film of water. In the high dark, under ceilings that have their own small climates, flocks roost on members that were trained, long ago, into perches, and the perches have thickened around the habit until the habit looks like design. Some of what you take for columns are not columns. They breathe on a period longer than attention. They have been load-bearing for so many generations that the difference between animal and architecture is a matter of who is consulting whom. You do not lean on these. The place has opinions about load, and they are old.

Nothing here is in ruin, and nothing here is new. Erosion and growth have argued themselves into a treaty. What dies is taken up before it can become a pile: fiber loosened by weather is rewoven by mouths and by the irritable tropisms of the living mass; a failed span does not fall so much as kneel and get grafted back into a lower duty, a bench, a drain, a place where water is allowed to pool because pooling there keeps it from pooling where a path must remain a path. The wet is constant without being flood. Joints weep. Capillary risers—built, unmistakably built, bundles of stem laid by hand and then forgotten by hands and remembered by sap—lift water from the marsh-floors into the high work, so that in places water moves upward with the calm of a rule rather than the urgency of a leak. Warmth follows the water. At this elevation the outside, if there is an outside, would be a thinner, colder country; you glimpse it only through the oculi, a hard blue too bright to love, and the glimpse never lasts, because the rim grows, and something bites the rim, and the coin of sky narrows until the chamber returns to its own dusk.

The breakdown is not a crack you can point to. It is the bookkeeping.

A gallery measured along its floor is a certain walk. The same gallery, measured by the thickness of the wall you just came through, cannot be that long; the wall was a single trained member, and you have been inside it, somehow, for hours. Perspective, consulted at the far end of a hollow, offers you a nearness your legs will spend a season refusing. Maps made in cord and charcoal—there are such maps, primitive, patient, corrected by people or by whatever still uses edged bone and fire-hardened points—fail at the folds, not because the makers were careless but because the folds are real. Space here is a material that was tensioned wrong, or right, at a scale where right and wrong stop being moral and become structural. You can stand in a place where your left foot is in a later hour than your right, not as vision but as fact: the condensate on one boot has already been rain and risen and rained again, while the other boot is still in the dry of the path. Growth rings, split open by a careful cut, contain weather. In the ring of a dry year there is dust that never existed in this elevation’s outside climate. In a wet ring, sometimes, a tiny preserved wind, a spiral stain, the fossil of a circulation that is still turning, larger now, in the chamber overhead.

Who made it is the wrong question, or a question the place has outgrown. Tool-marks are everywhere and nowhere attached to a maker you could meet. They have been translated outward by growth, the way a scar on a sapling becomes a feature of a tree and then of a forest and then of a roof. The tech never rose above the hand. No metal sings in the joins. What holds is lashing absorbed into bark, coppice-forks trained until they clasped, fire used not to clear but to persuade a surface to stop growing in one direction and start in another. If there are still makers, they are continuous with the maintenance fauna: small, quiet, abundant in sum and solitary in the moment, carrying water in leaf-cups to a graft that must not dry, chewing a encroaching cane because if they do not the oculus closes and a chamber loses the one argument it has with the sky. They do not speak in any register the galleries will carry. Their work is visible only as the absence of failure.

At the edges of the great hollows, where floor becomes wall by a curve too gradual to call a corner, the marsh thickens. Water stands among buttress-roots that are also, if you follow them, the pilings of a further gallery. Warm mist moves without wind. The smell is green iron and clean rot and the sweetness of sap under stress, never foul; the treaty includes sanitation. Things that must be broken down are broken down inside living thicknesses, out of the air. You can kneel—the floor gives, drinks the shape of your knee, begins, already, to forget you—and watch a film of water creep uphill along a bundle of stems no thicker than your wrist, headed for a height where it will weep out again and fall, in a week or in an hour, depending on which clock the bundle is keeping. Above you the ceiling is lost. Below you there is no below, only more made ground, older joins, water moving in dark conduits that were once footpaths and were retired downward as the mass thickened over them, a stratigraphy of decisions, all of them still alive enough to matter.

Walk long enough and the path climbs without your having chosen a mountain. The air cools a fraction and then changes its mind. An oculus ahead throws a blade of true daylight across the cord-path, and in that blade the abundance becomes briefly visible: moths like seeds, a scatter of bright beetles drinking the wet off the worn root, the long shadow of a flock crossing the opening so high above that their bodies are only a darkening of the coin. Then the blade is behind you, and the quiet returns, not as absence but as the native condition. Somewhere a column inhales. Somewhere a span, settled for centuries into the exact curve that its load requires, sheds a skin of fiber with a sound like a page turned in a room you are not in. The water finds its joints. The warmth holds. The vast interior, built entirely, occupied sparsely, continues its several slow processes, each one local, each one sufficient, none of them needing you to be weather, or witness, or the thing that keeps the rim from closing—though if you stay, and if you are the sort of animal that carries a cup, the place will not stop you. It has room. It was made of room. The room is what is alive.
