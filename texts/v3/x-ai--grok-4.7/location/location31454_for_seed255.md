The floor is not ground. It is a laid thing, and it never quite finishes being laid.

Low over a basin the size of a market town and the low hills that hold it, someone has been paving for longer than any living memory can name, and the work is still only partway through its first intention. Slabs of fired clay, split bone-white stone, and a dull grey brick that sweats in the cool meet edge to edge and then fail to meet. Between them run gutters that were never finished: some stop mid-span, some double back on themselves, some open into shallow cisterns no wider than a cartwheel. Water arrives as it pleases — a slow seep from a joint, a skin of condensation on a wall that has stood long enough to chill, an occasional fine rain that does not fall from any sky you can see but beads out of the dark overhead and runs, briefly, before the clay drinks it. Most of the basin stays dry. The damp places announce themselves by sound before they announce themselves by shine.

There is no plant in it. Nothing roots. The green you think you see in a corner is a stain in the mineral, copper or iron worked into a glaze and then abandoned when the kiln went cold. What grows, if that is the word, is the building itself. Walls rise where the paving thickens. They are hand-built, no mortar finer than mud and lime and crushed shell, courses slightly out of true, each course a decision someone made with a mallet and a string-line and then revised. Rooms accrete onto rooms. Passages pinch until two people cannot pass and then open, without warning, into courts paved three layers deep, the lowest layer still wet from a leak that has been leaking since the second layer went down. The whole basin is one fabric. You cannot find a place where the made thing ends and a natural substrate begins. Dig, and you find an older floor. Dig past that, and another. The oldest you can reach is still brick.

It is cool the way a cellar is cool, a temperature that does not change when you walk from the open courts into the stacked corridors. Breath shows, faintly, and then does not. The dark is not absolute. It is the dark of a workshop after the lamps have been taken elsewhere: a grey that lets you see the next three slabs and not the fourth. Here and there a shaft was left for light and then roofed over by a later course, so a pale seam runs along a ceiling and dies. Most of the work is done by touch and by the sound of your own foot finding a hollow.

The place is loud, and almost nothing in it is alive.

Water ticks in the unfinished gutters. A course of brick, still green in the middle, sighs as it dries and contracts, and somewhere a whole wall answers with a tick that runs the length of a street. Mallets. There are always mallets. You rarely see who holds them. The sound comes from inside a wall that was solid yesterday, or from under a floor that rings when you step, or from a court you have just left and that, when you turn, has grown a new pier. Clay is slapped. A string-line hums when someone plucks it to test the true. Kilns — low, clay-domed, fed by hand with whatever will burn, which is not much — breathe, and their breath is a soft roaring that carries strangely far in the dense fabric, so that three kilns at three distances sound like one weather. Pots knock. A cart with wooden wheels, unshod, crosses a court and the court answers in every loose slab. When rain beads out of the dark it hits a thousand unfinished lips of brick and becomes, for a minute, a roar, and then the clay takes it and the roar thins to ticking again.

Life is rare enough that a movement stops the work. Once, in a cistern that had held water long enough to film, something pale turned under the surface and was gone, and the mallets on that street stopped for the length of three breaths. No one spoke of it. The beasts, if they are beasts, keep to the wet pockets and the gaps between courses, and they do not announce themselves twice. You may walk a day and see nothing with an eye. You will hear, at the edge of the mallet-sound, a small dry scrape that is not a tool.

The strangeness is not a spectacle. It is a rule the place keeps and does not explain. Passages that were open in the morning are courses of brick by what passes for afternoon — you know the time only because the dark shifts half a shade when a kiln is opened somewhere upslope. A gutter you followed to a cistern leads, on the return, into a room that has a door cut for a person of your height, the edges still dusty with the drill of a hand-bow. Someone measured you. Or measured the idea of you, and built to it, and left. Tools lie where they were set down: wooden mallets, bronze points gone green, cords of twisted fiber, a plumb bob of fired clay. They are primitive and exact. No machine. No wheel that turns under power. The precision comes from repetition and from the fact that the builders do not seem to stop.

They are not often seen. A shoulder at the end of a corridor, already laying the next course. A hand withdrawing a line. Footprints in a skin of damp that fill, slowly, with seepage, and are gone. The work does not require an audience and does not pause for one. Where a wall is incomplete it is incomplete on purpose, or on a purpose you cannot yet read: a toothed edge left ready for the next day's brick, a socket for a beam that has not been cut, a threshold with no room beyond it, opening onto more paving and the low dark.

Stand in one of the larger courts — there are a handful, each the size of a small square, each paved in a different century of the same hand — and the basin shows itself as a single ongoing act. Low roofs step away in every direction, none higher than a tall tree would have been, if a tree had ever been allowed. Between them the gutters glint where water has found them. Sound comes in layers: near slap of clay, middle knock of pots, far roar of a kiln, and under all of it the tick of contraction as the newest courses decide whether they will hold. The air smells of wet mineral, of lime, of a smoke too thin to see. Nothing blooms. Nothing rots in the way of leaves. What fails simply returns to the courses, broken small, and is laid again.

If you wait long enough a fine rain begins without a cloud, and for a little while every unfinished lip in the basin speaks at once. Then the clay drinks, the mallets resume, and a passage behind you closes with the soft, exact sound of a brick set true.
