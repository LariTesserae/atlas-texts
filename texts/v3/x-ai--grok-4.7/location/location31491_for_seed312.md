The plateau does not announce itself. From its own surface it simply continues: pale stone under a hard, ordinary daylight, the horizon so far off that weather can be born on it and die before it reaches you. The air is thin enough that sound does not travel as it should. A herd can pass a bowshot away and arrive as a soft grit underfoot rather than a thunder. People learn to look, not listen.

The ground is young. Uplift is still in it. Ridges stand with edges that have not yet been worried round; fault scarps cut the flats in clean pale walls, and the talus at their feet is angular, blocks that still fit the faces they fell from if you had the patience to try. Rivers have only begun. They are not rivers so much as decisions: a seep that found a crack, a crack that became a slot, a slot that holds, for a season, a dark thread of melt. Most of the year the water is elsewhere—frost in the joints of stone, a skin of condensation on the shaded side of a wall at first light, a cistern that takes three days of low cloud to gain a hand’s depth. Rain comes, and is brief, and does not soak. It beads on the rock and is gone into the wind or into the jars.

The city is made of the same stone it stands on, and it is large in the way a weathering pattern is large: not a center with outskirts, but a persistence. Rooms are stacked where a scarp already offered a back wall. Lanes follow the natural joints. Roofs are flat slabs levered into place with poles and ramps and the bodies of many people, because there is no other machine. Timber is rare enough to be counted. What wood exists came up from lower country over weeks, or was grown in a pocket of shelter—dwarf shrubs no higher than a knee, twisted, and not cut if it can be helped. Most building is dry stone, hide lashings, bone pegs, clay tempered with grit. Metal, where it appears, is small: a knife, a needle, a band on a door. The craft is in the fitting. Walls are thick because thin walls fail in this cold, and because thickness is how you store the day’s heat for the night.

It is dense. Rooms share walls on three sides. Lanes are narrow enough that two laden animals must negotiate. Storage is vertical—grain in hide sacks hung from rafters, water in ranked jars, dung fuel dried on every south-facing ledge that catches the ordinary sun. People live close because closeness is warmth and because the good building sites, the ones with a wall already and a seep nearby, are not infinite even on a plateau this size. You can walk for a day through continuous construction and still see, between one roof and the next, the raw scarp the builders have not yet claimed.

The animals are more numerous than the people, and quieter than either expects. High grazers—compact, heavy-coated, with small ears and a patience for poor forage—move through the outer lanes as if the lanes were passes they have always used. They bed against house walls at night for the stored heat. Their dung is the fuel, their milk a large part of the diet, their bodies, in time, the leather and the bone. Birds nest in every unused corbel and in the fresh cracks of the scarps, and at dawn the thin air fills with a sound that does not carry: a close, papery chorusing that dies within a street. In the damp pockets—under eaves, in the throats of cisterns, along the young slots where water lingers—there are insects in numbers that surprise anyone from a greener country. They are the true abundance. The plateau looks empty of life until you kneel.

Plants are an exception the eye hunts for. Lichen maps the stone in slow continents of gray and rust. Cushion plants occupy the joints, green only for a short season, brown the rest, and are not grazed to nothing only because the herds prefer the wider flats where a thin sedge still holds. A tree, a real tree, would be an event. There are none in the city. Stories place them beyond the horizon, in country that drops, eventually, toward air thick enough to grow them. No one in ordinary life walks that far. The plateau generates its own wind and its own cloud and does not encourage leaving.

Change is measured in lifetimes of stone, not in seasons. A new room is a decade. A lane paved with flatter slabs is a generation’s boast. The scarps lengthen by less than a finger in a human life, and no one sees it, though everyone knows the cuts are fresher on the northern rise than on the southern, and that the northern rise is where the ground is still deciding to be higher. Weather cycles are broad and slow: a month of clear cold, a week of low cloud that wets every surface without ever becoming rain you can name, a wind that starts as a feeling in the teeth and is still the same wind three days later, pushing little, persistent, the kind of wind that moves dunes of grit one grain at a time across a roof until the roof’s edge is lined with a small dune and someone sweeps it into a jar to use as temper.

At midday the light is simply light—white, high, without theater. Shadows are sharp because the air is clean and the features are sharp. You can see a rider two hours off and not hear the animal until it is in the lane, and by then the city has already made room, the way it makes room for herds, for birds, for the slow arrival of another course of stone. At night the cold takes back what the walls stored. People and animals sleep in the same thick rooms. Outside, the plateau continues, vast enough to have weather of its own, quiet enough that the loudest thing for miles is water moving, somewhere under the stone, still deciding which way to cut.
