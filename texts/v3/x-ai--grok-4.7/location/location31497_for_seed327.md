The place is a bowl, and it is not large. From the lip of one wall to the lip opposite is a walk you could make before your breath fully failed, if the air were kinder and the stone were not wet. It sits so high that the sky above it has thinned to something you can feel in the teeth. Standing in the middle, you occupy almost nothing: a small warm animal in a volume of cold, scarce air. The hollow itself is the fact of the place. Rock rises around you in a closed circuit, old and settled, no fresh scar, no recent fall worth mentioning. The walls have been this shape longer than any path that leads to them.

Water does not arrive as weather so much as reside. It sweats from the stone in slow sheets, darkening the grain, gathering in the horizontal joints where the rock remembers an older bedding. In the floor it stands in shallow pans no deeper than a wrist, clear enough that the pebbles look varnished. Between pans the ground is not dry. Press a boot and it answers with a soft suck, then a thread of water finding a lower thread. Moss holds what the pans refuse. After a wind, droplets reappear on the north faces as if the air had been wrung. There is no river leaving. Whatever enters stays until the thinness of the height takes it back up, or until it slips under the lowest course of stone and is gone into the mountain's private plumbing. You hear that leaving more than you see it: a muffled pulse underfoot, irregular, like someone walking in a room below the room.

The vegetation should not be this sure of itself. At this height the rule is cushion and crust, a few gritted flowers, and then stone. Here the rule has been bent by the water that will not leave. Sedges stitch the pans. A low willow, no taller than a crouched person, repeats itself in a broken ring where seepage is constant, its leaves the color of worn tin on the undersides. Ferns occupy the weeping joints, not as a meadow but as a grammar of green in the cracks, each clump feeding on a film. Lichen maps the drier bosses in ochre and near-black, thick enough in places to take a thumbnail print. Nothing towers. The lushness is lateral and interstitial: a skin over the bowl, thickest where water is nearest the surface, thinning to almost nothing on the sunward rims. From above, if you had a way to be above, it would read as a green stain in a gray cup. From inside, it reads as a room that has been slowly upholstered.

Light is ordinary daylight, and that ordinariness is part of the unease. No perpetual dusk, no colored gloom. When the cloud lifts, sun arrives squarely and shows every wet surface for what it is. The trouble is how often the cloud does not lift far. It sits on the rim or just inside it, and the bowl becomes a lit interior under a low ceiling, bright enough to work by, too bright to be a cave, not bright enough to feel like a summit. Shadows are short-lived. A gap opens, a wall flashes, the gap closes. You keep misjudging distance because the light is honest and the air is not. Things look near. Your lungs disagree.

Sound does not behave as it should in a small place. The thin air carries poorly for speech and extravagantly for strike and wing. A single dislodged pebble ticks, then returns from three walls at slightly different times, so that the bowl seems to answer in a small committee. Water contributes a layered noise that never resolves into one stream: drip, film, the soft tear of a pan overflowing its moss lip, the underground pulse. Wind, when it finds the rim, does not roar across an open fell. It enters by notches the erosion has finished long ago, and those notches are tuned. Two of them speak in a low flute when the wind is steady. A third rattles, because a slab there is not quite seated and has not been quite seated for an age. The sound is loud without being violent. It fills the hollow the way water fills the pans: continuously, with little empty air left for quiet, even though the volume itself is mostly unoccupied.

The animals use that. They are many for a place this small and this high, and they do not crowd. They treat the bowl as a hall with good acoustics and reliable wet. Birds come in numbers that would stock a valley copse, but they are strung through the air column and the willow ring rather than heaped. A flock lifts from the sedge and the sound of it is bigger than the flock: wings, then wings again off the stone, then a thin cry that seems to originate in the wall. They nest in the joints where fern and drip keep the rock from fully freezing on ordinary nights, and some of the nests are old, rebuilt on the same cushions of lichen-moss until they have become part of the wall's furniture. Under the willow, shrew-small bodies make a constant dry whisper. You rarely see them. You see the sedge shiver a half-second after the whisper, as if the plants were thinking. Something larger hunts them on a circuit: a low, quick mammal the color of wet stone, appearing at the pan edges and disappearing into cover that should not hide it. Overhead, when the ceiling of cloud tears, a hunter hangs and does not need to hang long. The prey is loud. The bowl advertises it.

There is a rhythm to the day that is not a story so much as a set of overlapping machines. Morning, the pans smoke though the air is cool, not cold enough to rime except in the deepest notch. Insects rise off the water film in brief, glittering insults to the height, and the birds answer, and for an hour the sound thickens until speech would be a waste. Midday, if sun holds, the willow leaves turn their pale sides and the drip slows, not stops; the underground pulse continues as if it answers a schedule written in the rock, not in the sky. Afternoon wind finds the notches. The flutes start. The loose slab ticks. Animals shift to the lee walls. Near evening the temperature falls only a little — the bowl holds damp and therefore holds a grudging warmth — and the birds return to the joints with a noise like dry seed poured into a wooden box, amplified. Nothing here is still for long. Even the settled stone is in conversation with water that has been filing the same grooves since before the notches learned their notes. Established, not finished. In balance the way a wheel is in balance: moving, and meant to.

People have been here, and not as a crowd. The marks are few enough that you can count them without trying. On the east rim, where a notch gives the only reasonable entry, a line of small stones has been set on edge, not a wall, a reminder that the drop inside is more sudden than the approach suggests. Lower, tucked under an overhang that weeps just to one side of dry, someone built a shelter the size of a bunk: a few slabs leaned and chinked with moss, a floor of laid flat stones raised a hand above the suck of the ground. The roof has slipped. One slab rests now at an angle that still sheds most of the drip. Inside, the soot of old fires stains a patch of ceiling no wider than a face. The fires were small. Fuel would have been willow stem and the dry rind of lichen, and not much of either. There is no metal. A peg of bone, or perhaps hardened wood, is jammed in a crack near the shelter, worn smooth where a cord worried it. The cord is gone. In the willow ring, three stems have been bent and tied, long ago, into a low arch that no longer serves any obvious purpose; the ties are bark strips, black and tight. On a dry boss above the highest pan, someone pecked a short row of cups into the stone, each cup the size of a thumbpad, linked by a scratched line. They hold water when the air is condensing and empty when the sun has worked. Whether they were a map, a count, or a game is not available from the stone. They are precise. They took time. They face the entry notch, not the view, because from inside this bowl there is no view. The mountain outside is a rumor told by wind in the flutes.

The unusual thing is not a creature and not a light. It is the agreement between height and wetness and noise. The air is poor. Your pulse is loud in your own ears, and the bowl adds to it. You can see, on a clear tear of cloud, a distance that belongs to summits — a far ridge, snow on it or the memory of snow — and then the tear closes and you are returned to a room the size of a hamlet's single big building, lush in the joints, ringing with birds that should have thinner lives. The stone has accepted this. Its forms are rounded where hands of water have worked, sharp only where a notch still does the wind's work, and nothing looks interrupted. The shelter is the newest object, and it is already being edited: moss in the chinks, a fern in the slipped roof-joint, drip finding the edge of the laid floor and beginning, patiently, the same filing it has practiced on the mountain since the bowl first closed.

If you stay until the light goes ordinary-dark, the sound does not follow the light down. The flutes keep if the wind keeps. The underground pulse keeps regardless. Something small crosses a pan and the water reports it to every wall. You stand in the middle because the middle is the only place with a little space around the body, and even there you are not in an emptiness that is silent. You are in an emptiness that is occupied by processes: water changing address, air too thin to be a comfort and too full of echoes to be a void, green life drinking a summit it has no right to, and a few pecked cups on a boss, filling and emptying on the mountain's own clock, loud with the minute clap of a drop when one finally spills.
