The basin sits lower than the ground around it, but not by much—a shallow drop, the kind a person could step into without thinking of it as a descent. Its rim is a low ring of stone, weathered to the same dull warmth as everything else, and beyond that ring the land simply stops mattering. What holds is the interior: a place no larger than a small hall, walled on three sides by rock that has been weeping for longer than anyone has kept count, and open on the fourth only enough to let a gray light in.

The light never brightens. It arrives already used, filtered through a narrow cleft and then through the steam that hangs a hand’s breadth above the water. There is always steam. The air is warm—not hot, not the heat of a vent that would scald, but the steady warmth of stone that has soaked up sun somewhere upstream and given it back slowly, all day and all night, with no difference between them. Nothing here has a season. The temperature at the surface of the pools is the temperature it was yesterday, and the temperature the stone remembers from a long time before yesterday.

Water is everywhere and almost nowhere in motion. It stands in a chain of shallow basins the rock has made for itself, each one brim-full, each one feeding the next by a sheet so thin it does not sound like falling. It beads on the walls and runs in threads no wider than a finger, always in the same threads, darkening the same streaks of mineral. It films the floor between the pools so that stone and water are hard to tell apart until a foot finds one or the other. The smell is wet stone and a faint metallic sweetness, nothing rotten, because there is almost nothing here to rot.

What fills the space is not growth in any green sense. The basins are packed. Shelves of pale crust have built out from every edge, layer on layer, until the open water is only a set of dark eyes in a floor of cream and rust and dull white. Knobs and curtains of the same material hang from the low ceiling where the weep is strongest, close enough in places that a person would have to turn sideways. Between them the walls are armored in ripples, each ripple the width of a thumbnail, each one the record of water that arrived, paused, and left a little of itself behind. The crust is hard. It rings faintly if struck, though no one strikes it. It has reached the shape it is going to keep. New mineral still arrives, but so slowly, and so evenly, that the eye cannot catch the place becoming different. Erosion and deposit have agreed. Edges are soft. Corners have been argued down to curves. Nothing is fresh-broken. Nothing is about to fall.

There are no plants. Not a mat of moss in the wettest seam, not a seedling in a crack, not even the black-green film that usually finds warm water before anything else does. The stone is too mineral, or the light too poor, or both; whatever the reason, the rule holds all the way to the rim. Life, if it is here at all, is the kind that does not show. A pale scum in one pool might be mineral, or might not. It does not move.

What moves is rarer still. Now and then a small dark thing—too quick to name, the size of a thumb—crosses a wet ledge and is gone into a crevice that has always been there. That is the whole of the animal traffic. No birds come to the cleft. No insects stitch the steam. The quiet is not the quiet of a pause. It is the sound the place makes when it is being itself: a spaced drip from the same three points in the ceiling, a low sip where one basin gives to the next, and under that a hush so complete that a person’s own breath becomes the loudest event.

Someone has been here, and not only once. The marks are few, and they have been here long enough to join the weathering. On the open side, three steps have been cut into the approach, their edges rounded now, their treads polished by water and by feet that no longer come. Beside the largest pool a flat stone has been set level—not quarried to any great craft, only chosen and turned until it stopped rocking. On it, a shallow groove runs to the pool’s lip, the kind of channel a hand-tool makes when someone wants water to go one way and not another. A clay cup, thick-walled, unglazed, sits in a niche at knee height. It is full. It is always full; condensation, or a thumb of seep from the wall behind it, keeps it so. The cup’s rim is worn on one side, where a mouth would meet it. No metal anywhere. No mortar. The work is the work of people who had stone, cord, fire, and time, and who did not need this place to be anything but a place to drink and to sit.

They did not build a roof. The rock already was one. They did not clear the crust. There would have been no clearing it without breaking the thing they came for. What they left is scattered through the density rather than set against it: the steps, the seat-stone, the cup, and, low on the inner wall, a row of short vertical scratches, evenly spaced, too regular to be cracks. They might be a count. They might be nothing anyone alive could read. Lichen has not softened them, because lichen does not live here. Water has. The scratches are rounding at their ends, the way all the other edges have rounded, and they will outlast the meaning that made them.

Stand in the middle and the boundaries are immediate. Behind, the cleft and the gray day. To either side, wall. Ahead, the last pool, smaller, tucked under a curtain of crust that nearly meets the water. Past that there is no further room. The place does not open into a system. It does not suggest a larger cave. It is one room, fully occupied, warm, dim, and wet to the skin within a minute of standing still. Leave it, and the air outside feels thin and quick by comparison, though the outside is only a step up and a step away. Come back after a year, or ten, and the cup will still be full, the drip will still fall in the same three places, and the dark eyes of water in the pale floor will still be where you left them.
