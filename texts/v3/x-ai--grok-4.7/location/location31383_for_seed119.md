The place is a single room, and it has been a single room for so long that the idea of an outside has worn down to a rumor in the mortar.

From the low ground it occupies, you would miss it if the mouth were shut. The footprint is no larger than a hamlet’s common: a squat oval of wall, shoulder-high on the weather side, sinking on the other until the lip is only a curb. Everything above that curb was built. There is no bedrock showing, no bank of native soil, no seam where a hill was cut and the cut left honest. The wall is coursed block and packed clay, shell-lime gone gray, timber that was once posts and is now a dark grain inside the mass where the clay took it and kept it. The tools that made it were simple. You can still read them if you put a hand to the inner face: chisel-flats, the drag of a stone adze, finger-courses where someone thumbed a joint true before it set. A little metal, here and there, green as old rain — pins, not machines.

Inside, the floor drops. Not into grandeur. Into absence.

The hollow is cathedral only in the way a throat is cathedral: tall enough that your voice goes up and does not come back all at once, narrow enough that you can walk the water’s edge and return to your own footprints while the light is still the same light. The middle is empty. Not empty of event — empty of things. Piers stand at long intervals, four of them, maybe five, too few to be a forest and too regular to be a ruin. Between them the air is a volume with almost nothing in it. You can see across. You can hear across. What you cannot do is fill it. Density here is a kind of weather: vast space between presences, a hollow that has learned its own size and refuses clutter.

The floor is water more often than it is paving. A hand’s depth in the channels, a film on the flags, a black lens in the sump at the center where the builders, with no instruments but a plumb of strung pebble, found the low and honored it. Water does not arrive as weather from a sky you can see. It arrives as the room’s habit. The walls weep on established lines — the same joints, the same salt-crusted mouths, the same slow bright threads that have been bright so long the stone beneath them is softer than the stone beside them, and has stopped getting softer. Erosion here is not a crisis. It is the finished argument between making and time. The place has reached the shape it means to keep.

Cool, always. Dim, always. Light enters by slits high in the haunch of the wall, cut when the work was young and since then half-closed by the slow push of clay. What comes through is the color of tin left in a cupboard. It does not travel far. It lays a pale road on the water and gives up. The rest of the hollow holds its own dusk, not night, not day — the light of a room that was roofed before anyone now living could have seen it unroofed. Condensation beads on the underside of the corbels and falls when it must. The air is wet enough to taste. Your sleeves dampen without rain.

Vegetation is an afterthought the stone allowed and then limited. A gray-green crust in the weep-lines. A fist of rush in a joint that never quite dried. One shelf of something like fern, rooted in mortar dust and the powder of old timber, that has neither spread nor died within memory of the drips. No canopy. No understory. The room is not a garden. It is a vessel that leaks life in pinches, and those pinches have learned not to ask for more.

The life that is abundant does not grow. It moves.

They are many, and they do not occupy the hollow so much as traffic its skins. On the waterline, a pale traffic of small bodies — things with too many joints, things that skitter and then go still as if the drip-rhythm were a sentence they were waiting to finish. In the sump, flashes: not a school that fills the lens, a few dozen that cross it and leave it black again. Up in the unlit haunch, the sound of wings in numbers that would crowd a smaller room, here only a passing weather. They roost on the same corbels, hunt the same film of gnats that rise when the wall warms by a degree and fall when it cools. Something larger works the margin. You do not see it whole. You see the consequence: a ring on the water, a sudden absence in the skitter, a wet clap against a pier and then the long loud aftersound the hollow is built, accidentally or not, to keep.

They are loud because the room is empty. A single drip arrives as a syllable and leaves as a small crowd. Wingbeats knock. The skitter, multiplied by stone, becomes a dry rain that never quite starts. Calls — thin, glass-edged, not birds exactly and not frogs exactly — strike the far wall and return on a delay just long enough to seem answered by another creature. Sometimes they are. Predator and prey run on the same clock as the weeping: a quickening when the walls give more, a hush when a cold settles and the films retreat into cracks. Nothing here is still for the length of a watch. The processes overlap. Water migrates. Heat drains upward and comes back down as beads. The small hunters work the light-road and abandon it. The larger one makes its circuit, pier to pier, and the circuit is old enough to have worn a darker track in the algae film, a path that does not widen and does not heal.

The strangeness is not a monster in the sump. It is a rule, and the rule holds.

Nothing in the room eats what the room did not already contain. There is no door in use. The mouth at the curb is a slot a body could leave by, and the fauna do not. Generations have finished their lives between the same four piers. Their bones — tiny, numerous, pale as the lime — collect in the lee of the sump and do not leave either; the water sorts them into a beach no wider than a sleeve, and the beach is part of the equilibrium, neither growing into a hill nor washing away. Nutrient moves in a closed figure: film, grazer, hunter, dust, film. The plants stay sparse because the loop does not offer them a surplus. The animals stay many because the loop is fast, wet, and never interrupted by a winter hard enough to end it.

Another rule, quieter: sound pools. There are places, a step off the obvious path, where a drip you heard at the wall is louder at your ear than it was at its source, and places where your own footfall thins to nothing, as if the hollow had a pocket it preferred to keep private. The builders may have done this with curves and empties, the way a singer finds a note in a stair. Or the centuries of weeping may have tuned it, grain by grain, until the room learned where to keep a noise. Stand in the wrong spot and the colony in the haunch sounds like it is inside your hood. Step once, and it is far again. The animals know the map. They cross the loud places when they mean to be heard, and the thin places when they mean to hunt.

And the water, close to the surface always, has a tide that is not the moon’s. It breathes on a longer count than rain. Over the course of a day the film on the flags thickens, the sump takes a finger more, the weep-lines brighten; then, without a storm and without a drain you can see, it gives the water back into the clay of the walls, and the walls hold it the way a made thing holds a habit. You can watch the dark lens shrink. You can watch it return. It has done this through the whole settled life of the room, and the piers are stained at exactly the heights the breath requires, no higher. The stains are the calendar. There is no other.

If you stay, the scale corrects your sense of abundance. At first the noise says the hollow is full. Then your eyes adjust to the dim and you see the truth of the volume: almost nothing per arm’s reach of air, a few bodies on a long pier, a glint that is already gone, the wet shine of empty flags running away to the far curve. The fauna are a multitude with manners. They leave the middle alone. The middle is the room’s real estate, its wealth, the thing the builders — whoever they were, with their adzes and their pins and their patience — seem to have protected above every other function. A small place. A total construction. A weathered calm. And in the calm, loudness moving through emptiness, as if the point of making a world this size was to give the drips a hall, and the hunters a circuit, and the dark above the water a reason to keep breathing on its own slow count.
