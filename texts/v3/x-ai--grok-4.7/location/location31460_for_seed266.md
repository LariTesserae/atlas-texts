The height is the first fact, and it never quite becomes background. The land sits so far above any former sea that the horizon does not curve the way a lower eye expects; it simply fails, a pale smear where rock and air decide they are the same substance. Daylight is ordinary in color—a white-gold that could belong to any afternoon—but it arrives thin, already spent by the climb, and everything it touches looks both brightly lit and slightly underfed.

There is no water worth the name. What moisture exists is a rumor in the joints of machines and in the sealed tanks of the towns, recycled until it tastes of metal and old decisions. The ground is salt-crust, sintered dust, and a pale mineral that flakes like mica and rings, faintly, if a boot scuffs it wrong. Wind has had geological time to finish its argument. Ridges are rounded where they were once sharp; gullies are wide, shallow, and empty, their floors polished to a dull glass. Nothing here is young except what was recently built, and even that is already taking on the country’s color.

The settlements are real cities, not camps. They sit far apart—hours, sometimes a day, of empty plateau between one and the next—so that from the edge of one you cannot see the next, only the straight pale scar of a road or the repeated tooth of a transmission tower diminishing into glare. Inside the walls the density jumps: stacked housing in sun-baked brick and poured stone, gantries, rail yards, foundries whose stacks rise higher than the habitations because the air up here will not hold smoke the way a lower sky would. The industry is old enough to have manners. Engines turn at a patient rate. Conveyors move mineral from open cuts that have been widened for centuries until they look like natural basins. Electricity runs, but it does not chatter; the grid is overbuilt and underloaded, and the lights in the streets come on as if apologizing for the waste.

No trees. No grass. No green at any scale the eye can name. What stands in for growth is a slow mineral bloom—plates of copper-green and rust-orange that are not plants but the long weathering of ore bodies left open to warm air. They do not spread in a season. A child can watch the same face of a cut for a lifetime and swear it has not moved, and be nearly right.

And yet the animals are everywhere, in the way a sparse country can still be full if you learn where to look. They are small, mostly, and they do not graze. Flocks of pale, dry-winged things wheel over the cuts at midday and drop into the shade of machinery to pick at the films that form on warm metal. Larger animals, long-legged and almost colorless, travel the roads at dusk in loose strings, not herds so much as agreements; they lick salt from the verge and from the drip-trays under coolant lines, and they sleep standing, facing the wind. In the cities, something like lizards occupies the warm undersides of pipes. At night a softer population emerges from cracks in the sinter: many-legged, nearly silent, farming the slow chemistry of the dust itself. Nothing here eats a leaf because there is no leaf. The food web begins in oxidation, in static, in the thin bacterial mats no one bothers to call life, and in the deliberate waste of the foundries, which the wild things have learned as weather.

The strangeness is not a spectacle. It is a rule that does not match the body you brought. Shadows at this elevation fall a little too short for the hour. Sound does not carry. A hammer in a yard a street away arrives as a thought rather than a noise; the whole city conducts itself in a hush that would be eerie if it were not simply physics—thin air, soft ground, engines tuned for endurance rather than display. People speak close. Bells, where they exist, are large and low, built to push a tone through air that would otherwise swallow it.

Time moves the way erosion moves. A road is not repaired so much as allowed to become the next version of itself. Festivals, if they happen, mark the slow brightening of a mineral face or the decade when a particular cut finally yields a cleaner ore. Weather, such as it is, consists of warm winds that rearrange the dust into new maps and then leave them. Storms are rare and almost dry: a brown wall, a pressure drop, an hour of stung eyes, and afterward the same plateau, slightly rearranged, already looking as if it had always been that way.

Stand on the outer wall at late afternoon and the city behind you ticks and hums at a volume just above nothing. Ahead, the plateau runs out toward a horizon that refuses to finish. A string of the long-legged animals crosses a salt pan the size of a district, and their feet make no sound you can hear. Above them the thin daylight holds, ordinary and insufficient, over a country that has already done all its hurrying and now only continues.
