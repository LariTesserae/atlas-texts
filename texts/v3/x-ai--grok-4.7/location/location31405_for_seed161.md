The summit is not a peak. Whatever mountain this was has already finished its life as a mountain, and what remains is a floor held up in the sky: a table of dark stone, longer than it is wide, small enough that a person walking without hurry can make the whole circuit before the dim day has much changed. The cliffs do not crumble. They have reached the shape wind can keep. Below the lip the rock bells outward and then falls, clean, into a depth that has no floor you can usefully imagine; the drop is simply the edge of the livable world. Above, the sky is close and brassy, thickened by a mineral haze that never quite settles and never rains out. Light comes through it already tired. Shadows are soft. The stone underfoot is warm anyway, a stored warmth, as if the table spent a longer age in the sun than the air above it is willing to admit.

There is no water on it. No seep, no cistern gleam, no dark line in a gutter. Rain is not a season here; it is a story older than the footpaths, and the footpaths are worn into bedrock. What moisture exists is locked inside things — fat leaves, sap, blood, the damp of a shut room — and the place is organized around never letting it become a surface. Cut a leaf and it beads, slow and thick, more oil than drink. People press it. They have always pressed it. The presses are stone, hand-turned, stained black in the cups, and they sit in shared courtyards the way wells would sit in another country.

The green is real, and it is dense, and it does not look wet. Every joint in the paving has been argued into a planting pit: dust, ash, husk, and the crushed residue of old leaves, tamped down and planted again. The growth is waxy and grey-green, blue-green in the deeper shade, sword-leaved along the lanes and swollen into clubs and rosettes where a wall gives it a pocket. Nothing towers. The tallest growth is a man and a half, hard-stemmed, slow to make wood, pruned forever into the same knobbed shapes because timber is a generation’s work and fuel is the prunings. From above — if there were an above that wasn’t haze — the table would read as a single thicket with roofs in it. From inside, it reads as a town that has grown through its own orchard until the two can no longer be sorted.

The town is the table. There is no field beyond the last house, because the last house is built to the lip, its outer wall flush with the cliff, its roof sloping inward so that what little wind arrives does not take the thatch. The thatch is not for rain. It is for glare that no longer exists and for dust that does, layered cane and split leaf, patched rather than replaced, silvered on the weather side. Under it the streets are slots, stone on both sides, wide enough for two people and a burden if everyone has agreed to the passing. Where agreement fails, there are steps up and through: a second path across roof-edges and party walls, polished by bare feet and sandals into a second street in the air. Building is simple and exact. Fitted stone, clay tempered with plant fiber, wooden pins, a little soft metal for the things that must not split — hinge-pegs, knife-rivets, the rings in the presses. No engines. No carts with anywhere to go. Loads move on backs and on low sledges that shriek, because the polished caprock gives them nothing to bite.

The noise is the climate. It does not arrive and depart; it is the condition of the air, the way the haze is. Swifts — or something that lives as swifts live — nest in the undercut of the lip by the thousand, and their traffic at the edge is a continuous tearing sound, wings and thin cries, a surf with no water under it. In the thatch a smaller, drier scream goes on without reference to the hour: insects in the cane, patient, innumerable, loud enough that a newcomer shouts for a day and then stops noticing until they leave and the quiet elsewhere feels like a missing wall. Hooves tick. There are little cliff-footed beasts, goat-small, hornless, with the calm of animals that have never seen a predator large enough to hurry them; they sleep on party walls and drop into the planting pits to strip a rosette down to its bitter core, and someone leans out and strikes a stone with a stick, not in anger, in the old correction. Lizards tick too, on every warm sill. Birds that are not swifts work the pits for peel and seed and shout at each other from a distance of inches. Human voices fit themselves into what is left: short, projected, unhurried. A lane is a speaking-tube. You hear a bargain three turns away and the breath in it, because the air is thin and the walls are close and nothing is moving fast enough to Doppler.

Abundant, here, means you are never the only body in reach. A handspan of sun-warmed coping has a lizard and the husk of a beetle and the claw-marks of a roost. A doorway has a bird’s nest in the hinge side, tolerated because the bird eats the things that eat stored sap. Children sleep on roofs in the warm dark, and so do the little beasts, and so do the cats — if they are cats; they are the size of cats and have the same opinion of swifts. The fauna is not wild in the sense of elsewhere. It is civic. It has addresses. The nesting colony on the northern lip belongs to the northern quarter the way the press belongs to the courtyard, and people walk the cliff-path at the slow hour to look down into the rookery with the attention of householders checking a store.

Change is weak. The day warms and does not blaze; the haze sees to that. The night cools and does not bite; the stone gives back what it kept. Seasons are a slight shift in which leaves are worth pressing, which insects own the thatch, whether the swifts are thicker or merely louder. Dust drifts to the lee edge and stays. There are mounds there, older than the present street plan, made of ash and husk and bone and the grit the haze donates, and they have the look of landform rather than refuse. Nothing washes them away. Nothing is buried by flood or turned by frost. A thing set down stays until a hand moves it, and many things have not been moved. The oldest paths are grooves. The oldest thresholds are a finger-width lower than the rooms they enter, not by design but by the passage of feet across a stone that has already done all the eroding it intends to do and now only polishes.

The rules are small and strict, and nobody narrates them.

Moisture moves through bodies and is returned to the pits: the pressed leaf, the peel, the night-soil, the rinse that is barely a rinse — a cloth wrung back into a bowl, the bowl tipped onto the roots that earned it. Washing, in the lowland sense, is a waste nobody can afford; people oil their skin with the bitter fraction of the press, and the smell of the town is that oil gone warm, plus animal, plus the mineral breath of the stone, plus smoke from ovens that burn only what the thicket can spare. Bread is dense and a little sweet and mostly leaf-meal and seed. Meat is rare and loud in its absence, because every beast is counted and most of them are more useful alive, making more noise, making more dung for the pits.

You do not throw anything off the edge. The edge is not a dump. It is the wall, and below it the swifts, and below them a sheer that has killed, over the ages, exactly as many people as have treated it like a metaphor. There are stairs down, cut in a crease on the western side, so worn they are almost a ramp, and they do not go to water or to a lower town of any size. They go to the traveling, which is slow and seldom, and to the thin terraces clinging just under the lip where a few hard plants are grown for fiber, and then they stop being a path and become a story. Most lives happen entirely on the table. A person can know every face, every press, every roost, and still spend a decade without walking the western stairs.

Indoors the dimness completes itself. A room is a box of shade with a slit for air and a floor that holds the day’s heat into the night. People work where the slit lets them see: mending cane, knapping the poor flint that turns up in the dust-mounds, carving pins, twisting cord. The loudness comes in with the air. Nobody finds this strange. A quiet room would mean the thatch had failed, or the swifts had gone, and either of those would be the actual emergency — a change in the sound, which is the only fast thing the table knows how to do, and which it almost never does.

Stand at the center, where the lanes kink around an old depression no deeper than a knee, filled not with water but with the finest dust and a mat of the smallest rosettes. This is the lowest point, and it is still a summit. The roofs step up from it toward the lip in a shallow bowl of thatch and stone and grey-green clubs of leaves. Someone is turning a press; the wooden screw talks to itself. A sledge complains two lanes over. The insects do not pause for either. A child on a roof-path crosses above you, then a small beast, then the child coming back, none of them fast. The haze makes the far side of the town look a little less certain than the near side, which is the only distance available. Beyond the roofs, if you take a lane all the way, the floor simply ends. Warm stone, a last line of sword-leaf planted where the wind is least, a parapet shin-high and polished by hands, and then air, and the long sheer, and the surf-sound of wings rising up the face as if the cliff itself were breathing.

Nothing in that view is gathering itself to change. The table has already become what it is going to be. The town has fitted itself into that final shape and grown dense enough to fill it, leaf against wall against wing against voice, and the days repeat, warm and dim and dry, loud with lives that do not need a river to continue.
