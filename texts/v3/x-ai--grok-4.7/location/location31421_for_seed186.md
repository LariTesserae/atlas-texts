The height arrives before the ground does. The air thins, and with it the distance sound can travel, until even the gravel underfoot gives back only a short, dry tick and then nothing. What opens ahead is not a peak. It is a plain that has been a plain for so long the mountains it once was have been argued down to shoulders, shallow valleys, and a few towers that refused the last of the wearing and now stand as the land’s memory of being steep. The whole of it sits above the weather of lower country, in a cold that does not come and go so much as reside. The cold is the color of the stone. It has been here as long as the shapes have.

The dark is resident too. A lid of high cloud hangs just out of reach, and under it a haze of mineral dust too fine to see as dust, only as a shortening of the day that never quite begins. When the lid thins, the light that gets through is small, bruised, and quickly spent. The growth drinks what the cloud does not. Distances remain readable—near gravel from far gravel, a dark patch from a pale one—but nothing casts a hard shadow, and color stays close to the body, as if embarrassed to travel.

Water is a visitor that will not admit it lives here. Most of the open ground is only damp: a film on the shaded side of a stone, a rime that forms on whatever faces the wind and then loosens, grain by grain, into the gravel. Now and then a drizzle thickens out of the haze without warning, wets the back of a rise, and is gone before it can gather into a stream. There are no rivers. There are pockets. In the old hollows, tarns the size of ponds sit black and unfrozen, bitter with metal, breathing a faint warmth that has nothing to do with the sun. Around them the damp concentrates, and there the land stops being empty.

The emptiness is the rule and the growth is the exception, and the exception is lavish. Between the hollows you can walk a long time and meet only stone, rust-colored fines, and the occasional spine of pale sinter. Then a basin fills, wall to wall, with a thicket that would be forest if forest were the right word. Mats to the knee, then to the thigh; stands of black frond and copper-green filament; hairy surfaces, scaled surfaces, surfaces channeled like gutters, all of them built to comb water out of moving air and refuse to give it back. They are not waiting for light. Their plenty is chemical. A leaf will show, if you look closely enough and long enough, a sheen that is not dew—a thin plate of copper coming up through the skin, an underside laced with iron, a stem jacketed until it rings, very softly, like a small cold bell, and then the sound dies in the thin air as if it had been a mistake.

Stand in one of these basins and the place begins to disclose its work. The thicket is not a tangle. It is a sequence. The outer fringe takes the wind and strips it of moisture. The middle band, warmer by a fraction, plates what the water brought down—metals, salts, a dust of ore the haze has been carrying for miles. The inner band feeds the tarn, and the tarn’s slow chemistry vents warmth, and the warmth drives the next lifting of damp into the fronds, and the cycle turns over without a wheel. Each basin runs a version of the same machine. From a rise you can see several at once, dark islands on a pale reach, some with a gray column of their own weather standing over them while the gravel between stays dry. No two are identical. All of them are busy. None of them were built.

The towers that looked, at a distance, like stacks are the same process left to run past the thicket’s edge. Mineral water has been breathing out of the rock for an age, and the breath has piled itself into layered chimneys, hollow in places, warm at the mouth, smelling of wet iron and cold stone. They grow. When one grows past what its base will bear, it fails—not with a crack you can hear across the valley, but with a soft slump, a brief shiver in the soles of the feet, a new fan of rubble already being colonized by the black mat that knows what to do with fallen metal. The land adds and the weather subtracts at nearly the same rate. The profile you see is the truce.

There is charge in it. Between certain spines the fine hairs of the fronds lift, and the skin of the wrists prickles, and a stillness goes through the thicket that is not the stillness of fear so much as the stillness of a process changing stroke. The discharge, when it comes, is almost never lightning. It is a faint seam of light no brighter than a scratch on the eye, gone before it can be called a flash, leaving a smell of hot dust and a mat of fronds plated a little thicker than they were. The plateau spends current the way a works spends current. The product is more plateau.

Life is abundant in the way a region is abundant, not in the way a room is. Inside a thicket the small economy is crowded and quiet: things that tick through the mats, things that graze the metal-salt crust and the soft growth under it, things that take those. You know them by disturbances—a repeated nodding that stops, a lane opened in the filaments and closing again, a tarn-edge dimple where something large enough to matter has drunk and gone. On the open ground the same abundance is stretched until it resembles absence. Tracks appear, fresh, and then nothing for an hour, and then another track, the animal having kept a distance that would be courtesy if it were meant for you. The larger bodies are low, heavy, built for thin air and for not announcing themselves. Some have eyes wide enough for this light. Some seem to do without, and move instead along the electrical spines as if those were trails, or news.

They participate in the work without tending it. A herd—if a scatter of animals a valley apart can be called that—crosses a gravel reach and leaves, in its droppings and in the rubbed places where hide met sinter, a concentrate of what the fringe combed out of the wind. Other animals follow that concentrate. Over years the thickets lean toward the routes. Over longer years the routes shift, because the thickets themselves shift: growing toward the damp, dying on the side the damp has left, migrating across the plain at the speed of patience and leaving a sintered wake like the slag of a town that moved by growing. You will not see this happen. You can see that it has.

Sound is the one process the place declines to run in public. The air is too thin, the dust too fine, the mats too soft, the stone too long-weathered to ring. Weather arrives as a change of damp on the face. A tower’s failure arrives as a feeling in the feet. Predation arrives as a small silence inside the larger one, a motion removed from the mat. Even the animals’ traffic with one another seems to pass by way of the charge—flocks of prickling, answered prickling—so that a hillside can be fully occupied and still give you nothing to hear but your own breath, which is the loudest thing for a long way and feels, after a while, like a breach of the local law.

The law, so far as the land keeps one, is simple and old. Moisture is brief, so bank it. Light is scarce, so do not depend on it; run on gradients, on metal, on the warmth a reaction will spare. Do not build. Anything that looks made has been precipitated or grown, and if it looks finished it is only between failures. Keep your distance, because the open ground is the matrix and the richness is islanded, and the islands are already full of their own business. Add at the rate you subtract. The strange here is not a disturbance of the order. It is the order, weathered into balance, still turning—drizzle and clearing, plating and slump, crossing and feeding, rise and wear—under a dark that does not lift, in a quiet that is not peace so much as the sound of an engine too large and too slow to hear itself work.
