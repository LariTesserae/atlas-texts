The stone is newer than the weather that will someday soften it. High on a ridge that has only just learned it is a ridge, a slab of phonolite has split and not yet fallen in on itself. The break left a hooded recess: two cheeks of rock, a back wall, a floor you could cross in forty paces if anything would let you, and a lip of unweathered edge where the mountain simply stops. Beyond the lip is thin air and a dark that belongs to altitude. Inside, the dark belongs to the room.

The planes of the break are still planes. Arrises cut. A hand dragged along them would come away opened, and nothing here has had time to round that fact into gravel. The phonolite is the color of a closed eye — olive only when water finds it, and water finds it in films, not streams. Cold stone meets the warmth of too many bodies and sweats. Beads travel a palm’s width, hesitate in the grain, and are taken. In the lowest groove a bowl of water holds, lent and repaid, never deep enough to reflect anything but the idea of a surface. Now and then a cloud snags the spire and a fine rain comes in under the hood, taps the living mass, and is gone. Then the place returns to dampness: condensation, seepage, the wet sound of something unsticking from something else.

Almost nothing grows. In three cracks a black wire-moss persists, drinking what the films spare it. Near the bowl, one pale tuft stands in for grass, too starved of light to have committed to green. That is the vegetation. Everything else that seems to clothe the walls is not plant.

The dark is occupied to the point of contact. Flat animals tile the stone, overlapping like the scales of a single creature that never agreed to be one. They are ear-shaped, thin, and the color of unlit rock, so the walls appear to breathe without announcing why. Their margins drink the condensate as it forms. Where the film is thickest, the boundary fails: gel rises out of the damp, knots, and becomes another sheet, another flank, another mouth that is only a slit for moisture. The room makes its crowding out of its own sweat, slowly.

Under them the floor is a convection of heavier bodies, many-legged, near enough that a step is also a shove received and passed on. They do not stampede. The center of the mass tends, over hours, toward the bowl; the edges subside toward the walls; the whole herd turns the way weather turns. You could watch one animal for a long time and swear it had not decided anything. Only the gap it leaves, gradually, would show that decision is the wrong word.

Between floor and wall the air is not empty enough to be air. Small winged things hold a slow rotor in the nave of the cleft, wings near-touching, each beat a negotiation with a neighbor’s beat. Flight here is a kind of swimming. They do not dart. A startle, if it comes, arrives as a lowering of pitch that moves through the volume over minutes, a change in the weather of sound rather than a flash of escape. Eyes, where they exist, are for the flank an inch away. Nothing in the recess looks as far as the lip.

The loudness is the shape of the room. Phonolite rings, and the rock is young enough to ring cleanly. A few larger animals — not many, and never hurried — brace against the fresh arrises and draw a hardened flank along the edge. The tone comes back at once from the opposite cheek, then from the hood, then from the floor’s moving clutter, until the note is stacked on itself and has nowhere to thin out. Under it runs the tick of feet on glassy grain, the wet kiss of sheets lifting, a low continuous hum from the wall-tilers vibrating in their own damp. It is not a roar. It is a rookery sealed inside a bell, loud because the place is small and the stone has not yet learned to swallow what it is struck with. Outside the lip the wind has nothing to carry and sounds farther away than it is.

What they do with the edge is the only making. The arris is the tool; the body is the hand; the stroke grooms a neighbor, sheds a filament, polishes a line into the newest stone. Sometimes a shard, shattered off by the mountain’s own unfinished breaking, is crooked in a limb and worked once along a sheet, then dropped. No one keeps it. No one piles it. Come back after a season of lower-world weather and the gestures will have continued, and the recess will still look untouched: no hearth, no wall, no mark that could be called a site. The mountain is being used, and not built upon. In this way the young rock begins, very patiently, to become older — a bright line rubbed dull, an edge taught to knock less sharply — while the rest of the spire stays raw.

The rules are few and they hold.

Nothing leaves for long. Past the lip the air is a wall: too thin, too empty, too bright in the rare moments when cloud-glow from below finds the brink and dies an inch inside. The swarm is a climate with a boundary. Water does not travel. It beads, is drunk, is breathed back, beads again. Feeding is not a chase; it is contact, film, and the slow sharing of what the sheets slough. Light does not accumulate. A few animals carry a dim, infrequent gleam along a seam of the body, less a lamp than a syllable, and the dark spends it on the first surface it touches. You could not read by it. You could only learn that someone nearby had changed state.

Time here is the period between one full turn of the floor-herd and the next, and the longer period in which the bowl empties toward damp and fills toward water again. The sun’s path does not reach under the hood often enough to be a clock. Cycles are gradual: moisture thickening, the rotor of wings slowing a fraction, the bell-note dropping as the stroking animals tire and are replaced by others who have been waiting in the press, not eager, only next. Even the strangeness is patient. A new sheet condenses out of the film and is, within a day, indistinguishable from the old tiles. A leg that is not needed is drawn back into gel. The number of bodies is never quite the same and never feels different.

Stand at the scale of the recess and the greater height becomes a pressure in the chest, a coolness that never quite becomes cold, a sense that the floor is a shelf and the shelf is new. The mountain under you has not had time to grow a soil, a path, a history of feet. All the history is happening now, densely, in the dark, at the speed of circulation and drip. The loudness does not surge. It is already the condition, as damp is the condition, as the cut of the stone is the condition.

At the lip, if the rotor of wings parts for a moment — and it does, sometimes, the way a slow river shows its bed — there is a shock of almost-silence and almost-light, both thin, both immediately taken back. Then the cheeks of the cleft return the tone, the sheets settle their overlap, and the bowl answers with one small sound of water accepting a drop it will not keep. The place closes around that sound and goes on being itself: a chapel of new rock, unbuilt, crowded to the touch, ringing because it has not yet learned any quieter way to be.
