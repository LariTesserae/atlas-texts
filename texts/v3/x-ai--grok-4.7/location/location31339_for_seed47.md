The light is ordinary. That is the first fact, and for a long time the only one that behaves. It falls at a daytime angle, white and unremarkable, onto country that has forgotten how to interrupt it. There is no peak to take the sun early, no canyon to hoard the afternoon. Shade, when it exists, is a private event: the width of your own body, cool at once, as if the air had never agreed to store heat and was only waiting for an excuse to be cold again.

What you are standing on used to be mountains. The evidence is a flour of pale gravel no higher than the ankle, sorted into faint wind-rows that the wind no longer needs, because there is nothing left tall enough to make a lee that lasts. Elevation here is not a climb. It is the condition of the whole country. The air is already thin when you arrive, cool in the manner of stone rooms, and it stays that way across distances that would once have held weather. The horizon is not a line. It is a slight darkening where the air finally thickens enough to fail. Beyond that, the same ground continues, and continues.

There is no water doing the work of water. Seas are present only as a chemistry they left behind: cubic blooms of salt in the cracks, a bitter rime on the sinter, a thirst in the air that is not metaphorical. A closed bottle lightens. The skin of the wrist goes tight. Metals keep their faces for an age and then, when a fresh fracture turns them to the sky, flower all at once—rust, then a green that is not plant, a copper fire traveling the length of a plate the size of a county. You can walk a day and not see a sheen. What looks wet is only a new face of mineral, dark for an hour, then chalk again.

The country is flat because it is finished. Everything that could fall has fallen. Everything that could round has rounded. The oldest-stable-state of a world is this: a skin of vitrified crust over a bed of dust milled so fine it no longer remembers being stone. And the skin is not still.

Plates translate. Some are the breadth of cities, some of districts, some no wider than a road, and they move on that dust as if the dust had consented to be a fluid. The motion is continuous, and at the seams it is violent, and it does not make a sound you can use. A ridge two meters high—the tallest relief you may see before evening—migrates from your left to your right over an afternoon, not by growing but by being carried. At the lip where one plate shears past another, the crust spalls. A sheet of glitter rises and falls back. Impacts of real mass occur a few paces off, and the noise dies inside a body-length of its birth. Thin air will not carry theater. What reaches you is a tick in the teeth, a pressure behind the eyes like a change in altitude, and then a silence so complete you look down to confirm the grit on your boots.

This is the weather. Not rain—there is nothing to rain—but circulation. The grinding drives the dust-fluid into gyres the size of continents, slow enough that a single turn is a season, large enough to have a climate of their own. Where a gyre scours, the crust goes to glass and the walking is a pale ringing underfoot, felt more than heard. Where it slackens, drifts of the old flour gather, still only meters deep, because age has already removed everything that could pile higher. Before a veil, the light does not change color. The horizon simply loses its darkening and becomes a white wall. That is the warning, and it is generous. The veil can bury a standing figure to the knee and be gone by the next seam, scoured clean, the dust already enrolled in a different gyre. Creation and destruction here are housekeeping. The land is not becoming. It is rearranging a form it finished long ago.

Between seams the plates are empty in a way that takes practice to believe. Hours of ground with nothing on it that wants a name. The scale is not scenic. It is operational. A storm of grit can be born on one plate and die on another without ever approaching you, and you will watch the whole life of it as a pale column walking the middle distance, soundless, scheduled by physics you could write down and still not feel prepared for. Plans made in distance do not survive. The ground you crossed in the morning has gone elsewhere by dusk, wearing its dust into some other longitude, and a different ground has arrived under the same light to take the role of place.

Life is a side-effect with a narrow lease.

In the temporary lees—the downstream side of a seam, for as long as the flow holds—a crust forms. Gray-green, finger-thick in a good year, and years here are arguments. It takes ordinary light and the trace volatiles the grinding frees from sealed stone, and it spends both on persistence. A plate may carry three such stains, each the size of a room, and the next plate none. They do not colonize the open. The open is a sandblast that looks gentle from a distance and bares a boot to the weave in an afternoon. When a crust is carried into a seam it dies as powder. What passes for seed rides inside salt grains, and most grains never land where the pause lasts long enough to matter.

Animals are rarer than the crusts. You can cross a gyre and meet none. The one you do meet is low, the color of sinter, ribs visible because nothing here is fat, and it pauses whenever the vibration in the ground changes key. It eats a crust in strips, leaving a pale script, and it does not call. There would be no point. A shout, even yours, is a private event; past a few paces the air declines to be a messenger. If the animal has eyes they are small and lidded. You will not see two together. The energy of this country is the energy of moving rock plus the ordinary sun, and both are spent mostly on moving rock.

The exceptions are the places that do not move.

You miss them at first because they are the color of the work, and because distance eats them. A ring of dark metal flush with the plate, half-swallowed, still faintly warm. A broken spar repeating a pattern too fine to be a tool-mark and too regular to be a crystal. A rectangle of air, slightly thicker, standing where a doorway would be if there were a building. Every few dozen kilometers—kilometers being negotiable—something that is not geology holds a small argument with the shear.

The argument is stillness. Inside the radius, perhaps a hundred meters, dust settles and stays. A crust may become a mat, still sparse, never a grassland, but persistent enough to deserve the word soil if you are feeling generous. At the oldest of these holds, a few upright forms have managed a knee's height: jointed, brittle, more coral than shrub, growing a millimeter in a season that would elsewhere have been a career. Touch one and it rings, a thin true note, the loudest plant on the continent. They are the forest. There are not many forests.

The holds do something the local physics does not fully audit. They drink momentum out of the plate and spend it on duration, and they shed a little heat and a smaller wrongness. Noon-shadows at their edges fall a finger short, as if the light had taken a minor detour and thought better of mentioning it. Instruments agree outside the radius and disagree inside by amounts that are small, stable, and rude. Nothing theatrical. You can live with it. Surveyors do, if they mean to have a place to return to. Outside the holds, return is a story you tell about pins. Terrain is a tense that does not hold. Maps that try to remember ground instead of the relationships between holds are maps of a day, and the day has moved on.

Some holds are dead. Their plates tore free, and the ring is being milled down with everything else. Authorship is not a defense here. Given enough shear-cycles a machine becomes gravel, and the gravel becomes the fluid the next machine must stand in. The pattern of the living holds, if you could see it from a height this world no longer possesses, would be a grid gone to scatter: nodes still performing one clause of a sentence whose grammar has been ground away. Whatever larger courtesy they were built to extend—relief, perhaps, or weather with water in it—they no longer extend. They keep a circle of ground from finishing its last small motion. That is the whole remaining mercy, and it is posthumous.

A seam, up close, corrects any notion that the quiet is peace. The lip arrives as a bright line, sun on a fresh fracture. The line thickens into a trench the width of a road, walls traveling opposite ways at a walking pace or faster, depth not a useful concept. Coin-sized sparks. A draft narrow as a blade, strong enough to snap cloth once, and then the snap is over and the cloth hangs, because there is not enough air to continue the drama the eye is having. The lip does not clutch. It mills. A dropped tool is bright, then gone, then, on some other plate on some other day, a streak of oxide with no itinerary. People who study the slots where a seam yawns a little wider call them throats. Nothing lives in a throat long enough to be a resident. The air down there is the same air, only fuller of the powder of former heights.

At the holds, rarity concentrates, which is not the same as abundance. The low grazers know the radii. They time crossings the way a careful traveler times them: eat, rest in shade that is actually shade, leave before stillness becomes a habit visible from the dust. There is a predator that has learned the same lesson from the other side—a low ambush colored like the corrosion on the ring-metal, patient because the ground under it permits patience. It fails more often than not. The commoner finding is a shell, light as a basket, fused with salt at the edge of a dead radius, ringing if you tap it at a thinner pitch than the shrubs. Two living animals in one day is an event you will date other events by. Most days the ecology is the crust, the salt, and the fact of your own breath, which you cannot quite hear.

Once, far apart from the holds, there is a hall.

Half the roof has been shaved to the same terminal smoothness as the old ranges. The remaining slab is the dark material, and under it the drifted flour lies undisturbed across a floor that has not conveyed in a long time, or conveys so slowly the difference is academic. Step inside and the air changes office. It is being held—thicker, a pocket, a failed or partial gift of whatever system the holds belong to. You hear yourself. Breath, the click of a buckle, the small humane noise of cloth. Outdoors those sounds occur and are not delivered. In the hall they are, and the delivery is startling in proportion to how little there is to deliver. It is the loudest room in the country. Nothing else inhabits it. Marks on the inner wall, repeated angles, run to the place where the milling took the wall and stop mid-figure. Outside, on the plates, you sometimes find the rest of a figure, broken across a seam and shuffled, a sentence edited by the ground until it is only a habit of lines.

Night does not stop the plates. The sun goes down in the ordinary way, and the thin air, having stored nothing, gives up the day at once. Stars come large. Along the seams, now and then, a pale flash where a new face turns up to the sky: silent lightning at ground level, brief, useful to no one who intends to keep their boots. The gyres continue their slow offices in the dark. A veil may pass between you and a hold you were navigating by, and when it passes the hold-light—if the hold sheds any, and some shed a dull warmth you can mistake for a low star—will be in a slightly different relation to you, because you and it have both been translated, politely, in opposite senses.

By the third day the flatness has stopped meaning emptiness and started meaning completion. This is what a world looks like when erosion has won and been handed, afterward, a residual motion it did not ask for. The turbulence is not a storm arriving to break a calm. The calm was the end. The motion is what remains of an intervention that could not restore height, or seas, or sound worth traveling, and could only keep certain circles from joining the end on schedule. Walk long enough and the mind tries to make a road of it. The country will not take the offer. Roads are a cooperation between ground and time, and here the ground has other appointments.

Carry what you mean to drink. Sleep on a hold, or do not sleep. When the white wall stands up on the horizon, you are already late or you are early; there is no third condition. And if you set a tool down to look at a crust, look back sooner than habit suggests. The plate has no grievance against you. It also has no plan to be where you left it.
