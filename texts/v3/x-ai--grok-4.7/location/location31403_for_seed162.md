The first thing the height does is refuse the idea of a summit. There is no peak to stand on and no valley that has earned its name by wearing down. The land is new in the way a scar is new: lifted, not carved, a continental plate still remembering the shove that raised it. Edges are sharp enough to catch a glove. Scree has not yet learned to be scree; the slopes are slabs and blades, basalt fins standing like unsheathed tools, joints so clean they look machined. Warmth comes up through them. At this altitude the air should be thin and cold, and in places it is, a high clear emptiness you can taste — but the rock is a furnace with the door only cracked, and heat leaks in long seams, so that a hand laid on stone finds a living temperature, blood-warm, sometimes hotter, a dry radiance that makes the damp air above it shiver.

Moisture does not belong here and keeps arriving anyway. It beads on the undersides of leaves and on the cold faces of fins that have turned away from the vents. It gathers in pockets no larger than a bowl, sky-colored for an hour, then gone, drunk by porous stone or lifted into a haze that never quite becomes cloud and never quite becomes nothing. Rain, when it comes, is local and brief: a gray curtain walking a ridge for twenty minutes, wetting one flank of a blade and leaving the other pale and dry. The wet stone smells of metal and crushed green. Then the warmth takes the water back, and the only evidence is a darkening that fades while you watch.

The plants have not waited for soil. They root in cracks still hot from the uplift, in grit no thicker than a fingerprint, in the mineral crust that forms where vapor meets cold air. They are lush in the way a wound is lush — crowded, insistent, layered over surfaces that should still be bare. Mats of small leaves armor the gentler inclines. Taller growth rises in the sheltered lees of the fins, stems jointed, almost segmented, bark the color of wet iron. Nothing here has the look of an old forest. The canopy, where it exists, is young and greedy, leaves broad for the dimness, overlapping until the ground beneath is a green dusk even at what passes for noon. Condensation pearls along every midrib and falls, now and then, with a sound so small it barely qualifies.

Light is the thing the place withholds. The sky is not night and not storm; it is a thick, low amber, as if the air itself had been smoked. Whatever sun there is arrives already tired, strained through a high haze that may be dust from the uplift or vapor from the vents or something the elevation manufactures on its own. Shadows are soft and multiple. You can see a long way and still feel that you are seeing through cloth. Distances misbehave in that cloth. A ridge that was an hour’s walk is, after a turn of the haze, either beside you or gone, replaced by a fin that was not there, its edge still warm, its base already colonized by a mat of leaves that look weeks old.

That is the first honest crack in the place, and once you have noticed it you cannot stop noticing the others. The land is turbulent without being loud. Whole slopes rearrange between one glance and the next — not with the drama of collapse, but with the quiet industriousness of something being assembled. A fin lengthens. A joint opens. A sheet of stone extrudes from a seam the way slag extrudes from a lip, cooling as it comes, its surface already etched with a regularity no weathering could have made: parallel ridges, hexagonal pits, channels that catch the brief rains and lead them somewhere. Nothing built this. There are no walls, no roads, no marks of hands. And yet the plateau behaves like a works.

Follow one of the channels and the industry becomes harder to deny. In the warmer hollows the stone sweats a dark oil that is not oil, a thin mineral liquor that beads, runs, and in the running sorts itself — heavier fractions settling into black glass, lighter ones lifting as a sweet, metallic mist. The glass pools in shallow pans the land has made for the purpose, and in those pans, over hours, crystals nucleate in ranks, identical, as if drawn to a template. They grow until they touch, then stop, as if a quota had been met. A little later the pan flexes — you feel it through your boots more than you hear it, a low compliance in the rock — and the crystals are shrugged into a crevice, where something takes them. You do not see the something. You see the crevice empty, and a new pan forming upstream, already beading.

Elsewhere the work is electrical. The haze carries a charge you taste on your teeth. Between close fins, in the dim, pale threads of light walk the air without thunder, silent, branching, grounding themselves in the wet mats. Where they strike, the vegetation does not burn. It tightens. Leaves curl, then uncurl glossier, and along the stems a fine metallic frost appears, gone by the time you kneel to touch it, leaving only a smell like a struck bell. The frost, when it persists long enough to see, is not random: it traces the veins of the leaf in a second, brighter map, and then the leaf, sometimes, is no longer only a leaf. Its edge has acquired a tooth. Its stem has acquired a joint that was not grown but fitted. The plant continues, unbothered, photosynthesizing in the amber dusk, rooted in a crack, and also, in some register the eye keeps trying to refuse, a component.

Fauna fills every volume the vegetation leaves. They are abundant and almost entirely mute, as if sound were a resource the place had already allocated elsewhere. In the mats, something the size of a hand moves by rippling, many-legged, the color of the wet stone, so that motion is the only way to count them; stop looking and the slope is empty stone again, start looking and it is a slow traffic. In the jointed stems, clusters of small fliers cling and uncling without whirring. Their wings are filmed with the same condensate as the leaves. They rise in short, precise hops from one node to the next, and where they land they seem to test the node — a pause, a shift of weight — before moving on, as if inspection were their whole metabolism. Higher, in the haze itself, larger bodies turn without flapping, riding currents that do not match the wind you feel on your face. They are far enough, and the light poor enough, that their outlines will not settle: a wing, then a fin, then a hull-like curve, then a wing again. When one passes close the air smells briefly of hot resin and rain on metal.

None of them calls. The abundant life of the plateau conducts itself in a quiet so complete that your own breath becomes an intrusion. Footfalls on the mats are swallowed. Even the brief rains arrive without the hiss you expect; water touches leaf and stone and is simply there. The turbulence is visible and tactile and, in any ordinary sense, silent. A slope can founder and reform, crystals can be shrugged into crevices, a fin can lengthen by the length of a body, and the only sound is the small, occasional tick of condensate letting go of a midrib.

Scale is what makes the quiet frightening rather than peaceful. The plateau does not end. Walk toward what the haze suggests is an edge and the edge retreats, or rather the land continues to be produced ahead of you, young stone shouldering up through a skin of leaves that is already, impossibly, established on it. Weather forms in pockets that have nothing above them but more plateau: a warm column where a vent has opened, a cold lens where a new fin has thrown its shadow across a wet mat, a local wind that circulates for an hour inside a hollow and then ceases because the hollow has become a ridge. You can stand in still air and watch, a few hundred paces off, a silent rotation of mist and leaf-debris turning like a gear. It is weather, and it is also, plainly, part of the works — a convection cell maintained because something downstream requires the moisture delivered on that schedule.

The rules, once you have watched long enough, are not rules of a landscape. They are rules of a process that uses landscape as its medium. Sharpness is maintained because erosion is not allowed to finish; what would have been worn away is instead taken up, refined, and returned as some other sharpness elsewhere. Water is permitted only in the quantities the process can meter. Heat is leaked, not wasted. Charge is spent on the vegetation as if the vegetation were both crop and tooling. The animals — if animal is still the word — move through the assembly the way inspectors move through a floor, touching, testing, passing on, never in each other’s way, dense enough that their paths nearly touch and never quite collide.

Reality’s breakdown is not a spectacle pasted over this. It is the process exceeding the categories you brought with you. A fin is geology until its channels fill and sort a liquor into ranked crystals, and then it is a machine, and then a bird-thing lands on its crest and the crest adjusts, a fraction, to take the weight, and it is a perch, and then the haze thickens and the fin is farther away than the ground between you and it can account for, and it is a distance that has not been honestly measured. None of these readings cancels the others. They occupy the same stone. The leaves photosynthesize and also complete a circuit. The rain is weather and also a measured input. The quiet is an absence of animal noise and also the sound-profile of a works so vast, so continuous, and so unused to visitors that it has never needed to be loud.

Stand still long enough and the plateau will begin, gently, to include the place where you are standing in its next revision. The mat underfoot warms. A fine metallic bloom starts at the edge of a leaf near your boot and traces inward, precise, unhurried. Somewhere beneath the stone a pan is flexing, empty, ready. The amber light does not change. Nothing announces itself. The land, which has never been built and is never finished, simply continues — sharp, warm, damp in its pockets, green to the point of congestion, alive in every layer, and working, in a silence that has weather of its own, at a scale that does not require you to understand it in order to proceed.
