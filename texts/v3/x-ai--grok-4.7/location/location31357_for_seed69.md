The dark here is not weather and not a season. It does not thin. It is the country itself, as continuous as the stone, and it has been the country longer than the machines.

You learn it by the water first. At this height the air should be thin and the nights cold, and neither is true. The shelf is warm — a low, animal warmth, coming up through the soles — and the water sits just under the skin of the ground, so that every step finds a film. The rock weeps. Not in dramatic falls, though those exist, days apart, where a lip of the old limestone has kept its shape and the seep gathers into a rope of warm water and goes over. Mostly it is smaller than that: a steady sweating from joints in the stone, a shine you cannot see and can feel with a hand, channels no deeper than a wrist that have been running in the same beds so long the lips of them are rounded and quiet. The erosion finished its argument ages ago. What remains is the settlement. Water and stone have agreed on a shape, and they keep it.

The height is real. The shelf is a highland the size of a continent, lifted and then left, its sky a deck of condensate so thick and so permanent that no light comes through it and no wind from outside arrives unaltered. The deck is not far overhead. On the higher ridges you can put a pole up and touch the belly of it, and the pole comes back wet to the shoulder. Below the deck the air is close, mineral, sweet-sour with growth, and it makes its own weather in a closed loop: warm water rises off the weep and off the stations, climbs until it cannot climb, and falls again as rain that never saw a clearer sky. Rain here has no announcement. It starts as a change in the sound of the growth, a fattening of every drip, and then the air is full of it. When it stops, the stopping is just as plain. Nothing flashes. Nothing clears.

Because the rain belongs to the shelf, the shelf has climate the way a sea does. Cells of warmer vapor travel for days, following the long grain of the rock, and under them the weep runs harder and the animals shift their paths. You can stand in one watershed and hear the next storm already at work two valleys over — not thunder, but the raised voice of water on stone, and under that the different, organized note of whatever station lies in that valley, its heat feeding the cell. The country is loud in layers. Nearest is the tick and run of water on whatever is in front of you. Then the vegetation, which is never quiet. Then the beasts. Then, always, somewhere, an engine.

The growth is lush and it does not need the light it will never get. It feeds on the weep, on the warmth in the stone, on the mineral the stations have been breathing into the water for longer than the oldest pipe has kept its stamp. Near the ground it is a dense, giving mat, ankle to knee, fibrous, smelling of fermentation and wet metal. It holds water the way a sponge holds it, and it seals the small channels almost as fast as the weep opens them — part of the balance. Above the mat, soft columns, not quite trunks, rise and branch into straps that drink the condensate directly out of the air. They stand close enough that a body moves between them by turning sideways, and they are layered: a lower storey that brushes the face, a middle one that sheds a continuous rain of its own even when the sky-rain has stopped, an upper one that disappears into the deck and is known only by the weight of drops it sends down and by the creak it makes when a pressure-cell passes. Nothing about it is visible. You learn a stand by the give of it, by how long the echoes of your own movement take to die, by whether the smell is clean mineral or has the faint oil of a station somewhere up-drainage.

It is thick enough, and continuous enough, that the shelf has almost no open ground. What passes for a clearing is a seep-flat where the water lies finger-deep over rounded stone and the mat has not yet closed, and even those fill in. The old survey cuts — faint marks, a line of dressed stones, a cable swallowed to its core — are readable only where the growth is thinnest, on the noses of ridges where the weep is less generous. Most of them have become drainage. A rail put down in some earlier century of the work is now a long groove in the stone, flange and all weathered into the same soft profile as the natural beds, and water uses it because water uses whatever holds.

The animals are many, and they use the dark the way the plants use the weep: as the given condition, not as a lack. Herds of heavy, low animals work the seep-flats, heads down, mouths in the film, their feet a steady knocking on the rounded stone that carries a long way. They move in files along the old rails and the older channels, and the files can be heard for an hour before they arrive and an hour after. Above them, in the strap-forest, smaller bodies run the columns and call to each other in clicks and short whistles pitched to punch through the rain. The calls are constant. There is no dawn chorus because there is no dawn; there is only the chorus, thickening and thinning as the pressure-cells move and as the herds turn. Predators hunt by subtraction. A stretch of knocking that breaks, a corridor of clicks that goes thin, a new smell of warm iron in the wet air. They are not silent themselves. Nothing here is silent. The hunters announce, or the prey does, or the water does; the contest is about who parses the existing noise faster, not about who can hide inside quiet. Quiet does not occur.

What is strange is not a single marvel. It is the way the systems have borrowed each other until the borrowing is the form. The herds navigate by the engines. A station has a note — a particular stack of frequencies from its pumps and its conveyors — and that note is as reliable as a star would be on a world that had one, and steadier, because the stations do not set. The animals treat the gradients between notes as geography. So do the storms, in their way: a station's heat is a standing invitation to a pressure-cell, and the oldest stations have permanent local weather, a tighter rain, a warmer film on the stone, a growth that climbs the walls and seals the joints and has to be cut back, and is cut back, and climbs again. The cutting is audible. It is one of the sounds you learn not to fear, because it keeps a schedule.

The stations are few. That is the other fact that takes time to believe, because the industry is everywhere in the water and the air and the noise, and the buildings are not. Days of travel between them, across wet high country that would be called wilderness if the weep did not taste, faintly, of the work. A station, when you come to one, is a single structure, or a tight handful: a pumping hall, a long shed where something is reduced and loaded, a chimney that is only a chimney in the sense that heat leaves through it. There are no lamps. The electrification is in the motors and the buried grid, in the hum that organizes underfoot a valley before the building itself, in cable that surfaces and submerges like a root. If there are crews, they work by the same senses the herds use. More often the halls sound unmanned — full of process, empty of voices — and the doors stand open because nothing here needs a door against the dark, and the dark is not an intruder.

Around a station the balance is newer but it is still a balance. Slag and scale have gone soft at the edges and grown the same mat as the limestone. Channels cut for effluent have developed the rounded lips of the natural beds and now carry a mix no analysis could sort cleanly into waste and weep. The growth on the leeward side is taller, lusher, slightly wrong in its smell, and the animals prefer it and breed in it. Older stations, abandoned or cycling down, are already half-returned: a wall recognizable by its geometry under the hand, a turbine turning slowly on seep-water alone, no longer driving anything, its note dropped out of the navigation-song so that the herds have redrawn their files and left the building in a pocket of lesser traffic. The faint marks of the earlier periods — cairns, ceramic pipe, a flange stamped with a figure no one reads — sit in the stone at the same depth as fossils. The land does not distinguish the work from itself anymore. It has had time.

At the scale of the shelf, none of this is local. The same regime runs out to distances that make weather in their own right: further highland, the same deck, the same weep, stations at the same wide spacing, their notes faint and then gone and then replaced by others. Storms born over one pumping hall die over another three days' travel on, having rained the first hall's heat out of themselves and taken up the next. Herds the size of districts shift with the cells. The strap-forest is not a forest but a cover, unbroken except where the rock rises into those ridges that touch the deck, and even there the mat holds in the saddles. Sound travels under the deck strangely well. On still intervals between rains you can hear a station that is not yours, and a herd that is not your valley's, and the long low friction of water in a master-channel that drains a province, all of it stacked, none of it resolved into sight.

There is no hour at which it would be different. The processes do not wait. Rain assembles and falls; the weep answers by running harder in the beds it has already chosen; the growth takes the water and seals what it can; the animals parse the noise and move; the stations burn their fuel or their seep or whatever is piped to them and add their heat to the next cell. The dark does not lift to check on any of it. It does not need to. The country is legible without it, and has been, and the legibility is loud, warm, wet, and very large.
