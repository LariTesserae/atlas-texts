The light does not come from above. It comes from the walls, and the walls are still deciding what they are.

You are inside a cavity no larger than a small room, though “room” is the wrong word: nothing here was planned to hold a body, and a body, if it entered, would have to fold. The ceiling is a low, pale vault of rock that has not finished separating from itself. Lenses of milky mineral bulge out of the stone like unopened eyes, and every one of them burns with a steady white. Not fire. Not sun. A brightness the rock seems to be making as a side-effect of being born, cool as wet slate, and so even that shadows have nowhere to settle. They pool for a moment in a crease and are washed out again. The air is bright the way shallow water is bright—full, not glaring, a light you could read by if there were anything written.

There is moisture, but no weather. Condensation beads on every fresh face of stone and does not fall. It gathers, trembles, and stays. In the lowest seam a thread of water lies perfectly still, neither rising nor sinking, a pocket of damp held in a larger dryness of unworked mineral. The thread reflects the wall-light as a hard white line. Touch it and it is cold. It does not ripple. Nothing here ripples.

The geology is embryonic. Edges that in any other cave would have been worn smooth are still sharp, still deciding their grain. Sheets of stone peel in mid-separation and stop, translucent as onion skin, lit from behind. A crack has opened half a finger’s width and halted, its lips beaded, its interior glowing. You can see the next layer waiting under this one, paler, wetter, not yet committed. Erosion has not begun its long subtraction. What you are looking at is addition caught with its hand still on the work: raw faces, unrounded corners, a world under construction that has forgotten to continue. It will look exactly like this for as long as looking is possible.

Sparse does not mean empty. A few pale filaments of growth cling where the condensation is thickest—threads, not leaves, the color of bone left in water. They do not spread. They occupy their inches and stop, as if the rule of the place allows only so much green, and that allowance was spent at the first hour and never renewed. Between them the stone is bare, wet, and crowded with everything that is not plant.

The crowding is the fauna. They are small, and there is no space between them. The walls are shingled with bodies the size of a fingernail, flat, translucent, each one a little lamp of its own where the mineral light passes through. They do not crawl. They do not feed in any motion you can see. They simply occupy, overlapping, packed so tightly that the rock’s native color is a rumor under a living pavement. In the thread of water a denser congregation hangs, suspended, neither swimming nor sinking, their outlines shivering only with the light. Where one ends, another begins. There is no gap large enough for absence. Abundance here is not a population moving through a habitat. It is the habitat filled, once, completely, and then held.

Some of them have made marks that are almost structures. Not tools—there is nothing here that was carried in, nothing smelted or woven or cut by a hand that left. But along one seam a line of the flat bodies has fused into a low ridge, a lip of calcified life no higher than a knuckle, and in three places that ridge has been broken and mended with a different, milkier deposit, as if something tried twice to build a sill and left both attempts. Nearby, a scatter of empty husks, paper-thin, arranged with a regularity the stone itself does not have: a short arc, a gap, a shorter arc. You could call them remains. You could call them the first draft of a wall. They do not decay further. They do not get finished.

The sound is the hardest thing to hold still in the mind, because it does not vary and therefore begins, after a while, to seem like silence wearing a disguise. It is loud. A high, continuous grain, like grit poured forever onto a metal plate, or like a thousand tiny mouths drawing the same breath and never letting it out. It comes from the packed bodies and from the stone both, and there is no rhythm in it, no swell, no rest. It fills the cavity the way the light does: evenly, to the skin, with no corner spared. Speak, and your voice goes into it and does not return. The sound was already complete before you arrived. It does not acknowledge additions.

Nothing changes. That is the rule underneath the other rules, and it is absolute. The bead that is about to fall does not fall. The crack that is about to widen does not widen. The filament that looks as if it might put out one more inch has been about to do so for the whole age of the place. The fused ridge is mid-repair and will be mid-repair when whatever comes after you is gone. Even the strange things—the bodies that are also light, the walls that glow because they are unfinished, the husks laid in arcs no current could have drawn—do not develop their strangeness. They are strange at equilibrium. The weirdness is not an event. It is the steady state.

Stand as long as you can bear the fold of the ceiling and the roar that does not ebb, and the place will not reward you with a change of weather, a dimming, a single drop striking stone. It offers only itself, again, at the same brightness, the same coolness, the same packed and glittering occupation of every surface. A pocket below the world, still being made, and already finished with time.
