The height does not announce itself. There is no rim, no last ridge, no place where the climb becomes a view. The land simply continues, and the air is already thin in a way the body learns before the mind does: warm, yes, but warm the way a closed room is warm, not the way sun on stone is. Breathing takes a little more of the chest. Sound does not travel far.

What you are standing on is not ground in the old sense. Somewhere far beneath, stone still exists — a basement of rock worn so long that it has forgotten peaks, forgotten slope, forgotten the idea of shedding anything quickly. The plateau is what remains after mountains have been patient with weather for longer than weather usually is. It is nearly flat, but the flatness is a living decision. The surface rises and falls by the length of a body, by the swell of a root-bole, by the slow heave of tissue that has been adding itself for ages and compacting under its own rain. Walk far enough and the horizon does not drop. It hazes. The place is large enough to make its own days.

Water is everywhere and almost never a river. It beads on the undersides of leaves that function as ceilings. It films the clefts where one mass of growth has folded against another and never quite sealed. It weeps from cut edges when something heavy passes — not blood, not sap exactly, a clear thread that smells of wet bark and warm mineral and takes a long time to stop. After rain, and rain is frequent in a soft, unemphatic way, the plateau holds the water in chambers: bladders the size of rooms, felted mats that drink and then glisten, shallow lens-pools caught in the saddles between swellings. Nothing runs off. There is almost nowhere to run to. The grade is too old, too gentle, too occupied. Water moves by soaking, by condensing, by the slow internal weather of a body that covers a continent and does not know it is one thing.

The vegetation is the substrate. Soil, where it can still be called that, is a dark felt of dead fiber, insect casing, and leaf that never finished falling because it grew into the thing beneath it. Living tissue grades into it without a clean boundary. You can kneel and press a hand in and feel give, then resistance, then a cooler wetness, and if you are unwise and press farther you find the spring of hollow stem, the slick of an internal canal, the faint pulse of fluid being shifted from a wetter district toward a drier one. The plateau irrigates itself. In some places the architecture is obvious: columns of fused trunk, arches of root that left the dark and became buttress, mats of leaf layered until they are roofs. In others it is only surface — a continuous hide of low growth, ankle-deep, so dense that a foot does not find separate plants, only a nap, a direction of grain, a place where the green is blacker because it is thicker.

Light comes late and leaves early, and even at its best it is a stained, low light. The canopy is not always overhead. Sometimes you walk in open warm dimness under a sky the color of tin, and the dimness is the sky itself, an altitude haze, a permanent thin cloud the land breathes upward. Sometimes the canopy closes and the day becomes a green interior, lampless, with brightness only where a bladder of water acts as a lens and throws a trembling coin onto the mat below. Shadows do not sharpen. They pool. Warmth gathers in them anyway. The air does not chill when the light fails; it thickens.

There are animals, many of them, and they do not behave like animals in a forest you could name. Most are slow because speed is a kind of waste here. Grazers with low bodies and wide mouths move like weather fronts, heads down, taking the nap of the surface in long patient swaths that grow back before the season turns. Something larger lives in the canals: a presence you know by the way a pool’s skin tightens and then relaxes, by a wet sound like a lung deciding not to speak. Birds, if they are birds, do not flock so much as occupy stations. They sit in the same forks for so long that fiber grows around their feet, not trapping them — they shift, tear free with a soft rip, and settle one fork over — but recording them. Their calls are infrequent and close, a single note that does not echo. Echo would require stone, or distance, and both have been filled in.

The quiet is not emptiness. It is absorption. A footfall goes into the mat and stays there. Rain on the upper leaves is a hush, not a tick. Now and then a column releases a stored breath of warm air from some internal gallery, and the sound is a long, low pouring, like grain, lasting longer than you expect and then stopping without a conclusion. Insects exist in sheets and in veins. Their noise, when it rises, is a texture rather than a song, and it sinks again as if the plant has remembered them and taken the sound back into itself.

Strange, but not in the manner of a door opening onto another law. The strangeness is that the laws have had time to finish. A leaf may be the size of a table and still be only a scale on a larger leaf that has stopped being a leaf and become a province. A pool may have no bottom you can trust, because the bottom is a membrane over a chamber that is itself a lung for a district of the plateau. Colors are wrong in small ways: greens that hold a rust, reds that are not flowers but exposed internal fiber where the surface has split and healed shiny, pale flanges that glow only because they are wet and the dim light has nowhere else to go. You can walk a day and not see a flower. Reproduction happens in other currencies — by the laying down of another layer, by a runner that does not know it has left home, by a fruiting so internal it never meets the air.

Change is measurable only if you are willing to be old. A split in the hide knits. A lens-pool migrates, not by flowing, but by the upstream side dying back and the downstream side weaving a new lip, a hand’s width in a season. Grazing lanes darken and then green in bands so wide they look, from any local standing-place, like the natural color of the world. The plateau’s own weather is slower than the sky’s. Mist forms in the same hollows. Warm columns rise from the same galleries. Rain prefers the districts that already hold water, which is most of them. Storms happen, but they happen as a general leaning of the air, a day when every surface gives up a little more than it takes, and then the leaning passes and the canals are fuller and the quiet is deeper.

If you look for what was built, you have to learn a different eye. There are no machines. Nothing here was made to be used by a hand that expected to keep it. But there are marks. A line of pale flanges, too regular, running for a distance that would have been a road if roads could be persuaded to photosynthesize. A circle of columns whose spacing is almost architectural, each one a trunk that found an old post or an old bone or an old intention and grew around it until the intention became support. In one district the mat rises into low walls, not stacked, grown, with gaps at intervals a body could pass, and inside them the fiber is older, denser, polished by the passage of animals that have used the gaps longer than any memory. Farther, where the haze is the same in every direction, a single standing flange leans at an angle no growth would choose, and at its base the mat has refused, for a long time, to cover a patch of something harder — not metal, not tool, a fused glassy scar where heat once was, now warm only because everything is warm, beaded with the same weep as the rest. Whatever made it is not here. The plateau has kept the scar the way it keeps everything: by growing to the edge of it and stopping, patiently, as if the edge were a season that has not yet turned.

You can live in it, in the small sense. The mats hold. The water is drinkable if you take it from a moving canal and not from a bladder that has gone still. Fruit, when you find it, is buried, a soft weight under the nap, sweet and fibrous, meant for something with a lower mouth than yours. Shelter is a matter of recognizing which arches are still structural and which are the memory of structure, hollowed by the plateau’s habit of eating its own dead and replacing them from within. At night, which is only a deepening of the same dim, the warmth does not leave. Animals pass close because closeness is the ordinary condition. You hear them as pressure, as a change in the give of the mat, as a breath that is not yours and does not care to be.

The farther you go, the more the place resembles a single thought carried out at a scale that no longer needs witnesses. High, warm, wet, quiet, crowded with lives that do not hurry, built only in the way an old body is built — by repetition, by scar, by the refusal of certain small places to forget. The horizon never arrives. Under your feet the canals shift their slow freight of water from one dark province to another, and the stone, if it is still there, bears the weight without comment, already finished, already flat, already given over.
