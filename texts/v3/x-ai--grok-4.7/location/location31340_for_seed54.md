The ground is not finished. That is the first fact of the place, and it takes a long time to believe, because the city is already thick on it.

You come into the basin from the high lip, where the rock is still glass: black, green-black, the color of bottle-ends held to no light at all. It rings underfoot in a thin, wet way. Nothing has had time to dull it. The flows that made this rim are recent in the only sense that matters here — recent enough that the surface still remembers heat as a shape, not as warmth. The warmth is gone. What remains is cold that has settled into the mineral and does not leave, a cold you taste before you feel, metal and standing water.

Below the lip the land opens. Not a view. There is almost no light to have a view by. What opens is volume: a broad highland hollow, town-sized and then some, terraces stepping down toward a center you locate by sound rather than sight. The dark is not empty. It is occupied at every distance, a pressure of presences, and the air between them is wet enough to drink if you stand still. Moisture beads on the new rock, runs in threads, gathers where the glass has cracked and has not yet learned to be stone. In the lower shelves it stands. Black water, shallow, mineral, edged with rinds of precipitate the color of old iron. Marsh, if marsh can be a word for a place with no stems in it. The water is frequent and close to the surface everywhere, as if the basin is still weeping itself into being and has nowhere to send the tears but sideways.

There are no plants. Not a crust of moss, not a film that photosynthesizes, not the patient green that high wet places usually invent to spend their rain. Whatever lives here does not eat light. The dark is too complete for that bargain, and it has been complete long enough that nothing still tries. The condensate that should have fed leaves feeds nothing of that kind. It slicks the glass, fills the joints of the buildings, and passes through the animals.

The city is built into the unfinished ground as if the builders refused to wait for a finished world. Foundations grip flows that are still, elsewhere on the rim, only just solid. Walls rise in courses that are not all the same material and not all the same century of intention, though the centuries here move slowly and are hard to tell apart. Lower streets are cut as trenches in the glass and lined with something denser, a poured mineral that does not ring, that takes water into itself and gives it back as a continuous seep along the base of every wall. Upper work rides on struts and ribs that do not look quarried. They look decided: lattices too fine for masonry, spanning gaps the new rock has not yet closed, holding platforms out over water that did not exist when the first span was set and will not exist, in quite this shape, in another long age. The engineering does not announce itself with glare. There are no lamps worth the name. What light there is comes in rare, mean glints off wet edges, and from the animals.

They are the loudness, and they are almost the whole of the life.

At first you take the sound for water, because water is everywhere and the basin is a bowl. Then the water separates out — a low, patient ticking of drops off eaves of rock, a thicker pour where a terrace lip spills into the shelf below, the soft clap of standing pools when something heavy shifts their edge — and what remains is animal. Not one voice. A weather of voices. Close, the click and wet chop of bodies working the seep-lines along the walls, mouths or mouth-like structures rasping precipitate, the mineral rinds that are this place's only crop. Farther, across the open dark of the basin, a vast low drumming, thousands of feet or bellies or wing-cases striking shallow water in a cycle so slow you can walk a street and still be inside the same beat. Higher, in the lattices, a thinner sound: the skirr of things that are not birds and not insects in any clean sense, packing the spans until the spans buzz, lifting and settling in sheets that you feel as changes in the air against your face.

They do not make way. Density here is not a style of building only; it is the animals using every volume the building and the raw rock have left them. The trenches are floored with them, a moving silt of small cold bodies, legged or not, hard to count because they overlap and because counting wants light. They climb the seep-walls in dark mats. They hang in the fine struts in clumps that bow the ribs and do not break them — the ribs were made to be bowed, or have learned to bear it. In the standing water of the lower marsh-shelves, larger shapes move with a patience that matches the stone: backs like wet armor, surfacing on a rhythm you could time a watch by if you had the years. They displace so much water that the shelves fill and fall by a hand's breadth on their passage, and the city drains have been cut to expect exactly that. You understand, after a while, that the infrastructure is in conversation with the herds. Channels sized to a tide that is not lunar. Platforms set at a height that assumes the air will be full. Walls given a texture the climbers can hold, and a hardness their rasping will not finish in any span of time a single life could resent.

Nothing here hurries. The rock is still being born, but birth at this elevation, in this cold, is a long labor. A new lip of glass noses out of a fissure at the basin's eastern wall and will take an age you cannot sit through to cool into something a street might trust. The city has left it a margin. A gap in the terraces, a deliberate unfinishedness, as if the builders' art included knowing where not to close the plan. Water films that lip already, steaming only in the sense that it vanishes into colder air and returns as beads; there is no heat left in the extrusion worth the word steam. The animals have not claimed it yet. They will. Their cycles are the other slow thing: not migration, not season — there is no season you can see, no sun to hinge one on — but a gradual fattening and thinning of the swarms as the precipitate grows and is eaten and grows again. The mineral crusts are the crop. The water brings the mineral. The unfinished geology keeps offering new surface for the crusts to take. The city keeps offering wall.

You walk, because the streets allow it, barely. They were not cleared for visitors. They are corridors the animals have agreed, by long use, to leave as seams: a shoulder's width here, a cart's width farther in where something heavier than a person is meant to pass and sometimes does. You do not see the heavy things. You hear them, a distance off, a grinding patience, and you feel the small bodies redistribute around the space those sounds imply. The tech that moves the heavy things is not visible as machines in the ordinary sense. In places the street itself flexes, a slow peristalsis in the poured lining, carrying silt and shed armor and the husks of the dead — there are dead; the density requires it — toward shafts that breathe a colder air than the streets. The shafts do not smell of rot for long. Something in the lining takes the organic wreckage apart faster than the cold should allow, down to a grit that the seep can use. You do not watch this closely. It is not hidden. It is simply not performed for eyes.

What is unusual is not any single invention. It is the fit. A city of this thickness, this deliberation, standing in a dark that has abolished plants, on ground that has not finished cooling into permanence, crowded past comfort by animals that treat masonry as habitat and habitat as mouth. The weirdness never tips into miracle. No one speaks the stone into obedience. No light answers when you call. The laws you can feel still hold: water finds the low place, cold keeps flesh slow, glass breaks with a bright small noise if you strike it, and the strike is swallowed at once by the larger noise. What exceeds the laws you know is mostly patience and mostly scale of artifice — spans that should sag, and do not; drains that know a tide made of bodies; a waste-cycle running clean in a temperature that should have frozen the chemistry still. Someone, or something continuous with someone, understood matter well enough to ask it for behaviors a quarry and a furnace do not provide. They asked quietly. The city does not shine with the asking.

Sound fills what light does not. Near the central shelves it becomes difficult to think in sentences. The drumming of the wading shapes, the rasp of the wall-mat, the skirr overhead, the pour of every lip, the occasional deep flex of a street clearing its throat — these do not blend into one roar so much as occupy every frequency you have, so that silence is not a gap you can find. You stop under a rib and put a hand to it. It thrums. Not mechanically, or not only. The things packed along its length are part of the note. Through the wet on your palm you feel how cold the rib is, and how the cold does not distress whatever grips it. Their blood, if blood is the word, has made other arrangements.

Out across the basin, if you wait, the dark differentiates. Not into sight. Into arrangement. The terraces declare themselves as bands of different sound: drier clicking where the glass is new and the crust is thin, fuller rasping where the walls have been up long enough to farm, a heavy slosh where the herds turn. The far rim is a faint high ringing, wind you cannot feel at this level moving over unbroken glass, a bright edge to all the lower noise. The scale of the place arrives that way — not as a panorama, as a map drawn in loudness, regional, multi-featured, larger than the street you occupy and smaller than a country. You could cross it in a day of walking if the animals let you walk, and they will, along the seams, at their pace, which is the pace of everything here.

Above, there is no weather you can see arriving. The wet is already the weather. It condenses, it films, it falls in drops too fine to be rain and too constant to be anything else. Now and then a longer cycle asserts itself: the air thickens, the films on the glass grow heavy, the standing shelves rise a finger, and the herds shift their drumming upslope by one terrace, unhurried, as if they have kept this appointment for as long as the appointment has existed. Then, across a duration that makes a human wait feel like a spasm, it eases. The shelves sink. The rasping resumes on the lower walls. Nothing has been decided quickly. Nothing will be.

You understand, standing there with your hand on the thrumming rib, why the foundations were sunk into rock that is still coming into being. The city is not perched on a finished height, enduring it. It is a layer of the same slow emergence — organized, dense, loud with borrowed life, wet to its joints, dark in a way that has become a condition rather than an hour. The animals are not infestation. They are the population the mineral can carry, and the city has been built as the vessel that carries more of them than the raw glass could. In the seams, you are the thinness. Everything else is near enough to touch, and most of it is touching something else, and the water moves between all of it without haste, cold, and always almost at the surface, as if the world has not yet chosen how deep it intends to be.
