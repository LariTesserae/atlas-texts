The place sits high, and the height is the last thing you trust.

What reads, from far off, as a pale ridge is a lip of masonry, worn down until it is almost a horizon. Beyond it the land does not fall away into valley or plain. It holds. A regional sheet of water lies on the built world the way a lid lies on a box: cold, dim, and heavier than the stone that contains it. There is no shore in the ordinary sense. The water begins where the last course of blocks stops pretending to be dry, and from there it runs, chamber to chamber, for as far as the light will travel.

Nothing underfoot is substrate. The bed is paving, flag after flag, fitted so long ago that the joints have closed into hairlines and then into nothing. Terraces step down by a hand’s breadth, then by less, the original measure eaten until the descent is a suggestion. You can walk it where the water is only ankle-deep, and the stone gives back a slick, mineral cold, no grit of soil, no root-crack, no moss. Where the paving has failed it has failed into more paving: rubble re-settled, edges rounded, a second floor the water made by worrying the first. Erosion here is not drama. It is completion. The place has arrived at the shape it was always going to keep.

There are no plants. Not a film of green on the lee of a wall, not a holdfast in a joint. Whatever once grew has been gone longer than the carving. The mineral world is the whole world: limestone gone the color of old bone, basalt sills set as curbs, a pale gravel of shell and tooth that is not decoration but the slow product of the living. In the dimness the stone reads as one substance, until a current turns and a course of darker blocks shows through, a street that was a street, still aligned, still leading somewhere the water has not bothered to rename.

The water is the weather. It does not sit. Even where it looks still, a skin of colder water is sliding under a skin of slightly less cold, and the boundary between them shivers like oiled silk. Farther in, where the old halls drop their roofs and become open basins, the motion turns frank. A surge arrives without wind—there is no air worth speaking of above the deeper reaches, only a low mist that never quite becomes rain and never quite stops being water—and the surge shoulders through a colonnade, piles against a transverse wall, and spills in a sheet no higher than a knee. It withdraws. It comes again from another quarter. Chambers fill until their sills weep and empty until the paving shows, black and immediate, then vanish. Nothing announces these turns. The turbulence is structural: the built plan was never meant to hold a single level, and now every unequal floor is a throat. Water argues with water in silence, and the stone takes the argument without comment.

Sound is what the argument leaves out. A slap where a sheet falls onto a lower court. A long, low knock when two flows meet in a corridor and cancel. Otherwise quiet, the thick quiet of cold water over worked stone, pressure in the ears if you go under, and above the surface only the small noise of your own movement. No birds. No insect tick. The quiet is not peace. It is the register the place has settled into, while everything visible keeps rearranging.

Light comes from above and does not go far. A gray noon, or what passes for noon, lies on the surface in a dull plate. Under it the columns shorten. Twenty paces in, a wall is a softer wall. Forty, and you are navigating by the change in your footing and by the pale flick of bodies. The dimness is even. No shaft, no lamp, no fired thing. Whatever intelligence stacked these courses did it without devices that survived, or without devices at all—block on block, gravity, a knowledge of water that the water has since outlived.

The life is the abundance, and it does not need the green world. Fish the length of a hand move in loose schools through the colonnades, pale, almost translucent at the fin, gathering where two currents shear and the small suspended grit thickens. They are many, and they are never a crowd: the basins are wide, the schools thin, a brightness that crosses a court and is gone into the next dark. Crustaceans pick the joints, not hiding so much as occupying a scale you only notice when you stop. In the deepest courts, where the roof-courses have collapsed into a rough circle and the water stands over your head, larger shapes turn once and fade—slow, cold-blooded, uninterested. They crop the gravel of shell. They crop each other. The ecology is a closed account written in mineral and flesh, and it balances because nothing here is trying to be elsewhere.

Now and then a wall gives. Not with a crack you hear. A course that has been a course for an age of water simply ceases to be load-bearing, and a slab settles, and a new throat opens between two courts that had been separate since the builders left. The surge finds it at once. Fish find it by the next turn of the current. On the far side a basin that had been easing toward stillness begins, patiently, to empty into the new cut, and somewhere a lower hall starts to fill, lifting a skin of gravel, laying it down again in a bar that will, given enough seasons, become another sill. Creation and destruction here are the same gesture, performed too slowly to cheer and too steadily to call weather. The plan changes. The place does not.

If you keep to the high lip, where the masonry is only washed and not drowned, you can see the regional extent of it: court after court, a low geometry repeating until haze and the curve of the water eat the repetition. Sparse. Most of what you see is empty surface, empty paving, empty dim. The presences—school, shelf of crabs, the long turn of something larger—are incidents on a field of stone and cold. Nothing towers. The elevation is in the whole plate, lifted and held, not in peaks. The strangeness is quiet too: that a made thing should be this old and still be the only ground; that life should be this many and this unattached to leaf or sun; that a storm can run through a city forever and never raise its voice.

Stand long enough in one court and the level at your calves becomes a level at your knees, and you understand you are not watching the landscape. You are in a sentence it has not finished, and will not finish, and has been saying, without tools, without green, without noise, since the stone agreed to stay.
