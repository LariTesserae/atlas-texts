The terrace does not announce itself. You come up through a last slope of powdered mineral — pale, sharp, older than any weather still has a name for — and the ground simply changes allegiance. Stone ends. What takes its place is not soil. It is a continuous body, warm through the sole, slightly yielding, already mended where your weight pressed it. By the time you notice you have left the mineral behind, the mineral is a rim at your back, a bright dead lip no wider than a road, and ahead of you the living mass runs without a gap to the far haze.

There is no water in it. Not in the sense of streams, dew, a darkening after heat. The chemistry here resigned from water so long ago that thirst, as a category, has no local object. What moves in the tissues is resin, pitch, a thin oil that smells of hot metal and crushed leaf and something faintly electrical, like a coil left running. It beads only when a duct is torn. It does not wet. It skins over in a minute and the skin is already bark. The rim behind you is salt and silica, a beach that never met a sea — the last unpaid dust of a high plateau ground down to its final grade. The terrace sits on that dust the way a foundry sits on its own clinker: not placed, accumulated, and then, at some point nobody remains to remember, no longer distinguishable from the decision to remain.

The height is not a peak. The air is thin enough that heat does not lift away cleanly, thick enough that you can still breathe if you do not hurry. Mid-elevation, a shelf the size of a valley system, tilted so slightly that the tilt only shows itself in the way pressure prefers one direction. Features repeat at the scale of districts rather than horizons. There is a darker quarter where the fiber has braided itself into long parallel ridges, like looms abandoned in the act of weaving, each ridge a bundle of trunks fused side to side until the word trunk is a courtesy. There is a lower sink, still high above the salt rim, where the mass runs almost smooth, a hide stretched over old machinery, and the heat there is worse because nothing stands up to break it. There is a knot near what passes for center — you learn it by temperature, not by sight — where the engines were set, or grew, or were grown around, and have not stopped.

Light fails early and then fails again. Whatever sun this elevation once owned is stoppered by the overgrowth. The canopy is not a canopy. It is the upper face of the same body you are walking in, folded back over itself until day is a rumor conducted poorly through translucent younger sheaths. Most of the interior lives at the edge of visibility: a red that is not quite light, a black that is not quite absence, the occasional pale seam where a resin duct runs close to a surface and glows with its own slow chemistry. You navigate by heat-gradient and by the give of the floor. Eyes adjust to a world the color of the inside of a kiln after the door has been shut.

It is hot in the way of rooms that have been working. Not a sun-hot. A process-hot. Friction of fiber on fiber, the dull combustion of oils the tissues crack for their own maintenance, and under that the industrial remainder: housings, boilers, geared hearts, bus bars swallowed and re-lined by wood. Nothing here is unmade. The plants are the construction, and the construction is the plants. A wall is a rank of stems trained, centuries of centuries ago, into a load-bearing weave, then left, then overgrown by their own training until the original stakes — iron, ceramic, a glass that has gone cloudy — exist only as bones the living mass refused to spit out. Floors are compression wood, polished by endless passage of nothing with feet. Ceilings are roots that decided, in the long dark, to become joists. You can find a bolt head flush with a branch and not be able to say which one closed over the other. The tech is not advanced and not primitive. It is industrial in the old sense: engines, scaled production, power moved as heat and pressure and rotation. The product is the factory. The factory eats its offcuts and calls that growth.

No animals. Not a track, not a husk, not the particular silence that means something with a nervous system has just gone still. The ecology is a pure rivalry of tissues. One growth habit sheets across another and is sheeted in turn. A slower lignin outlasts a faster pith and inherits its ducts. Mutualisms exist, but they are botanical: a fibrous mat that wicks oil toward a swollen node, a node that vents heat so the mat does not cook. Where a foreign chemistry intrudes — a salt seep from the rim, a pocket of trapped gas — the mass seals it the way bark seals a nail, and the seal becomes a landmark until the next rearrangement digests the landmark too. You will not be eaten in any sudden way. You will be included, if you stay, on the same schedule as a forgotten brace.

The strangeness is not theatrical. It is procedural. Paths do not persist. A corridor you used to cross a gallery of ridge-wood may, on the return, be a suture: two flanks grown shut, the seam still warm, the oil barely skinned. The terrace practices a standing turbulence, old enough to be a climate. Creation and deletion run at the same average rate, so from the salt rim the whole shelf looks settled — a final landform, flattened by an erosion that no longer needs weather because the tissues abrade each other. Up close the settlement is a lie told at the wrong timescale. Ribs split. Ducts reroute with soft, hydraulic sighs. A machine-heart that has pumped pitch for an age seizes, cooks its housing, and is jacketed by emergency wood before the seizure can propagate; somewhere else a jacketed engine cracks its jacket and starts again, because the pattern of starting again is older than the particular engine. The oldest stable state of this place is motion. Flattened, packed, final — and unable to hold still.

Sound dies in the packing. Density is the acoustic law. A collapse of ridge-wood a district away arrives as a change in the floor's temperature and a pressure in the ears, not as a noise you could point to. Near the central knot you hear the engines as a vibration in the teeth, a low cycling that might be rotation and might be the slow peristalsis of a duct the size of a street. Resin popping as it skins. The tick of cooling metal where a housing has briefly outrun its sheath. Footsteps, if you make them, come back wrong — swallowed, then returned from an unexpected angle, as if the terrace were listening with surfaces that are not ears. Mostly it is quiet the way a full warehouse is quiet: not empty, occupied by a process that does not need to announce itself.

Rules, such as they are, are physical rather than decreed.

Nothing flows that you can drink. Oils will blister and then seal the blister with your own burned skin incorporated into the seal. Do not break a glowing seam to see inside; the glow is heat leaving, and the inside is doing work.

Do not trust a passage to be there when you need it. Move along gradients — cooler toward the rim, brighter-red toward thinner sheaths, heavier vibration toward the knot — and accept that the map is a description of a moment.

Metal is not safe ground and wood is not safe ground. They are the same ground. A rung may be sound and still be in the act of being occluded. Test with weight, not with sight.

The heat is the life and the life is the heat. Where a hollow has gone cold, the mass is dying back or sealing a mistake; those places can be crossed, briefly, and they smell of dry sugar and old iron. Where the heat spikes without warning, a seizure is cooking through. Leave.

There is no night in the ordinary sense and no day. Rest when your own heat starts to match the floor's. The terrace does not sleep, but it has phases, long and uneven, in which a district locks into a slower weave. Those are the only shelters, and they end when the weave decides to revise itself.

Out at the rim, if you work back to it, the salt-field is a relief so complete it feels like a violence of another kind: open air, a sky that is only dark rather than stopped, a wind with nothing in it. The mineral is ancient in the simple way — ground down, finished, incapable of another form. The terrace leans against that finish and refuses it. From the rim the living shelf looks low, almost modest, a regional smudge of black-red on a pale high plain, emitting a haze that is not steam. Stand long enough and you can watch a ridge subside and another shoulder up, silent, at the pace of a thought you are not part of. The plain will outlast the watching. The terrace, which has already outlasted the plain's last weather, will not outlast itself. It is not trying to. It is the end-state that keeps ending, packed edge to edge, built out of the only material left that still knows how to change.
