The water is older than the ground it lies on, and higher than the weather beneath it.

From the low country you see only a lid: a warm cap of cloud sitting on a lifted bowl, its edge clean as a cut, its underside the color of steeped leaf. The bowl itself is new. The stone still carries the heat of whatever shoved it up—joints bright, faces unrounded, breaks sharp enough to open a hand. Nothing has had time to become a valley. There are no rivers. Rain arrives and stays. Seepage beads from the fractures and does not run; it stands, thickens, and deepens, until the middle of the bowl is a mere you cannot touch bottom in, and the ridges are only just learning they are shores.

The surface sits above the cloud line. To be in the town is to be submerged and elevated at once. Light comes through two filters and fails both of them: the cloud-lid, and then the water, which is not clear. It holds a fine mineral meal shed by rock that has not yet learned to be soil. Day is the color of tea held up to a small window. You can see a mooring-post, a neighbor’s ladder, the pale flank of the next platform. Beyond that the lanes thicken into brown-green and end. At noon the deepest quarter of the mere is only a darker thought under the boats. Night is nearly total, except where a hearth shows through a reed wall, or where wet stone gives back a dull sheen, as if the rock remembers a brighter place.

It is warm in a way that does not fluctuate. The mere is bath-temperature to the wrist and stays so after dark; the young stone keeps what it was given. Steam loosens off the rim where the cap thins, and the outer paths are always damp without ever being cold. People dress lightly and sleep on mats that never quite dry. Mold would own a place like this if the water were ordinary. It is not quite. It deposits more than it rots. Leave a post in it and a skin forms—pale, flinty, a little green—thickening by seasons, not by days. The same skin crawls the undersides of platforms and the lips of new ridges. Erosion, here, has not begun its work. Making has not finished.

That is the rule the town lives by, though no one would put it so. Water does not cut. Water crusts. Stone arrives already jointed, as if it had been stacked in the dark and only now pushed into air. A ridge can breach at dawn where last season there was only a slow bulge in the brown, and by the time the crust has taken, someone has claimed the spine of it with a line of stakes. The oldest arguments in the place are about mornings: who saw the stone first, whose ladder touched it while it was still weeping.

The settlement is a town in the strict sense—districts, a landing, a long house on the highest dry ridge, lanes with names—but nothing in it is older than the great-grandparents, and much of it is younger than the people using it. They build with what the bowl offers before it weathers: uncut blocks levered along shore and rafted, green poles that root if you wait, fiber twisted wet, mats of a broad leaf that grows with its feet in the mere and its head in the dim air. Metal is rare and accidental, nodules knocked out of a fracture and hammered cold into hooks. There are no wheels. The lanes are gaps between platforms, knee-deep or chest-deep depending on which generation laid them. You pole, or you swim the short distances, or you walk the ladders where a ridge has been terraced into steps still sharp underfoot.

It is crowded. Platforms nearly touch. Gardens hang over gardens. A house will have another house’s drying-rack in its window-light, and below both of them a submerged shelf of plants grown for their stems. The density is vertical as much as lateral: root-mat, walkway, sleeping-loft, and above that the canopy, which has closed over most of the shallows so that even the surface-day is a dusk. You move through layers of other people’s work. Voices carry farther than they should in water-air this still, so the habit is to speak low. A shout is an event. Children learn the difference between a call and a noise before they learn the far shore’s name.

And it is quiet, for all the nearness. No falls. No wind with anywhere to go; the cloud-cap sits on the rim and the air inside is almost ponded. Stone on stone, now and then, when a block is walked up a new ridge—slow, patient impacts, then the suck of warm water closing over the work. A pole set down. The small talk of crust forming, which is not a sound so much as a tick you stop noticing. Far off, sometimes, the knock of a ridge settling as it rises: the land still coming up, a hand’s breadth in a lifetime, enough that a door cut square in one generation kisses its sill wrong in the next. They do not fight this. They rebuild upward. A lower lane that was a path becomes a canal without ceremony. The town migrates by patience, the way the plants do.

The plants are the true populace. Soil has not accumulated; what you walk on, where you walk on anything dry, is rock-meal and leaf-litter a few fingers deep, and under that the unweathered face. The growth does not seem to mind. A reed-tree stands with its base crusted into the mere, drawing what it needs from stone-milk and warm seep. Broad pads tile the sheltered water until a lane has to be kept open by hand, or it closes. On the new ridges, before anyone builds, a green rind takes—the lichen that is also a mineral, or the mineral that has learned the trick of a leaf. It is the strangest ordinary thing in the bowl. Break it and it smells like wet flint and crushed stem together. Leave it and it thickens into a turf you can crop, and under the turf the stone is already softer, not from wear but from being fed on. The town’s gardens are slash-and-wait: cut the rind, eat what comes, and trust the ridge to brew another. They do not plow. There is nothing yet to turn.

Animals have barely found the place, or have not wanted it. A few pale fish drift in the mid-water, slow as the cycles that govern everything else, rarely caught, more often seen hanging beside a piling as if listening. On the outer rim, where steam meets cooler air, there are snails no bigger than a thumb, and their shells are the same stuff as the crust on the posts—built, not grown in the usual way, so that a dead snail looks like a failed attempt at masonry. Birds do not cross the lid often. Insects are thin. At night you do not hear the usual chorus of a warm wet country. You hear water adjusting its level by a hair, and a neighbor turning over, and the long house’s fire, which is the loudest thing most evenings: a small, dry argument against a world that wants to be damp and mineral and green.

The far shore is a cliff still being born. It shows tool-marks of no one’s making—planes, edges, a joint a mason would trust—and at its foot the mere goes dark. People do not cross the deep for errands. The town occupies the near shelves and the island-ridges, a region you can pole around in a long day if you know the open lanes: the landing, the rind-gardens, the stilt quarter where the bottom fell away and the houses followed it down on longer legs, the upper walk along the driest spine, the kiln-shelf on the rim where they can get a fire hot enough, briefly, to set clay before the mist takes it back. Beyond that is only emergence. New stone. Unnamed water. A bottom no one has seen, not because it is forbidden, but because light gives out and the meal in the water turns every descent into the same brown room.

Change, when it comes, comes as a correction you notice after the fact. A platform sits higher relative to its neighbor because the ridge under one of them shrugged. A lane everyone used in childhood is a swimming-cut now, and the children use a different one, and no one has written the change down because there is little writing and less need. The cloud-cap does not lift. The warmth does not break. Somewhere under the meal, in the dark of the unfinished middle, the bowl is still rising into its own water, and the water is still deciding to stay, and the rind is still learning, very slowly, how to be a ground.
