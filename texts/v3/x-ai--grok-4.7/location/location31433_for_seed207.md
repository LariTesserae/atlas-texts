The first fact is the closeness of the cold. It is not a wind and not a depth you fall through; it is a room-temperature of the stone itself, settled so long that the air has taken the same degree and stopped trading with it. Nothing circulates. A breath, if one were offered, would whiten and hang, then thin into the same chill, leaving no path behind it. The dark is of the same order: not a shadow cast by something nearer a lamp, but the ordinary condition of a volume that has no opening onto day. Against it, the eye invents grains and then gives them up. What remains knowable is pressure, temperature, and the small give of surfaces.

The pocket is not much larger than the span of two arms and the height of a low stoop. Its ceiling is the underside of a greater mass — you feel the weight as a steadiness rather than a threat — and the walls were once cut, or grown to a cut, on a gentle curve. That intention has been rounded off. Edges that might have been sharp are lips now, mineral-soft, the way a step in an old cistern loses its corner and becomes a place water used to hesitate. Water still hesitates here, but it does not gather. There is no pool, no thread of a stream, no drip timed to a crack. Moisture lives as film: a cold skin on the stone, a colder skin on the leaves, beads that fatten over hours and then flatten again into the general damp, transferred rather than fallen. Touch a wall and the hand comes away with a slickness that is not quite wet enough to run down the wrist. The air smells of crushed stem and clean mineral, faintly sweet, without the rank of anything that has died in company.

What fills the volume is growth, packed until the original room is mostly a suggestion. Leaves overlap leaves until layering is the only architecture. They are broad for the space, thin, cold as cloth left in a cellar, and entire — no chewed margins, no bore-holes, no web across a gap. Nothing animal has ever edited them. They arise from a felted floor that is not quite soil: compressed generations of the same tissue, taken back into itself, damp all through, yielding a finger's depth and then firm. Stems stand so near one another that a passage would have to be made by pushing, and nothing has pushed. From the ceiling, narrower blades hang and almost meet the upright leaves; in the narrows between, a third growth, paler and closer to the stone, sheets the seams in a nap like frost that is not frost. The pocket is lush in the way a closed hand is full. Color is a rumor. If the leaves are green, or blood-dark, or the white of things that never saw sun, the dark keeps the answer. They are known by rib, by edge, by the slight tack of condensate, by a smell that strengthens when a leaf is pinched and then fades back into the general green-mineral quiet.

Under and inside the stone, something older than the present balance is still at work, and it has stopped looking like a device. Along certain seams the cold is fractionally less — not warmth, only a difference a careful palm could map — and the thickest roots follow those seams with the patience of ice finding a joint. The rock there has a grain too regular to have been laid down only by settling: a lattice, finer than the eye would use even if light were given, mineralized, crusted, broken into stretches and resumed. It does not hum. It does not glow in any sense that would help a visitor. It keeps a gradient, and the plants drink the gradient, and the gradient is maintained by a physics that has been ordinary here long enough to weather. The engineering has entered the erosion cycle. What was installed has become a bedding the way a shell becomes limestone, still doing the small work it was shaped for, no longer separable from the hill that holds it.

A few marks of making remain as marks, not as machines. In the lower curve of one wall a sill of poured material rises a hand's breadth, now a ridge under roots, its surface pitted to the same texture as the stone beside it. Opposite, a shallow dish — ceramic, or something that has learned ceramic's patience — holds no water. A single thick leaf has grown into its concavity and fitted there, the way a lichen fits a north face, neither prize nor scar. Between two stems a filament of harder stuff surfaces for the length of a finger, then dives, bright only in the sense that it is smoother than everything touching it. These are not ruins in the dramatic sense. They are the pocket's fossils of intention, scattered, half-swallowed, in no arrangement that still gives orders. The place does not seem to be waiting to be switched back on. It seems to have finished becoming what it is.

The quiet is almost complete, and what breaks it is too small to be called sound from any distance. Now and then a film of water lets go of one surface and is taken by another: not a drip onto stone, a soft transfer, minutes or hours apart. Tissue creaks at the edge of hearing when the gradient shifts by a degree no season aboveground would bother to name. The mass overhead changes pressure on a cycle too slow to watch; the pocket answers by a tightening and a slackening of the air, a breath measured in days. Leaves do not toss. They reorient by millimeters, over durations that make weather look hasty, leaning their ribs toward the seams that feed them. Old growth is not shed in a fall. It thins, pales, and is recruited back into the mat, so the floor rises and is consumed at rates that have agreed to match. That agreement is the law here. The hollow does not close. The hollow does not enlarge. Erosion rounds; growth packs; the lattice holds the volume; the three have been in conversation long enough that the conversation looks like stillness.

There is no track in the felt, because there is nothing to track. No shell, no scat, no husk of an insect, no feather of dust arranged by a small body passing. Reproduction, if the word applies, is a matter of spread and of whatever the seams contribute — a chemistry handed across a root hair, a bud thickening where the film stays longest. The ecology is closed and unhurried: plant, mineral, the invisible work in the film, and the buried grain that keeps the cold from becoming ice and the damp from becoming a sump. Decay does not stink, because almost nothing is allowed to become waste. The sweetness in the air is the smell of that thrift.

Stand in it — though standing means bowing, and bowing means being among leaves that do not lean away — and the larger world reduces to an inference. Something built, or grown to a plan, continues above and around this pause. The pocket is a joint that was left, or a gland, or a room for a task so small it could be abandoned without the task's absence being fatal. Access, if it still exists, is not obvious; the dark offers no seam of lesser dark, no draft from a corridor. Embedded is the right word. The place is hidden the way a knot is hidden in a beam: contained, load-bearing in a minor way, not secret so much as unconsulted.

Nothing here is marvelous in the manner of a prodigy. The laws are plain once the hands have learned them. Water stays a skin. Cold stays. Dark stays. Growth fills what cold and dark and a buried gradient will permit, and stops where the volume stops. Change is real and too slow to catch in the act, visible only as the fitted leaf in the dish, the rounded sill, the roots that have chosen their seams and kept them. In the long run the pocket will be this: a dense, damp, lightless room under the weight of its own making, quiet except for the occasional soft trade of a bead of water from stone to leaf, lush with a life that has never needed an animal to complete it, and balanced — not preserved, not ruined — in the state it has practiced for longer than the difference matters.
