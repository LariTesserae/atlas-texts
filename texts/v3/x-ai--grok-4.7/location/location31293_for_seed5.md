The place sits below the level of the country around it, not as a valley you could name from a ridge, but as a missed step. From the flat above, there is only a darker crease in the turf, a line of squat horsetail, and then the ground simply fails. The lip is old. Its rim has been walked into a polish the width of a foot, and the polish does not shine; it has the matte of stone that has forgotten how to be rough. Nothing is freshly broken. The cut that holds the town was made so long ago that even the explanation of it has weathered down to the same grade as the walls.

You go down by a stair that is not quite a stair. Slabs have been set into the face where the face already wanted them, and between the slabs the moss has made its own treads, springy, cold, never muddy. The drop is not great. By the time your breath has gone white once, you are at the bottom, and the country above is already gone, replaced by a rim of pale rock no higher than a warehouse and as continuous as a bowl. The sky over the bowl is a hard white, without sun-disk and without cloud-shadow, and it does not travel. Shadows, where they exist, are the color of the things that cast them and they do not lengthen. It is always this bright, and it is always this cold. The cold is not a wind. It is a property of the surfaces. Stone takes it from your palm. Wood gives it back more slowly. Nothing in the air moves enough to carry it.

There is water, but you will not hear it. It lives as a film. On the undersides of eaves it gathers into drops that swell to a certain size and stop, as if size itself were a law. In the joints of the paving it darkens the grain and goes no further. Every dwelling has, at its threshold, a shallow basin cut from the same pale stone as the rim, and in each basin a still lens of water, clear down to a skin of green that is not algae exactly and not moss, a living glaze that does not spread and does not die back. If you touch the lens it seals over your finger without a ripple worth the name. Higher on the walls, where the old water-line of whatever once occupied this cut has been left as a darker band, condensation beads and is taken at once into the plants that own that band. There is no stream. There is no rain you could stand in. The damp is intermittent only in the sense that it prefers some materials to others: fiber, grain, the rims of eyes, the lips of the basins. The open centers of the lanes stay merely cold.

The town is the whole floor of the bowl and most of the walls. It was not planned in the way of a later city and it was not grown in the way of a thicket; it was fitted, over a time long enough that fitting became the only weather. Dwellings of three kinds lock into one another until the distinction stops mattering. There are stone cells cut back into the rim, their mouths reduced by walls of fitted rubble no taller than a person stooping. There are timber rooms stacked against those mouths, frames of split pale wood lashed with a fiber that looks like root and has the strength of one, floors of boards worn concave where knees and heels have kept the same appointments for longer than the boards' species still grows nearby. And there are gardens that are also rooms: platforms of woven reed thick enough to walk, held out from the walls on brackets of bone and pegged wood, each platform a dense mat of leaf. Nothing has a gap beside it. Eaves touch eaves. A ladder is also the side of someone's store. A cistern is also a step. Where a lane exists it exists because two walls agreed, long ago, to leave a slot the width of shoulders and a basket, and the agreement has been kept so exactly that the edges of the slot are rounded like river stone. You cannot see far down any of them. The town turns. It has always turned this way.

The plants are the packing material and also the crop and also, in places, the wall. They are lush in mass and poor in kind, which suits a cold that does not let go. Mats of a small stiff leaf, dark as wet slate and glossy as if oiled, cover every horizontal that is not a path. From the brackets hang ropes of a flatter plant, each rope a ladder of overlapping blades beaded with the permanent damp, and these do not swing. In the basins the green glaze. In the joints a moss so fine it reads as velvet until you are close enough to see it is a forest. On the rim above, the horsetails, and inside, their smaller cousins used as screens, each stem a tube, each tube beaded, none of them rustling because the air does not ask them to. Flowers, where there are flowers, are tight and pale and do not fall off. Nothing here sheds on a schedule. A leaf that has finished stays until a hand takes it, and the hands take them at the same rate the plants make them, so the green neither advances nor retreats. The lushness is an occupied lushness. Every frond has its place the way every door has its place.

The light finds all of it. Because the plants grow out and up in sheets rather than in crowns, they do not close the sky, and the pale stone of the rim throws the white day back down into the slots until even the deepest cell has a bright threshold and a bright band along its inner wall. Colors do not bleach. The cold seems to fix them: the green stays green, the wood stays the color of old bone, the stone a low cream darkening only where the damp prefers it. There is no hour in which the town becomes golden or blue. People work by this light and stop working by custom, not by dusk. Their custom is old enough to have worn grooves in the day the way feet have worn grooves in the stair.

They build with what the bowl yields and with what can be carried down the polished lip without a wheel, because there is no wheel. Wood is split, not sawn; the marks of wedges are still readable on beams that have otherwise been handled into anonymity. Stone is dressed by pecking and rubbing. Cord is rolled on the thigh from the fiber under the bark of the rim plants and from a tougher thread drawn out of the hanging blades. Fired clay appears as thin-walled jars set into niches sized for them exactly, and as pipes — short, straightened, socketed into one another — that carry nothing most of the time and the basin-water when a basin is tipped, which is rarely. A little copper exists, cold-worked into hooks, needles, and the lips of a few lamps that are never lit, because the day does not fail and the fuel would be a plant better eaten or plaited. No one is melting anything. No one is in a hurry that would require a better edge than a ground stone knife. The tools are beautiful in the manner of things that have been correct for so long that correctness has become invisible. They hang on pegs that have darkened in the shape of the tools. An empty peg would be an event. There are no empty pegs.

The people are many for the ground they stand on, and they fit the way the buildings fit. They move along the slots without brushing, which is a skill maintained by the narrowness itself. Their clothes are layered plant fiber, beaten and stitched, pale where the stone is pale, darker at the hems where the damp climbs and has been allowed to, because the damp is not dirt here and is not fought. Faces are bare. Breath shows in short clouds that hang and thin without drifting, each cloud reabsorbed by the air in the same place it was made. They speak, if speech is the right word, by placing things. A bundle shifted from the left of a threshold to the right is a message that the whole lane can read and that the next lane cannot, and this is enough. When they must use the voice it comes out small and stops at the moss. You can be a body's length away and know you were not meant to hear, because you did not. Children — there are children, though you cannot tell who has been a child for how long — learn the placements before they learn to balance a jar. No one raises a voice to teach them. The town is already arranged as a lesson in where things go, and the lesson does not change, so the learning does not have to be repeated in sound.

The animals are everywhere and they are quiet in the same structural way. On the hanging blades cling moth-bodied things with wings too heavy to flick, patterned like the leaf they match, feeding without a sound you could separate from the general absence. In the basins, fish no longer than a hand hold station above the green glaze, fins moving at a rate that does not disturb the lens. They are many. Every basin has its number, and the number is full. Birds the size of figs occupy holes in the timber where pegs were never set, and also holes that were set for them; the distinction was given up. They do not call. They shift their weight and the wood accepts it. Along the moss-runs at the base of the walls move small animals with close fur and bare tails, neither rats nor quite not, their paths so habitual that the moss has grown into two tones, the darker where feet fall, the lighter in the margins that are never stepped on. Nothing hunts in a way that would empty a niche. What eats, eats the surplus the plants are always making and not exceeding, and what would be prey is kept at the count the bowls and holes can hold. You do not see a kill. You do not see a birth. You are ready to believe both happen, somewhere just beside the brightness, in the same quantity.

The unusualness of the place is not a wonder standing in a square. There is no square. It is the way continuance has replaced event. A crack in a lintel was repaired in the language of the lintel, so long ago that the repair has the same depth of patina as the stone, and no later crack has asked. Footprints in the damp joints would be informative if the damp did not settle back to the same darkness in the same shapes, which are not foot shapes but the shapes of the joints themselves. Food is taken from the mats at the rate the mats allow, and the mats allow a rate that keeps every platform full. People sleep in the cells when the custom says sleep, and the white sky goes on providing the same light to the thresholds of the sleepers. If someone dies, the town has a way for that too, and the way returns the fiber, the little copper, the place in the slot, to the count. You will not find a marker. The rim above has no graves because the bowl does not give anything back to the flat country, and does not take from it either. What is carried down the stair comes down rarely enough that the stair's polish can be attributed entirely to the people who already live here, going up to look at the horsetails and coming back because the horsetails are only the rim of what they already have.

Stand in a slot and the town is a thickness of edges: stone lip, wood lip, leaf lip, water lip, each beaded or bare according to its nature, all of them sharp in the shadowless light and none of them new. Lean out of a woven platform and the opposite wall is a tapestry of the same decisions, repeating not by ornament but by necessity, bright from top to bottom, green where a hand can reach, pale where the sky strikes, dark only in the permanent damp-lines that were old when the first socketed pipe was set. There is a smell, and it is quiet too: cold leaf, wet stone, the faint sweetness of fiber curing in its own time, the clean animal smell of fur that is never wet enough to sour. It does not travel. It stays where it is made, as the breath stays, as the drops stay at their size.

Nothing is about to happen. That is the rule you feel in your hands before you understand it, and it is why the packedness does not feel like pressure. A crowded place usually implies a future collision. Here the crowding has already been resolved, and the resolution is the town. The basins will be full at the same line tomorrow, and the line is not a measure of tomorrow because tomorrow is this light again, this cold again, this hush that is not the hush of waiting. The moths will be on their blades. The fish will be at their stations. Someone will move a bundle from the left of a threshold to the right, and the lane will know, and the moss will take the sound of the bundle, if it makes any, and keep it.
