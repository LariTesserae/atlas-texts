The plateau does not announce itself as high. It has been high so long that height is only a fact you notice when the air fails you, or when a cut in the stone shows older cuts beneath it, each one rounded, each one older than the mills that sit in them. The land is a shelf broad enough to breed its own weather. Clouds form over it and do not leave. They thicken in the afternoons, thin at the edges by morning, and rain in a warm, unemphatic way that never quite becomes a storm and never quite stops. Between rains the stone sweats. Water stands in every low joint of the rock, films the vertical faces, beads along iron and along the lips of cisterns. You can put a hand to a wall at noon and come away wet, and the wall was not recently rained on. It has simply been wet for centuries.

Nothing green grows. There are no trees on the ridges, no grass in the joints, no moss that a botanist would claim. The wet stone carries only a skin: mineral stain, a brown-gold film that smells faintly of metal and warm dust, bacterial, not leafy. Where water pools longest the film darkens to near-black and lifts in curling flakes when the level drops. People scrape it. Animals eat it. It is not a crop. It returns on its own schedule, which is the schedule of damp and heat, and both of those are nearly constant.

The cities were built into this and then weathered until they read as part of the shelf. From a distance a district is a change in the grain of the stone: tighter jointing, more right angles, a paler dust where limestone facing has gone to powder against the darker country rock. Closer, the streets are narrow because the plateau does not offer wide flats, and what flats exist were taken early for water. Housing climbs. Workshops climb. Walkways cross above walkways, and the lower levels are dim not from any absence of day but from the stacking. Daylight here is ordinary daylight, a white-yellow sun behind a high thin haze, strong enough to throw shadows and to warm iron until it is uncomfortable to lean on. The haze is moisture and the constant traffic of small bodies in the air. Shadows are soft-edged. You can read by them. You cannot see far.

The mills are not new and they are not dying. They turn because they have turned, on drives that were sized for loads that no longer fluctuate much. Water wheels sit in races cut from the living rock, fed by the same weeping that feeds the cisterns; the races are lined with a glaze of mineral so old it looks poured. Elsewhere the power is shaft and belt, engine-houses with tall stacks that do not smoke black so much as breathe a warm exhaust into air that is already wet. The sound of them is a low organized throb under everything else, regular enough that after an hour you stop separating it from the sound of water in the races. Production here is a climate. Cloth, ceramic, ground mineral, rendered fat, wire. The works run through the day and through most of the night at a reduced clatter, not because anyone is urgent, but because stopping a line that has found its speed costs more, in heat and in labor, than letting it continue. Change, when it comes, comes as a district slowly retools over years. A stack cools. Another, three streets over, is bricked up around a newer boiler. The old stack remains. Birds fill it.

The animals are the difficult fact of the place, and after a while the ordinary one. They are not a spectacle at the margins. They are the density. Every cornice holds nests of a dull gray bird that lives on the mineral film, on spilled meal, on the insects that live on both. The insects are a grit in the light: moths the color of wet stone, flies that gather where the bacterial skin is thickest, beetles in the warm gaps behind pipe. In the lower streets, where water stands ankle-deep along one side of the paving, there are animals the size of dogs that are not dogs, low, dark, numerous, moving in loose bands between the pilings. They nose the film. They nose each other. They do not startle easily; there are too many of them for startling to be a useful habit. Above the roofs the air is never empty. Flocks drift rather than wheel, broad slow sheets of birds that take half a morning to cross a district, feeding as they go, their passage a long soft roar that rises and does not peak. At times the roar is the loudest thing on the shelf, louder than the mills, and people stop talking until it thins. It always thins. It never finishes.

There are larger animals, fewer, and they are still too many. On the open stone between districts, where the city loosens into causeways and tank-farms and the long roofs of drying sheds, herds of heavy-shouldered grazers work the flats. They are not livestock in any tidy sense. They were here in some earlier form, or they came with the first works and stayed, and now they are managed only to the extent of being turned aside from a race or a rail line. They crop the film. They drink from the standing pools, which are warm, tea-colored, and smell of stone. Their dung is part of the cycle that feeds the flies that feed the birds. At night they bed down against the outer walls of the lower works, and the walls are worn concave where generations of shoulders have rubbed the same course of masonry.

Sound is constant and layered, never a single source. Water, first, in more registers than a dry country has: ticking from seep-lines, a flat pour in the races, the knock of a loose board in a cistern, the small bright noise of drops falling from iron into a full channel. Then the mills, the throb and the higher stitching of belts. Then animals, which occupy every remaining frequency: the chalky cry of the cornice birds, the wet mutter of the bands in the flooded gutters, wingbeat when a flock is low, the occasional deep note from something out on the flats that carries farther than it should in wet air. Human voices fit into the gaps. Markets run under awnings in the middle levels, and the calling there is brisk, but it does not dominate. You learn to speak closer to a person's ear than you would in a drier city. The loudness is not aggression. It is occupancy. Every volume of air already has a tenant.

The rules of the shelf are the rules of a place that has settled. Water is not fought; it is graded, stored, and allowed its paths. A street that holds a finger of standing water along its lower edge is a street in working order. A street swept dry is a street whose drains have failed, and it will be wet again by evening in a worse place, inside a wall. Building is slow because the stone is the structure and the stone is also the watershed. You do not cut a new foundation without knowing whose seep you will steal. Districts have water courts for this, old rooms with maps that are mostly stains, where the argument is about a centimeter of grade across a thousand meters of causeway. The arguments last months. The grade, once set, lasts lifetimes.

Heat is not extreme. It is a warm that does not leave at night, a warmth held by wet stone and by the mass of the works. People dress for damp more than for cold. Cloth mildews if it is stored wrong; the drying sheds exist because nothing dries by being left in the air. Food is animal, film, and what the mills render. There is no salad, no orchard, no green market stall. There is meat, eggs in quantities that seem impossible until you have stood under a flock, rendered fats, mineral salts scraped and baked, and a sour fermented paste made from the darker pool-film that outsiders will not eat twice and that everyone here eats without comment. The paste smells like the streets. That is not an accident. The streets smell like the paste, like warm iron, like wet feather, like the clean nothing-smell of rain that has already fallen and is about to fall again.

At the scale of the shelf, weather is local and also not. A district can sit under a thicker cap of cloud while the causeway you came in on, half a day's walk back, was in thinner light. The difference is not dramatic. It is a change in how far you can see the next stack, and in whether the film on the outer walls is glossy or merely damp. Storms, when they organize, organize slowly. You watch a bank build on the western edge of the plateau through a whole afternoon, a denser gray with a lower edge, and you know it will arrive after dark or tomorrow, and that it will be heavy and warm and that the races will run hard and the lower streets will take on another hand's breadth of water and the birds will pack the lees of every wall. Then it will thin. The mills will not have stopped. Someone will have opened an extra spillway, a procedure older than the current engines, and closed it again when the level in the great cisterns — vast rectangular cuts, some of them older than the districts that drink from them — sits at the mark everyone recognizes and no one remembers setting.

Travel across the shelf does not bring a new country. It brings the same country with different ages of masonry. Older districts have worn their corners off; their mills are lower, their stacks broader, their animals more continuous with the flats, as if the boundary between works and herd had never been firm. Newer districts, newer only by a century or two, still show the cut of the saw in sheltered joints and have more iron, more belt, a sharper smell of hot oil under the wet. Even these are already stained to the color of the plateau. Even these have nests in every place a nest can hold. A traveler who stays a month stops looking for the edge of the city and starts looking for the edge of the wet, and does not find it. The wet is the shelf. The shelf is high, and broad, and inhabited in every layer it has: film, water, animal, wall, wheel, and the slow white daylight that comes down through all of them and leaves them, by evening, exactly as warm as they were at noon.
