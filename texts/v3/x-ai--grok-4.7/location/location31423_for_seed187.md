The country around it has already finished. It lies low and level, worn past the stage where weather still cuts new lines, and what remains of the ground does not show itself as ground. For a long way in every direction the surface is the same pressed fiber, old stems laid flat by their own weight and by years that no longer rearrange them, a mat so continuous that the eye finds no stone, no bare soil, no bank. The air is cool in an even way, with no warm slope and no cold hollow to walk toward. Light is ordinary daylight under a sky that does not seem to be deciding anything.

The only break is small enough to walk around. A ring of living mass, grove-sized, no larger than the floor of a church, rises out of the flatness and closes on itself. From outside it looks like a wall. Once you are at it, the height revises itself downward: not a nave, not a cliff, only the slumped angle of plant matter that stopped slumping. The outer face is thick, soft at the hand, and stratified where it has split. Layer under layer of stems, some still green at the living skin, most of them straw-colored and packed, the oldest of them dark and fibrous and no longer distinguishable from one another. The split is a split. Nothing here has been cut.

Inside, the floor is the same material, and the air above it is almost empty. You can cross the open middle in a short walk and meet nothing: no shrub standing apart from the ring, no fallen trunk that has become its own object, no animal paused in the open. The volume is the feature. Plant fills every surface that could have been land or shelter, and then stops, so that what the place actually offers is a room of cool air held in a body of vegetation. The ceiling is sky. The walls are the inner face of the ring, higher than a person and thick enough that the plain outside disappears once you step down, but not so high that the light fails. It is normal light, diffuse, enough to see the grain of the fiber and the few dark cups in the floor. There is no shaft, no traveling edge of sun, no corner appreciably dimmer than the center. The place is not organized by shadow.

It is damp, and only damp. Moisture does not arrive as weather you can watch. It is already on the inner stems as a film, cool against the fingers, beading where a joint leans inward and letting go, now and then, from the same joints, at a rate that does not hurry or tire. The drops tick into pockets in the floor. The pockets are small, still, and separate. They do not feed one another. Some hold a finger's depth of brown water; some are only a wet darkening in the fiber that never quite becomes a pool and never quite dries. There is no channel leaving the ring. Whatever river once worked this low country finished its work ages ago and left no bed you can find, only this held moisture, the same in every part of the room, not wetter toward one wall, not drier toward the middle.

Nothing in the open belongs to anyone's making. No post, no cord, no brick pressed into the mat, no path worn by feet. A step springs back. The fiber does not take a print, or takes it and loses it while you are still looking, which comes to the same thing. The equilibrium is tight enough that the room does not record you.

The sound does. It is loud before it is identifiable, a full-body noise that makes you raise your voice and then makes raising it pointless. After a while it separates, not because it changes, but because attention does.

The nearest layer is the ring itself. Air moves through hollow stems in a rush like flues, steady, one pitch, the sound of a great many narrow pipes of nearly the same age and bore. Under that, and also inside the wall, is the animals. They are abundant, and they are not in the room. The open air stays empty; the presences are in the fiber, packed along the living thickness and the wet pockets, with a great deal of stem and root between any one of them and the next visible gap. Put an ear to the inner face and the mat is occupied: clicks of beetles in the packed generations, the small bright insistence of wrens somewhere in the green skin, the rasp of warblers that never come out to the rim, frogs calling from pockets you cannot see from the pockets you can, the tick and seep of smaller bodies in the wet. A single bird may cross the open sky and be gone. Nothing else enters the volume. The abundance is a property of the wall, and it does not thin out while you listen, and it does not gather into a flock, a swarm, a crossing. It is simply ongoing, at a density the air refuses.

Under both of those, lower and larger, is a third note that the plants are not making. It has the evenness of something electrified and left on: no cough, no hunt for speed, no cycle you can count. Somewhere past the outer stems, not in this place and not visible from inside it, engines are running at the scale of a works. The flatness carries them. The ring takes them up and holds them, so that inside the room the machine sound seems to come from the floor. With it, faintly, a warmth that is not temperature: hot oil, metal, the smell of scaled production drifting across vegetation and never quite winning. No stack shows above the rim. No wire comes in. The industrial fact of the world stops at the boundary as an object and continues, unaltered, as noise.

Nothing here is becoming. The mat outside has reached the flattest state erosion can leave, and the ring has reached the shape a living mass keeps when it is no longer building itself upward and no longer falling in. The water will not rise to cover the floor and will not leave the pockets. The light will not stage the room. The animals will not come out into the empty middle in any number that would fill it. The engines will not rest and will not grow louder. If you leave and return, the same joint is dripping, the same pockets hold, the same three notes occupy the air in the same balance, and the space between presences is still the greater part of the place. It is a small room of cool daylight, bounded, hollow, and finished, loud with lives and machines it does not contain.
