The old ground stops at a lip that has not yet learned to be a hill. It is a cut, not a slope: gravel still angular, clay still blue where a block calved last season and has not been rained into roundness. Ordinary daylight falls across it without drama. On the far side of the drop, the same daylight is doing the same work, only lower — a broad floor set beneath the country that made it, open enough that the opposite rim is a real horizon and not a wall, and young enough that nothing on that floor has had time to pretend it was always there.

Cold air has already found the place and stayed. It lies in the bowl the way water lies, thicker toward the middle, so that the rims can be merely sharp while the floor is winter-adjacent even when the light says day. Breath would show, if anything here breathed in a way that asked to be seen. Most of what breathes does not.

Water is not a feature of the floor. It is the floor’s condition. It stands in sheets between stems, moves in threads through them, wells from the scarp in dark lines that never quite become falls, and hangs in the air as a fine condensate that beads on every edge and does not fall so much as change its mind and join the leaf it was already touching. The young river — it is a river, though it has not earned meanders — runs too straight down the axis of the basin, green-gray, full to its banks and over them, braiding where yesterday’s slump shoved gravel into it and it has not yet cleared its throat. Springs come up through the till still milky with rock flour. None of this sounds like much. Wet stems and cold air take the noise and keep it.

What grows here should, by the cold, be spare. It is not. The substrate is new and undepleted, and the water never leaves, so life has answered with quantity instead of stature. There is no high canopy to close the sky. There is a pelt. Sedges in ranks so tight a hand put down would not find mud, only the sides of other leaves. Horsetail in rust-green brakes along every seep, jointed and hollow and packed enough to look built. Willow and low alder in belts, not groves — whips of this year and the year before, bark still bright, roots already knitting the raw banks before the banks can fall again. Moss in a living film rather than a memory of peat; there has not been time for peat. Cottongrass in the slightly higher frozen-edged pans, seed-heads lighting the cold without warming it. In the spring pools, where the water is a degree less bitter, charophyte meadows and broad leaves lie on the surface edge to edge, no open mirror left. The lushness is a skin over bones. Scrape it and you are in gravel.

Under and through that skin the basin has organized itself at the scale of a town, and then kept going until the town is the valley. Nothing here was designed, and nothing here is empty, and the two facts have produced infrastructure.

The canals are the first thing that reads as streets. They are not accidents of slope. They run where they are kept open: ramps of peeled stick and packed mud at calculated intervals, low walls that hold a head of water on one side and a feeding platform on the other, a lattice that steps the young river’s excess sideways into the sedges and brings it back when the sedges have used it. Lodges sit on the slight rises of unsorted till — and the rises themselves are new, dump-piles and old ice-contact mounds not yet gentled — and the lodges are layered in the way of any place that builds every season on last season’s work. Mud, stick, root, a winter cap of frozen grass, another storey. Some have merged. Between them the paths are not footpaths. They are slots in the vegetation worn by bodies that use the same line until the line is a ditch, and then keep the ditch, because a ditch in this country is a road that also feeds you.

On the scarps, where a mammal would not bother, other builders have taken the fresh ledges. Every horizontal that collapse left — and collapse leaves many, because the rock and the till are still breaking on their own joints — carries a nest, a bracket of woven reed, a stain of guano that is already a soil, a nursery of flies that is already a reason for something else to stand there all day. The weeping faces of the cut are occupied to the limit of grip. Moss where seep keeps the stone wet. Crusts of red iron and pale mineral where the water leaves what it carried. Crack-roots in fissures that did not exist a decade ago. No ledge is ornamental. The wall is a district.

Toward the middle the canopy-that-is-not-a-canopy lowers further and the water rises, and the city becomes a shallow-flooded ward of platforms. Muskrat houses, bird-built mats, rafts of last year’s stems trapped and then grown through until they no longer remember being debris. You could not row it even if you had a boat made of something other than the basin; there is no lane that stays a lane for longer than the animals need it, and they need all of them. Fish move in the dark under the mats in numbers that dimple the surface in continuous small rings. The rings overlap. From a rise the open water, such as it is, looks hammered.

The rises are the only places the eye gets out of the pack, and from them the pattern repeats without thinning. Canal, lodge-row, sedge floor, spring mist, scarp. Again. The far rim is far in the way of a walk that would take the day and the next morning, not in the way of a view from a mountain. Scale here is civic: you can hold the whole in mind as one settlement, and you still cannot see its edge from its middle. Density does not drop at a outskirts, because there are no outskirts. Where the floor meets the rim, occupancy changes materials and continues.

Nothing in it is a device. No cut wood in the sense of a measurement. No fire-scar as a hearth, no dressed stone, no line that was made level because level was a word. The straightness is water seeking and being corrected by tooth and haunch and the habit of a colony. The layering is annual. The walls fail and are replaced before weather can round them, which is why the basin still looks young even where it is most built: repair outruns erosion. A dam abandoned for a season would slump into the soft geometry of ordinary banks. None are abandoned. The sharpness of the place is a maintained sharpness, the way a city’s streets stay streets only because they are used harder than rain uses them.

The animals are everywhere the structures are, which means everywhere, and they are the reason the sound never rises to match them. Cold, wet, windless air carries poorly, and the stems are a baffle, and most of the business is conducted at the volume of a body slipping into water it already expected. A heron takes a fish and the event is a small collapse of rings. Ducks shovel the muck in flocks that would be a roar of wings if they flew; on the water they are a moving texture. Beavers work at dusk and in the day without ceremony, the occasional soft collapse of a stick into mud, then nothing. Otters thread the canals as if the canals were theirs, which they partly are. Voles tick through the sedge bases in such numbers that the floor faintly seethes, a motion you see in the stems a moment after it has happened. On the rim, where the cold pool is shallower and the prey is dense against the wall, foxes and, less often, larger hunters work the edge and do not need to call. Insect life is a haze over the spring wards, visually loud, nearly silent. Predation is constant and ordinary: the canals are pantry and street at once, and something is always being eaten at a scale too small or too wet to clap.

The day itself is busy in the way weather is busy when it has no audience. Melt on the rim arrives as a pulse, not a flood announcement — a higher standing of the sheets by midmorning, a thread of new water down a scarp that was damp and is now running, a fresh slip of gravel into a pool that clouds and clears. By afternoon the pulse has been absorbed into the lattice, held, spilled, used. Toward evening the edges of the pans rime. The rime forms on living leaves as readily as on mud, a white edging that does not kill the stand beneath it; by the next clear morning it is gone off the south faces and still there in the cold traps, and the plants are no less packed for having worn it. A block lets go from the scarp with a sound so short and so swallowed that the new raw face is the news, not the noise. Within a day the seeps have found it. Within a season it will have a nest and a film of green and a stain that is the beginning of a soil it will not be allowed to finish, because something else will calve beside it.

That is the rule under the others. The basin is younger than the habits using it, so succession cannot settle into the old story of bare ground, then moss, then shrub, then quiet shade. Shade never comes; the growth stays low and total. Bare ground never returns except as a wound, and wounds are occupied before they can widen. Water is not a season. It is the street level, varying by a hand’s breadth and never absent. Cold is not a pause. It is the climate of the floor, pooled and reliable, selecting for whatever can be lush without being tall and active without being loud. Construction is a biological pace, not an event: if you watched a single lodge you would say the place was still; if you looked across a ward you would see the city being continued in a thousand small edits.

By full day the light is simply light — no cathedral shafts, no cave gloom, no snow-glare. It comes in flat and complete and shows the colors as they are: the blue of unoxidized clay in a fresh slip, the iron-red of a seep that has been weeping since the cut opened, the many greens of a flora that has not agreed on one green, the dark of water under mats, the pale of seed-heads and rime. Mist stands in the spring quarter and does not travel; the rest of the basin is clear enough to count the repeated works back to the rim. Sound remains what the density and the cold make of it. A low, nearly theoretical murmur where the young river shoulders a dam. The tick of rime leaving a stem. Wings, rarely, and then brief.

If there is a center, it is only the place where the straight new river still remembers it was a spillway and not yet a street, and even that is failing in the ordinary way — banks being fixed, flow being taxed by a hundred canals, the spillway learning the grid. Around it the packed city of mud and tooth and root and cold water runs on to the scarps and up them, fully occupied, recently emerged, and already older in its habits than the ground those habits stand on. Nothing in it is waiting to become something else. It is busy being the thing the new low ground permitted, all at once, and quietly, and without pause.
