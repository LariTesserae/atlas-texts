The ground is already warm before the sun clears the far rim. Heat comes up through the soles, through the sinter, as if the plateau were a lid set on something that never quite went out. The air above it is thin enough that a shout dies inside a few paces, and the light—ordinary daylight, hard-edged, neither dim nor glaring—falls on a country you can see across for hours and still not find an empty place to set a foot.

This is a high bench, not a peak. The elevation is extreme and settled: an old volcanic plain planed by weather into broad steps, fins, and shallow basins, then left alone long enough that the forms no longer argue with the wind. Erosion here is not a drama. It is a finished grammar. Gullies have their widths. Talus has found its angle and stays. The rock is a pale, porous sinter, stained rust where iron has sweated out and chalk where salts have bloomed, and every surface carries the small polish of long exposure. Nothing is fresh. Nothing is collapsing. The country has arrived at the shape it means to keep.

From any rise the scale opens. A valley-system of benches runs away to a blue-gray rim that may be a day’s walk or three; between here and there the land repeats itself in variations—a dry pan, a dark tongue of older lava, a line of standing fins like the ribs of a hull. You can hold the whole region in one look. What you cannot do is find a margin inside it that is unoccupied.

The packing is not of trees or of houses. Vegetation is scant: a scatter of cushion plants no higher than a fist, black crusts of lichen on the north faces of stones, a few wiry shrubs rooted in cracks where last month’s rain still ghosts as damp. The green, such as it is, reads as punctuation. What fills the sentence is everything else. Beetles the color of the sinter move in sheets across warm rock and stop, indistinguishable from grit, the moment a shadow crosses them. Lizards hold every ledge that offers a palm’s width of shade. Birds—small, kiln-brown, with bills stained white at the base—nest in densities that would be absurd in a greener country: in fissures, under overhangs, inside the bells of abandoned pipes, three and four to a cavity, their territories measured in inches. At dusk the fins are stippled with them, each bird a dark seed on the pale stone, and the air fills with a soft, directionless ticking of wings that never quite becomes a sound you can point to. Thin air takes voices and gives back almost nothing. Even a flock lifting is mostly a visual event.

Water is a rumor the land keeps making true in small ways. There is no river you could follow with confidence. There are seeps: dark ovals at the foot of fins, ringed with salt, alive for a few hours after the night’s condensation has run down the rock and pooled. There are cisterns cut or poured by whoever built here, their rims furred with mineral, their water warm, tasting of metal and stone. Rain comes seldom and does not stay. When it does come it arrives as a brief, hard scattering that darkens the sinter for minutes, runs in bright threads down channels that already know their work, and vanishes into the porous ground or into the old tanks. By afternoon the surface is pale again, except in the sheltered pockets, where damp persists as a cooler smell and a darker skin. Animals arrange their day around those pockets. You can read the schedule in the trails: narrow, polished, overlapping, leading from shade to seep to shade, worn not by many years of few feet but by many feet every day.

The built things are scattered and partial, and they are not ruins in the mournful sense. A pump house squats on a bench with its door gone and its flywheel still turning, slow, geared to a shaft that disappears into the sinter—geothermal, or whatever residual heat the plateau spends so freely. Farther along, a cableway’s towers march toward the rim, most of them empty, one of them still carrying a single bucket that creaks outward at intervals and returns, as if the line had forgotten it had no one left to serve and kept the habit. Evaporation pans, shallow and rectangular, hold skins of brine. A rail spur ends in gravel. A tin-roofed shed has become a nest-cliff: every joist, every gap under the eaves, claimed. The industry is real and scaled—these were works, not cabins—but it is thinned to occasional machines still doing one job each, and the jobs are quiet. Engines here do not roar. In this air a working motor is a vibration you feel in a rail before you hear it, a tremor in the sole, a faint oily sweetness near the housing. The birds prefer the housings that still run. The vibration keeps the mites off. That is the bargain, and it has been in force long enough that the nests are built to the rhythm: cups of salt-crust and fiber, tucked where the metal is warm but not scalding, repaired after every molt.

The unusual thing is not a marvel. It is a rule you notice only after you have walked. Life here is thickest where the rock is hottest and the machines still move, and sparsest where a green person would look for ease. The cushion plants, few as they are, grow on the cooler margins and are barely browsed. The abundance feeds on the condensate cycle, on the brine flies that rise off the pans in soundless clouds, on each other, and on a microbial skin that colors the seeps olive and rust and is not quite a plant. Predators are abundant in proportion: a long gray raptor that hunts by walking the cable lines, pausing, dropping; a lizard large enough to take nestlings, patterned so exactly like flaking sinter that you step beside it before you see it. The food web is short, fast, and local. A rain, a seep, a hatch of flies, a pulse of nesting, a walk of the raptor along the wire—then the dry interval, and the same sequence waiting in the cisterns and the gear-housings for the next wetting.

By midmorning the heat is the main weather. Shadows are small and precise. The far rim wavers, not with haze of moisture but with the shimmer off the benches. Sound remains a local property. A bucket ticks against a tower. A lizard’s belly scrapes. Somewhere a valve lifts and seats, a soft mechanical inhale, and the seep below that pump house rises a finger’s width, and thirty birds adjust their stance on the rim without calling. The country is in motion all day—animals trading places, water appearing and failing, machines keeping their reduced duties, heat breathing up through old, balanced stone—and almost none of it announces itself beyond the reach of an arm.

Toward evening the plateau does something that explains the damp better than the rain does. As the ground’s heat keeps rising and the thin air cools, a film of condensation forms on every shaded face and on every piece of metal that has not been in the sun. It beads on the cable, on the tin roof, on the undersides of fins. For an hour the industrial remains look jeweled. The birds drink from the beads. The beetles come up out of the grit to the wet. Then the film thins, the beads fail, and the night is dry again except in the pockets that have always been pockets. The engines continue. A bucket goes out and comes back. Across the open benches, under ordinary starlight, the packed life of the place settles into its cracks and its housings and its warm machines, and the quiet is not emptiness. It is a country so fully occupied, and so high, that it has no need to be heard.
