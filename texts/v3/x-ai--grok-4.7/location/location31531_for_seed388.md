The place is smaller than a parish and older than its machines. You enter it at the lip of a sunken court, no wider than a ropewalk and not much longer, walled on three sides by brick that has gone soft at the edges and black in the joints. The fourth side is not a wall. It is a weir of iron, bolted in overlapping plates, over which water arrives without ever quite arriving—sheet after sheet, warm, the color of weak tea, falling into a channel that has already decided, centuries ago, where it will go.

Everything here sits low. The court floor is below the street you came from, and the street itself is below the canals that feed it, and those canals are below the weather. Rain does not fall so much as condense out of a close air and join water already standing. Flagstones never dry. They hold a film that mirrors the lamps in broken coins. Between the stones, and in the mortar, and in every shelf the masons left by accident, something green has taken the lease and kept it. Not a garden. A tenancy. Ferns thick as a man’s wrist come out of the weep-holes. Moss pads the treads of the iron stair until the stair is a soft diagonal. Vines have learned the geometry of pipe runs and repeat it, leaf over leaf, until a valve wheel looks like a flower that forgot to open.

The works never stopped. That is the first strangeness, and it does not announce itself. Somewhere under the court a pair of engines turns at a rate you could count if you had the afternoon. They are not large. Cathedral-small: you could walk their length in thirty paces, if the catwalks allowed it, which they do not, being layered three deep and close enough that a coat-sleeve brushes warm casing on one side and wet brick on the other. Steam leaves through a forest of short stacks and does not rise far. It hangs at head-height, then folds back into the vines. The smell is metal, leaf-rot, and a sweet hydrocarbon that the plants seem to drink without complaint.

Light is a ration. Day comes in as a gray lid over the court and does not reach the lower galleries. What illuminates the machines is their own: caged bulbs gone amber with age, a few arcs that stutter and then hold, and the dull red of a furnace door that is never quite shut and never quite needed. Shadows do not flicker. They have positions, like furniture.

There are no animals. Not a rat in the sump, not a moth at the bulbs, not the thin whistle of a bird in the stacks. The lushness is only plant, and the plants do not behave as if anything browses them. Leaves overlap until there is no gap an insect could claim, and still nothing claims it. Seedlings rise in the drip-line of a leaking flange and are not eaten, not even by each other beyond the ordinary crowding. The silence of that absence is not peaceful. It is a missing register, the way a chord is wrong when one note has been left out and the others play on, louder for it.

Loud, yes. Water on iron, continuous, a white noise with a pulse where the weir lip is uneven. Engines in a slow knock that carries through the brick into the soles of your feet. A belt somewhere slapping, then not, then slapping, on a cycle longer than a breath and shorter than a shift. Steam valves sighing open on a schedule no one visible is keeping. When a downpipe clears its throat, the sound fills the court and then the vines take it, leaf against leaf, and return a softer version, as if the greenery were a second set of baffles installed without drawings.

The built thing and the grown thing have reached a settlement. Erosion here is not ruin. Corners have rounded. Bolts have bled their rust into perfect fans on the plates beneath them, and the fans have not spread in years. A limestone sill, brought in from somewhere higher and drier, has been worried by water into a shallow bowl, and in the bowl a brake-fern has rooted so exactly that it looks placed. Mortar has gone. Roots have taken the work of mortar. Where a wall should have failed, it has instead become a trellis that happens to be load-bearing. The pumps still lift. The lamps still burn. Production, if that is the word, continues at a volume sized to the court: not a city’s worth, a room’s worth, repeated without haste.

Walk the lower gallery and the rules become legible. Water is allowed everywhere except inside the windings. Plants are allowed everywhere except across the sight-glasses; someone, or something, keeps those ovals clear, a perfect thumb-wipe in a world otherwise overgrown. Heat belongs to the engines and is loaned to the air. Nothing hurries. A drip that has been forming on a coupling since you entered finally lets go, strikes a leaf, and the leaf nods once, as if confirming a receipt. Overhead, the weir keeps its one long sentence. Underfoot, the engines answer in a shorter one. Between them the court holds still—dense, warm, dim, loud with work and water, green to the point of pressure—and does not ask to be larger than it is.
