The country does not fall so much as let go. On the high ground the surface is the old one: a pale, wind-planed crust, hard underfoot, with a scatter of knee-high scrub and nothing that could be called a wood. Then the crust simply ends. A fresh lip, still sharp enough to cut a sole, runs for miles in both directions, and beyond it the land has dropped a long step and stopped. The drop is not a canyon. Nothing has had time to wear it into one. The walls are fracture faces, planar, pale where they have just opened to weather and darker where last night’s damp still clings in the grain. You can see, if you stand on the lip at midday under an ordinary sky, the matching wall on the far side, small with distance, the same unsoftened stone. Between them lies a floor wide enough that a town can lose itself in it and still not fill it.

The floor is new in the only sense that matters here. The blocks have settled, tilted a little against one another, and then held. Their edges have not yet rounded. Joints still show as dark lines rather than as gullies. There is no river, because a river would require the ground to have agreed, over years, on a lowest path, and the ground has not agreed. Water arrives the way it must on a surface that has not learned to drain: as rain that does not know where to go, as seep along the fresh joints, as a cold film that forms on stone after the air in the basin has sat still all night. By midmorning most of it is only damp—darkened clay in the cracks, a glaze on north-facing walls, puddles no deeper than a hand in the hollows where a block has sagged. A few of those hollows keep a skim of water through a dry week. People treat them as wells without ever having dug them. By afternoon the thinner films are gone, and the stone is only cool.

Cool is the condition of the whole floor. The high country takes the wind and gives it back as a dry moving cold; down here the air settles and stays. Mornings begin under a low brightness, not dim, only unemphatic—the sky open above the rims, cloud often enough to gray the light without hiding the far wall. You can work by it from the first clear hour. What you cannot do is get warm by standing still. The stone under the houses never quite loses the night.

They built anyway, and they built until there was no interval left to build in. The settlement does not sit in the basin so much as occupy it, shoulder to shoulder, from a margin of clear ground along the near wall out across the blocks until the roofs become a single texture and the far workings blur into the distance. It is not a city of monuments. It is a city of necessity and of a material that the drop itself supplied. The clay that squeezes up in the joints is the brick. The fracture faces are the quarry, and the quarry is also the back wall of the outer houses, so that in places a dwelling is only three built sides and one side the planet. Timber is scarce—the scrub on the rim will not yield beams—and what little wood comes down is rationed to lintels, to the short bridges over joints that are still opening by a finger’s width a season, and to the drying racks that stand on every roof because the ground has no room for them.

There is no room. Streets are gaps between walls, narrow enough that two people with a plank between them must negotiate. Courtyards exist only as light wells the width of a blanket. Storage is vertical: jars stacked in stair-niches, brine crocks on the landings, baskets hung from pegs so that the floor can still be walked. Even the work is packed. Brick-molding, fiber-twisting, the slow knapping of stone tools, the mending of hides—all of it happens in the same rooms where people sleep, or on the roofs, which have become a second town, flatter and more crowded in the dry part of the day, emptied when the rain comes in its brief, irresolute way and everyone drops below to keep the unfired clay from going back to mud.

The tools match the scarcity. Stone, bone, fired clay, a little soft metal beaten into knife-blades and awls and the occasional hoop for a well-jar. No wheel loud enough to matter; loads move on the body or on sledges of split rim-wood that whisper over damp stone. Kilns are small, dug into the waste faces at the margin, and they are fired when the weather allows, not when a schedule demands. The technology of the place is mostly the technology of repeating a simple thing until the repetition becomes a wall.

What the density does not produce is noise. Clay drinks sound. The streets turn too often for a voice to carry. There are no carts with iron tires, no animals in any number, no bells larger than a hand. From a roof at midday the active fact of the town registers as motion more than as sound: people crossing from shadow to light, a hide being scraped, smoke from a kiln leaning and then thinning, a line of children carrying water skins from a seep-pocket to a house that has gone dry. You hear the nearest work and, past it, almost nothing—a faint composite, like grain shifting in a jar. Weather is louder than the town. Rain on packed roofs. The occasional soft report when a settling block gives up another hairline and a wall answers it. At night the basin is quiet enough that a single voice on a far roof can be placed, if not understood.

The living things that are not people are few, and most of them are new in the same way the stone is new. Lichen has begun on the damper fracture faces, a gray-green stipple that does not yet soften an edge. In the joints where water stands longest, a rush has taken, and moss in the wall-shadows that never fully dry. Nothing arboreal has committed itself to the floor; roots would be guessing. On the rims the scrub holds a few small birds that sometimes drop into the basin and do not stay. Swifts use the cracks above the outer houses, and their wings make the only quick sound that belongs entirely to animals. Midges rise off the seep-pools in the late light. A fox is sometimes seen on the lip at dusk, looking down, never coming. The rest of the fauna is the town itself: bodies, smoke, the yeast in the jars, the beetles in the thatch-scraps used as bedding over clay. Predation, where it exists, is domestic and small—a hand over a mouse in the grain, a child flicking midges from a water skin.

The processes that keep the place from being still are ordinary and concurrent, and none of them has finished. The blocks are still easing. A house built square in the spring is slightly racked by autumn, and the remedy is not engineering but attention: a shim, a rebuilt corner, a lintel reseated, a joint in the floor acknowledged by laying a board across it rather than by pretending the ground has stopped. Water redraws itself every night. A puddle that was at the end of a street last week is now inside a doorway, because the block tipped, and the household has moved the jars and accepted the new interior shoreline. Rain comes in short visits, wets everything, and leaves the kilns idle for a day. People time the firing, the brick-turning, the washing of fiber, the spreading of hides, to those visits and to the cool clear intervals between them. Children learn the joints the way children elsewhere learn hedgerows: which crack is still moving, which seep is reliable, which roof will take you across six houses without a descent.

There is no myth required to explain any of it. The ground dropped. Water found the drop and stayed as damp rather than as a river. People followed the water and the clay and the shelter from the plateau wind, and they filled the usable floor until filling it became the form of the city. The light is daylight. The cold is the cold of stone that has not been in the sun long, geologically speaking, and of air that has nowhere lower to drain. The quiet is what clay and closeness and a lack of beasts and machines produce when they coincide. The unfinishedness is visible in every sharp edge and in every wall that has been repaired twice and will be repaired again before the edge has weathered enough to be called old.

Stand on the lip in the late afternoon, when the normal light goes long and a little gold along the far fracture, and the basin reads as one thing: a raw room in the earth, cool, damp at the seams, roofed only by sky, and packed to its margins with the patient, nearly silent work of staying.
