The plateau does not rise so much as continue. From any edge you can find, the stone runs on until the eye gives up before the land does, a single high table broken into blades, shelves, and fresh scarps that have not yet learned to be hills. The rock is young in the way a new tooth is young: edges still sharp, faces still pale, joints still bright where the last split opened. Nothing has had time to round. Nothing seems to be taking that time either.

It is cold the way height is cold, not the cold of a season. Breath does not hang so much as thicken. The air itself is wet — not rainy, not clear — a condensing medium that beads on every face the moment stone is exposed. Water lives near the surface and refuses to go down. It sheets off the scarps in skins too thin to call falls, collects in angles that should have drained and somehow do not, stands in marshes no wider than a road and longer than a day’s walk. The ground weeps from seams. Underfoot the new rock is already slick, already dark in its cracks, already growing the first velvet of growth in every place water pauses.

The plants are everywhere the water is, and the water is frequent, so the plateau reads as lush until you understand the spacing. Mats of leaf, ankle-deep and then waist-deep, fill the seep-lines and the flat pans. They are cold plants: small leaves, crowded, waxy, blue-green to the color of wet slate, flowering without scent in cups that hold a permanent drop. In the broader hollows they rise into stands — not trees exactly, more like frozen fountains of stem, each one a radial of stiff green, packed so tightly a person could not pass between them, and then a gap of bare stone fifty times as wide as the stand. Lushness here is local and absolute. Emptiness is the rule of the volume. Between one thicket and the next there is often nothing but sharp rock, a skin of water, and air that feels under-occupied, as if the plateau were a hall built for a crowd that never arrived.

There are no animals. Not a lacking you notice at first and then confirm. No tracks in the seep-mud, no shells, no cocoons on the leaf-mats, no quick motion at the edge of sight. The plants do not appear eaten. The water does not appear drunk except by roots. Whatever pollinates the cups, if anything does, is not a body you can find. The ecology is a conversation between stone, water, and growth, and it has been holding the same sentence for as long as the sentence has existed.

Light is ordinary. That is the strange part of the light. A sun, or something that occupies the office of a sun, stands at a fixed angle — not dawn, not noon, a mid-morning that does not advance. Shadows are crisp on the young scarps and do not swing. Color is honest: gray stone, green mats, the pale metal of distant roofs, the white of water moving over dark rock. You can see a long way. Seeing a long way does not bring you to an end.

The settlements are cities in the organizational sense and absences in every other. You come on them after hours of nothing — a grid of piers and low halls set into a seep-basin, foundations cut into stone that still shows tool-marks as fresh as the natural fractures. Chimneys. Gantries. A rail that arrives from the haze and does not appear to terminate so much as thin out. Electrified lines stitch from mast to mast and then stop, and the next mast is a speculation on the horizon. Inside the works the scale turns industrial without becoming crowded: engines housed in sheds with more empty floor than machine, vats fed by flumes that tap the weeping seams, presses and kilns and ranked housings whose purpose is production at a volume the emptiness around them makes almost theoretical. Housing exists — stacked, orderly, dry above the mats — and reads as inhabited by the evidence of maintenance, not by the evidence of lives crossing a square. Doors are shut. Lamps, where they burn, burn at one brightness. No one is late. No one is early.

The cities do not form a network you can hold in the mind. They are points. Between them the plateau reasserts its own size, large enough to carry weather that never quite becomes an event. Cloud strips lie along the scarps and do not advance. Fog pools in the long marshes and does not lift and does not thicken. A pressure system, if that is the word, occupies a region the size of a country and holds its shape the way a building holds its shape. Rain, where it is raining, has been raining at that rate. Where it is not, the air is still beading on the rock at the same rate it always beads. There is no front. There is no clearing. The plateau has weather the way it has cities: present, organized, and not going anywhere.

Sound is the property that refuses the emptiness. It should be quiet. It is not. The volume of the place is loud in a continuous way, without attack or decay, as if loudness were a material laid over the stone. Water on new edges makes a high, steady tear. The leaf-mats tick — not with insects; the cups overflow and the drops strike lower leaves in a pattern so regular it becomes a tone. Far off, and then not far off, the works contribute a harmonic: turbines at constant load, flumes at constant head, metal expanding and not contracting because the temperature does not move. Wind, such as it is, does not gust. It holds a speed, and the sharp scarps convert that speed into a whistle that does not wander in pitch. Stand in the open and the sound is large. Stand in a thicket and it is larger, because the stems add their own note and nothing in the system dampens. There is no night chorus to replace a day chorus. There is no day. There is this, at this level, and it does not swell and does not fail.

The rules, once you have walked long enough, are simple and intolerable in the way perfect machines are intolerable.

Nothing erodes. The young stone stays young. Tool-marks and fracture-faces share the same freshness, which means the plateau is not waiting to be worn down; wear has been set to zero and the appearance of recent birth is the permanent condition. Water moves and does not lower its basins. Leaves do not fall in a way that accumulates; the mats are as deep as they are and no deeper. Engines do not cycle toward maintenance. The rail does not deliver more than it removes, or removes more than it delivers — you cannot catch it in the act of changing a stockpile, because the stockpiles are already at the level they are for.

Nothing arrives and nothing leaves in any sense that would count as an event. If there are people, they are distributed the way the engines are distributed: necessary to the diagram, not visible as motion. If the plants spread, they spread only into the exact footprint they already occupy. The weirdness is not a creature in the fog or a door into another sky. It is the completeness of the equilibrium. A landscape this large should have gradients — wetter to the west, colder in the lee, younger at the scarps and older on the flats. It does not. Wetness, cold, height, lushness, emptiness, industry, noise, and newness are the same in every district you can reach. The plateau has found a single state that satisfies all of its physics at once, and it is holding that state with the effortlessness of something that has never had another state to compare it to.

Walk. The next city is a day away, or three, and will be the same city in all but the orientation of its flumes. The marsh you skirt will be beaded at the same density. A stem will tick. A turbine, somewhere under the fixed light, will hold its note. The sharp edge under your hand will be wet, and cold, and new, and it will be those things when you are no longer touching it, and when whatever walks here has stopped walking, and when the sound — which does not notice you — goes on being the size of the land.
