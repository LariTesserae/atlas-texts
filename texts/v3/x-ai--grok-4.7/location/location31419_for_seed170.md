The height is a thinness first. The ground has been lifted and then left, and the air never quite came back to it. Heat sits in that thinness without hurry. It is not the snapping heat of a low desert noon; it is a heat that has been resident so long it has the manners of climate. By day the light is ordinary. You can read color. You can trust a shadow for its edge, if not for its angle. The wrongness does not arrive as darkness or glare. It arrives as the fact that plain daylight is sufficient to see it.

There is no water, and there has not been water in any span that the stone still bothers to record. What pale crusts cover the flats are not sea-salts. They are sinters and metal blooms, dry films left by vapors that were never steam. They powder under a step and do not cake. Nothing in them invites a mouth. The basins hold light the way other countries hold lakes, a pale standing brightness with no surface tension, and when the wind moves it does not ripple. It only thins.

Nothing grows. The upright things are glass. Old glass, not the knife-edge of a fresh flow. Heat and the small persistent wind have worked it past sharpness into a settled grammar of spires, blades, and low welts, each form arrived at and then kept. Erosion here is not an attack. It finished its argument ages ago and remains only as the shape of the settlement. Ridges have the look of things that will be the same ridge at the far end of a life. A crack, once opened, stays open at the same width, dark and dry, a seam rather than a wound.

The country is open enough to hold several of these arguments at once. From any rise you see a glass-field, then a step of darker oxide, then a basin of white sinter, then another rise fretted with standing blades. The pieces are the size of valleys. They repeat without becoming a pattern you could farm or settle. There is no track that is not the track of the ground itself. Nothing has been built. No wall, no post, no slag-heap left by hands. If you look for a made thing you will fail, and the failure will not comfort you, because the country is plainly at work.

The work sounds like industry because it is industry, only it was not installed. Somewhere under the oxide a plate rises and falls on a period of many minutes, a piston that geology grew and then never stopped. Dry gas leaves a vent with the steady manners of an exhaust stack and vanishes without cloud. Between two metal seams a pale arc stands in the air and does not strike. It is lightning that has chosen to be a landform: thin, continuous, faintly hissing, old enough that the rock beneath it has glazed and then weathered dull again. Charge moves in the seams the way sap would move if anything here had bothered with sap. The production has no product you can carry away. It produces heat, glass, more seam, more of itself. Scaled, mechanized, patient. A factory that eroded into balance and kept its engines.

You can walk a long while and meet nothing you would call a body. The volume of the place is mostly empty in the ordinary sense: vast air, far rims, a single dark mote that might be a blade of glass catching and losing the sun. Emptiness, though, is not vacancy. The mote is not far. Or it is far, and also under the boot, as a pressure with no source you can point to. Distance is one of the agreements this country no longer keeps in full. A presence on the western rim occupies the basin you are crossing. You are not approaching it. You have been in it since the last step-down, and you will still be in it on the far side, only then you will be in another as well, or in the same one counted differently.

They are few. That is the only mercy and it is not a mercy. In a reach that might elsewhere hold a town there may be three, or one seen three ways, or a number that changes when you look at the sinter instead of the sky. Calling them fauna is a courtesy to the part of the mind that wants a slot. They are overwhelming the way a single machine is overwhelming when you are inside its housing: not by crowding, by saturation. Their bodies, where a body can be persuaded to stay in one place, are ridges that breathe. A flank of organized air. A heat with a hide. Plates along a spine that lift and seat themselves with the industrial thud, so slow you could sit between beats and believe the land had gone quiet, except the land is never quiet.

The sound is the true density. It fills what the bodies leave open. A low stroke, minutes apart, arriving through the soles and also, wrongly, from inside the ear, as if the ear were another vent in the same system. Over it a hiss of standing arcs, steady as a belt running somewhere out of sight. Under it the dry tick of glass adjusting to a heat that never leaves. No cry, no flock, no sudden startle. Loudness here is not an event. It is the settled weather of the place, and it does not spike. If you wait for it to crescendo into catastrophe you will wait past the usefulness of waiting. The cycle is gradual. A plate that was high at morning is a hand's width lower by dark, and the dark itself is only a dimming of ordinary light, the arcs remaining visible as pale stitches, the thump continuing on its own clock.

What they consume is not plant and not water. There is nothing here to graze. They appear to live on the gradient itself: heat leaving the deep rock, charge slipping between seams, the slow refusal of the glass to finish cooling. Some of them vent what others draw. A chemistry without any aqueous step, metal and gas and heat passed flank to flank across kilometers of apparently empty basin. You can stand in the exchange and feel it as a wind that has weight, a thermal you could lean on if you trusted it not to lean back. Whether they are many organisms or one metabolism folded through the region until it looks like a population is not a question the country answers. Individuals, here, are a local habit of counting, and counting does not survive contact.

The breakdown is not fresh. That is the part that takes longest to accept, because the daylight keeps offering you a readable world. Shadows fall a few degrees off the sun, consistently, as if illumination and the dark it casts had signed separate treaties and then kept them. A glass spire you passed on the way out is not absent on the way back; it is joined by another that the stone around it has already weathered to match, patina continuous, no raw break, as though the second spire had always participated in the erosion. The arc between two seams is sometimes also an arc between a seam and a point in the air where no seam is, and the rock under that empty point is glazed anyway. None of this flickers. None of it apologizes. Reality failed here long enough ago that the failure has been worked, the way glass is worked, past the stage of shards and into a grammar. The impossible has a patina. It is in its own state.

Walk long enough and the rules become as plain as the light, which is to say plain and not wholly reliable. You will not find water if you descend; descent only thickens the heat and the hiss. You will not find shade that is merely shade; dark cracks are often vents, and the dark in them is occupied. You cannot walk out of a presence by crossing what looks like its edge. Edges are courtesies. You can only change which flank you are crossing, and the sound will tell you sooner than the eye, because the sound does not respect the rim. Nothing hunts in any way that involves speed. Attention, if that is the word, moves at the speed of the plates. A change in the thump's interval, a thickening of the hiss, a thermal leaning a degree toward the place you stand: that is as close as the country comes to noticing you, and it may not be notice. It may be the engine accounting for a new small heat in its slow sum.

By late light the sinter basins brighten as if they held something, and hold nothing. The standing arcs go from nearly invisible to stitched and exact. The glass blades take a color that is almost green and is only iron and thickness. Somewhere a plate seats itself. The stroke comes up through the ground, through the chest, and from no direction. Then the long interval begins again, the same interval, the country continuing its production of itself, loud, hot, high, empty of everything except the few vast errors that have lived here long enough to become the ecology, and weathered enough that the breakdown no longer looks like an emergency. It looks like the land.
