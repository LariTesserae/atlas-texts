The floor has forgotten it was ever a river. What remains is a broad, cool vault, low enough that the roof feels like weather and wide enough that the far wall is only a change in the dimness. The stone has been worked down to almost nothing by a water that is no longer here: gentle swells, long pavements of pale mineral, salt in thin crusts that never wet the hand. You can walk for a long time and not meet a slope worth naming.

There is no stream, no seep, no dark pool in the low places. The air is dry in the mouth and cool against the skin, the kind of cool that has sat in stone for longer than seasons. Whatever moisture the old galleries once held has gone into the living things and stayed there. The ground itself gives nothing back.

Growth stands in islands. Between them the salt-pavement runs empty, scratched only by the long polish of feet and by hairline cracks that no longer carry anything. Where the growth rises it is thick: waist-high mats of a gray-green, jointed plant that looks more like bundled reed than leaf, and taller stands of the same stuff, branching into dull fans. It is lush in the way a hayfield is lush after the color has left it — dense, soft-edged, heavy — but it does not shine. Break a stem and the break is moist, faintly sweet, and the moisture does not run. The plants keep what they have. They grow so slowly that a fallen fan will lie among the living ones for years and still match them.

The city is built of the same patience. It is not ruin and it is not toolwork. Low walls of cemented grit and plant-fiber run in loose grids across the swells, never high, never straight for long, repaired where they slump by the same small jaws that first raised them. There are towers, but they are mounds: broad at the base, vented with finger-wide holes, spaced so far apart that from one you can see three others and a great deal of empty floor. Streets are only the lanes the mats have not taken, packed hard and pale. No metal, no cut block, no lamp, no wheel. The organization is the organization of a colony that has had geological time to become a town: wards, granaries of dried stem, nurseries sunk a little into the cooler stone, and wide plazas where nothing is built because nothing needs to be.

The animals are everywhere and nowhere crowded. Small, pale, many-legged things move in the mats with a dry tick. Larger ones, cat-sized, low to the ground, travel the lanes in ones and twos, stopping to strip a fan and carry the pieces home. In the roof-dark, flocks of swift flyers turn and turn, never settling for long, their bodies the only quick motion in a place that otherwise changes by the decade. You rarely see a mass of them. You hear all of them.

The sound is the size of the vault. It is not a roar from one throat. It is a layered, continuous weather: the high stitching of the small ones in the mats, the wooden clack of the mound-builders at their vents, the thin screaming of the flyers multiplied by every curve of stone until it has no source. Close your eyes and it is a single pressure. Open them and the floor is still mostly empty, the mats still islands, the nearest mover still a small shape a long way off. The echoes do the crowding. Speech, if you tried it, would not carry as words. The stone has already filled that space.

Light comes from nowhere you can point to. A gray leak, perhaps from cracks in a roof too thick and too old to show them, lies on the salt and dies in the mats. Shadows are soft and short of contrast. Nothing flashes. Nothing is fully dark except the mouths of the mound-vents and the undersides of the fans. In that half-light the city reads as a repeating calm: wall, gap, mound, gap, grove, a long reach of bare pavement, then the same again, smaller with distance, until the gallery bends and the pattern continues out of sight.

Nothing hurries. A mound grows by a hand’s width in a lifetime of the things that build it, and their lifetimes are not short. The mats advance and retreat across the pavements on a cycle longer than memory, leaving pale scars where they stood. The flyers’ paths are old paths. Even the deafening has a slow body to it: it swells and thins across hours, never breaking, never starting, as if the vault has been sounding in this same key since the last water left and the stone lay down to be flat.
