The height is not a summit. It is a condition the land has kept so long that the memory of anything lower has been planed out of it. What you stand on is a shield the size of its own weather: pale, lifted, and old, its nearer edge a day’s walk of nothing in particular and its farther edge not an edge at all, only the place where sight fails in clean air. The forms are settled. Ridges have lost their arguments with the wind. Basins are shallow enough to miss until you are in them, and then they are only a change in the grain underfoot and a slightly longer shadow at your heel.

There is no water. Not as a hidden seam, not as a night film, not as a cloud that might decide otherwise. The cuts that cross the shield look, at a distance, like drainages, but their floors are paved with sorted mineral — grains the size of seeds, locked under a skin of silica and salt that set when the last volatiles left and were never replaced. Walk them and they answer in small dry clicks, then forget you. The wind uses them as troughs. Nothing else does.

The light is hard and continuous, a high-altitude brightness with the heat mostly gone out of it. Thin air takes the warmth as fast as the ground receives it, so noon can glare and still be cool against the face; the sun is a white coin, shadows blue and exact, and every pebble throws a little darkness sharp enough to trip the eye. Color, where it exists, is not lush in the wet sense. It is saturated anyway: bone, ash, a cold yellow in the salt crust, and then — far apart — the metal-green of the stands.

Those are the vegetation, and they are the contradiction the place has learned to hold. Between them the shield is empty at the scale of a walk. Within them, growth is thick, waxy, almost indecent in its abundance. Each stand is a dozen individuals or a hundred, never a forest that closes overhead, never a meadow you wade. A single plant is a column of stacked shields, leaves as thick as hide, outer faces silvered so they throw the sun back, inner folds nearly black where a pigment drinks what the silver refuses. They do not wait for rain. They take mineral dust on their skins and work it, slowly, into bulk. Break a leaf and what beads out is not sap in any watery meaning: a clear oil, slow, unfrozen at this cold, smelling faintly of crushed stone and something sweet that has no orchard to belong to. By afternoon the shields have tilted. By evening they have tilted back. The motion is too slow to watch and obvious if you look away and look again.

Under everything else, at the scale of a hand, the ground is not bare. A living pavement — lichen, or something that occupies lichen’s office — coats the crust in a continuous nap, gray-green, millimeters deep, lush only when you kneel. It is the true vegetation of the shield, continent-wide, and it is what keeps the erosion honest. Where it holds, wind polishes and does not cut. Where a hoof or a collapse opens it, the wind worries the breach into a pan, and the pan deepens until its own rim gives shelter, and the pavement seals the wound and the form stops changing. That is the balance. Nothing here is young. Nothing here is finished in the way a ruin is finished. The land is in its own state, and has been long enough that catastrophe and repair have the same patience.

Weather belongs to the shield rather than arriving from elsewhere. Days begin in a stillness so complete that the oil-leaves do not tick. Then a katabatic thread finds a swale and moves down it, invisible except as a darker sorting of dust along the ground. By late morning the dust has lofted into sheets, not clouds — lenticular veils of mineral, dry, sometimes ringed with a pale glory where the sun takes them edge-on. They dim nothing for long. They reorganize the light, lay brief bands of cooler shadow across stands kilometers apart, and settle again as a finer powder on the silver leaves, which is a kind of feeding. There are no storms of water. There are seasons of wind, and seasons when the wind seems to have stepped off the shield entirely and left the brightness to ring.

Sound follows that vacancy. The loudest thing most hours is your own step, and after a while you stop trusting it, because the crust sometimes answers and sometimes does not, and the difference has no visible cause. In a stand, if you wait, a leaf shifts against another with a sound like a thumbnail on parchment. Far off, once, a pan of loose grit slumps under its own graded weight and goes quiet in the same breath. Animals are the exception that proves how seldom anything with a body crosses here. Tracks appear that were not there at dawn: three-toed, wide-set, pressed just deep enough to craze the pavement and no deeper, running the empty distances between stands as if the stands were stations on a route measured in weeks. Whatever makes them is large, spare, and almost never seen — a pale flank once, at the limit of the glare, moving with the economy of something that cannot afford to sweat and has arranged not to. Smaller life exists mostly as suggestion: a husk like a seed-case that is not a seed, waxed shut, tumbled into the lee of a leaf and already being sanded thin. Nothing cries. Nothing hums in any number. If there are wings, they are elsewhere, or they make no sound the air will carry.

Death here is abrasion and drying, not rot. A fallen column lies for years as itself, silver gone dull, oil soaked into the grit beneath it in a dark oval that the pavement is slow to cross. When the pavement does cross, the oval remains as a richer nap, and the next stand, if one comes, roots a little nearer that stain than chance would like. Seeds — or whatever the shields release, hard bright grains that ring when they strike stone — travel farther than any animal. A single wind-week can move them across a weather of their own. Most land on pavement and are polished to nothing. A few land in a breach and become, after a human lifetime or several, another scatter of metal-green on the pale shield.

There is no made thing. No cairn, no cut, no glass that is not a mineral face. The brightness is not a lamp. The rules are only the ones the place keeps for itself: that height and cold and dryness can persist together at continental scale; that lushness can be a property of bodies rather than of cover; that emptiness between those bodies is not failure but the distance the chemistry requires; that weather can be active and still almost silent; that a land eroded into balance will look, to a new eye, as if nothing has ever happened to it, which is the opposite of the truth. Stand long enough and the stands tilt a fraction you cannot measure. The dust-veil thins. The tracks, if you walk back, have already begun to lose their edges to the same wind that made the morning move. The shield continues, bright, cool, and unconcerned, large enough that your departure does not register as weather.
