The basin holds its shape the way a bowl holds water: low, open, and full. From the outer rim you can see across it—several hours’ walk of roofs, lanes, and the pale mineral ground between them—but you cannot see out. The horizon is the city’s own edge, where the last walls give onto flats of the same raw stone the builders cut. Nothing older than the city has been worn smooth. The rock still shows the grain it had when it first lay exposed: jointed, glassy in places, sharp at the corners, the color of wet ash and warm bone. Rain and springs have not yet had time, or have not been allowed time, to round it. Edges stay edges. Dust does not accumulate. What falls is taken up, packed into mortar, or simply never settles, because the air itself is busy.

The air is warm and close, not hot, and it carries water the way a cloth carries dye. It does not rain so much as weep. Moisture beads on every underside—lintels, the bellies of eaves, the cool faces of standing stones—and runs in thin, constant threads into gutters that were cut before the walls went up. In the lanes the water is ankle-deep in the middle and dry only where feet have packed a ridge of clay and crushed shell. Under the city the water table stands so near the surface that a post driven a forearm deep comes up dark and smelling of iron. Foundations are not dug; they are set on the wet stone and weighted, because a trench fills before it can be finished. The people build as if the ground were a skin over a bladder, and they are not wrong.

There are no plants. No moss on the north sides, no weed in the cracks, no reed at the margins of the standing water. The wet stone stays bare. What grows is not growth in the green sense. Across the open pools and along every gutter there is a film, copper-brown and gray-green, slick under the hand, neither leaf nor root: a skin of minute life that feeds on the minerals the springs keep bringing up. It does not rise. It sheets. Children scrape it with wooden blades into baskets, and it is food, or the start of food—the base the rest of the city eats through other mouths.

The mouths are everywhere, and they are loud.

The sound does not rise and fall. It holds. From the first waking moment to the last it is the same pressure in the ear: the slap of water against stone, the click and grind of hand-mills, the cries of animals packed into pens and onto roofs and into the shallows, human voices pitched to carry over all of it and therefore never soft. There is no quiet hour. Night does not thin the noise; it only changes which animals are calling. The city has arranged itself so that every necessary sound happens all the time—milling, hauling, the turning of wooden wheels in races cut shallow into the rock—because a pause would let water back up, or let a pen go unwatched, or let the film in a pool be stripped by something that got there first. Equilibrium here is not stillness. It is a volume that never drops.

Light is poor even at midday. The sky over the basin is a low, even lid, warm-gray, bright enough to throw no real shadow and dim enough that color drains out of things at twenty paces. Lamps are not much used outdoors. Indoors, in the windowless lower rooms where stores are kept dry on raised clay platforms, people work by slits and by the reflected sheen off wet walls. Stone that has never weathered takes a polish quickly under hands and feet, and the lanes, in the dim, look oiled.

The buildings are dense and low and almost touching. Timber is scarce—there are no trees—so most of what stands is stone lifted in blocks no larger than a person can roll, set with clay, shell-lime, and the pounded mineral film dried into a binder. Roofs are split flags overlapped like scales, weighed with more stone, because wind, when it comes, comes across open ground with nothing to break it. Lanes are narrow enough that two people with baskets turn sideways. Upper stories, where they exist, are reached by ladders lashed with hide and fiber from animals, not from plants; the fiber is tendon, gut, and strips of skin cured stiff. Nothing metal shows except the rare small blade, and those are hoarded, resharpened until they are wires, passed down rather than replaced. Tools are stone, bone, fired clay, and wood so old and so carefully mended that a single beam may be older than the house it holds. The city does not invent new forms. It repeats the ones that already keep the water moving and the animals in.

And the animals fill every volume the people do not.

In the shallows between house-rows, fish the length of a hand school so thick the water looks granular, feeding on the film and on each other. Wading birds—long-legged, gray, voiceless in the general roar only because every other voice is louder—stand in ranks along the pool edges and stab downward without pause. Their numbers do not vary by season. There is no season. On the roofs and in the rafters, colonies of small bats and of a leathery, day-flying thing like a bat that is not a bat cling shoulder to shoulder, dropping guano in sheets that people collect before it can foul the drinking cisterns. That guano, and the scraped film, and the bodies of fish and birds, are the fields. There is no soil worth the name. Fertility is a circuit: mineral spring, film, animal, waste, mineral again, and the people standing in the circuit with baskets and nets and clubs.

The pens are not at the edge of town. There is no edge in that sense; the city and its animals are the same fabric from the center out to the rim. Low walls of unshaped rock hold things that look, in the dim, like goats and are not quite goats—compact, horned, warm-blooded, feeding on film scraped into troughs and on the boiled remains of fish. They are loud. They shove. Their hooves on wet stone add a constant clatter to the water-sound. Between pens, narrower animals—something like otters, something like large rats—move in the water itself, tolerated because they keep the races clear of carcasses and because their pelts, when taken, are the warm clothing the climate almost does not need and the bindings the climate’s damp would otherwise rot. Flies and their kin are a haze at mouth-height. People wrap cloth across the lower face not for modesty but because the air is edible and they prefer not to eat it by accident.

Nothing is arriving and nothing is leaving. The basin does not drain to a river you can see; the water that weeps in also sinks, somewhere under the raw stone, and returns as spring, same warmth, same iron taste. The animals do not migrate. Birth replaces death at a rate the pens and the nets can hold, and the rate does not swing. A count taken on any day matches a count taken on any other, within the error of a person shouting numbers over the noise. Houses are repaired with the same stones. A wall that falls is set back in the same line by the same hands or by hands taught the same line. Children learn the lanes by sound more than by sight: the mill-race on the left, the bird-colony roof on the right, the place where the spring under the market square makes a higher note because the channel narrows.

The market is not a clearing. It is a density inside the density—stalls of lashed bone and clay, no canvas, goods set on stone: dried fish in stacks, cakes of film pressed and fired until they keep, bundles of gut-cord, lamp-fat in clay bulbs, blades of knapped stone wrapped in hide so they do not chip against their neighbors. People bargain at a shout. The price of a fish does not change. It has not changed. Memory in this place is short on events and long on procedure, because events do not occur in a way that would mark a year. There is no harvest moon, no flood season, no first frost. Warmth holds. Water holds. The gray lid of sky holds. A person can walk the full width of the basin—from the spring-square, through the fish-lanes, past the roof-colonies and the outer pens, to the last course of walls—and find the same arrangement repeating at a slightly lower pitch of activity, never a different kind. Beyond the last wall the stone continues, jointed and sharp and wet, filmed in the low places, empty of buildings for a little way, and then, if you kept walking, you would only meet weather that does not vary and ground that has not yet decided to become anything but what it already is. No one keeps walking. There is nothing out there the baskets can use, and the noise of the city, which at the rim is still loud, is the only signal that says where the work is.

At the center, if center is the right word for a place without a monument, the largest spring rises through a cut in the raw rock and sheets outward under a ring of milling platforms. Wooden wheels, pegged and banded with hide, turn in the flow without stopping. They have turned since they were set. Someone is always at them, not because the work ends and must be restarted, but because the work is the condition of being fed. Grain is not what they grind. They grind dried film, dried fish, bone for meal, clay for slip. The paste that comes off the stones is warm from friction and from the water, and it smells of iron and of the sea the basin has never seen. People eat it with salt scraped from the rims of the shallowest pools, where the constant weep and the constant warmth leave a crust that renews as fast as it is taken. Salt, like everything else, does not run out and does not accumulate. Hands know exactly how much to lift.

If you stand still in a lane—hard to do; someone will need the space—you feel the city as a single warm animal with too many hearts: water moving under the soles, breath and wingbeat and hoofbeat in the air, stone still new under all of it, edges not yet taught to be old. The dim light makes distances soft. The sound does not. It tells you, without pause and without news, that every pool is occupied, every wheel is turning, every roof holds weight, and the ground, which has only just become ground, is already entirely spoken for.
