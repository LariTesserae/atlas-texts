The height does not announce itself. There is no peak to stand under and no drop to fear at the first step. The land has already finished falling. What remains is a warm, pale table of stone, so long exposed that its edges have been argued away, and the sky sits on it with the ordinary weight of midday.

From a distance it reads as one surface. Closer, it is a pavement of old plates, each one the color of bone left in sun, fitted so tightly that the joints are only darker threads. Nothing here is sharp anymore. Even the low rises have been planed into suggestions. A person could walk for a long time and believe the world had been pressed flat on purpose, and only later notice that the flatness itself has grain: a slow tilt toward a basin no wider than a town, a seam that has been a seam since before anything living had a name for water, a place where the stone is slightly softer underfoot because something once stood in it and then became it.

The air is warm without glare. Light falls straight and untheatrical, the kind of day that does not bother to be beautiful. It shows dust on the wrist, the faint mica in the rock, the way every hollow holds a thinner color. Shadows are short and honest. There is no weather moving across the sky worth following. What moisture exists does not arrive as weather so often as it condenses out of patience. At the cool undersides of ledges, on the night-facing lips of cracks, water beads and does not run. By late morning most of it is gone again, leaving a dark breath on the stone and a smell like metal warmed in a hand. In the basin the damp lasts longer. There the plates sag by a hand's width, and a skin of wet gathers in the joints, never enough to be called a pool, enough that a finger comes away shining.

Plants are an exception the land tolerates. A grass no taller than a knuckle grows where a crack has collected grit. It is the color of old rope. Here and there a shrub stands with its leaves reduced to scales, casting a shadow smaller than itself. Nothing makes a thicket. The green, such as it is, is scattered the way missed nails are scattered on a workshop floor: you notice one, then, looking for it, find that there have always been more, and still the floor is mostly floor.

What fills the place is not vegetation. The stone is occupied.

At first the occupation looks mineral. Nodules. Lichen-dark freckles. Low domes no higher than an ankle, spaced as if someone had set them out and then forgotten the measuring. Many of them are warm. Not sun-warm. Body-warm, the dull heat of something that has been holding still long enough to be mistaken for geology. Their skins are continuous with the pavement: the same bone color, the same polish of age, except that along a seam a slow pulse travels, a darkening that takes the length of a breath and longer, and then the surface is stone again. They do not flock. They do not flee. They are simply numerous, and the numerousness becomes the character of the ground. In some stretches you cannot put a foot down without choosing among them.

Between the domes, the true small life moves, and even that movement is reluctant. A carpet of things like seeds, except that seeds do not reorient themselves toward the last of the condensate. They flow by a method closer to settling than to walking: a million minute adjustments, a slow migration toward the damp joint and away from the open plate, silent enough that the ear keeps insisting the plain is empty. Stand long enough and the pattern changes. The dark threads in the stone are not all cracks. Some of them are rivers of bodies, narrow as a pen-stroke, traveling at the speed of cooling.

Larger animals use the domes as the domes use the stone. Low-shouldered grazers, if grazing is the word for what they do to a crust that may itself be halfway alive, stand with their heads down and do not lift them when approached. Their hides have taken on the pavement's color so completely that a herd at rest is only a change in the rhythm of the ground. They breathe on a long interval. Between breaths the quiet returns so thoroughly that the last breath seems like something you imagined. Their mouths work sideways, patient, pulling at the black freckles and the beaded film. Where they have fed, the stone shows a paler kiss, and within a day the freckles have begun to close over it again. Nothing here is in a hurry to be eaten or to heal. The accounts balance across seasons, not afternoons.

Birds exist, but they do not behave like birds of lower countries. They stand. On the domes, on the grazers' backs, on the rare shrub, they hold their wings in the posture of things that have forgotten flight is for more than a correction. When one does lift, it is a short, flat transit to the next warm mound, and the sound it makes is not a call but the soft slap of air, already gone. There are more of them than the quiet can explain. The eye, once it learns the shape, finds them everywhere: a dark notch on every third dome, a row along a grazer's spine like stitching. They do not sing. Perhaps there is nothing in this country that answers.

The basin holds the damp and therefore holds the thickest life. There the domes crowd until they touch, and the touching has made them irregular, lobed, fused along old contacts into low ridges that wind without destination. Water films the contacts and does not sink. A smell rises, not foul, not sweet: the smell of warm clay and a long-closed room opened at noon. Things live in the film that do not live on the open plates. They are translucent, coin-sized, and they travel under the water-skin without breaking it, so that the surface wrinkles and then forgets. At the basin's center the plates have been ground, by nothing visible, into a shallow dish of powder. The powder is damp to the depth of a fingernail. Tracks cross it and do not match any of the feet you can see. The tracks are broad, and they are old in the way this whole country is old: not yesterday's, not this season's, simply the shape the powder prefers, redrawn so slowly that a single crossing might take longer than a watcher's patience.

Above the basin, on the side the light leaves first, the land remembers a different use. Not buildings. Nothing here was quarried or mortared. But there are arrangements. Circles of domes that have died and kept their shapes, pale rings a little higher than the living ones, their warmth gone, their seams sealed. Inside two of the rings the stone is smoother, as if bodies had turned there for a great many years and the turning had been a kind of lathe. A third ring is broken on its western side, and through the break a path of paler freckles runs out onto the plain and thins and vanishes. Whatever used these places finished with them. The rings are not ruins in the human sense. They are the final posture of a crowd.

Farther out, where the table begins its imperceptible sag toward a lower country you cannot see from here, single domes stand apart from the mass, larger, cracked at the crown. From the cracks a slow paste emerges and hardens in the light into fins no thicker than a leaf. The fins ring if tapped, a small ceramic note, and then the quiet puts itself back. Inside one crack, if you look without leaning too close, the paste is moving. It extrudes, stops, skins over, and the skin is already the color of the pavement. Given enough years the fin will be stone, and the dome will be a low rise, and the rise will be planed away, and the table will be flat again, which is what it has been practicing all this time.

The rules of the place are simple and not kind in any personal way. Warmth stays in whatever can hold it. Water does not travel; it appears, is used, and is gone. Anything that moves quickly is rare enough to be an event, and the country does not produce events for an audience. Sound is a leakage, not a habit. The overwhelming fact is number: number of bodies, number of slow processes sharing the same sunlit inch, number of years already spent arriving at this surface. You can cross it in a day and meet no one who will mark your crossing. You can stop, and within an hour the seed-things will have adjusted around your boots, and a bird will have added you to the inventory of warm objects, and the plain will be as quiet as when you arrived.

By late light the condensate begins again on the undersides, a darkening that is not shadow. The domes hold their heat. The grazers do not lift their heads. Somewhere in the powder a track lengthens by the width of a finger, and no one hears it. The table of stone, ancient and nearly finished, continues its long work of being flat, while everything that lives on it continues, just as patiently, the work of not being stone yet.
