The plateau has not yet learned how to be old.

It rose. Not in any memory that still walks it, and not in any memory the stone keeps either, except as a grain in the grain: a shove from underneath so recent that the fracture faces still hold their first color. The rock is a pale, almost metallic grey-green where it has not been covered, and it breaks in plates the size of weather. Edges have not rounded. Spurs stand up like unfinished tools. Screes at the foot of the scarps are thin, a single generation of shatter, and already something is living in the gaps between the shards, knitting them before they can settle into the long slide that older mountains know.

From any one ridge the next ridge is visible, and the one after that, and then the air itself becomes the limit, not the land. The plateau does not decline toward a coast inside a day’s looking. It generates its own sky. Clouds form on it and die on it. They do not arrive from elsewhere. In the mornings a skin of condensation beads on every upward face and on every living surface, and by the time the light is full the beads have been taken. There are no rivers worth the name. The stone is too new and too broken for a drainage to have agreed on itself; water moves as film, as rime, as brief sheets after the short rains that come sideways out of cloud the plateau made an hour earlier and will unmake by dusk. Pockets persist in the joints of the scarps, black and still, warm only relative to the air, and around each of them the growth thickens until the water is no longer visible, only the sound of something drinking in a crowd.

It is cold in the way high places are cold when the sun does not care. The light is excessive. It comes from a sky that has been thinned by altitude until color has nowhere to hide, a hard white-blue that throws no mercy and, strangely, throws very little shadow. Shadows here are short and stained, as if the brightness were not only falling but also being returned from the living cover, a low continuous glare off wet leaf, off shell, off the pale rinds of whatever passes for bark. You can see too far and too clearly. Distance does not soften. A scarp eighty miles off is as sharp as the one under your hand, and both of them are occupied to the last crack.

The vegetation is not forest and not tundra and not anything that keeps a courteous spacing. It is a continuous upholstery. Low, dense, interlocked, a felt of blades and pads and jointed stems that never exceed the height of a crouching body and never leave a palm’s width of bare stone once they have claimed it. They root in grit, in each other, in the damp film. Their greens are dark and slightly oily, and among them run veins of a brighter, almost mineral yellow where some other growth has plated itself over the first, feeding on what the first excretes. Nothing here is in a hurry to be tall. Height is a liability in wind that has a continent to run on, and the wind does run, but slowly, a pressure more than a gust, hours long, bending the whole mat in one direction until the light on it changes phase, like a field of metal turning.

Under the mat, and in it, and above it in a layer no thicker than the mat itself, is the rest of the occupation.

There is no room. That is the first fact the body learns, before cold, before brightness. Every surface holds something that is doing work. The small things are a grit of life: armored specks that click when the mat flexes, larvae sealed in resin blisters along the stems, threads of a pale animal-fungus that moves by fractions of an inch between one rain and the next and leaves a track like a hairline crack healing. The medium things are the ones you would step on if there were anywhere to step that was not already a back or a nest or a feeding lane. Flat, many-legged, the color of wet slate, they travel in sheets. Their legs are short and numerous and they do not hurry; a sheet may take a day to cross a single joint in the rock, and while it crosses it is grazing the yellow veins, reducing them, and laying down behind it a paste that hardens into a crust the next generation of pads will root in. They sound, up close, like dry seeds poured slowly from a height. At the scale of a slope they sound like a mill.

The large things do not look like animals until they move, and they move so gradually that the first impression is furniture, or geology that has made a mistake. Lichen-grey masses the size of sheds, anchored, humped, their hides plated in the same mineral yellow as the veins. They are not plants. Along their flanks run apertures that open and close on a cycle of many minutes, and from the apertures comes a breath that smells of hot metal and crushed green, and a low continuous tone, more felt in the teeth than heard. They are processing. Stone dust, dragged up by smaller lives and delivered in lines so worn they have become troughs in the mat, goes in at one end of each mass. What comes out, over hours, is a thread of glassy filament, still warm, which other organisms catch and weave into the mat as reinforcement, or which simply falls and is eaten before it cools. There are thousands of these masses on a single scarp face. There are scarp faces beyond counting. No one built them. They grew into the work, or the work grew them; on a land this new there has not been time for a distinction to matter.

Higher, only relatively, are the things that fly, or that occupy the air the way the sheets occupy the ground. They are not many in kind. They are past counting in number. The commonest are narrow, almost winged blades of gristle and membrane, no longer than a forearm, and they do not soar. They hang in the wind the plateau makes and they vibrate, each one a small engine burning the sugars of the mat and the static of the dry cold air. Singly the vibration is a buzz at the edge of hearing. Together, over a valley that is only a sharper fold in the plateau, they are a roar with no source and no edge, a factory room the size of a province. They move when they must, in slow veils, tracking the brightness, thickening where the light is hardest and thinning, never vanishing, where a cloud the land itself exhaled throws a brief dimness. Their droppings are a fine dust that glitters and is gone into the mat before it can accumulate. Nothing accumulates. Everything is taken.

At night, which is not dark so much as less bright, the apertures on the great masses glow a dull red from the work inside, and the hanging blades dim their vibration and lock to the stems, and the sound drops from roar to a vast uneasy ticking, the planet’s machinery idling. Condensation returns. The sheets continue. They do not sleep on any schedule a visitor would recognize. Sleep, if it happens, happens inside the continuity, a fraction of each body always in motion so that the lane is never broken.

There are no structures. No walls, no roads, no cleared lines except the feeding troughs, which close behind the sheets as the mat heals. No tools left lying, because the tools are bodies and the bodies are in use. What reads as industry is a metabolism that has reached a size where it performs the operations industry would recognize: extraction, transport, heat, extrusion, a division of process so complete that no single organism completes a task, only a step, and the steps tile the continent. The electricity, if it is fair to call it that, lives in the vibrating blades and in certain ridges of the young rock where quartz was shocked into alignment by the uplift and now sheds charge into any wet life that bridges the plates. Storms, when they come, are brief and local and vicious, and afterward the mat smells of ozone and cooked sugar, and the sheets divert, slowly, toward the strike points, which are rich for a day.

The rules are simple and not kind. Nothing is allowed to be empty. Nothing is allowed to be fast. A fall of rock, and the young stone still falls, white and sharp and loud as a shot that echoes for a minute and then is swallowed, will be colonized at the margins before the dust has settled in the joints, and fully occupied before the next rain. A body that stops being useful is not mourned and not buried. It is entered. The apertures of the great masses accept meat as readily as dust; the sheets do not distinguish. The plateau does not keep relics. It keeps throughput.

Stand long enough and the brightness begins to feel like a material, something laid over the eyes in sheets. The sound separates into layers: the mill of the sheets, the organ-tone of the masses, the high industrial roar of the air, and under all of it a slower beat, hours apart, which is only the mat itself flexing as the long wind leans and releases, the whole continent breathing on a cycle too patient to watch and too large to be anywhere else. Far off, a scarp you have been using as a horizon shifts its glare by a degree. Another mass has opened. Another thread of glass is being born into the cold, and something is already waiting to take it, and the waiting has been going on since the stone was lifted, and will go on, at this same unhurried saturation, until the edges finally soften and the water finally learns the way down, and some other age, slower even than this one, begins.
