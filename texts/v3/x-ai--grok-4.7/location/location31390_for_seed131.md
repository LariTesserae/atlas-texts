The noise is already there before the room is. It does not arrive. It occupies the stair, the last landing, the short passage whose ceiling has been lowered by a run of lagged pipe, and it occupies them at the same loudness it will keep on the far side of the door. Nothing in it rises or falls. It is not an alarm and not a rhythm you can tap. It is the sound of a machine that has been doing one thing for so long that the doing has worn smooth, a broad pressurized roar with a thinner metallic edge inside it, as if sheet steel were being bowed and released a thousand times a second and had forgotten how to ring as anything but one note.

The door is iron, painted so many times the paint has become a skin, and the skin has been polished by sleeves. The latch is gone. What remains is a tongue of metal worn oval, warm under the hand. When it gives, the room does not get louder. The passage had already been full. What changes is that the sound acquires a source, and the source is small.

It is a low room, and it is not large. Four strides would take you from the threshold to the far wall if the floor allowed it. The ceiling is close enough that a tall person would know it without looking up: a grid of shallow beams, once sharp, now rounded at every arris, painted the same exhausted gray-green as the walls. Between the beams sit the lamps. They are ordinary, glass and metal, and they burn at a steady white that does not flicker. Their light is the light of a workshop at midmorning, neither kind nor unkind, and it reaches every corner. There is no shadow deep enough to hide in. Dust does not drift in it. The air is moving too hard and too evenly for dust.

Under that light the room is mostly vacant volume. The emptiness is the first fact of the place after the noise, and it is not abandonment in the usual sense. It is designed clearance. Whatever once stood in the middle has been gone longer than the paint remembers. What remains has withdrawn to the edges and simplified itself there, the way a stone in a stream loses its corners and keeps only the form the water will permit.

Along the left wall, and turning the corner to run under the far wall, is a basin. It is concrete, or was. The lip has been rounded to the radius of a thumb, the corners blown out into slow curves, the inner face stained in permanent bands the color of tea and iron. Water stands in it to a line that does not vary. You can see the line because it is darker than the concrete above it and lighter than the concrete below, a horizon drawn by centuries of the same depth. The surface is not still. It is trout-backed with small standing waves driven by whatever turns beneath the housing at the near end, a low rectangular casing of cast metal whose bolts have been planed flush with their flanges. No nameplate remains. The seams weep. Not in pulses: in a constant thin sheet, as if the metal had become slightly porous and accepted that as its final state. The sheet breaks into drops only where an old flange interrupts it, and the drops fall into the basin at a rate that matches, exactly, the rate at which a throat at the far end swallows water and sends it back. Nothing accumulates. Nothing drains down. The level is a law.

The housing is warm. The basin wall is warm. The air is warm in the way a laundry is warm, not hot, not fresh, a warmth that has nowhere else to go and has stopped trying. It carries water. Not fog — the lamps would show fog — but a heaviness on the skin, a film that forms on the wrist and does not cool. Every vertical surface above the basin glistens in a fine even sweat. The sweat does not run. It stands, and is replaced, and is replaced.

There is no soil, and there is no stone that was not poured or cut and set. The floor beyond the basin is plate, diamond tread worn down to a suggestion, a faint crosshatch you feel more than see, slick where the weep from the housing migrates and is squeegeed back by the same unending draft. The walls are lined. Behind the paint, if paint can be said to be in front of anything anymore, is more plate, riveted, the rivet heads reduced to coins. In the corner where basin meets basin, a stack of manufactured trays has been built into the wall like a staircase that was never meant to be climbed. The trays are the same metal as the housing. Their lips are gone. Each holds a dark fibrous medium that is not earth: a pressed mat, black-brown, fibrous as felt, engineered once to hold moisture and minerals and never since renewed, because it has not needed to be. It is saturated to a precise degree and has been saturated to that degree since before the paint lost its edges.

Out of the mats the growth comes, and it is thick, and it does not trespass.

Ferns, mostly. Not a wild assortment — a few kinds, repeated, as if the room admitted only what could live on warm metal, constant wet, and ordinary unchanging light. Their fronds are large for the trays. They arch out over the basin and stop where the moving air will not let them drink more than the trays provide. The undersides are pale, beaded, and the beads do not fall; they gather and return. Between the ferns, a mat of small round leaves, dark and waxed, packed so tightly the medium does not show. On the wall above the upper tray a vine has taken the paint for a trellis. It is not climbing toward anything. It has reached the length the room allows and turned back along itself, a green rope with leaves at regular intervals, some of them resting on the warm plate as if the plate were bark. Nothing is in flower. Nothing is yellowing. New fiddleheads stand among fully opened fronds in a ratio that does not shift from one visit to the next, if visits can be said to happen. Growth and drop are equal. A frond that browns does so somewhere you are not watching, and is replaced by the time you look, or else the browning is slow enough to be a property of the green rather than an event.

The plants are the lushness, and they are also the proof of how empty the room is. They occupy surfaces. They do not occupy air. Between the arch of the nearest fern and the housing, between the vine and the lamps, there is nothing: no web, no hanging root, no clutter of tools, no cable that sags. A single conduit runs the length of the ceiling beam and disappears into the wall, straight, simplified, its hangers worn to hooks. You could pass an arm through most of the room and touch only warmth and moving air.

Under the sound there is, if you insist, the water: a smaller hiss where the sheet leaves the housing, a folded gulp at the throat. They do not compete with the roar. They are manners inside it. Your own breath is inaudible. A word, if you spent one, would be spent into the metal and not returned. The roar has no direction you can face away from. It comes off the plate, off the water, off the close ceiling. It has sanded the air the way the water has sanded the concrete. After a minute the body stops bracing for a change in it. There will not be a change. The machine is not cycling. It is not loading and unloading. It is holding a pressure, or a temperature, or a level — holding the room itself at the only state the room has left — and the holding is loud because everything else that might have been loud has already been worn away.

No insect crosses the lamps. No track shows in the film on the plate, and the film would show one. At the throat, where the water turns down, there is sometimes a slight irregularity, a soft displacement no larger than a thumb, gone before it can be called a shape. It may be a trick of the standing waves. If it is an animal it has been here as long as the line on the concrete, and it does not surface, and it does not need to. Nothing hunts in the room. Nothing is hunted. The ferns are not browsed. The air smells of warm wet metal and of the clean green of leaves that are never crushed, a smell with no rot in it, because rot would be a change and the room has finished with change.

The rules are simple because they have had time to become simple.

Water enters only as weep and leaves only as swallow, and the difference is zero. Heat enters through the housing and leaves through the plate and the passage, and the difference is a warmth you stop noticing. Light is on because the lamps are part of the same holding; they do not have a night. The plants drink what the mats are given and return it from the fronds, and the mats neither shrink nor swell. The sound does not describe an event. It describes a maintained fact. If you stay, you will not see the room become something else. You will see it continue to be the thing it wore itself down to: a pocket at the bottom of a made world, low, wet, green at the edges, hollow in the middle, loud with the effort of remaining exactly as it is.

The passage behind you still roars at the same pitch. Closing the door, if the tongue will seat, changes nothing you can hear. The room does not mind the door. It has not needed an outside for longer than the bolts have had heads.
