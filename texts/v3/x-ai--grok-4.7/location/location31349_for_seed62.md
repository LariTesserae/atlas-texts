The light arrives before the valley does. It comes off the new stone as a hard, cool brightness, the kind that makes distances look rinsed and a little too exact. The ridges have not yet learned to be mountains in the old sense. They are still sharp: edges that catch and throw the day, faces of pale rock with only a thin weathering rind, joints that have not had time to round. Uplift here is recent enough that the skyline still looks assembled rather than worn. You can see, from the outer lip, how the blocks rose and tilted, how a whole floor of country was levered into the air and left there, unfinished.

Below that lip the land opens without becoming empty. It is a basin of middle size—large enough that the far wall is a separate weather, small enough that you could walk the circuit in a long day if the undergrowth allowed it, which it does not. The openness is all vertical and lateral at once: terraces stepping down, spurs cutting in, a pale central flat that might once have been a lakebed and is now only a brighter green. Nothing in it is sparse. The vegetation has taken the young stone as if the stone had been waiting for it, and the result is a saturation that reads, at first, as a single mass, then as layers, then as more layers still.

It should not be this green at this height, in air this cool. The temperature sits a little above the bite of cold and never climbs toward warmth. Breath shows in the mornings and sometimes at noon. Yet the slopes carry a forest that behaves like a lowland thicket lifted intact: broad leaves, strap leaves, fern-roofs, ropes of vine, moss that has already quilted the freshest scree. The trick is moisture without much water. Rain is occasional and uncommitted. What the basin really has is condensation. At night the clear high air dumps its little water onto every surface that can hold a bead—leaf tips, wire, stone lips, the undersides of metal. By day the brightness burns the film back to a dampness that never quite becomes a flow. There are pockets: a black pool in a joint of rock, a seep that darkens a wall for twenty paces and then forgets itself, a trough along an old foundation where water stands finger-deep and still. No river earns the name. The basin sweats, and the plants drink the sweat.

Sound does not travel here. Not muffled—absent. The leaf mass is part of it, and the lack of wind, and something in the air itself that seems to take a noise and decline to return it. A footfall is a pressure in the sole and not an event. A branch, bent and released, returns to place without a tick. Even the rare animal—if one is near—is known by displacement: a fern laid flat, a dark gap in the vines that closes as you look. Birds do not work this light. Insects, if they exist, do not announce themselves. The basin’s fauna is mostly the evidence of having been eaten around the edges, and even that is old. You can stand in the brightest part of the day and hear the blood in your own ears as the loudest thing present, and then not even that, once you stop listening for it.

What breaks the green, sparingly, is work. Not a town. Not ruins in the picturesque sense. A scatter of industrial intention left where it was set down and never gathered into a street. On one terrace a long shed of riveted plate, its roof a sawtooth of glass gone milky, still shedding condensation in slow internal rain. On another, a rank of condensers like upright combs, copper gone green, each tooth beaded, each bead finding the same path down into a collector trough that has been feeding the same pipe for years. The pipe does not gush. It delivers, patiently, into a cistern cut in the young rock, and from the cistern a second line runs—somewhere—under the roots. Elsewhere a cableway’s towers stand at intervals too wide for comfort, the cable itself gone or lying in the ferns like a dropped measure. A single stack, short, never smoked enough to stain itself. A drum-house with its wheel inside, locked to a driveshaft that turns, if it turns, because the day side of a black-painted wall is warmer than the night side of the same wall by a degree or two. The mechanism is built for that insultingly small difference. It does not clank. It does not need to. Gears of that patience make no sound a person can claim.

The strangeness is not that the machines are here. It is that the forest and the plant seem to have agreed on a pace. Leaves have not smashed the glass; they have leaned on it until the panes are a kind of lattice, light coming through in clean bars that the understory has organized itself around. Vines use the condenser combs as trellis and in doing so increase the surface that gathers dew, so the machines collect more because they are overgrown, and the overgrowth persists because the machines keep a little water moving on the dry days. Nothing is ruinous yet. Nothing is new. The rivets are bright where drips polish them and dull where moss has begun a felt. Inside the shed, if you part the hang of leaves at the doorway, the looms—or whatever the frames are; they look like looms, warped with a fiber that might be bark beaten out and might be a polymer drawn from sap—hold half-finished webs. The webs are not rotting. They are thickening, thread by thread, on a cycle longer than a season. Dust does not lie on them. Condensation does, and the brightness, and a faint industrial sheen, as if the cloth were being sized by the air itself.

The rules of the place are simple and slow. Stone rises faster than it weathers, so the outlines stay cruel and the soils stay thin, and everything that wants to live must live on surfaces. Water is a film, not a body, and it moves downhill only when a surface insists. Light is excessive and cool; shade exists, but it is striped, mechanical, never the deep shade of an old wood. Animals do not thrive; the quiet may be cause or symptom. What people built here they built as instruments rather than as shelter—collectors, differentials, frames—and then left the instruments to their gradients. No one is in the basin now. The faint marks of them are a boot-scrape on a galvanized step, a numbered tag on a pipe, a tin cup on a windowsill inside the shed, furred with mineral from years of the same drip falling into it. The cup is full. It has been full. It will be full differently tomorrow, by a line too small to see.

If you stay, the day does not dramatize itself. Brightness holds. The far wall stays sharp. A cloud may form against one ridge and come apart without raining, a brief dimming that the leaves notice and the machines, perhaps, notice too, their slow wheels easing by a fraction as the temperature difference collapses and then, when the cloud goes, resuming. Somewhere under the central flat a pipe ticks—no. It doesn’t tick. You only think it should. What you get instead is the sense of a regional metabolism: uplift, condensation, growth, a little extraction, a little cloth, all of it proceeding in a silence so complete it begins to feel like a material, something the basin manufactures as surely as water-film and thread.

By late light the stone goes from white to a thin gold and the leaves do not. They stay a deep, saturated green, drinking the last brightness the way they drink the dew. The scattered roofs answer the sky in small hard flashes. Nothing calls. Nothing answers. The valley, young and overfilled and cool, continues its work at the only speed it has.
