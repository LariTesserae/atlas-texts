The chamber is no larger than a parish church, and it has no door you can see until you have already been inside it for a while. Everything you can put a hand to has been made. The floor is a pour of slag and brick-dust that set generations ago and has since been worn into a shallow dish, the center polished by whatever passed and the edges left gritty, still holding the pour-marks of the forms. Walls rise not as masonry so much as as accumulation: pipe laid against plate, plate lagged with a cracked mineral wool that has gone the color of old teeth, wool pinned under straps of hoop-iron that have rusted into the wool and the wool into the iron, so that nothing can be peeled free of anything else. The ceiling is low enough that the heat lives in it. You feel it in the crown of the head before you feel it anywhere else.

There is no sky and almost no light. What light there is comes from a slit where two casings have drifted apart, a thumb-wide seam that does not brighten the room so much as prove that the room has an outside of a sort — another room, or a shaft, or only a thickness of dark with a warmer dark behind it. In that seam the air sometimes takes a sheen, as if a film of moisture were sliding down a surface you cannot quite locate. The rest of the place you learn by touch and by the small sounds metal makes when it is no longer new.

Heat is the constant. It does not flare. It has settled into the mass of the construction the way heat settles into a stove long after the fire has been banked: radiated, stored, given back slowly. The lagging is too tired to do its work. Where you lean a shoulder against a column of pipe the metal is not scalding, only insistently warm, the warmth of a thing that has not been cool in living memory and does not expect to be. Sweat starts at the hairline and does not dry. It beads, hesitates, and is taken back into the air, which is close and faintly sweet with oil that has gone thin and with the mineral smell of hot brick.

Water is not a feature so much as a habit the room has not been able to give up. There is no stream. There are pockets. In the dish of the floor a skin of water stands for days and then is gone, leaving a tide-line of pale scale no higher than a fingernail. Condensation gathers on the undersides of the horizontal runs, swells, and lets go one drop at a time onto a lower run, which receives it with a sound so small it registers as a thought rather than a noise. Somewhere behind the west bulkhead — if west means anything here — a seep has been worrying the same joint since the joint was new. It has not flooded anything. It has only, over a long patience, stained a fan of rust down three courses of plate and fed a dark velvet of growth no bigger than a hand, the only green in the place, and not very green: a lichenous crust, sparse, clinging where the damp and the heat agree, dying back where they do not. A second patch, smaller, holds on in a bolt-hole where washers have trapped a teaspoon of wet. Nothing else roots. There is no soil, only what industry has deposited and time has sorted.

The density is the thing that takes longest to understand, because at first the dark reads as empty. It is not empty. The volume is filled almost to contact. Conduits cross conduits. A gearbox housing, casing cracked along an old repair, sits between two uprights with no clearance worth the name; to pass it you turn sideways and the casing takes the heat of your ribs. Valves, long seized, present wheels you could turn if there were room to put both hands on them. There is not. Cable, cloth-wrapped and then metal-wrapped and then, in the newer layers, bare where the wrap has frayed, loops and sags and has been tied up again with whatever was to hand — wire, a strip of belting, once a length of chain whose links have eaten into the insulation and been left that way, because moving them would mean moving everything they touch. The industrial logic is still legible if you are willing to read it slowly: something was pumped, something was driven, something was exhausted upward through a stack that no longer meets an opening. The machines are not ruined in the dramatic sense. They are established. Their rust is not collapse; it is patina in the old meaning, a surface the object and the air have negotiated and then stopped negotiating. Balance, of a kind. A flywheel has taken a permanent lean into its housing and the housing has yielded by exactly that much, and neither has moved since.

Sound is mostly the absence of the sounds you expect from engines. Nothing turns. The quiet is not silence. It is the tick of cooling that never finishes, because the mass never finishes giving up heat it is also, somehow, still receiving — from below, from the rock the foundations were cut into, from a process elsewhere in the complex that this room is only the forgotten end of. Now and then a drop. Now and then a soft tick as a scale of rust lets go of a high pipe and falls onto a lower one, a sound like a fingernail on a cup, and then the long quiet again. If you stay, you begin to hear the building breathe in a cycle too slow to call breathing: a sigh in the ductwork when pressure somewhere far off shifts by a fraction, a answering creep in a joint. Hours between. Days, maybe. The dynamic of the place is the dynamic of things that have already happened and are content to continue having happened.

Fauna is almost a rumor. In the seam of light, once, a movement that might have been a moth and might have been a flake of lagging turning in a thermal. Along the base of the west bulkhead, where the seep feeds the dark crust, the dust is marked by tracks too fine to assign: something small, something that comes when the pocket of water is present and does not come when it is not. You will not see it. The room is not arranged for seeing. What lives here lives on the margin of the damp and the margin of the heat, and there is not much margin.

The unusual thing is not a creature or a glow. It is the floor's memory of use. The dish is too even to be accident, and too localized. Someone, or many someones, stood in the center for a long time — not passing through, standing — and the brick-dust pour recorded them the way stone records a threshold. Around that dish the density of machinery draws back by a hand's breadth, as if the original fitters had left a station, a place to watch a gauge whose glass is now blind, a place to listen to an engine that has since been throttled down to nothing and left in place because removing it would have meant opening the room to a larger room, and no one wanted the larger room. The gauge is still there. Its needle rests a little off the peg, not at zero. It has rested there so long the dust has outlined it. Whatever it was measuring is either gone or so constant that the needle no longer needs to move to tell the truth.

If you put your palm on the gauge housing it is hot, and faintly damp where your skin cools the metal enough for the air to give up its water. That is the whole weather of the place: heat held in made things, darkness held in the gaps between them, a little moisture arriving and departing on a schedule no one keeps, and the slow assurance that none of this is coming apart. It has already become what it is. It is only continuing.
