The high plain does not announce itself as high. There is no rim in the ordinary sense, no last switchback where the air thins and a valley opens underneath you. The land has already done that work and forgotten it. What remains is a country planed to nearly nothing, still lifted, as if a range the size of a continent had been ground down to its own memory and the memory kept the altitude. Underfoot the stone is old in a way that has stopped being dramatic. Grain shows, then doesn't. A pebble you turn over is the same color all the way through, not because it is pure, but because every rind it ever grew has been taken off and the taking finished so long ago that the interior has become the surface by custom.

Cold lives here the way a law lives, not the way a wind does. It does not rush. It is simply the temperature the stone agreed to and then declined to renegotiate. Breath, if breath were a thing the plain bothered to host, would show; moisture does show, and that is the plain's one generosity. Damp gathers in the grain of the rock, in the shallow dishes left where a last ridge lay down and did not get up, in the seams of fitted walls far off, on the undersides of leaves that have no business being as thick as they are. Rain comes as a decision rather than a storm. A district darkens. The darkening walks. For an hour, or for a measure that is only pretending to be an hour, water exists in quantity, beading, sheeting, finding the old threads of drainage that no longer know downhill with any confidence. Then the decision is withdrawn. The stone drinks what it can. The rest stands in pools that do not ripple, because there is nothing to ripple them, and because the air has become reluctant to push.

Between those pools the distances are indecent. You can see weather being born on the plain and not coming toward you, a pale front assembling itself out of condensation and refusing to travel, content to be a climate for a single empty reach and then to unmake itself when the reach has had enough. The plateau is large enough to keep its own sky. Clouds form with no coast to explain them and no range to lift them; they lift because the cold and the damp have started a conversation and the conversation needs a room. Some of those clouds never leave the ground. They move at the height of a wall, white, slow, and when they pass over a pool they deposit a second, thinner pool on top of the first, a skin of water that is not yet rain and not quite fog. Under it, color is ordinary. The light here is honest light, the kind that tells you the true color of stone and fiber and wet leaf. What it will not tell you is the hour. Noon holds in one ward of the distance while the next ward, plainly visible, is already in the long shade of a cloud that has not arrived and may not be coming to you at all. Both lights are correct. They do not argue. They simply fail to be the same fact.

The vegetation does not cover the plain. It occupies it, in stands, and the stands are lush to the point of discourtesy. Where a pocket of water persists for more than a season — and season is a word the plain uses loosely — the growth comes in as if it had been waiting under the stone for the stone to admit moisture. Mats of leaf thicken into cushions you could lose a hand in. Blades, if they are blades, rise and lock together into brakes that glitter with held damp and do not rustle. There are trees, or things that have chosen the obligations of trees: trunks pale as the gravel, bark that beads, canopies that knit until the light underneath is still normal light, only greener, only closer. They drink cold. They do not seem to mind it. A leaf torn — though nothing tears them; the gesture is hypothetical — would be wet all the way to its middle, sweet-smelling or smelling of nothing, dense with a pulp that is half weather. In the open between stands, the stone goes on for so long that the eye starts to invent groves and then, correctly, deletes them. The lushness is real. The emptiness around it is also real, and larger. A forest here is an event. You can walk out of it in an afternoon and not meet another for a stretch of days whose count will not agree with anyone else's count, should anyone else be walking, which they are not.

Nothing hunts. Nothing flocks, burrows, settles, or crosses. The absence is not a season and not a silence after a killing. It is structural. The plain does not complete animals. Where a ledge might have held a nest, the ledge holds a cup of gravel identical to every other cup of gravel, and the similarity is so exact it feels like a refusal. Tracks do not appear in the damp even when the damp is soft enough to take them. Seeds move, but they move by water and by the slow lean of the stands, not by being carried. If you watch a brake long enough you will see it shift its edge by a hand's width toward a pool that has decided to last, and you will not hear the shift. Motion here is mostly quiet. The active processes — and the plain is busy — conduct themselves below the threshold of noise, or aside from it, in a register the air has given up carrying. Rain inks a district and the ink makes no patter. A cloud at wall-height drags its belly over a canopy and the canopy bows and straightens without a sigh. Far off, a front builds and spends itself, and the only sign it was loud somewhere is that sometimes, much later, a sound arrives without a source: a single knock, a rush like gravel poured, the clap of a wet sheet. It is not happening. It happened, on a scale of time the ear is not built for, and the plain delivered it late, the way it delivers everything it still bothers to deliver.

The settlements are the part the mind resists, and then accepts, because they are too consistent to be mirages and too empty to be occupied in any ordinary way.

They sit in the lush pockets and also, stubbornly, on the bare stone, as if whoever planned them — and there is planning, visible, patient, urban — refused to let water be the only reason for a town. From a distance a settlement is a low staining of geometry on the plateau's older geometry. Nearer, it becomes streets. The streets are wide for the traffic they do not have, paved with fitted slabs whose joints have been reset so many times that the resets are the history. Walls of dry stone stand to the height of a story or two, courses tighter than pride would require, capped sometimes with a simple metal, a dull pin or a strap gone the color of the damp, doing a job that fiber could almost do and metal does without comment. There are lintels. There are stairs that go up onto roofs that have become other streets, a second town laid lightly on the first, and in places a third, not ruined, not exactly used, simply continued. Clay cisterns, fired and refired, squat at the corners of wards and collect what the air gives them. Channels cut with hand tools — you can see the bite of the tools, a rhythm, a correction where the cutter's line wandered and was brought back — lead from cistern to cistern to the fields of leaf. The channels work. Water moves in them when water is present. No one opens a gate. The gates are stones that have learned, over a depth of time, which way to lean.

Markets occupy squares, and the squares are not metaphorical. Goods are set out in rows that respect aisles. Bundles of the pale fiber the trees yield if yielded is the word. Knapped blades of the plateau stone, still sharp, still arranged by size. Coils of cord. Fired cups nested. Simple metal rings, not jewelry so much as fasteners, green at the edges. Nothing is taken. Nothing spoils in the way food spoils, because nothing here is food; there is no mouth the plain is willing to permit. And yet the rows change. A bundle that was at the end of an aisle is, after a cloud has passed low and the light has gone back to being everybody's noon, in the middle of another aisle, retied. A cup has been turned so its lip faces the channel. Work is being done at the level of arrangement. The hands are not available to be counted, because hands would be animals, and animals are a category the world declines to finish. What remains is the civic pattern: wards, repairs, the redistribution of tools, the slow extension of a street by one slab, then another, toward a stand of leaf that has shifted its edge and must be met with a curb.

Walk long enough inside one of these towns — a day, a day that will not match the day on the plain outside — and the primitive means stop looking primitive. They look chosen. There is no wheel turning, no fire tended, no mechanism more ambitious than a cistern lip and a leaned gate and a wall built so a second wall can stand on it. The ambition is elsewhere. It is in the persistence of plan across a ground that has been replaced. In the oldest quarter, if age can be assigned where erosion has already finished with the very idea of sequence, the paving sits on paving sits on a thinness of soil that sits on paving again. The lowest stones are younger than the street they carry. You can see it in the tool marks, fresher, almost green in the metal pins, while the alignment above them is worn into something smoother than wear, an alignment that has outlived the rock it was drawn on. The town remembers its map more reliably than the plateau remembers its mountains. When the plateau forgets badly, the town accommodates without comment: a street will kink, gently, around a pool that was not there at the start of the kink, and the buildings on either side will have always had that corner.

This is where the plain stops being a place you can hold still in the mind, and starts being a set of agreements that do not all apply at once.

Distances fail first, because they are the easiest promise to break at this scale. Two cistern districts, visible from one another across a brake of leaf, can be an hour apart if you follow the channel and a different kind of interval if you follow the street that claims to parallel the channel. The street is not longer. It is simply not participating in the same addition. People — but there are no people — would say the town is larger inside than out. The more accurate trouble is that "inside" and "out" are local courtesies. Step through a lintel and the light remains normal, the cold remains the law, the damp still beads on the underside of a stone shelf; and yet the shelf's shadow falls on a wall that, from the street, was the outer face. You have not been turned around. The building has more surfaces than its volume allows, and it uses them politely, one at a time, never so many that the eye is asked to panic. Panic would require a witness of the animal kind. The architecture does not need one. It only needs the rule that a room continues until its plan is satisfied, and the plan is older than the room's stone, and so the room continues a little past the place where stone runs out, into a stretch of air that has agreed, for the length of the plan, to hold weight.

Outside, the ancient flatness misbehaves in the other direction. The plateau is done with mountains. It has been done with them for so long that the gravel is not debris but bedrock that got tired of being bedrock and lay down. And still, on certain days, when a private front is spending itself over an empty reach and the late sound of it has not yet arrived, a ridge stands up on the horizon that was not there at the start of the watching. It is not a cloud. It has the color of the gravel and the bluntness of something already eroded, and it stands for as long as the weather needs a reason. Then it lies back, and where it lay the stone is undisturbed, because the standing was not an event in the stone. It was the plain remembering a shape loudly enough that the light, which is otherwise so honest, went along with it. Afterward the horizon is flat again, and a single knock comes in from nowhere, the late sound, and fades, and the pools do not ripple.

The stands of vegetation participate. A grove that was east of a town — east being another courtesy, honored until it isn't — will be north of it after a condensation has walked through, not because the grove walked, exactly, though its edge does move, but because the town's street, obeying a plan older than the ground, has reassigned the direction in order to keep the curb aimed at the leaf. Direction here is a municipal service. It is maintained where there are walls and allowed to lapse where there are not. In the deep empty, between settlements, you can lose a heading without moving. The light stays normal. The cold stays. A pool remains a pool. Only the sense that one far town lies to your left goes thin, and then belongs to the town, not to you, and the town is not where the thinning said. Travelers would die of this, or go mad of it, and the plain has arranged not to have travelers. The arrangement is the same arrangement as the absence of animals: a kindness so complete it has become geology.

What moves, moves on schedules that overlap and do not synchronize, which is the plain's idea of a living day.

Condensation builds by mid-light and withdraws when the light, locally, decides it has been noon long enough. The private weathers form, rain on a district, dry on the next, and the boundary between them can lie along a street as if the street had been surveyed to hold weather the way it holds a ward. Leaves thicken. A brake extends. A cistern takes the gift and passes it, gate-stone leaning, channel running with a sound so small it is more a change in the quality of quiet than a noise. Somewhere a slab is added to a street. Somewhere a bundle of fiber is retied and set in a different row. Somewhere a ridge remembers itself and is forgiven. These are not metaphors for one process. They are multiple, and they are all underway, and they do not culminate. There is no season in which the plain finishes. Erosion has already finished; what remains is maintenance of a final form, and the maintenance is active, civic, botanical, meteorological, and slightly impossible, all at once, at a volume just under hearing.

If you stand at the edge of a town where the last curb gives up and the stone resumes its long unassignment, the view is of damp gravel, a far stain of leaf, a cloud kneeling over a reach it will not share, and light that would, in any other country, tell you it was afternoon. It may be afternoon. It may be afternoon only here, for the length of this curb, while the grove you can see is still in the morning it started and does not intend to leave. Nothing calls. Nothing answers. A cup of water on a market stone, if you walked back to look, would still be there, turned, catching an ordinary glint. The glint would be accurate. The water would be cold. And the plain would go on, vast enough to generate a sky it does not owe to any other country, old enough to have survived its own mountains, sparse enough that a city can be a scattering of decisions across an emptiness, lush only where the damp stays, busy without voices, and just broken enough that every true thing you notice is true in a jurisdiction that ends, without a marker, a few steps from where you noticed it.
