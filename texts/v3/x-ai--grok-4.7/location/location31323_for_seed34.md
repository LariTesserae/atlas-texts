The first thing the air does is arrive already used. It has crossed so much of the same country that it comes in cool and thin and faintly sweet with wet stone, carrying no salt and no dust, only the memory of other pools. Under a sky of ordinary light—high, colorless, neither morning nor late—the land does not rise to a horizon so much as continue. What looks, from any one standing-place, like the edge of a district is only the next lip of the same work, repeated until weather itself has to organize around it.

It is high. Not a peak-country; the summits, if they were ever here, have been worn down to a broadness that no longer bothers with drama. The whole plateau sits up in the cooler band of the air, and the wind, when it moves, moves as a slow exchange between one basin and the next rather than as a traveler from elsewhere. You feel the height in the way sound fails. A step on wet rock gives back almost nothing. Even the water, which is everywhere, refuses to announce itself.

The water does not run so much as occupy. It lies in sheets a finger deep across pavements of gray and bone-colored stone, gathers in rectangular and kidney-shaped basins whose rims are no higher than a knee, and weeps from every vertical face in a continuous film. Between basins the rock is dark with permanent damp. After rain—and rain is not an event here so much as a thickening of what the air was already doing—the films join, and for a while the districts become one shallow sea broken by walls. Then the excess finds the old slots, the thumb-wide channels cut not by hands but by centuries of the same overflow taking the same path, and the sea withdraws to its rooms. The channels are infrastructure. They have gradients a builder would recognize. They branch, rejoin, drop a few inches at sills of harder mineral, and deliver their small loads into cisterns that never quite empty. Nothing maintains them except the water that uses them. Nothing has to.

What reads, at a distance, as a city is the habit of calcium and related salts to fall out of cool water wherever the water pauses. Over a time longer than weather and shorter than mountains, the pauses have built walls. The walls are close. In the denser quarters you can stand in a basin and touch two rims at once, and beyond those rims are more basins, their surfaces level with yours or a hand lower, a hand higher, receding in steps that have no single direction of descent. Some walls are sharp and new-white where deposition is still winning. Others have gone soft and pitted, stained tea-brown, colonized by a shadow of algae no thicker than a breath. Erosion and accretion have argued themselves into a truce. Nothing is ruins. Nothing is finished. The place is in its own state, and has been long enough that the state looks like law.

The law is simple and local, and it holds across a span large enough to make its own clouds. Air climbs the long rise of the plateau, cools, and condenses against the stone. The condensate does not fall far. Often it beads on the lips of walls and runs back into the basin it came from, so that a district drinks its own breath. By afternoon, if the light has been steady, a skin of mist stands a few feet above the water and no higher, a ceiling you can put your hand through. Under it the light is normal—flat, readable, without theater—but distances collapse. A wall thirty paces off is clear. A wall three hundred is a suggestion. Beyond that the country continues, and continues, and the continuation is the weather: each cluster of basins a cool sink, each low ridge a place where the mist thins and the next sink begins. There are storms, but they are wide and slow, sheets of rain walking for days across stone that is already wet, rearranging which cisterns are full and which are only filmed. Flood, here, means the walls disappear for an afternoon and then reappear in the same places, rinsed.

Vegetation is a rumor the stone mostly declines. In cracks that stay damp without being scoured, a black-green crust takes hold, and sometimes a tuft of stiff, colorless sedge no taller than a finger. These do not gather into meadows. They occur the way lamps occur in a city that has mostly gone dark: one, then none, then one far enough away that it does not belong to the first. Where a channel has undercut a wall and left a sheltered ledge, moss makes a velvet the color of wet iron. It does not spread onto the open pavements. The open pavements belong to mineral and to light. From above—if there were an above that was not simply more of the same—the plateau would read as pale, a lace of white rims and dark water, with green so sparse it would not register as cover, only as a defect in the pattern.

Animals are rarer still, and the quiet is not the quiet of absence so much as the quiet of things that do not need to call. A dark bird, small, sometimes crosses from one rim to another and is gone, its wingbeat the loudest event of an hour. In the deeper cisterns, where the water is a greenish glass and the bottom is out of reach of the hand, something slow disturbs the silt on no schedule you can learn—once, a plume, then the water righting itself. On the moss ledges, after rain, a scatter of tiny shells suggests a life that climbs out, feeds, and returns before the films dry to their ordinary damp. You do not see the bodies. You see the evidence, and not often. Whatever hunts whatever else does it by patience and by the geography of rims: a world of edges, ambush measured in inches, no herd, no flock, no chorus at dusk because dusk changes the light without changing the terms. The biological pace is real. It is simply conducted at volumes the air does not bother to carry.

The density is not of life. It is of presences that do not move when you look at them and yet are all in process. Every wall is a crowd of older walls entombed in newer deposit. Break a rim—though nothing here invites breaking, and the stone, wet, holds—and the cross-section is laminated like a stack of cities, each lamina a season or a century of overflow, some thick, some paper-fine, some stained where a crust of algae was buried and became part of the architecture. Basins nest. A broad shallow pan contains, in one corner, a tighter pool with its own rim, and that pool has a sill that feeds a slot that feeds a pan you cannot see until you walk the channel to its end and find it opening under a low arch of its own making. The arches are common. Water finds a soft seam, leaves it, and the seam becomes a passage, then a vault, then a street with a ceiling of dripping stone, tall enough to stoop through, long enough to have weather of a meaner kind: drip, echo too small to be called echo, a private fog. These understreets connect districts the surface map does not admit are connected. You can travel a long way without coming fully into the light, following infrastructure that no one designed and that would collapse into meaninglessness if the water ever stopped.

It does not stop. That is the other law. The plateau is vast enough that its water cycle is partly its own: what lifts in the morning from a thousand thousand surfaces returns by evening to a thousand thousand others, and the excess that escapes the plateau's edge—somewhere, days of walking beyond any visible change—is replaced by what the high air still carries in from lower countries and is forced to set down. The balance is old. Channels are not deepening into gorges. Walls are not racing upward into towers. The established form is this mid-height, this close packing, this knee-deep urbanism of pools. A place can be active without going anywhere. Rain rearranges levels. Deposition adds a grain. A crust of algae browns and is skinned over by white. A bird crosses. A plume of silt rises and settles. The mist lifts and lies down. All of it happens inside a silence so complete that the small sounds become the structure: the tick of a drip finding the same cup it has found for years; the faint seethe of a film moving down a face of rock; the soft collapse of a bubble under an arch. Stand long enough and those sounds separate into neighborhoods. One cistern ticks faster. One channel has a lower note. The district you are in has a pitch, and the district beyond the next rim has another, and you could, if you cared to, navigate by that.

There is no device. No cut mark that is not the cut of water. No joint that is not a change in the rate of falling-out. No fire ring, no track worn by feet in numbers, no post. If anyone has walked here, the water has agreed to forget them by the next rain, which is to say by sometime soon. The organization you feel—the sense of streets, of quarters denser and quarters more open, of cisterns placed where cisterns ought to be—is the organization of a process that has run without interruption until its habits became indistinguishable from plan. In the more open quarters the basins widen and the walls sink until you are crossing a pavement of continuous shallow water, each step a small cold shock, the stone underfoot gritty with fresh precipitate, slick where the algae has dared a foothold. In the tight quarters you turn sideways between rims, and the sky is a slot, and your breath fogs and joins the general fog, and it is possible to believe you are indoors, in a building whose rooms are all flooded to the same intentional depth.

Normal light does the place a favor by refusing to symbolize it. At midday the white rims are simply white, the water simply dark, the wet faces simply reflective. You can see the bottom of the shallow basins: a lace of mineral, sometimes a leaf of sedge drowned and going translucent, sometimes nothing but the color of the stone. Shadows are short and honest. Toward evening the mist thickens by a degree and the light goes slightly gold and then does not; the plateau is high enough, and broad enough, that sunset is a thinning rather than a spectacle, a lowering of contrast until the rims and the water are the same value and the city, for a while, becomes pure texture. Then the dark is dark, and the sounds continue at the same volumes, because nothing here was waiting on the light in order to work.

What changes at night is only your certainty about distance. The ticking of remote cisterns seems nearer. A drip under an arch can be mistaken for a step. There are no steps. In the morning the levels have shifted by inches according to where the night's condensation preferred to fall, and a channel that was silent has a voice again, and one that spoke has gone back to a film. You could map it. The map would be wrong by the following dawn in every particular and right forever in its grammar: walls, sills, slots, pans, arches, the pale open pavements, the tight stacked quarters, the rare green, the rarer movement, the cool air always a little wetter than the stone wants to admit, the silence doing the work that in other countries is done by wind and birds and wheels.

Walk outward. The instruction is a kind of joke the plateau makes. Outward is a direction the land agrees to imitate for a morning and then abandons. The districts change character—the rims lower, the basins broader, a stretch where the water smells faintly of iron, a stretch where the deposit is yellow and the walls have a waxy look—but the change is dialect, not border. Weather you watched forming over a far cluster of basins arrives, hours later, as the same rain, and moves on into country you will not reach, where it will fill cisterns whose rims were laid down by the same patience. The scale does not announce itself with a view. It announces itself by the failure of any view to contain a difference. You are always in the middle of the works. The works are always in the middle of their own maintenance. Somewhere there is an edge, a fall to lower air, a river beginning out of a thousand obedient channels. It is not a place you are required to imagine as near. Near is the next rim, and the film of water finding its old path down the face of it, and the small cold sound that path has always made, and will make again, in a city that builds itself by refusing every tool but time.
