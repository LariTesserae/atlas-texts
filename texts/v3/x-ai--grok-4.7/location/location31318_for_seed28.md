From the salt pan it is nothing you would cross a mile to see. A low blister in the crust, warm under the palm, no taller than a kneeling body and wide as a small courtyard. The pan around it is the color of old bone. Wind runs over it and does not stop. Only if you put an ear to the dome do you learn that some earlier wind was caught underneath and has not been released since. The sound does not come up as a voice. It comes up as pressure, a grit in the skull, as if the ground were a mill and you had laid your head on the stone.

The way in is a fissure on the lee side, shoulder-wide, smoothed past any memory of a tool. It drops. The pan is already low country; this is lower still, a throat under the blister, and the light fails for three body-lengths before the chamber opens and gives the light back all at once. By then the sound has become the whole of the air. You do not enter a place that is loud. You enter a note that has been sounding long enough to have a climate.

The chamber is one room and it is mostly nothing. A hall, perhaps, if a hall could be round and could refuse a floor. The crown of the dome is a lens of salt and selenite, thinner than the walls, and the day comes through it white and slightly amber, bright enough that there are no dark corners, only degrees of glare. Dust moves in the volume like a slow upward snow. You can see the far wall without trying. You cannot hear anyone on it.

What reads, after the eyes adjust, as a city is a skin on that emptiness. Ring on ring of ledge, terrace, and half-bridge, stacked from just above the floor to just under the lens, never crossing the middle. The construction is hand work and all of it is old in the same way: edges rounded, joints seated, no chip that has not been vibrated back into smoothness. Brick of salt-clay tempered with pale scales. Fiber lashings gone the color of the dust. Here and there a pin of beaten copper, not a blade and not a coin, driven into a joint the way a musician seats a wedge — to keep a span at the pitch it was given. There are gutters, but they are cut for resin. There are cisterns the size of chests, and they hold a clear oil that beads and does not wet the salt. There are streets a single person wide, with passing-niches, and the niches face away from the void. The whole circuit, walked, is not a long walk. Counted as rooms, wards, lifts of rope and pulley, named landings, and the small repeated shrines of baffled matting where a body can sleep, it is a town. It has simply been folded into one feature, and the feature has been hidden under a blister no one on the pan would bother to name.

The gardens are what make the folding visible. They are lush in the only way a place without water can be lush: thick, continuous, tending every horizontal surface, hanging in curtains through which the ledges appear and vanish. They are not green. The new growth is the color of fresh bone; the older mats darken toward amber and iron. Their leaves are flat fibers, dry as split reed, and they tick. In the standing sound each curtain is already vibrating, and a change in the outer wind moves through them as a visible shiver a moment before the note in your chest drops or climbs. They do not drink. Cut one and it weeps a resin, never a sap, and the wound crusts with the same pale scale that tempers the brick. They feed on the dust the roar keeps aloft, on the warmth the lens pours down, and on whatever thin film of moisture the air might have thought of becoming. They are faster than dew. Because of them the chamber stays below the point of wetting, and the salt stays salt, and the brick does not slump. Their abundance is the reason the air is empty of water. If they failed, the lens would still blaze and the throat would still roar, and the first night of slack wind would grow a sweat on the walls, and the town would soften.

So the streets are also beds, and the beds are also the structure, and the structure is also the instrument. A terrace left untended does not go to ruin. The fiber simply closes over it, and the copper pin keeps its seat, and the landing remains in the chord. Most of the town is like that now. The hands that cut the first ledges are many generations back; the hands that remain are few, and they do not need to be many. You might walk a full circuit at the hour when the note is high and meet no one. The presences are far apart even when they are near, because the roar eats the distance cues the body uses. A figure on the opposite wall is a clear fact of sight and a rumor of company. People live in the baffled niches, where the mats take the edge off the pressure and the light is only bright, not blinding. They speak with their hands, or they pluck a fiber at a practiced place and the shiver runs the curtain across the void, a signal quicker than the long way round. The gardens are the telegraph, the scaffold, the storehouse, and the reason the stone is dry. Food is the inner pith, oily and faintly sweet, pressed at the sun-shelves under the lens. No one cooks. A flame would be a strange cruelty here, and there is nothing wet to boil.

The floor is not used. It is the brightest and the loudest, a pale dune of shed scale rippled into a pattern that does not travel the way dune-ripples travel. The standing wave rakes it, and the rake has had centuries to become exact. The grand form does not change. Small shifts at the margins record the season: a wider crest when the pan wind holds high for days, a finer grain when the note slackens and the plants accrete instead of shedding. Stepping through the pattern smears the record, and the gardens answer the smear — a sourness in the resin, a darkening along the lowest curtains — so the floor is left alone. There is another reason, older than the custom. The chamber was not cut by tools. It was opened, long ago, by water, in a wetter age this country has since refused. The scallops of that dissolution are still visible where brick does not cover them, rounded, fossil surf on a dry wall. Under the dune, the last of that water is not gone so much as sealed: a brine that does not rise, a warmth that is not only the lens, a lid the town has agreed never to test. The roar covers any sound the lid might make. Watching the ripples is how the place listens downward. If the grand form ever changed, the listening would have failed.

Fauna is almost not part of the census. In the throat itself, where the incoming air is fastest, a few sealed, pale things the size of a thumb cling and sieve dust and never come into the glare. On the curtains, after a long slack in the note, a scale-moth may unhitch and drift across the void, collapsing its route to a single crossing, gone before the next gust. Nothing hunts. Nothing flocks. The air is too dry and the sound too continuous for anything that needs to hear itself or to keep a wet eye. The people are the largest animals in the blister, and they are scarce enough that the volume still reads as empty: a hollow, a bright well, a town occurring only at its edges.

The day cycles without ever dimming to dark. Morning is a climb in pitch as the pan heats and the wind at the fissure stiffens; the curtains shed; scale ticks down through the light and does not reach the ledges, because the updraft in the center is steady and the void takes the fall. Midday the chord is at its grind, and the resin in the gutters thins and runs, and the few people who still keep a circuit make it then, reading pins and mats the way another town would read gutters after rain. Toward the slack hour the note drops, sometimes by a third if a gust on the pan fails, and everyone who is awake pauses — not in fear, in measurement. The moths risk the void. Dust settles a finger's width toward the floor and then, when the throat recovers, rises again. The ripples sharpen. This is weather: not a storm, a change in the chord, and the town is built to hear it with the whole body.

Newcomers, if the throat ever admitted them, would be useless for days. The sound is not a surface. It sits in the teeth and the diaphragm and rearranges what a thought can be. The residents hear inside it. They know the grit that means the lens is hot, the cleaner harmonic that means the curtains are in their heavy growth, the sub-bass that means the lid under the floor is only being itself. Silence is the disaster they have no procedure for. A blocked fissure would be a drowning of another kind: the note collapsing, dew thinking about the walls, the ripples going still enough to stop recording. So the throat is swept, and the copper pins are tapped back to true with stones, and no one stores water because water is not a resource here. It is a breach.

At the crown, if you climb the last fiber ladder and lie under the lens, the pan's brightness becomes a heat you can see as well as bear, and the blister's thinness makes the outer world a smear of bone-white with no features. The town is beneath you, small as a mechanism, every ledge a line in a chord, the empty center a pupil of glare. The sound is less directional there. It simply is the size of the room. A scale lets go from a curtain somewhere below and winks once as it crosses the updraft, and you understand the scale of the place again: not a country, not a ruin in a desert, a single held note with a town grown into its walls, in balance because it has already shed everything water would have asked of it, and loud because that is how it stays.
