The stone has already finished becoming what it is. Whatever ridges once stood here were taken down so long ago that the taking itself has no remainder but a grain, a rounding, a patience in the surface. The land lies low and open, a basin worn past the age of slopes, and the horizon is not a wall but a fading of the same wet floor into dim air. You can walk a long time and still be in the first idea of the place. Only slowly do the differences arrive: a darker mere, a shoulder of bedrock no higher than a knee, a line of fitted slabs, the sheen of standing water widening and thinning like breath on metal.

Water is not a feature of this country. It is the condition under which the country is allowed to exist. It sheets across the polished stone in a skin sometimes thinner than a finger, sometimes pooled to the ankle in hollows the erosion forgot to erase completely. Seams weep. The air, cool enough that wet hands ache and breath shows, sits forever near the dew point, so that any upright thing—a cairn, a raised head, the fold of a coat—darkens and begins to drip. Groundwater is a little warmer than rain. You learn this with your wrist. Where a seep breathes out of a crack, the film over the stone does not chill you quite so fast, and the darker staining there is thicker, oily, rust-red to bruise-purple, a living varnish that is not a plant and does not pretend to be one.

Nothing green stands. There is no litter of leaves, no reed, no moss cushion, no drift of pollen, no rooted dark in the mud that would mean soil rather than mineral paste. The ground is chert-flour, iron floc, clay without humus, silica skins that crease when the water draws back a little and then seal again. Decay, when it happens, smells of metal, wet stone, and animal fat. The primary life is the film itself: slim mats breathing iron and sulfur and whatever faint chemistry the seeps still offer, laying down rust and purple in slow tides no one would call a season unless they had lived inside the roar long enough to notice that the mats fatten, pale, and fatten again.

The roar is the climate. Flat water and finished stone refuse to absorb anything, and there is no vegetation to do the work either, so sound does not die here. It crosses the basin and comes back altered and not smaller. The baseline is a crackle that seems to come out of the sheet itself—an innumerable scatter of small clickers living in the films, each snap slight, the aggregate a continuous frying hiss that presses on the teeth and the sternum. Over that, at wider intervals, something larger booms from the meres: a low, wet concussion you feel in the ribs before you agree it was a voice. Wind adds a sheen of noise with nothing tall to catch it. Rain, when it comes, does not make the place wetter in any way that matters. It changes the pitch. Speech, if you brought any with you, is a private activity conducted at the ear. Beyond an arm’s length you are already translating yourself into gesture, or into the decision not to be heard as anything particular.

Because the loudness is regional, the eye is misled into thinking the land empty. Density is low. In any one glance: a great deal of shining stone, three pale shapes feeding, a cairn, a darkening of water that might be deeper than it looks. The abundance is elsewhere and everywhere—the knowledge, earned by standing still, that every low swell within a day’s walk holds its own scatter, and that the hiss does not thin out when you move. The animals are many. They are simply not crowded. Cover is meaningless in a country without plants and almost without relief, so life stays low, pale, and spaced, the color of wet bone and old ivory, close to the film it eats.

The grazers move with a side-to-side economy, tongues or rasps working the mineral skin, leaving paler trails that the mats close again in a few days. Their feet are broad and quiet on their own account, which hardly matters; the basin reports them anyway—the change in the clicker-hiss where a body shadows the sheet, the small slap of feeding, the long groan of one animal shifting its weight on resonant stone. They do not bunch. A bunch would be a single target and a single acoustic event. Between them the distance is deliberate, a rule you can measure by how far a struggle carries.

Death carries. A kill, or even a bad entanglement in soft mineral mud, is not a local fact. The hiss stutters in a patch, the boomers answer or refuse, and across the open ground the scavengers adjust their headings while the event is still happening. Predators know this and have become creatures of the lull. They are few, wide-ranging, and patient in the only shadows the basin can offer: the slight sound-break behind a bedrock shoulder, the lee of a cairn, the brief baffling of a body held flat in deeper water. They do not chase across open sheet. A chase would be a announcement. They wait for weather, or for a mistake, or for the rare minute when a cold front stills the clickers and the sudden drop in the roar is so violent it feels like deafness arriving from the other side. In that minute the basin seems to inhale. Everything that hunts begins to move. Everything that feeds stops rasping and lowers itself until the film closes over its back. Then the hiss rebuilds, grain by grain, and the ordinary deafening returns like a mercy.

The structures are scattered and low, never a town, never a wall that could hold a wind. Fitted slabs, circles of chert flakes pressed into the paste, a grindstone gone concave, cairns of flat rock wedged rather than mortared. No timber—there has been nothing to timber for longer than these works. What remains is stone, bone, shell hash, and the ghosts of sinew. Some of the cairns are so settled into the flatness that only the regularity of their stones gives them away; erosion is finishing them by the same slow method it finished the hills. Others have been re-wedged. A slab that should have lain flush is lifted and braced with a spall, not yesterday, but not in the geological sense either. Something still practices the early gesture: lift, test, set, listen. Whether that something is the last of a people or an animal with a simple inheritance of tool-use is not settled by looking. The works are too few, and too quiet in their intention, to announce a maker. What they announce is acoustic. Stand near a cairn and the hiss acquires an edge, a small bright return. Walk the line of an old net-weight row—stones at even intervals, the cord long gone—and the booms from the mere arrive in a different order. The built things are faint marks, but they are marks in a place where a faint mark can be heard for a long way.

Light does not clarify this. It is dim in the way of a low, permanent weather, not night and not a storm you can wait out. Contrast is weak. Shadows are suggestions. The brightest events are reflections: a dull coin of sky in a hoof-print, the mercurial shiver of sheetwater when a boom crosses it, the wet streak on a cairn’s upright face. Distance is a loss of detail rather than a change of color. The far mere and the far shoulder and the haze-line beyond them exist in the same gray-brown key as the stone under you, separated only by how much of their sound has had time to arrive.

The place is not still, though its form is finished. Weather crosses it as a change in the total voice. You hear a front before any darkening of the already dim air: the clickers falter in a band, the boomers shift their timing, the sheet begins to tick where rain, still far, has started to stipple a resonant reach of bedrock. Animals drift toward the warmer seeps or away from them according to a calculus of chill and film-richness. Pools rise by a finger. The purple mats slick over and shine like oil. For a while the basin is all process—migration without a path, feeding, the repositioning of bodies in relation to sound-shadows, the small work of water finding its level on a floor that has almost no level left to find. Then the front passes, the hiss evens, and the land looks as ancient as it did, which is to say completed, the oldest stable shape, a final mineral sentence that nonetheless has a living weather moving on top of it.

At the dimmest hours the air grows heavier and some of the thinner sheets rime at their edges, a white mineral frost that is not deep and does not last. The booms space out. The clickers do not stop. Nothing in this country offers a true silence, and if one came—if the hiss failed across the whole open reach at once—it would not be peace. It would be the signal that every rule had been suspended, and that whatever still moves here had decided, all at once, to listen. Until then the basin keeps its ordinary condition: cool, wet to the skin, empty to the eye, loud enough to organize a life. The stone will not grow a forest over its ending. It has already arrived at what it is. Everything else lives in the sound that will not lie down on it.
