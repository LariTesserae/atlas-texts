You find it by a split in the apron, no wider than your shoulders, where the concrete has opened along a line too clean to be old. The chalked arrow beside it has already lost its head. A loop of wire holds a tin tag with no number left. Beyond the split the works continue—pipe-run, berm, the pale flat of a warm low coast—but the split does not belong to that surface. It is a mouth. The air that comes up is skin-warm and faintly sweet, and it does not smell of the salt-grass outside.

You go down a body’s length. That is the whole descent. The elevation was already nothing; this is only the underneath of nothing, a room embedded in the berm the way a seed sits in a fold of cloth. Ordinary daylight enters with you and stays ordinary. It does not turn gold or green. It simply stops going far. The air is grained—mineral flour still sifting off the break, and a finer dust that lifts and settles on a rhythm slower than breath—and the light thickens in it the way light thickens in a shed with the door half shut. You can see everything. You cannot see past the room.

The room is new. That is the first correction, and it keeps being true. The ceiling slab parted along a joint that has not yet learned to be a crack. Edges are sharp enough to catch thread from a cuff. There is no soft drift of old dust in the corners, no rounding, no stain that has had years to think about where to settle. What lies on the surfaces is a warm rime and a flour so recent it squeaks if you drag a nail through it. Somewhere in the wall a process-heat is still being conducted from the works you can no longer see. The concrete is a little above the temperature of your hands. Sweat forms and does not know whether it is yours.

Water is not a pool and not a river. Mid-morning a dark thread appears along the lowest joint, no wider than a finger, and advances by hesitations. It vanishes in the late afternoon and leaves a varnish, a darker map of where it was willing to go. On the underside of a broken flange, beads gather and hold past the size at which beads should fall. Some of them travel sideways along the metal before they drop, as if the flange had a grain the water prefers. When the thread is gone the varnish stays tacky for an hour. Then it is only a sheen, and the room is dry except for what the metal keeps.

Almost nothing is rooted. A clump of salt-grass came down with the collapse and has gone the color of old paper, roots in air, not yet a soil. Where the thread rested longest there is a film of green no thicker than paint, already grazed into lanes. One yellow stem has pushed from a bolt-hole and stopped, as if it understood the terms. The rest of the green you think you see is not plant. It is the packed small life on the film, too fine to separate until the light rakes, and then the lanes are moving.

The room has no empty interval. That is the fact that revises the size of it. A chapel’s worth of air, and not a palm’s width that is only air. Thumbnail crabs stand along the water-thread in territories of a few centimeters, carapaces the matte of the concrete, each one touching the next by a formality of claws so that the line is continuous. Where the thread has withdrawn they remain, queued over the varnish, waiting for a return they seem to count. Between them the cast skins lie in such number that the floor’s lower edge is a second, paler shoreline. Above that, on every vertical face out of the direct fall of the aperture, moths the color of galvanized sheet rest wing-to-wing. They lift and settle together, not as a cloud but as a surface respiring. A lizard so flat it reads as a stain occupies the warm side of a conduit and does not blink until a moth misjudges the lift. Two birds come in on the hour the light reaches the gauge. They do not call. Their nests, crammed into the mouths of two dead pipes, are built of stripped insulation, scale, and the same gray wing-dust. They enter, turn, and are still. In the film, in the flour, in the beads, the smaller kinds are a continuous occupation: springtails like pepper, larvae laceworking the drip-line, a grit of mites that rises when the crabs shift and settles when they stop.

You understand, after a while, why the plants fail and the edges stay sharp. The life is eating the beginnings of weather. Biofilm is grazed before it can soften stone. Mineral flour is taken as it falls. Cast skins and wing-dust are harvested into the nests and, you notice later, into the slow freight of the belt. Nothing is allowed to accumulate into the gentleness of an old place. The room is kept young by being fully occupied. What would have been erosion is instead a harvest. The walls are not worn. They are maintained in the state of having just broken.

The built things are few and they are not ruins in the way of abandoned towns. They are organs. A pressure gauge with a starred glass, its needle making a small repeated journey across a few marks, on a period of minutes, with no tick you can hear. A belt, short, disappearing into the wall at both ends, creeping at the speed of the flaw in its rubber—one pale scuff that returns if you are patient. A junction box warm under the palm, silent, a hand’s-width of bare metal around it that the moths refuse except in the hour before the thread arrives, when they pack against the warm face and the bare ring closes. A clog, one, wooden, left under the gauge as if the foot stepped out and the errand continued. A stamped plate with the middle letters gone, the first and last still bright because something polishes them, or eats the tarnish. Faint marks: the chalk outside, a grease-pencil slash on the slab, a ring of cleaner concrete where a bucket used to sit. Scattered. Partial. Enough to say that hands have been here and not enough to say that hands are the point.

The industry has not left. It has shrunk to the room’s own manufacture. The belt is in circuit with a works you cannot see and cannot hear; what it carries out, if you look at the scuff’s slow return and at the dust that finds the rubber, is the room’s harvest—wing-dust, mineral flour, the powder of cast skins—moved at a rate that would be nothing in a yard and is everything here. The gauge is not decorative. When its needle finishes a traverse, the beads on the flange let go together, a small simultaneous surrender, and the thread either begins or fails to begin. The junction’s warmth swells and ebbs with a longer pulse, out of phase with the daylight. Electrified, mechanized, still producing: not a commodity with a name, but a scaled process folded into a pocket, the way a gland is folded into a body. Somewhere beyond the berm a larger plant may be roaring. In the concrete you feel it as a pressure in the teeth. You do not hear it. The room has been built, or has built itself, to keep its work quiet.

Hold your breath and the soundscape declares its terms. A finger on a bottle rim, very faint, when the seep decides. The dry tick of crab legs, countable, polite. The microscopic shuffle of the moth surface, which is not a sound so much as a change in the thickness of the air. A single click from the belt housing once every long while, as if a tooth found its place and declined to celebrate. Your clothes are louder. The birds’ wings, when they enter, are a soft displacement and then nothing. Nothing vocalizes. You try, once, a low word, and the word does not vanish so much as fail to become separate from the pressure in the concrete. The room is not mute. It is occupied by processes that have no use for announcement. Quiet is not emptiness. Quiet is the condition under which this density can continue.

The rules surface one at a time, and each one makes the earlier picture insufficient.

The water keeps a schedule that is not the rain. Rain, when it comes to the coast, is an occasional darkening of the aperture and a few drops that fall straight and are gone into the flour. The thread does not answer the rain. It answers the gauge, and the gauge answers a pulse from outside, and the pulse may answer a tide in a channel you cannot see, or a shift change, or the cooling of a vat. Moisture here is intermittent, local, and salaried. Condensation on the flange is the room’s own weather, a cycle of beading and sideways travel and release, repeating through the warm afternoon whether or not the sky participates.

The stone is still being born. Overnight—if you stay, and the birds permit the idea of staying—the ceiling’s fresh edge gains a hairline of crystal, geometric, inclined toward the conduit’s warmth. By midday the crabs have reoriented their queue by a few degrees, as if the crystal were a correction to a map. The crystal is not erosion and not ornament. It is the break continuing to decide what shape the break is. Early formation. The place has not finished emerging. What looks like a completed chamber is a single feature still arriving, sharp because arrival is not the same as age.

The fauna do not treat the machines as ruins to be nested in. They treat the intervals—the bolt spacing, the flange drip, the warm ring, the belt’s period—as the grammar of the habitat. Predator and prey are timed to the grammar. The lizard takes moths only on the lift, which happens when the junction cools a fraction. The crabs contest territory only when the thread advances, and the contest is a mutual edging that ends with both still in line, because a gap in the line would be a kind of silence the room does not allow. The birds arrive when the light reaches the gauge, take a portion of the larvae exposed by the morning’s grazing, and are still again. Nothing hunts at random. The ecosystem is in motion the way a clock is in motion: multiple processes, meshed, none of them loud.

There is a stranger rule, and it takes longest to trust. The moths’ dust is not waste. Where it settles on the warm metal it crusts, and the crust, warmed, softens to a sweet film—the smell you noticed at the mouth—and the smallest life eats the film, and the crabs eat the smallest life, and the belt carries the uneaten dust out through the wall, and what returns, if anything returns, is a faint grease on the rubber that the moths will not land on. A closed circuit of manufacture running through bodies. The room is a mill that uses no grinding except living. Scaled production, pocket-held. You will not find the product outside. By the time it leaves the berm it has already been something else in the works, anonymized into a larger flow. Only here is it visible as what it is: a gray yield, continuous, quiet, dependent on the room staying packed and young and warm.

Light remains plain. That is part of the strangeness, not a relief from it. No colored shaft, no unnatural gloom. Noon is noon, reduced to a bright rectangle on the floor that the crabs avoid and the moths border exactly, their wing-edges making a clean frame around the patch as if light were a territory with the same rules as water. When a cloud passes over the coast, the rectangle dims and every surface-animal stills for the length of the dimming, then resumes. They are not waiting for wonder. They are reading an ordinary sky through a single aperture, and the reading is precise.

If you shift your weight you displace a life. There is no neutral stance. A heel comes down on skins and on the edge of a territory, and the line of crabs reforms around the intrusion without panic and without sound, closing until the packed condition is restored. The room’s density is not crowding in the sense of shortage. It is saturation as a law. A gap is a malfunction. The belt’s scuff, the gauge’s needle, the crystal’s nightly addition, the thread’s advance, the moth surface’s respiration—all of these are presences, and the spaces between them are also occupied, by dust in transit, by the pressure of the unheard plant, by the warmth moving through the wall. Fully occupied, and still mostly quiet, because occupation here is not commotion. It is fit.

Toward evening the thread fails to renew. The varnish dulls. The birds leave in the order they came, a soft displacement at the mouth, and are gone into a coast that has no knowledge of them. The moths loosen by a fraction; you can see, briefly, the concrete between wings, a grid of hairline absences, and then they settle tighter, as if the loosening had been a risk. The junction cools toward the temperature of the slab. The beads stop traveling and simply hang, heavy, reflecting the last ordinary light. The crystal on the ceiling edge glints once and goes out of distinction. Somewhere in the wall the belt’s flaw is still on its way back. You will not hear it arrive.

What remains, when you are back on the apron and the split is only a split again, is the sense of a world with a small number of hard courtesies. Do not leave a gap. Do not weather. Do not call out. Take the moisture when it is offered and do not ask it to be a stream. Let the machines keep their circuit; they are not remains, they are the tempo. The place is hidden not because it is secret in the manner of a vault, but because it is a single feature, complete at the scale of a room, embedded in a low warm industrial flat that has no reason to advertise an organ. It is still becoming itself. That is why the edges cut. That is why, if you return tomorrow, the chalk arrow will be fainter, the crystal longer by a hair, the clog turned a fraction by something that needed the space beneath the gauge, and the room—damp where it must be, bare of green, loud with nothing, packed past the possibility of an empty inch—will be a little more exactly what it already was.
