The plain has forgotten how to be anything but itself. From the low glass swells that pass for hills you can see the whole of it, and the whole of it is nearly level: a high, cool shelf worn past the memory of mountains, open enough that a town sits in it the way a knot sits in a rope, not the way a city sits on a map. Distance here is honest. Nothing rises far enough to hide the next thing. What hides things is the light, and the light is a long gray-amber dusk that never quite arrives and never quite leaves, milked down through a hanging weather of shed scales.

There is no water. The last of it went into the stone so long ago that the stone no longer remembers being wet. What moves instead is a closed oil, thick and cool, never beading on the outside of anything. You can walk all day and the dust stays dry on your boots. If you cut the ground, the cut does not bleed outward. The solvent climbs back into the tissue that made it, and the wound draws shut with a sound like a thumb dragged across a drumhead.

The ground is the plant. Not a covering, not a crop: the substrate itself, a living crust grown into its final form and then kept there. Erosion finished its work ages ago. What remains cannot be worn much further, because the crust heals at the speed of weather and slightly faster than the wind. It is thin in most places, a felt of interlocked ribs and waxy plates the color of old bone and tarnished copper, and it is total. Every path is a taught flatness in that same body. Every wall of the settlement is a thickened rib, persuaded, over centuries, to stand. Between the ribs the plain goes on, sparse as a held breath: much sky, much crust, few verticals. You can see the town from far off as a scatter of hollow towers, not a mass. They are far enough apart that a person walking the lanes spends more time between buildings than inside their shadow.

The scales in the air are the crust’s own bloom, microscopic platelets shed on a cycle the people mark as evening even though the sky does not darken in any way you could swear to. They hang, and they dim the sun, and they make a soft dry hiss when the wind takes them in sheets across the open. Under that, the crust itself sounds. Its tissues hold a charge the way a string holds a note. Wind walking the swells plays it. Footsteps play it. The plated grazers, slow and numerous, comb the bloom with mouths that are not teeth so much as rakes, and each combing is a bright tick that the plain repeats for a long way. They do not look abundant until you stop. Then you hear that the emptiness is full of them: a scattered herd on the near swell, another beyond the glass rise, and between them the air stitched with click-fliers, membrane kites no larger than a hand, riding static and talking to each other in hard little cracks. The predators are fewer and worse to see. They do not hunt by sight. They stand with their long heads against the crust and listen for a rhythm that has stumbled.

The town is built the way the plain grows, and then built past that. The ribs have been taught to carry more than oil. In the oldest lanes the floors ring true, a civic surface, and if you kneel you can see the newer work: hair-fine lattices seated in the living wall, not metal, not quite light, a threading that current speech has no plain word for. They drink the same charge the crust drinks. They remember. A terrace that has been walked for a few centuries keeps a raised ghost of the common routes, a polish and a low ridge, and the lattices read that polish back as a kind of slow map. Doors are not hung so much as opened in the rib and held open by a tension the wall agrees to. At night—which is only a deepening of the same dim—the walls give up a little of what they stored, a vein-glow at ankle height, enough to walk by if you know the lanes. People still carry lamps. The lamps are small and selfish. The plain does not like a brighter light than it makes itself; brighter light makes the scale-weather swirl and the song go thin.

Sound is the real infrastructure, louder than the place looks. The towers are acoustic as much as they are shelter. Each one is hollow, grown around a throat of stiffened tissue, and they speak to each other across the open without needing the lattices. A change in wind arrives first as a change in the town’s voice: the western tower drops a third, the market rib answers high and short, and people who have lived here adjust their work before they bother to look up. Market is a poor word. Goods move, but so does weather, and the distinction is sloppy. The oil-conduits in the ribs are also the cool of the place, bleeding heat out into the thin high air, so the town is always a little colder than the plain, and the cold has a pitch. Stand in the central lane at the hour when the grazers turn and the whole settlement is a chord with too many notes in it. The click-fliers come in to the glow and add their cracks. Somewhere a tower is being persuaded to add a story, a centimeter this season, and that persuasion creaks.

Nothing pools. That is the rule the children learn before they learn the lanes. Spill a lamp’s oil, or the solvent from a cut conduit, and it does not lie there. It climbs. Skin of the crust, hem of a coat, the side of a boot: it finds a tissue it recognizes and goes home. Rain is a story from lower country, told badly. What the sky does here is shed, charge, and occasionally strike. Dry lightning walks the swells in season, blue-white and without thunder in the way lowlands mean thunder—the thunder is the plain answering, a roar that starts underfoot and arrives in the ear already old. The crust drinks those strikes. For a day afterward the vein-glow runs higher in the walls, and the grazers avoid the struck rises, and the town’s lattices dream in public: faint, repeating ridges of old footsteps appearing and fading on terraces that have not been walked since morning. People do not step on the dreams if they can help it. It is not forbidden. It is only that the dreams sound, very softly, like someone still there.

The ecology is all one metabolism wearing different shapes. The crust feeds on charge, on the thin gases, on the mineral dust the wind still manages to lift off the glass swells, and on the careful wounding of the grazers, which crop bloom and dead plate and leave the living rib alone. The click-fliers eat the smaller motes that ride the scale-weather. The listeners eat whatever makes a clumsy rhythm. People eat a pressed cake of bloom and conduit-sap, and they eat the grazers when a herd agrees, by some long bargain, to be diminished. Nothing in this bargain requires a river. The solvent stays inside. Waste is taken back into the rib and taught, by the lattices and by older gardeners’ hands, to become wall again. A death on the plain is loud and then brief. The crust closes over what it can use, and the towers report the new silence, and after a while the silence is just distance again.

There are districts where the town stops being legible as town. The ribs flatten. The lattices thin out. You are on the open crust, and the nearest tower is a pale pipe on the horizon, still singing. Out there the weirdness of the place stops performing and simply continues. Sound carries wrong. A voice spoken toward the glass swell comes back from the side, late, with a word added that you did not say—usually a word the wind has been saying all afternoon in the towers, now borrowed. The horizon flexes, not with heat-mirage of water, there is no water to counterfeit, but with a slow bend in the scale-haze that makes the next swell seem to breathe. Animals cross these bends without trouble. People get lost when they navigate by eye. The instruction, older than the lattices, is to walk by the song: if the crust under you goes quiet, you have stepped into a listening, and something heavy has stopped moving so it can hear you back.

The settlement’s edge is not a wall. It is a decision the crust renews. Beyond the last persuaded rib, the plain is still plant, still total, still the same body, only untaught. It could be more town. It does not care to be. Engineers from the towers sometimes lay a new lattice-line out across the open, a gesture toward a road or a second market, and for a season the crust accepts it and rings underfoot like a lane. Then the line dulls. The oil reroutes. The song of that path detunes until only the grazers use it, and they were using the plain anyway. The town takes this without offense. It has been the same size, roughly, for longer than its records, which are kept as resonances in the oldest throats and occasionally come out as weather. Growth here is a thickening, not a spread. Height is a courtesy the towers make to each other so their voices do not tangle. The final form was reached before anyone now living decided to live in it. They inhabit a conclusion.

If you stay through a full shedding and a full strike-season, the place stops being a set of wonders and becomes a pace. Morning is when the western tower climbs back to its ordinary fifth and the scale-haze lifts from ankle to head. Midday is not brighter so much as busier: combs ticking, kites cracking, the conduits cool enough to fog a breath that vanishes at once in the dry air. The long dim is when the walls give their glow back and the listeners come in closer to the lanes, polite, hoping for a stumbled step. People sleep in the ribs, where the song is loudest and therefore safest. Quiet would mean the crust had found something more interesting than their hearts. They sleep well. The oil moves under them, cool, sealed, endless in its circuit and nowhere to be seen. Above the hollow towers the platelets drift, and the high shelf holds its shape, ancient and level and alive, making the only weather it has left.
