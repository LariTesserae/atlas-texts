The air up here is too thin to hold a shout and too hot to reward one. It arrives already used—scraped over new stone, passed through vents, warmed from underneath—so that breathing feels like drawing on a kiln door left ajar. The sun is ordinary. Shadows fall where they should. What is wrong is the ground, which has not yet decided what it is, and the city, which has decided anyway.

The range is still being born. From the outer rim, looking in, the land does not read as mountains so much as a slow extrusion: pale ridges shouldering up out of a high basin, their faces unstained, their edges still sharp enough to cut a glove. Nothing has had time to round them. Rain, when it comes, is a brief metallic smell and a darkening that never quite becomes weather; it beads on the rock and is gone before it can cut a gully. Erosion here is a rumor the stone has only just begun to hear. In the freshest cuts you can see the grain of the making—flow-bands, glassy seams, a sugar of crystals that have not yet been dulled by any century of wind. Put a hand to a south face at midday and the heat is not stored sunlight alone. The rock is still cooling from its own arrival. In places it ticks, very quietly, as it contracts.

The basin is wide enough that the far rim goes blue with distance even in this thin air, a regional bowl of unfinished country, and every shelf of it is occupied. The settlement does not sit on the land so much as continue it. Terraces of works follow the young ridges the way fields would follow a river in a softer country, except there are no fields, and the river is a fault that sweats. Stacked sheds, condenser halls, rail beds cut into faces that were bare mineral a generation ago, cable-ways slung between spurs, cisterns cupped into the stone where the stone has sagged. Density is vertical as much as lateral. You can stand on a catwalk and touch the wall of the level above with a raised hand, and still see, between two stacks, a kilometer of further stacks diminishing toward the rim. The city is a town in law and a region in extent. Its streets are service lanes. Its plazas are yards where slag is allowed to cool in measured sheets.

Industry here does not clatter. The machines are too large and too slow for that, and the air is a poor messenger. Turbines the size of houses turn on cycles measured in days. Kilns hold their heat the way the ridges do, banking it, releasing it on schedules posted in chalk that the sun fades before the work is done. Presses bite rock into brick, into pipe, into the facing-stone the city uses to persuade the new ground to stay put. Electrified lines run in trays along every terrace, but the current is a low, patient thing; you feel it more as a sweetness in the teeth when you lean on a rail than as any bright arc. Production is scaled, mechanized, and almost ceremonial. A shift can pass with nothing louder than a bearing warming up. The quiet is not peace. It is the sound of processes that do not need witnesses.

Water keeps to pockets and films. There is no living river. There is a daily condensation line, a sourceless fog that climbs out of the lower vents around the middle of the morning and hangs at a fixed elevation like a second horizon, wetting the undersides of gantries and leaving the upper walks dry and glaring. Below that line the metal grows a fur of mineral scale, white and rust and a green that is not botanical—copper, or something that behaves like copper and is not. Above it, the heat takes moisture off the tongue. People drink from cisterns sunk into fault-cups, where steam has found a hollow and cooled into a dark, metallic water that tastes of the rock’s childhood. After the rare rain, those cups overfill and sheet down the faces in brief silver, and for an hour the whole basin glitters, and then the glitter is gone and the cuts it made are so shallow you can miss them unless you knew the face yesterday. The land is learning to be worn. It is not good at it yet.

Nothing grows in the way a valley expects. No grass in the joints, no tree making a claim on a ledge, no weed in the grit along the rails. The ecology is a mineral one, and it is busy. Scale blooms and flakes. Salt creeps up the outsides of the cisterns in delicate boxes that collapse if you breathe on them. In the damp band, films spread over stone—slick, iridescent, not plant, not quite lifeless—feeding on whatever the steam brings up from below and on the slow leak of the works. They map the moisture. Where a pipe sweats, a darker fan appears by evening and is paler by noon. Workers scrape them on a schedule, not from hatred but because a film left alone will eventually lift a facing-plate. The scrapings are kiln-fodder. The city eats its own weather.

Animals are an event. Most days you will see none. The heat and the lack of anything soft to eat have kept the ordinary traffic of life to a rumor on the outer rim, where the rock is a little older and a little more willing to hold a crack. What lives inside the basin is rare enough that people stop for it. There is a moth, or something that occupies the office of a moth, pale as the new stone, that settles on warm metal and seems to drink the scale. There are, in the deepest cisterns, slow things with shells like cooled slag, almost never seen, known by the clean rings they leave where they have grazed a film. Once or twice a season a high, thin flock crosses the basin without landing, and the yards go quieter than quiet, as if the machines had noticed. No one farms them. No one quite knows what they are doing here. Their rarity is part of the climate.

The strangeness is not a monster at the edge. It is a rule that does not match the rules you carried up with you. The newest ridges extrude, in places, already geometric—faces too plane, corners too near a right angle, as if the crust, pushing up, had caught the city’s habit of making and begun to imitate it from underneath. Surveyors argue about this in the way of people who must stake foundations on it. Some say the works have been here long enough to teach the stone. Some say the stone was always going to come up in planes, and the city only recognized a kinship and built along it. Either way, the boundary fails. A wall begun as masonry will, over years, take on the flow-band of the ridge it stands against, or the ridge will develop a course of blocks that no mason set. Foundations rise and settle by millimeters across a decade. The important profession is not engineering in the lowland sense but continuous re-leveling. Streets acquire a lean. Doors are rehung. A lane that was flat when your parents learned it will meet your ankle wrong. The city does not fight this so much as budget for it. Change is slow enough to live inside and too steady to ignore.

Light stays honest. Morning is hard and white on the fresh faces, noon is a pressure on the shoulders, evening lays long shadows down the terraces so that the stacked works read, briefly, as a single instrument. Color is the color of raw mineral and of what the kilns have done to it: bone, sulfur, iron, the black of glass where a face cooled too fast, the dull red of brick still giving up its heat after dark. At night the basin does not blaze. A few lamps, the dull mouths of kilns, the occasional pale square of a condenser hall. The stars are insultingly clear. You can hear, if you hold still on an upper walk, the tick of cooling facing-stone and, far below the condensation line, a long breath as some vent decides to ease. That is most of the night’s noise. People speak low, not from law but because the air taxes a raised voice and returns so little of it. Conversations happen at arm’s length. News travels by slate and by the angle of a signal arm on a ridge, moving once an hour.

There is a place, out near the unfinished rim, where the city thins into survey stakes and the rock is so new it still smells faintly sweet when the sun is on it, a smell like hot glass and wet iron. No film yet. No cistern. The ground underfoot rings, thin and high, the way a bowl rings before it is fired all the way. Stand there and the basin of works lies open below you, dense as a circuit, quiet as a held breath, steaming along its middle elevation in a line as level as a rule. Beyond the far rim there is only more height, paler, not yet claimed, still shouldering up. The heat comes off it in a shimmer that does not ripple the way lowland heat ripples. It stands. It is patient. The city will reach it, or it will reach the city, and the difference, by then, will be a matter for the surveyors, who already have the stakes cut and stacked in the shade, waiting on a process that does not hurry and does not stop.
