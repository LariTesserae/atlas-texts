The basin sits high enough that the air should be thin and the mornings cold, and neither of those things is true. You come up onto it by a long shoulder of stone and the heat meets you before the view does: a dry, even warmth, the kind that belongs to late afternoon in a low country, held here all day as if the elevation had been negotiated away. The light is already full when you arrive. It does not climb. It is simply present, white-gold, with almost no haze, so that a ridge two hours' walk off looks close enough to count the seams in it.

The land itself is finished. That is the first impression that lasts. Gullies have cut as far as they mean to cut. Talus has settled into fans whose edges no longer creep. The pale rock — a warm limestone shot with iron, going rose where the light hits it edge-on — shows the rounding of a very long weather, and then stops. Nothing is crumbling today. Nothing is being built up. The profiles have the look of a sentence that found its last word centuries ago and has been content, since, to be read.

Across the middle of the basin a shallow trough runs, not quite a valley, not quite a wash. In it the stone darkens. That is where the water lives, and it does not live loudly. There is no river you can follow with your ear. What there is: a thread of damp in the lowest crease, sometimes a hand's width of actual flow, sometimes only a darkening that you feel cooler when you kneel. Side pockets hold water longer — rock cups, old solution hollows, a few places where a slab has tilted and made a shadow-tank. In the brightest hours a film comes out of the air and beads on the shaded undersides of ledges, even while the sun is high and the open ground is hot to the palm. The beads do not fall. They travel, slowly, along grooves too regular to be only joints in the stone, and arrive, eventually, at the cups. By late day the cups are a little fuller than they were at dawn. Overnight some of that is given back to the air. The exchange is quiet enough that you can stand in the trough and hear your own swallow.

Plants keep to those receipts of moisture and do not pretend otherwise. On the open shelves there is almost nothing: a rust-colored crust that is not quite lichen, a scatter of hard tufts no higher than an ankle, bleached at the tips. In the damp crease the growth thickens without ever becoming cover — knee-high shrubs with small gray leaves, a mat of succulent rosettes in the cups, and, where a seep has been faithful for a long time, a single dark tree, trunk twisted, canopy no wider than a room, casting a shadow that is the only deep shade for a long way in any direction. You can see five or six of these trees from a high point, each one a decision the water made and kept. Between them the ground is stone, grit, and light.

The animals do not seem to have read the vegetation. They are everywhere the eye learns to look, and they are not loud about it. A herd of long-legged grazers — dun, narrow, with a pale blaze that catches the sun — moves across a slope that appears to offer them nothing. They pause often, heads low, not cropping so much as pressing their muzzles to the crust, and when they lift them their jaws work on something you cannot see leave the rock. Smaller things use the same ground differently: a low, quick mammal the color of the iron stain, hunting the cracks; birds that do not call, only relocate, their shadows flicking over the shelves and gone. In the water-cups there are things with too many joints, pale, that come up into the brightness for a few minutes and then fold back under the lip of stone. None of this chorus makes a chorus. Hooves tick. Wings displace air. A grazer snorts once, and the sound goes out across the basin and does not return. The quiet is not emptiness. It is the register the place has chosen, and the life in it has matched.

If you stay through the middle of the day you begin to see the other motion, the one that is not animals. Heat rises off the shelves in slow sheets, visible only where it bends a far ridge. The condensation films on the ledges thicken and thin on a period shorter than weather — you can watch a groove fill, empty, fill again, while the sky stays clear. A grazer herd that was scattered draws together without a signal you can hear, travels a precise distance, and disperses again onto a new crust. Something has hunted, somewhere: you find the evidence as a brief darkening on stone and a ring of the small crack-mammals standing unusually still, and then that too is over, and the stillness loosens. The basin is busy. It conducts its business at the volume of cloth moving.

The built things are easy to miss, and then, once seen, a little hard to stop seeing. They are not ruins in the sentimental sense. On a spur above the trough, a low wall of the same limestone, three courses, no mortar you can find, ending where it ends. In the throat of a side gully, a flat disk set flush with the ground, the size of a table, its surface a degree smoother than any water-worn face nearby, and warm — warmer than the sun accounts for — if you lay a hand on it. Farther out, where the basin lifts toward its rim, a line of posts no taller than a person, spaced so widely that the line is an idea more than a fence. Some have lost their tops. One has a hollow in it that is not a socket and not a nest, a recess whose inner surface is darker than the outer stone and very slightly iridescent when the light rakes it. Nothing is inscribed. Nothing is recent in the way of fresh cuts, and nothing is abandoned in the way of collapse. They sit in the finished landscape as if they had weathered into the same equilibrium as the gullies, and then declined to weather any further.

What they are doing is plainer in the processes than in the objects. The warmth at this height does not leak away at dusk the way it should; it steps down, over an hour, to a night that is mild and then holds. The cups do not fail in a dry week. The crust the grazers feed on regenerates its color behind them within a day, a mineral film knitting back across the scraped place, and if you look at the knitting with a glass it has a grain too even for chemistry left alone — a repeat, a preference, a decision at a scale smaller than the crack-mammals' claws. The posts, the disks, the short walls are not the machinery. They are the places where the machinery becomes briefly willing to be touched. The rest of it is distributed through the stone and the air and the habit of the water, an engineering that does not need housing because the basin is the housing. It does not announce itself. It keeps the books.

There is a strangeness, and it is not theatrical. Your shadow at noon is a little too short for the height of the sun you think you see, and a second, fainter shadow sometimes lies a hand's width to the left of the first, as if a light you cannot find were standing just off the bright one. The grazers, when they gather, all face the same empty quarter of the basin for the length of a breath, then forget it. Dew climbs. A bird will hang in the air longer than its wingbeats justify, not hovering so much as being held, and then remember itself and go. None of this frightens the other animals. They have arranged their lives around it the way river fish arrange themselves around a current. The rules here are stable. They are only not entirely the rules of the stone as stone.

By the time the light thins — it does not set so much as consent to lessen — the basin has shown you its scale. You can walk it in a day if you do not linger, and you will linger. From the rim you see the trough, the five dark trees, the spur with its wall, the far ridge with its seams, and beyond that ridge another shelf of the same rose stone, and the suggestion of a third. It is a region, not a world: a run of high, warm, finished country, repeating itself with small variations, each basin keeping its own cups and its own herds and its own few quiet artifacts. Sound does not carry from one to the next. What passes between them is the light, the mild night, and whatever agreement the stone has made with the work inside it.

If you sleep on the spur, the disk below you stays warm through the dark. The herds bed down in loose scatter, visible as paler shapes on pale ground. Something small ticks in a crack and then decides against it. The condensation begins its night travel along the grooves. You can hear, if you hold still long enough, the minute sound of water choosing a path it has chosen before — a soft tick, a softer answer — and then even that subsides, and the basin continues, active and nearly silent, under a sky that never quite empties of brightness.
