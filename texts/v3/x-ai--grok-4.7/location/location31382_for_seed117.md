The place is smaller than the weather that feeds it. You come down into it the way you come down into a cistern: a few steps of cut stone, then the air changes, and the sky, if there still is one, becomes a slot. The whole of it would fit inside a church nave if a church were sunk to its roofline and left to take water. Walls close in before the eye expects them. Overhead, a lip of rock and old concrete holds a thin gray light, not enough to throw a shadow that stays.

Everything here is already wet and has been wet so long that dryness would look like damage. Water does not arrive as a stream so much as it reports itself: beading on the undersides of flanges, sliding out of hairline cracks in the stone, standing in every dish the ground has learned to make. The floor is a shallow, irregular sheet, rarely deeper than a hand, broken by ridges of sinter and by the rounded backs of buried iron. Where you put a foot, the film parts and closes again with almost no sound. The rock weeps in places that have wept for decades; the streaks are the color of tea and old copper, and they do not move while you watch, though by evening they have lengthened by a finger.

There is no green. Nothing rooted, nothing leafed, nothing that could be called a stem. What covers the stone is mineral and film: a glaze the thickness of paint, olive-black in the dim, copper where the light finds a fresh seep, chalk-white where a drip has built a little chimney no taller than a thumb. It is alive in the way scale is alive in a boiler—layered, feeding, not a plant. Scrape it and it smells of metal and cellar. Leave it and it knits back over the bright place in a day. The walls are upholstered in it. So are the pipes.

The works are scattered, not a factory so much as the furniture a factory leaves when it is finished being a factory and becomes a drain. A pump housing the size of a wardrobe, its paint gone to a sugary rust that does not flake anymore; the rust has settled into the same patience as the stone. A run of rail, three meters, ending in nothing, its top polished not by wheels but by the passage of something low and heavy that comes through at night. A bank of ceramic insulators, cracked, each cup holding its own still water. Cable that has become a root in reverse: it enters the wall and does not come out, and the wall has grown a lip around it as if accepting a graft. None of it is maintained. None of it is quite dead. Somewhere under the floor a motor turns on a long, stupid cycle—an hour, then silence, then an hour—and when it turns, a tremor goes through the standing water in rings too small to see unless you are looking at a reflection. The exhaust of that work is heat so slight you only notice it as a place where the cool does not quite win. Everywhere else the air is cool the way a cave is cool, the way wet cloth is cool against the wrist.

The animals are many, and they are close, and they do not announce themselves. In the first minutes you think the place is empty except for the drip. Then the density resolves. Along the waterline, dozens of shells like flattened rivets, each the color of the film they graze. They move when the motor’s tremor passes, a collective shift of a few millimeters, then stillness. In the flanges of the pump, nests of pale, jointed things, finger-length, that hold still with their undersides against the iron, drinking warmth the way other animals drink at a bank. They have no eyes you can find. When one relocates it does it in a single fast pour, then locks again, and the sound is a wet tick. Higher, in the ceramic cups, something smaller breeds in water that never quite empties: clouds of larvae like ground pepper, rising and settling on a rhythm that is not the motor’s. They are food for a creature you hear before you place—a dry, careful scratch inside a pipe, stopping when you stop, starting when you start. It is not large. The pipe is not large. There are many pipes.

Nothing hunts in the open. The abundance is layered, each layer almost touching the next: film, grazers, the jointed drinkers, the scratchers in the conduits, and above them, where the slot of light is strongest, a scatter of bats or bat-like things that do not roost so much as adhere, wings folded into the same silhouette as the broken insulators, so that you count fixtures and then count again and the number has changed. They leave and return without cries. Their droppings do not feed plants; they feed the film, and the film is what the shells graze, and the circle is small enough that you can see most of it from one dry patch of iron if you do not move.

Sound stays low because everything that could be loud has already happened and settled. The drip is not a drip so much as a continuous small transfer, many points, none of them dramatic. The motor is a presence more than a noise, a pressure in the teeth. Animals pass with the acoustics of wet leather on wet stone. Now and then a bubble rises from somewhere under the sheet and breaks with a sound like a fingertip on a bottle. If you speak, the place takes the voice and returns a short, dull version of it, as if the film on the walls were eating the edges of the words.

The strangeness is not spectacle. It is proportion and diet. Things here are the size of hardware, and they live on hardware, and the water that should have grown reeds has instead grown a skin that everything else agrees to eat. A shell will cross a bolt and pause, and the pause has the look of recognition, as if the bolt were a landmark older than the animal and younger than the rock. At the far end of the basin—twenty paces, no more—a seam in the concrete breathes. Not air. A slow extrusion of clear gel, cold, beading and breaking off and being taken immediately by the grazers, which gather at that seam more thickly than anywhere else and do not compete so much as queue. Whatever is behind the seam is not visible and does not need to be. The balance has included it for a long time. The stain under the seam is the same stain as the weeping rock: tea, copper, patience.

Weather happens here in miniature. The slot overhead darkens and the light goes from gray to almost nothing, and the condensation increases until the air itself feels occupied. Then a fine fall, not quite rain, sifts down and rings the water in a pattern too dense to follow. The shells clamp. The jointed drinkers flatten harder against the iron. The motor, indifferent, begins its hour. When the slot lightens again, the sheet has risen by a centimeter and begun, very quietly, to find the old overflow: a notch in the far wall, worn smooth, carrying the excess away into a darkness that is not this place. The basin keeps what it can hold. It has been keeping it long enough that the notch, the film, the rust, and the animals are one settled fact.

You can stand in the middle of it and touch, without stepping, a wall, a pipe, the skin of the water, and the cool. Everything is near. Nothing is growing in the way a field grows. And still the place is busy—transfer, grazing, the stupid faithful motor, the seam giving up its gel, the overflow taking its due—busy at a volume just under the level of notice, in a room the earth has finished making and has not yet decided to close.
