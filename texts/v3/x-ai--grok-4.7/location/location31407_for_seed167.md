The basin holds a town the way a mouth holds a word it no longer says. Low ground, old ground: a shallow inland sea no deeper than a few long breaths in most places, deeper only where the streets once ran and the water has kept them. The surface, if there is one, is not a clean line. It is a skin of warm film, pollen-fine silt, and slow rain that has been falling so long it has forgotten it is rain. Light does not come down through it. What light there is is body-light — a dull green seam along a flank, a pulse the color of old copper — and even that is rare enough that the ordinary condition is black water, warm as breath, thick enough that a hand pushed forward meets resistance before it meets anything else.

The settlement is not ruined in the way of fallen stone. It has finished becoming what the water asked of it. Walls stand because generations of shell-makers have mortared themselves to whatever last hard edge remained, and then to each other, until the original courses are only a rumor inside the mass. Streets are lanes of slower current between those masses. Roofs are living lids: plates of calcium, overlapping, worn smooth on their upper faces by the constant small traffic of bodies, pitted underneath where something has been feeding in the dark for longer than any single life. Nothing here was cut or joined by a tool. The city is a habit of animals that build, repeated until the habit has the size of a district and the patience of weather.

You can feel the far side of it. Not a horizon — there is no horizon — but a change in the push of the water, a cooler thread where the basin wall rises and the town thins out into bare marl and old roots that never became forest. The whole place is about the reach of a long walk if walking were possible: several quarters, a central sink where three lanes meet and the water turns in a slow wheel, outlying mounds that might once have been yards. Beyond that the basin closes. This is not an ocean. It is a held place, valley-sized, with edges.

Vegetation is almost an absence. A few pale ribbons cling where a seep brings minerals up through the floor; they do not branch, and they do not make shade, because there is no light to shade. Most of the growth that looks like plant is not. It is the body of the builders themselves — tubes, fans, low crusts — arranged with the unintentional order of anything that must leave a gap for water to pass. Between them the water is crowded. Life here does not spread out. It stacks.

In any arm’s length there are more animals than the dark can sort. Tiny armored things tick along the plates, so many that a wall has a grain, like cloth. Larger ones cruise the lanes at the height where the current is least work: blunt, warm-blooded in the loose sense of anything that keeps its own heat in water already warm, brushing the masonry with a flank and correcting without urgency. In the wheel at the center they circle in layers, each layer a different size, none of them hurrying, all of them feeding on what the layer above sheds. The smallest are a grit in the water itself. You do not see them. You taste the water as slightly alive, and the taste does not change from one quarter to the next.

They are quiet. Not silent — silence would be a kind of drama — but pared down to what water cannot help making. A soft knock when two plates settle against each other after something large has passed. The minute sand-rush of a school changing depth. Now and then a single click, structural, as a tube seals or a joint in the crust gives along a line it has been giving along for years. No voices. Whatever these animals know about each other, they do not put it in the air. The rain on the skin above is the loudest thing, and even that arrives already blurred, a hush with weather inside it.

The place is working. Sediment lifts in the wheel and settles again in the side lanes, a little higher each season on the thresholds, a little lower where a seep keeps a patch scoured clean. A wall sloughs a palm-sized scale; before the scale has drifted to the floor, three different kinds of tenant have claimed the new edge, and by the time the scale lands it has its own colony on the underside, already beginning the long argument of which face will be up. Feeding happens in pulses you could set a slow clock by: a dimming of the small lights, a thickening of the grit, then the gradual return of the ordinary murk as what was eaten becomes what will be built. Predators are not dramatic. They are the ones who move across the grain instead of with it, and the grain closes behind them without comment.

Nothing is new here in the sense of unfinished. The flood is not an event. It is the climate of the basin, established past the point of commentary, and the town has the look of something that has already negotiated its terms — which cracks may widen, which lanes must stay open, how much weight a roof of shells can carry before it becomes floor for the next roof. Erosion and growth have the same pace. A corner rounded by centuries of warm silt is also a corner being sharpened again by the animals that prefer an edge. The balance is not peaceful. It is occupied.

If there is anything unusual, it is only this: the city remembers streets. Not as memory, not as intention, but as a stubbornness in the plan. Lanes do not wander the way a reef wanders. They keep a rough grid, correcting themselves when a collapse tries to bend them, because the current prefers the old lines and the builders follow the current. Stand in the central wheel — if standing were a thing the water allowed — and you would feel four darker pulls, four ways out, and above you the rain still arriving, and around you the dense, warm, unspeaking press of a town that has no lamps, no tools, no name it can speak, and no need of one. It is simply what low ground does, given enough time and enough bodies, once the water has decided to stay.
