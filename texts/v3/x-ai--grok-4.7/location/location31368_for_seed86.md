The sound arrives before any shape does. It is not an echo looking for a wall; it is already the weather of the place, a continuous low roar with a higher, drier skitter threaded through it, as if rain were falling on a roof of wings. Standing at the lip, you feel it in the sternum more than you hear it with the ears. The lip itself is new. The rock there has not had time to round. Fracture faces still show the grain of the break, pale where the outer country rock is darker, edges keen enough to catch what little light comes over the rim and throw it back as a thin metallic line.

What failed was a ceiling. Not long ago, in the life of stone — recently enough that the talus below still looks shoveled rather than settled — a broad plate of the upper country dropped. The surface you would call ground is now a rim, a ragged oval many miles in circuit, and everything that matters is beneath it. The collapse did not open a pit so much as unroof a valley that had already been carving itself in the dark. You look down into a country: several basins stepping away from one another, low divides of unbroken rock between them, a far wall that takes a long time to resolve because the light does not help you. Town-sized, if a town were only absence. Valley-sized, if a valley had no sky worth the name.

The light is what leaks. At the rim it is ordinary day, cool and a little thin, and by the time it has fallen the height of the walls it is a permanent late dusk, gray-brown, without a sun-disk and without a true night. Nothing down there casts a hard shadow. The illumination does not travel; it pools, weaker in the farther basins, slightly stronger where a notch in the rim lets a shaft lie across a slope for part of the day and then slide off. There is no weather to speak of inside. The air is cool the way a cellar is cool, the same cool in what would be summer and what would be winter, and it does not move except where the animals move it. Breath, if you stand long enough, shows and then doesn't, a small private fog that the larger dampness swallows.

Water is present the way damp is present in a cloth left overnight, not the way a river is present. Seeps emerge on the fresh faces — not springs with mouths, just darkenings that widen, film that gathers, drops that form with the patience of the rock and fall into a sound already too full to notice them. Shallow pools occupy the lows of the basins, tea-colored, still at their centers, ringed with a mineral crust no thicker than a fingernail. Between pools the floor is dry to the touch and cool, and then, twenty paces on, damp again where drip from an unseen joint has been falling in the same place since the roof came down. Condensation beads on the undersides of ledges and on the backs of stones. There is enough moisture to keep the air from being sharp, not enough to drink without searching, and no channel has yet cut itself a bed. The water has not had time. It collects, stalls, and leaves.

The stone agrees with that youth. This is not a country of worn corridors and softened pillars. The walls are joint-bounded plates, some of them still seated, some tipped and jammed, their edges bright with the unweathered mineral. Screes at the wall-foot are angular; the pieces have not yet begun to fit one another. A few large blocks lie isolated on the basin floors, whole fragments of the old roof, their upper surfaces still carrying a skin of the soil and the sparse turf that grew on them when they were the world above. That turf is dying in place, browning from the margins, because the light that reaches it is no longer the light it was made for. Around those blocks the new floor is bare shattered rock, pale dust in the joints, nothing accumulated. Erosion here is only the first gesture: grit loosened by drip, a grain at a time, far too little to soften a single edge.

Plants have barely begun, and they will not do much. Where a seep keeps a face wet and a notch gives a few hours of stronger dimness, a film of algae darkens the stone, and a moss, small and tight, holds the film in place. In the soil-capped blocks a few grasses persist, yellowing. Near one pool a fern has taken, waist-high, its fronds the brightest green in the basin and still not bright. No trees. No understory. Roots from the rim country dangle in a few places where the collapse tore them free, pale, already drying, not exploring. The vegetation is an incident, not a cover. You can walk for a long time and see only rock, water-skin, and the droppings.

The life is not in the plants. The life is the air, the walls, the sound.

They occupy the vertical. The basins' floors are almost empty — wide, stony, scattered with blocks and pools, the kind of openness that makes a lone figure look misplaced — and everything living has chosen the stone above that emptiness. The lower walls, within reach of the dimmest light, are ledged with birds: not a decorative scattering but sheets of them, shoulder to shoulder along every horizontal that will take a foot, thousands on a single face, and the face runs on. They are ordinary cliff birds, the kind that use a crack and need nothing built, gray-backed, pale underneath, with the quick aggressive turns of animals that have never had to leave a wall to find what they eat. Higher, where the light fails into true dusk, the roosts change character. Wings fold differently. The stone disappears under a living nap of small bodies hung and crowded, and when some signal passes through them a portion lifts and the nap ripples and reseats, and the roar changes pitch by a degree you feel more than hear.

Between the two bands, and in the open volume of the basins, the insects work. They are not a cloud you can walk around. They are a layer, a moving thickness at about the height of the ledges, drifting, reversing, pouring toward a seep and then away from it, loud in a dry papery way that rides on top of the deeper roar. They settle on the damp faces and lift again. They fall to the pools in a continuous small rain and are taken, or are not, and the ones that are not simply continue. In the pools themselves there is movement under the surface: shoals of small fish, or what read as fish in the poor light, holding in the tea-colored water in such numbers that the pool looks granular, and scattering only when a shadow — almost always a wing — crosses them.

Nothing hunts in a way that changes the picture. The predation is constant and local and already priced into the numbers. A bird drops, takes, returns to the same ledge. Something small and quick moves among the scree and is not often seen. The colonies do not thin. They also do not grow in any way you could watch. The place has the look of a number that has already been reached.

That is the strangest ordinary fact of it, and it is not strange so much as strict. The rock says *new*. The life says *settled*. Whatever the collapse unroofed, or drove down into, or simply revealed, the animals were either already there in the dark or arrived in a single irreversible pouring-in, and since then the count has not moved. There is no dusk chorus and no dawn thinning, because there is no dawn and no dusk, only the same dim and the same weight of bodies. Individuals die — you see the evidence, a stiffness on a ledge, a scatter of pale bone fragments among the grit, cleaned quickly — and individuals replace them, and the sheet of birds on the face remains the same sheet. The insect layer does not pass through a season. The fish hold their stations. If there is migration into this country, it happens at a scale or a slowness that leaves the view unchanged; standing here a day, or a month, you would inventory the same walls.

No one has made anything. There is no course of set stones, no soot on a ceiling, no cut mark, no discarded tool, no path worn smoother than drip would wear it. The animals build nothing that outlasts the use of it. The birds take the cracks the collapse made. The roosts above are only the grip of feet. Nests, where any exist, are a scrape and a few feathers in a pocket, gone after the season that is not really a season. If hands ever came down from the rim with a simple blade or a hafted stone, they took their work back out with them. The damp would have softened fiber, and the sheer traffic of beaks and feet would have worried any small object to pieces, but there is not even the ghost of that. The floor is innocent of making. It is only young rock, sparse water, and the refuse of an enormous, steady appetite.

The rules, such as they are, are physical and plain. Sound does not fade; it is produced as fast as it dies, so the roar is a property of the air, like temperature. Light does not improve and does not fail. Water arrives by seep and by drip at the rate it leaves by slow evaporation and by the drinking of millions of small mouths, and the pools hold their levels. Stone falls rarely now — the collapse spent itself — and when a fragment does come away it is a single event in a silence that does not actually exist, a crack lost inside the roar, and the scree accepts it without looking different afterward. You can cross a basin floor in an hour and meet nothing but angular rock, a block with its dying cap of turf, a pool dark with fish, and the drip. The walls on either side will be loud the entire way. The next basin, beyond a low divide of fresh rock, will be the same country again: open, dim, cool, barely grown, barely wet, and inhabited past any reasonable count, in a balance that does not drift.

What the collapse began, it also finished. Below the rim the world is not becoming. It has already become, all at once, and it is holding.
