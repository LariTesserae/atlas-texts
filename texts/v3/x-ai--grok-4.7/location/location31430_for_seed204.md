The place does not announce itself from the outside. At this height the mountain is still being made—edges bright, faces unrounded, a pale horn of rock pushed up faster than weather can soften it—and the only sign of the settlement is a seam, no wider than a person turned sideways, where two fresh planes failed to meet. Wind moves over the seam and does not go in. What goes in is moisture, lifted off lower air and forced against cold stone until it gives up and beads.

Inside, the seam opens into one room. Not a cavern in the old sense: no worn throat, no rubble fan, no dark that has had centuries to settle. The pocket is a single contained volume, high-walled and longer than it is wide, with a floor of sharp mineral that has not yet learned to be dust. You could walk its length in an hour if the ways were straight. They are not. The city is built along the walls and across a few spans, and the middle is left mostly empty, a pale aisle of bare rock with water moving in it.

Light comes from above and is ordinary. A lens of thinned stone—worked, not natural, though it reads as sky—lets the day through without glare. Clouds pass. At midday the floor is the color of wet salt. Toward evening the light goes blue and the water darkens first, as water does. There is no second light except what the structures keep for their own work, and that stays low, local, easy to ignore.

The rock weeps. It is young enough that its joints are still open, and cold enough that anything warmer than the stone arrives as vapor and leaves as film. Water does not pool into marshes; there is no soil to hold it. It sheets down faces, gathers in cut channels, stands in shallow pans where the floor was leveled, and disappears into slots that are part geology and part plumbing. After a weather-pulse—warm air driven up the outer slopes, then a drop—the walls glaze and clear again within hours. You can watch a condensation front travel the length of the pocket like a slow eyelid.

Nothing green has taken. There is no litter of leaves, no moss darkening the wet side, no crack where a seed found purchase. The surfaces that stay damp grow, at most, a thin mineral stain: iron, carbonate, a faint yellow that is chemistry rather than life. In a few of the oldest pans—oldest here meaning seasons, not eras—a skin forms and breaks when the level changes. It is not a plant. It does not spread. When it is gone the rock looks the same.

The settlement is real, and sparse. It has the bones of a town: levels, routes, reservoirs, a logic of districts strung along the two long walls, bridges that exist because someone decided a crossing should be in a certain place. It does not have the press of a town. Between one structure and the next there is often nothing but wall and the sound of water finding its level. Walkways are narrow and exact. Rooms are cut into the rock or held off it on frames that do not ring when struck. Materials are local where they can be—sintered stone, glass drawn from the same melt that made the lens—and foreign where they must be: spans that should not hold at that thinness, seams that shed ice without heat you can feel, channels that keep flow even when the air locks up near freezing.

The engineering exceeds what the stone, by itself, can explain. Load paths continue through empty air for a few body-lengths and then become stone again. Water is lifted without wheels you can see; it simply arrives at an upper cistern on a schedule and leaves by another. Heat is borrowed from deeper in the mountain—there is still warmth under the young rock, not much, enough—and spent so carefully that the pocket stays cold without becoming a trap of ice. None of this is loud. The systems work at the volume of a room with the door closed. If something fails, the failure is a change in where the water goes, not a noise.

People are not the density. The infrastructure is. You can pass long stretches where the only signs of occupation are a dry patch kept clear of film, a rail at the edge of a drop, a door set flush and shut. When someone is present they are usually doing a small thing with the water: clearing a lip, reading a level, walking a route that exists to be walked so the route stays known. Voices do not carry as voices. The pocket takes them and returns a short, dull version. Footsteps fare better. In the empty middle they arrive from farther away than seems fair.

Sound, otherwise, is water and the rock's own small motions. Drips where a weep-line finds a point. A continuous low movement in the main channel, more pressure than melody. Now and then a tick—sharp, single, mineral—when a new face adjusts. The young stone is still settling. Nothing here has been eroded into politeness. Corners cut. A misstep on an unworked ledge draws blood easily, and the blood looks very red against the pale floor, and then the water takes the trace of it downhill and the place is clean again.

Fauna is almost a rumor. High, cold, new, and without plants, the pocket should be empty of animals, and nearly is. What persists is small and infrequent: a pale thing in the lower channels that feeds on the mineral skin and is itself mostly missed; sometimes, after a warm pulse, a scatter of minute swimmers in a pan that will be dry again by night; once in a long while a winged speck that came in on the moisture and will not last. No flocks. No cries. No tracks that accumulate into a story of an ecosystem. The cycles here are not predator and prey. They are thermal, hydraulic, and maintained.

The day has weather even though the room is one room. The lens shows the outer sky, and the pocket answers it late and mildly: a bright morning clears the upper walls; a front outside thickens the air inside until sound shortens and the far bridge softens; a hard drop after sunset rimes the unworked shelves and leaves the channeled water moving under a skin of ice that the systems break before morning, quietly, by a change in level rather than by force. Dust does not blow. There is no dust yet. What moves in the air is condensate, and the occasional fine glitter when a fresh fracture spalls a grain into the light.

Rules hold, and they are ordinary rules wearing an unusual house. Cold slows everything that can be slowed and does not slow the water much, because the water is managed. Sound travels, then dies against wet stone. Nothing roots. The map is not stable in the way old cities expect: a new seep opens in a wall that was dry last month; a ledge that was a path glazes and is abandoned; a span is extended by a hand's width of new deposit and no one treats this as remarkable. The outside mountain remains sharp and airless in feeling, a place you cross to reach the seam and do not stay. Habitation is the pocket. The pocket is the city. The city is mostly interval—stone, water, light at the correct hour, and the long quiet between one small human action and the next.

If you stand in the middle aisle near the lens and look along the length, the settlement reads as a thin bright instrument laid against both walls, precise and underused, while the floor carries its slow river and the rock above still shows the fracture that made the room: two planes, recent, unfit, holding between them a climate no one would have predicted from the summit, and a town that occupies it the way a careful thought occupies a pause.
