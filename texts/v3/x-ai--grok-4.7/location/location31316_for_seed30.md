The bowl is not finished. That is the first thing the air tells you, before the eye has a word for it: the rock still tastes of making. Heat comes off the seams in slow pulses, and water beads on every face that has not yet decided what it is.

It is a high place, but not a summit. Someone—or something—has lifted a room into the weather and left the door ajar. The whole of it could be walked in an afternoon if the ground would hold still. Cathedral, if the cathedral were being poured rather than built: a shallow cup of new stone, maybe a few hundred paces across, rimmed by walls that rise unevenly, higher on the windward side where the mountain is still arriving. Beyond the rim the light falls away into a dim gulf. You do not look down for long. The gulf has no color yet.

Inside, the light is low and without a source you can point to. It sits in the vapor the way a bruise sits in skin—gray-amber, warm, never quite clarifying. Noon and dusk are the same hour here. Condensation writes and rewrites itself on the walls. Water is not a feature of the place so much as a habit of the air: it films the rock, threads the cracks, stands in shallow lenses where the floor has not learned to drain. The pools are warm. Put a hand in and the warmth is not the warmth of sun; it comes up from below, from stone that is still setting, the way a kiln keeps its heat after the fire has been banked. Nothing here is old enough to be cold at altitude.

The geology refuses age. Joints are soft at the edges, almost waxy, as if the mineral has not committed to crystal. In places the floor is a skin over something that flexes—barely, a breath every few minutes—and the skin weeps. New ribs of stone push up through older ribs that are themselves only days or seasons old; you can see the sequence if you kneel, pale bands stacked like unfinished sentences. Erosion has not begun its work. There are no talus fans, no smoothed lips, no grit sorted by water. The streams that should be cutting channels instead stand on the surface, confused, and sink when the floor remembers it is porous. A crack opens while you watch. It does not widen with violence. It simply becomes, a dark line where there was continuity, and a thin sheet of warm water finds it at once and makes a sound like cloth being torn slowly, for a long time.

Vegetation is an afterthought the place has not gotten around to. A few mats of something between moss and mineral crust cling where the weep is steadiest—ocher, not green, and they smell faintly of hot metal and sugar. A single stand of reed-like rods grows from a seam near the center, hollow, translucent, no taller than a person, and they ring when the air moves through them. That is almost all. The rest is bare new rock, wet sheen, and space. Distances inside the bowl feel larger than they measure, because so little occupies them. You can see from one wall to the other. What you see between is mostly weather.

The animals do not seem to mind the lack of green. They are numerous, and they are loud, and most of them are not interested in plants.

Low over the pools, clouds of bead-bodied fliers turn in tight helices, each no bigger than a seed, their wings a continuous dry tick that becomes a roar when a helix passes your ear. They drink the film on the stone. When a seam flexes they lift all at once—a pale curtain—and settle again on the new warmth. Among them move slower things: flat, many-legged shapes the color of wet clay, palm-sized, that patrol the rims of pools and lap at the mineral skin with a sound like a tongue on a plate. They are not cautious. They cross your boots. One will climb a shin if you stand still, test the salt of you, and drop off disappointed, already calling in a series of hard clicks that other clay-flat bodies answer from across the bowl. The answers come quickly. The place is small enough that every call finds its reply before the echo dies.

Larger bodies keep to the walls. Something like a bird, if a bird were built of wet slate plates that overlap and shed water in rhythmic sheets, launches from a ledge, crosses the open in three wingbeats, and slams into the opposite wall hard enough to shake condensate loose in a brief rain. It is not injured. The impact is how it feeds: the blow knocks loose a crust of the ocher mats, and it hangs there, plates flaring, scraping. Others answer the slam with slams of their own. For a while the bowl is a drum. Then they settle, and the only loudness is the fliers and the water finding new cracks and the reed-rods ringing without wind.

There are sounds that do not belong to any body you can see. A low grinding, periodic, under the floor—the stone still arranging its grains. A chime, high and clean, that repeats every nine or ten breaths from somewhere in the eastern wall, too regular for an animal and too musical for drip. When it sounds, the bead-fliers hesitate in their helices, a visible stutter, then resume. They have learned it. Or they are part of what produces it. Hard to say. The chime does not come from a throat.

Scattered through the unfinished room are the marks of whatever is making it, or keeping it from finishing.

Near the northern rim, half-sunk in stone that grew around it rather than being cut to fit, is a hoop of dark material no thicker than a finger, standing vertical, taller than a door. It is not metal in any way the hand recognizes: it drinks the dim light and gives back a faint edge of color that is not in the spectrum you came with, a color the eye keeps trying to name and failing. Water beads on it and does not run. The beads crawl, slowly, toward the top of the hoop, against sense, and vanish at the crown. The air inside the hoop is a fraction clearer than the air beside it. Step through and the sound of the bowl drops, not in volume exactly but in nearness, as if you have put a hand's width of distance between yourself and every process at once. Step back and the loudness returns like a door opening onto a mill. The hoop is warm on one side, cool on the other. It has no joint, no inscription, no cable. Stone laps its base in soft lips, still growing, not yet gripping.

Elsewhere the interventions are smaller and more careless, or more finished, it is difficult to tell which. A line of pins, bright as fresh fracture, set into a seam at intervals of a pace. Touch one and the seam under your feet stills for a moment; the flex pauses; the water stops finding new paths. Take the hand away and the making resumes, a little faster, as if annoyed. Three shallow dishes carved—or extruded—into a ledge, each holding a different clarity of water: one milky, one so clear it seems absent, one dark and slowly rotating though nothing stirs it. The rotating dish hums at the edge of hearing. Clay-flat animals will not approach that ledge. The slate-birds slam every wall but the one that holds the dishes.

On the floor near the reed-rods, a partial thing: an arc of the same light-drinking material as the hoop, broken or unfinished, ending in a fan of filaments finer than hair that disappear into the wet stone. The filaments pulse, dimly, in time with the underfloor grind. Where they enter the rock the mineral has gone glassy and wrong, a little window into a depth that should not be visible, and in that depth something moves with the slow logic of a mechanism rather than a creature—gears, if gears could be made of temperature, turning. You cannot see them directly. You see the water above the window shiver in a pattern too even to be chance.

None of these objects explain themselves. They do not look abandoned, and they do not look tended. They look like sentences left mid-word by a speaker who intends to return before the listener notices the pause. The bowl is the pause. The mountain around it—what you can sense of it beyond the rim, in the drop of light and the change in the wind—is older, colder, finished. This room was opened in it, or lifted out of it, and the opening is still in progress. New ribs. New cracks. Pins holding a seam so it does not close before whatever the pins are for has happened. A hoop that edits the nearness of sound. Dishes sorting water into kinds.

The rules, insofar as a short stay can learn them, are these.

The stone is alive in the way a construction site is alive: not conscious, but incomplete, and biased toward becoming rather than wearing away. Water does not erode it because the rock is not yet in the state that erosion requires. It drinks, it weeps, it flexes, and occasionally it extrudes a rib or a rod of the translucent reed-stuff, which the animals treat as furniture. Do not stand on a seam that is pulsing. It will not swallow you, but it will open under one foot and close on the other, and the close is warm enough to blister.

The animals are abundant because the making feeds them. Heat, mineral film, the ocher crust that grows fastest where the weep is newest, the concussion of walls that are still settling their stresses. They are not a stable ecology in the old sense. They are a crowd at a site where food is being manufactured by the site itself. If the making stopped, most of them would leave or die within days. They know the chime. They know the hoop. They avoid the rotating dish. They have opinions about the interventions, which means the interventions have been here longer than a single breeding, even if the stone around them looks hours old. Time is not keeping one clock.

Sound carries too well. The bowl focuses it. A slate-bird's impact on the far wall arrives at your chest as a shove. The fliers' tick, multiplied, can make thought difficult if a helix decides your head is a useful thermal. Speak, and you will be answered—not in words, but in clicks, and sometimes in a brief, uncanny stilling of the underfloor grind, as if something below the skin of the floor has noted the vibration and filed it. Whether that noting is instrument or animal is one of the questions the place is still writing.

At intervals the light dims further, not into night but into a thicker amber, and the condensation increases until the far wall is a rumor. In that interval the hoop's false color brightens, and the filaments in the floor pulse harder, and new cracks prefer to open then. It may be a cycle of the machinery. It may be weather, invented locally, a rain that does not fall from clouds but condenses out of a decision. When it passes, the bowl is louder, wetter, and slightly larger: a new rib, a pool that was not there, a reed-rod that rings at a pitch the others do not share.

You can leave the way you came, if you remember which gap in the rim was the gap and not a crack that opened while you were listening. Outside, the finished mountain takes the body back—cold, thin air, ordinary stone, a wind that has been blowing long enough to have a direction. The loudness cuts off at the threshold, clean as a hand taken from a bell. Behind you the room continues its work. You can hear it, faintly, if you stop: impacts, ticks, the nine-breath chime, water deciding where to go in a landscape that has not yet decided what it is.

It will not look the same if you return. That is not weathering. That is the sentence resuming.
