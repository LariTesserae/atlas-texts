The basin is younger than its weather. That is the first thing the ground admits, if you put a hand to it: the stone has not yet learned to be tired. Ridges stand up in clean planes, edges still crisp enough to catch a sleeve, gullies cut as if someone had drawn them with a straight instrument and only afterward allowed water to notice. Low country, all of it — no summit worth the name, just a long shallow bowl the size of a district, opening outward until the far rim is a darker rule against a darker sky. The sky does not brighten. It holds a permanent late-hour color, warm as breath on glass, never quite night and never noon. You can read a face at arm’s length. You cannot read the horizon.

Moisture comes the way rumors do. A film on the undersides of leaves. A seam of standing water in a fold of fresh rock that will be gone by the time you walk back. Occasional rain that does not commit, arriving as a warm drift and leaving the air heavier than the ground. There are no proper rivers. There are places where the air has agreed, for an hour, to be wetter than elsewhere, and the young stone answers by darkening along its grain. Condensation beads on anything that holds still. Most things do not hold still.

Where the damp lingers, growth is indecent. Not a forest in the old sense — there is too much room for that — but stands of vegetation so thick they read, from a distance, as single dark organs set down in the bowl. Inside one, the light drops another grade and the air goes green-black and sweet. Vines the width of wrists climb structures that have not finished deciding whether they are trunks or scaffolds. Leaves overlap until they make a roof. Fruit, if that is the word, hangs in clusters that tick, very softly, as if something inside each one is keeping a count. Between these stands the land is almost bare: pale new gravel, sharp shelves, a skin of lichen no thicker than breath. You can walk for a long time and meet nothing taller than your ankle. Then a wall of green arrives all at once, loud with its own water, and the rules change.

The emptiness is not an absence of life. It is a spacing. Things refuse to crowd. A tower stands, then a kilometer of open warm dimness, then a low arc of roadway that does not touch the ground except at two points, then more air. The settlements are real — organized, repeated, clearly meant — and they are also mostly interval. Blocks of built matter, layered as if several centuries of intention had been folded into a single season: streets that pass through the insides of other streets, rooms whose floors are the ceilings of rooms you cannot enter from here, bridges that end in gardens and gardens that end in instrument housings. No one dense quarter. A town smeared across a valley until the town is the valley’s punctuation rather than its mass. You can see three structures at once and still feel you are alone in a hall.

The engineering does not apologize for itself. Some of the built forms are stone-honest, cut from the same young rock, joints still bright. Others are not made of anything you can confidently name. A road surface that is only a insistence: your foot meets resistance where the eye meets nothing. A tower whose upper third is a slow rotation of filaments, each filament thinner than a thought, combing the dim air and laying what it collects into a trough that never overflows and never empties. Windows that are not openings but agreements about light — you look, and the far side of the basin is nearer than it should be, and warmer, and then the agreement lapses and the distance returns. No smoke. No grid-hum you recognize. The technology seems less invented than arrived at, the way a river arrives at a bend: inevitable once the prior conditions are granted, and the prior conditions are not fully available to you.

Animals are everywhere and almost never beside one another. A flock crosses the open middle of the basin as a single long sentence of wings, each bird separated from the next by a gap you could put a house in, the formation holding anyway, as if the gaps were load-bearing. In the green stands, things move at different speeds in the same thicket: a slow climber the color of wet bark; a scatter of bright insects that travel in a sheet and then, all at once, are gone into the leaf-dark, leaving only the tick of the fruit; something larger that you do not see except as a displacement of vines and a smell like heated metal and sugar. On the bare shelves, lizards the length of a forearm hold so still they seem part of the new geology, until one of them opens a mouth and the sound that comes out does not belong to a body that small.

The sound is the true density. The basin is hollow of matter and packed with noise, and the noise does not fade with distance the way honest sound should. It carries. It stacks. A low tectonic ticking from the young rock, still settling, still sharp, a click-click that you feel in the teeth more than the ears. Over that, the weather’s own machinery: warm updrafts along the clean ridges, sudden soft collapses of air when a pocket of damp gives up and falls. Over that, the green stands roaring with insects and with water moving where you cannot see water. Over that, the animals — cries spaced far apart in the world and not at all far apart in the ear, so that a bird a kilometer off and a lizard at your boot arrive as neighbors. Over that, the built things. The filament towers sing a thin, continuous wire-note. The floating agreements of roadway thrum when something crosses them, and the thrum remains after the crossing, circulating the bowl like a coin in a basin. Sometimes a structure answers another structure. You hear the call and the reply and cannot tell which began.

It is not chaotic. That is the strangeness that takes longest to accept. The deafening has parts. If you stay, the parts separate the way instruments do in a room you have finally stopped fighting. There is a cycle. The dim light warms and cools by a degree you notice only on skin. Moisture gathers, travels, abandons a shelf, invests a stand. The flocks revise their spacing and do not break it. Predators — you come to believe in them by the pattern of absences, a sudden hole in the insect-sheet, a vine-stand gone quiet in a shape the size of a room — work the edges and then return to the intervals, where they are almost impossible to see because there is so much nothing to hide in. The nothing hides them. The sound does not.

At the scale of the bowl, processes overlap without queueing. A rain-drift passes over a roadway that is also a roost; the birds lift in their enormous punctuation and settle again before the damp has finished beading on the filaments. A new crack opens in a ridge with a sound like a book being torn slowly, and within what feels like a day — time is slippery here; the light offers no clock — a thread of green has found the crack and thickened. The land is too young to have a soil worth the name, and the vegetation does not seem to mind. It feeds on the damp, on the warmth, on whatever the towers comb out of the air and let fall in their thin overflows. Some of the built forms are already being climbed. Some of the climbers are already being incorporated, leaf and filament hard to tell apart at the joint.

Rules, once you stop demanding the old ones:

Matter prefers distance. Life prefers number. The two are not in conflict because life here spends itself in calls, in crossings, in brief occupations of a leaf or a ledge, and then returns to the interval. You can walk all afternoon and be approached by nothing, and still know you were counted.

Water is an event, not a place. Chase it and you will find only the place it has just left, darker stone, a smell of iron and cut green.

Light will not improve. Plan for the dim. The warm is reliable; the bright is not on offer.

Sound is infrastructure. It maps the bowl more accurately than your eyes. A change in the wire-note means a tower has altered its combing. A gap in the insect-roar means something has entered a stand. The tectonic click speeds up before a fresh face of rock decides to exist. People who live here — and there are people, or something that uses the rooms; you see wash hung on a line that has no visible posts, a bowl set to catch overflow, a path worn only in the lichen — navigate by ear the way coastal towns once navigated by swell. They speak close to the mouth, or they do not speak. Conversation at a distance is a superstition. The basin will take your sentence and deliver it, amplified and late, to a listener who did not ask.

The city is not abandoned. It is diluted. Its builders, whoever or whatever they are, seem to have understood that in this bowl a wall is a kind of shout, and so they built sparsely, and high, and in materials that sometimes decline to be fully material, leaving the volume for the weather and the flocks and the noise. Maintenance is continuous and mostly invisible. You will watch a joint brighten, a filament rethread itself, a roadway’s agreement strengthen under your foot as if the road were grateful, or merely precise.

Do not camp in the open interval during the hour when the clicks cluster. The young stone is still inventing itself, and it does not negotiate with sleep.

Do not assume the fruit is fruit.

If you must cross a green stand, cross while the insects are at full roar. Quiet is information.

The far rim never quite arrives. You walk toward it and the bowl revises, another shelf, another isolated stack of rooms flowering with vines, another flock writing its wide sentence across the dim warm air. The landscape is large enough to hold a town and empty enough to hold a silence, except that it has spent its silence already, on the day it rose, and everything since has been sound finding something to live in. The something is this: sharp new ground, wet in pockets, thick where it chooses to be thick, hollow everywhere else, ringing, ringing, and not yet old enough to have forgotten how it started.
