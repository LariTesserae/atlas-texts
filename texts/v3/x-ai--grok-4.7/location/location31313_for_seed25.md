The basin does not announce itself. It simply continues, and after a while you understand that the continuation is the place.

It is low in the way a palm is low: not a pit with walls you could name, but a long settling of the land, a shallow inland trough whose far edge is a rumor of higher ground you never quite reach. The stone underfoot has been worked for so long that it no longer looks worked. Channels have lost their sharpness. Ridges have gone blunt and gray-brown, the color of old iron left in a shed. Where the rock splits, the breaks are rounded at the lip, polished by water that does not stay and by the passage of bodies that do. Nothing here is young, and nothing is finished. The weathering and the life have agreed on a shape and are keeping it.

There are no plants. Not a film of green, not a crust that photosynthesizes in any color you would call living from a distance. The surfaces that look stained are mineral: manganese bloom, pale salts, a rust that feathers out from every seep and then stops, as if the water lost interest. Moisture is a habit rather than a season. Fog beads on the stone and runs nowhere in particular. Now and then a thin rain arrives without wind, ticks across the basin for an hour, and is gone, leaving the channels a little louder and the air no clearer. In the hollows, water stands in lenses no deeper than a wrist — cold, still at the edges, busy in the middle, because something is always moving through them. Between the lenses the ground is only damp, slick where the grain of the rock has been ground to flour, gritty where a fresh flake has not yet been walked flat.

The cold is not dramatic. It sits in the stone and comes up through whatever you are standing on. Breath shows, then thins. Nothing freezes hard enough to lock the place. Nothing warms enough to dry it.

What the basin has instead of vegetation is traffic.

They are everywhere, and they are not one thing. The first layer is a hide of small bodies, jointed and pale, the size of fingernails and thumbs, packed so tightly on the flatter shelves that the rock reads as a single shifting surface. They do not graze. There is nothing to graze. They lap the damp and worry at mineral films with mouthparts that leave the stone faintly crosshatched, a texture you only notice when a patch clears and the hatching is already being written again. They move in local agreements: a ripple starts, travels a few body-lengths, dies, starts elsewhere. Where two currents meet, they climb over one another without apparent offense. The sound of them is a dry, continuous ticking, like seeds poured into a metal tray and never allowed to stop.

Over and through that hide move the larger ones. Low, many-legged, the color of wet slate, they are built for the channels and treat the smaller life as both road and weather. Some carry a collar of cemented grit that has grown into them — not armor they made on purpose, so much as a habit of the water, grains caught in a secretion and never shed, until an animal goes about with a little geology on its shoulders. They are not rare. In any stretch of channel you can count a dozen, then lose count because another dozen has come around the bend, antennae or their equivalent testing the air, which is full of the chemical news of damp stone and of each other. When they pass close they brush, and the brush has a sound: a soft knock, repeated, the basin’s smallest drum.

The pools hold a different congress. Things like flattened hands lie under the surface, edges lifting and settling, stirring a slow milk of mineral dust. They do not swim so much as redistribute themselves. Now and then one rises until a margin breaks the water and a sound comes off it — not a voice, a wet clap, as if the pool itself had decided to speak in one syllable. Other pools answer. The claps travel. For a minute the lenses talk across the shelves in a pattern that is almost spatial, a map made of single notes, and then the pattern dissolves into the general noise and you cannot say whether it meant anything or only happened.

Above the ground-layer, the air is not empty, though it is dark. The sky, what there is of it, is a low lid of cloud the color of unlit slate, and under it the basin generates its own dimness: mist, the matte of wet stone, the absence of any leaf to catch a highlight. Light exists as a gray leak, enough to tell near from far if you wait for your eyes, not enough to make a shadow with an edge. In that leak, fliers move in sheets. They are not birds. They are closer to the small clickers, caught in an older trick of the air — membranous, rapid, present in such numbers that a gust of them darkens what little light there was and then returns it, ragged. Their wings, or whatever serves, make the high part of the noise: a keen that does not resolve into individuals, a pressure more than a tone. When a sheet turns, the keen dopplers across the trough and comes back off the far stone a fraction later, so the basin briefly has two skies.

You cannot find a quiet acre. The ticking, the knocks, the claps, the keen, and under them a lower sound you eventually separate from the rest: a long, frictional hum, many-throated, made by the largest bodies in the place as they breathe or grind or sing against the channels they have inherited. These are scarce compared with the rest and still too many. You see one as a moving absence first — a lane opening in the clicker-hide — and then the body that opens it, long as a toppled column, low to the stone, hide plated with the same salts that stain the seeps. It does not hunt in any way that looks like a strike. It travels, and the smaller life folds aside and closes behind, and as it travels it drags that hum with it, a note so broad it seems to come from the ground. Another answers from a distance you cannot measure. The two notes do not harmonize. They occupy the air until the air feels narrow, and then one stops, and the sudden lack is as loud as the sound was.

Nothing constructed stands here. No wall, no tool left where a hand might have set it down, no cut that is not the cut of water or of mouths. If minds live among these bodies, they have not built, or what they built was only more body, more cemented grit, more pattern in the clapping of pools. The technology of the basin, if the word can be forced, is metabolic and acoustic: secretions that bind grains, signals that move faster than any of the walkers, a memory kept in the polished shape of channels that generations have continued to use because the shape already works.

The strangeness is not a violation of the place. It is the place’s ordinary physics, once you stop expecting green and quiet. Water beads upward along some of the salt-collars and hangs there, heavier than it should, before falling all at once. A sheet of fliers will hold a still formation for longer than wings can explain, a dark lenticular mark in the gray, and then shatter into individuals as if the mark had only been an agreement. In the deepest pools the dust does not always settle down; sometimes it settles sideways, toward a particular stone, and the hand-shaped things arrange themselves along that same drift without touching one another. None of this breaks the basin. It has been doing it long enough that the eroded forms include it. The channels bend where the sideways dust has, over centuries, encouraged a little more passage on one side than the other. The life has edited the geology, and the geology has edited the routes, and the two edits have worn each other smooth.

Scale arrives slowly, the way sound does. You think you are in a local crowding — this shelf, this pool, this lane — and then the keen shifts and you hear the same keen, thinner, from a direction the mist had closed, and you understand the shelf is one of many, repeated across a trough wide enough to hold weather of its own. Rain, when it comes, does not wet the whole basin at once. You can watch a grayness walk the far channels, hear the ticking change pitch as the drops reach the hide, and stand, still dry, inside the older noise. Pools clap in the rained-on distance and go unanswered nearby, then the answers start, late, local, as if the news had to be carried by bodies rather than by air. The place is large enough for delay. It is dense enough that delay is never silence.

By the time the light leaks a little more — not dawn, only a thinning of the lid, a paler gray that still casts no shadow — the traffic has not diminished. It does not seem to sleep so much as change shift. The fliers drop and the ground-hide thickens. The column-bodied ones move off the main channels into side-seeps, humming shorter notes, and the side-seeps answer with a fretwork of knocks. Somewhere a pool-clap sequence runs for thirty strokes and stops clean, and in the stop you hear, for the first time, the small true sound of water: a thread moving in a groove, almost shy. Then the hide closes over that groove and the thread is lost again inside the ticking.

Nothing here is waiting to become a forest. The mineral films will be licked and rewritten. The salts will crust and crack and crust. Fog will bead, rain will cross, the lenses will speak their one-syllable language and forget it. The basin keeps its balance the way a shore keeps a tide mark: not by resting, but by being worn, at every hour, into the same shape by a life that has no use for leaves and no memory of quiet.
