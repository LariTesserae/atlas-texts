The highland is still being lifted, and the water has not yet learned how to leave it.

From any of the cities the eye runs out across stone the color of wet iron, a plain so broad it carries its own weather and so young it has almost no valleys. The sun is an ordinary sun. Light falls level and pale, the kind that shows distance without warming it, and the cold sits in that light the way cold sits in metal: not dramatic, simply the condition of the air. Breath shows. Hands stiffen. The rock underfoot is dark because it is damp, not because it is old.

The plateau is continental in the practical sense. A front can be born over its middle and die at its edge without ever having come from anywhere else. In the morning the condensate lies in a skin a few meters thick, bright, almost still, pooled in every shallow flexure the uplift has made. By afternoon the skin has burned into towers and the towers have walked. You can watch a fog-bank detach from a seep-field and travel for hours as a discrete body, shading the herds, laying rime on the lattice piers of a road that will not see another bank until the next cycle. At dusk the water comes back down. It does not fall so much as agree to be liquid again. Stone that was pale at noon is black by night, filmed, ticking.

There are no deep rivers because there has not been time. Erosion here is a future tense. The crust rose faster than weather could cut it, and it is still rising — not as spectacle, as maintenance. Survey marks set into fresh faces gain a finger’s width of sky in a working life. Microfractures tick in the quiet the way cooling engines tick, and then they don’t. Water lies on the land instead of opening it: sheet seeps, tarns no deeper than a knee, marshes that are only marshes because a fold in the new stone happens to hold what the air keeps giving back. At the edges of those folds the rock weeps in lines, a slow mineral paste, iron and lime, building rims that will someday be rims and are presently only wet.

Soil is the scarce thing, not water. The green is a stain. Lichen crusts the windward faces in maps the color of oxidized copper and old bone. Cushion plants occupy the lee of every boulder large enough to make a lee, each one a tight argument against the wind. Along the tarn margins a sedge grows, sparse, ankle-high, enough to mark the waterline and not enough to hide it. There are no forests. There is barely shade. Where a city has terraced engineered substrate — dark, fibrous, made rather than weathered — the contrast is immediate: a rectangle of actual turf, startling as a wound, and around it the original plain, which is stone learning, slowly, to be a place plants can use.

The animals are many and the ground between them is empty. That is the character of the abundance: not a thicket of life, a budget of life spread across a floor that has only just become available. Herds of high grazers work the lichen and the sedge in groups small enough to see between, gray-white, long-faced, moving in the same direction as the fog when the fog is moving and otherwise according to a seep they can smell and you cannot. They do not call. Their sound is hoof on wet stone, and even that is local. Between one band and the next there may be an hour of walking and nothing but film-water and the tick of cooling rock.

The tarns hold more. Waders arrive in numbers that make the shallows look paved, then lift all at once and are a weather of their own, a pale rotor turning above the water, silent at a distance, a soft torn sound only if you are under them. They are not specialized yet. The waters have not been separate long enough for that. The same generalist bodies show up in tarns a hundred kilometers apart, feeding on the same cold invertebrates, breeding on the same new mud. Predators follow at the scale of the plateau rather than the scale of a thicket: a long-winged hunter that waits on the thermal boundary where fog meets clear air, a low runner that uses the herd-paths and is gone between one rise and the next. At the active margins, where a fresh face has just split and the mineral paste is new, scavengers and a dense, quiet life of small things gather for the nutrient pulse. You smell it before you see it — cold mud, bird, iron, and something sweet off the weathering stone.

The quiet is not an absence of process. It is what this much open, this little vegetation, and this kind of water sound like. Wind crosses bare rock without a voice to catch in. Seeps tick. A wingbeat arrives and is gone. Far off, if a herd is running, there is a faint dry thunder that dies the moment they stop. The cities add almost nothing. Their transit displaces rather than burns; the sound at the guide-way is a hush, like cloth, and then the plain again.

The cities stand far apart, each one a low geometric weather on a horizon that otherwise repeats. They were not founded on the promise of soil. They were founded on the water that would not leave and on the stone that would not stop rising, and the engineering that holds them is the sort that does not need to be explained to the people who live with it, only retensioned. The piers go down into the young crust and read its strain. As a district lifts faster than its neighbor, the lattice lengthens, a few millimeters at a time, and the deck above stays level. You can put a hand on a street that has been a street for eighty years and feel, if you are still enough, the small correction. It is not dramatic. It is the opposite of drama. The city is agreeing with the ground.

Because the grade changes, the cities are layered in the vertical as much as the plan. The oldest decks are the lowest now, packed with the machines that harvest condensate and the reservoirs that take the seep before it can sheet away. Above them, streets of a later settlement, and above those the light decks, where the pale sun actually arrives and the apartments face the plain. Walking down through a city is walking backward through the uplift: brighter, then dimmer, then the mineral smell of the service levels, where pipes sweat in the same grammar as the rocks outside. The materials are not stone and not quite metal. They hold a temperature. They shed the rime that would glaze ordinary fabric. In the civic halls the floors are warm enough that people stand and talk without stamping, which is the local definition of luxury.

There is infrastructure between the cities, but it does not fill the plain. Guide-ways run like single dark lines, raised a body’s height so the sheet-water can pass under them, and along them at long intervals the condensate towers — open frameworks, ordinary-looking until you notice they are drinking in a fog-bank and giving nothing visible back. The towers are why the roads can be so far apart. A settlement does not need a river. It needs the cycle, and the cycle is the size of the plateau.

People move at the speed of that sparseness. A journey is a day, or three, of wet stone and ordinary light, herds at the edge of vision, a tarn bright as a dropped piece of sky, and then the next city’s correction-towers picking themselves out of the haze. No one treats the distance as empty. The plain is working the whole time: fog assembling, grazers shifting to a new seep, a flexure ponding water that was not a tarn last season and will be a mapped water next, a survey mark gaining its finger of sky. The rules are simple because the land has not had time to grow complicated ones. You do not dam what has not found a channel. You harvest what the air returns. You build so the rise can continue without asking your permission. You leave the space between herds alone; the abundance depends on it.

At the far edge, where the plateau finally steps down and the young stone gives way to country the water has already begun to cut, the sound changes before the view does. A real river, small still, practicing. Wind in something taller than sedge. The quiet you have been inside reveals itself as a climate, not a pause. Behind you the Sheet goes on generating its own afternoon, its towers drinking, its cities agreeing millimeter by millimeter with the ground, its birds turning over water that has not yet decided where to go. The land is wet, cold, bright, and unfinished. It is large enough that unfinished is a stable condition.
