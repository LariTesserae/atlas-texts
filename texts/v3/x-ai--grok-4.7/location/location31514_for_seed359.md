The floor is still sharp. Every joint in it remembers the hand that set it, and none of them have learned weather. You stand in a low room the size of a small church, though church is the wrong word and room is only almost right, and the ceiling sits close enough that a raised arm would find it if the arm could find a clear path. Nothing here has been worn round. The edges of the paving, the lips of the low walls, the ribs that hold the roof, all of them keep the crispness of work finished yesterday and left in a warmth that does not weather things the way rain would. There is no rain. There has never been water in this place, not as mist, not as seep, not as the dark memory in a joint. The air is dry the way kiln-air is dry, warm enough to feel on the insides of the wrists, and it does not move.

Someone built all of it. There is no bedrock showing, no soil that was not first mixed and pressed and laid. The ground is a mosaic of hand-cut tiles in colors that have no mineral names you trust: a dull blood-orange, a green that reads as leaf until you kneel and see the tool-marks, a pale cream shot with hairline fractures that stop exactly where a maker’s thumb would have stopped them. Between the tiles, in grooves cut for that purpose and no other, the growth begins.

It is lush, and it is not plants, or it is plants that have forgotten the contract. What rises from the grooves is dense enough that walking is a matter of turning sideways. Stalks of braided fiber, each braid begun by fingers and then continued by something that is no longer fingers, crowd one another until their surfaces touch and stay touching. Some of them are clearly woven: you can see the over-under, the place a strand was tucked, the slight irregularity of tension that means a person was tired. Higher up, the weave loosens into structures no hand could have planned and every hand seems to have permitted. Blades, fans, tubes, plates. They are dry all the way through. Break one — they break with a clean, papery crack, young, unweathered — and the inside is the same warm dryness as the air, layered like a wasp’s nest or like cloth stored too long, and it smells faintly of warmed resin and of nothing wet at all.

They drink, if drinking is the word, from the tiles. You can watch it if you are willing to stand still longer than comfort likes. A stalk darkens by a shade at its base over the course of an afternoon. The tile beneath it lightens by the same shade. No droplet forms. The exchange is a slow paling and deepening, color moving the way heat moves through a wall. By what would be evening, if this place kept evenings, the tile has given up a little of its green and the growth has taken it into a new leaf that was not there at noon and already looks as if it had always been woven there.

The boundaries of the place are close and absolute and also, lately, unconvincing. You can walk the perimeter in a few minutes if the growth allows it. Low walls, no higher than a shoulder, built of the same worked stuff as the floor, meet at corners sharp enough to cut a sleeve. Beyond them there is not an outside so much as a refusal: the light continues, ordinary daylight, shadowless in the way of a high overcast or of a sky that has been stretched like hide over a frame, and then, a step past the wall, the light is still there and the step is not. People who have tried report that the foot goes forward and arrives back on the tile it left, with a small delay, as if the distance had been considered and declined. The delay is lengthening. Last season it was the space of a blink. Now it is long enough to notice your own breath in it.

Light falls evenly. You can see every tool-mark, every braid, every small animal. There are a great many small animals. They fit the density the way the growth does: not a swarm, not a pasture, a packing. Between the stalks, against the undersides of the plates, in the angles where wall meets floor, bodies the size of thumbs and bodies the size of two cupped hands occupy almost every volume that is not already occupied by made-and-living fiber. They are quiet. A place this full should tick and rustle and click. It does not. What sound there is comes late and seems to belong to a movement you missed: a soft ceramic tap, already finished, from a limb set down with care; a dry whisper of plates brushing plates, so faint you assign it to your own clothing and then remember you are standing still.

They do not drink either. You have watched. They press their undersides to the tiles, or to the stalks, and over a long while a color leaves one and enters the other, or sometimes does not enter the other and simply goes out of the world. A few of them are unambiguous artifacts: clay bodies, pinch-marks at the joints, a hole where a maker’s wire cut the form off the board, and these move with the same patience as the ones whose skins look grown. A few are unambiguous animals, warm to the near-touch, breathing so slowly the rise of the flank can be mistaken for the slow swell of the growth around them. Most are neither, or both, and the distinction is what has been failing.

That failure is the weather here, insofar as there is weather. It proceeds at the speed of the color-exchange, which is to say you cannot watch it happen and you cannot come back tomorrow and claim nothing has changed. A stalk that was woven at its base and living at its tip will, across days, invert. The living tip develops the regularity of a pattern someone repeated until their wrist hurt. The woven base frays into filaments that seek the grooves and seat themselves with a competence no fray should have. A clay animal, left against a wall, develops a seam that was not sculpted, and along the seam a dampness that is not water — a darkening, a give, a place your finger sinks a fraction and comes away dusty. The dust, on the tile, begins to organize. By the time you think to look again it has the start of a braid.

Nothing here rots. Nothing here is old enough. The whole enclosure has the freshness of a thing just taken from the mold, still carrying the heat of its making, and the warmth in the air may be that heat, not yet finished leaving. Joints have not opened. Corners have not softened. Where two stalks touch they do not abrade; they adhere, slowly, into a single thickness with two histories. Where an animal rests long enough, the tile beneath it keeps an outline sharper than a shadow, a recess beginning, as if the floor were still green and still willing to take an impression. The makers, whoever they were, worked in a technology of hands: blades of struck stone, awls, cord, fire hot enough to harden clay and not hot enough to smelt. You can find their offcuts in the growth, not discarded so much as incorporated — a flint flake become the hard midrib of a fan, a coil of cord that has started, at one end, to be a vein.

There is a center, or there was. A slightly lower paving, a shallow dish of tiles set in a spiral a person could have laid in an afternoon. It was perhaps meant to hold water. It holds nothing. The spiral is the sharpest work in the room, each tile’s edge still keen, and the growth has not yet entered it, though the nearest stalks lean, and have been leaning for what feels like a season, and their lean is a little farther today than the lean you remember, if memory is still a trustworthy record of a place that edits its own recent past.

Stand long enough and the quiet acquires a grain. Not sound exactly. A pressure, rhythmic, very slow, like a pulse taken through a thick wall. It matches nothing you can see moving. It may be the exchange itself: color leaving tile, entering stalk, leaving stalk, entering the small pressed bodies, leaving them into the dust that is learning to braid. A circulation with no fluid. The room is performing the work of a wetland, or a lung, or a kiln that has not been allowed to cool, and it is doing it with the only materials it was given — made things, dry things, new things — and with a patience that does not need you to understand which of those it still is.

If you touch the wall at the corner, where the two planes meet in a line that could divide light, your hand sometimes returns with a fine pale powder that was not on the wall and is not on your skin when you look again. Sometimes the corner is, for the length of that same lengthening delay, not a corner but a continuation, the wall going on into a second room identical in every sharp joint and every crowded stalk, and then it is a corner again, and you are holding nothing, and the animals nearest your feet have shifted their weight by the width of a finger, silently, as if to acknowledge that the place considered being larger and decided, for now, to remain what was built.
