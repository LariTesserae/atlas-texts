The cut is newer than the town that fills it. You can still read the fracture in the walls: a clean, almost polite shear, pale rock with a skin of ochre where the air first touched it, edges that have not yet rounded. Something opened here not long ago, in the slow sense of the word—recent enough that the rim above still looks raw, old enough that the people who arrived afterward have already forgotten they arrived. They call the place the Floor, as if it had always been the bottom of something.

It is not deep, not in the way of mines. The daylight that falls from the lip is a short fall, a few tens of meters, and it dies before it reaches the streets. What light there is lives lower: small, tired bulbs in iron cages, the dull red of a furnace throat behind a shutter, the pale film of condensation catching a lamp and spreading it into a smear. At noon the upper ledges go the color of weak tea. By midafternoon the Floor is night again, and the night is hot.

Heat rises from the rock itself. Not a dramatic venting—no roar, no plume you could point at—only a patient warmth that never quite leaves the stone, so the air sits thick and close, and metal rails are unpleasant to hold with a bare hand. Moisture does not fall as weather so much as appear. A pipe sweats. A seam in the wall darkens overnight and is dry by the next evening. In one alley a thread of water travels the length of a gutter, vanishes into a grate, and is gone; you can stand there for an hour and never see it strengthen or fail. Pockets of it collect in the low corners of courtyards, warm as bathwater, filmed with a green that is not quite algae and not quite oil.

The vegetation has taken the heat and the damp as permission. It is not wild in the forest sense. It is urban, opportunistic, and nearly complete. Every joint in the masonry has a lip of leaf. Vines the color of old brass run the length of conduit and seem, at a distance, to be part of the conduit. Ferns pack the sills of windows that no longer open. In the narrowest lanes the canopy of stems meets overhead and the passage becomes a green tube, lit from within by whatever lamp is still burning behind it. The growth is lush but obedient to the plan of the streets. It does not tear the town down. It furnishes it. Where a wall was built at a right angle, the leaves hold the right angle. Where a stair turns, the moss turns with it, step by step, as if the plant had been issued a drawing.

The town is small enough to walk across before the heat makes you stop. A single parish of industry: three worksheds, a row of houses stacked so their balconies nearly touch, a market stall that is also a pump house, a chapel whose nave was clearly once a boiler room and has not entirely stopped being one. The construction is layered and recent, sharp-cornered brick and riveted plate, poured sills that still show the board-marks of their forms. Nothing has been weathered into softness. Corners catch your sleeve. New pipes run over older pipes without apology, and both are already green. You can see the whole arrangement from the stair that climbs the shear wall, and from there it looks less like a settlement than like a machine that decided to house people, or a house that decided to keep running.

The engines do run. Somewhere under the chapel a piston works on a long cycle, and the sound it makes is not the sound of industry as people upstairs would recognize it. It is a single, spaced exhalation—present, then absent for so long you begin to doubt you heard it, then present again. Belts turn in a shed whose doors are left open because the heat would be worse if they were shut. No one shouts over them. There is no need. The pace is low enough that a person can speak at the volume used in a library and still be heard at the end of the lane. Footsteps carry. A ladle set down on a stone sill carries. The rest is the small continuous noises of a place that is damp and hot and full: a tick of condensation falling from a leaf onto tin, the faint creak of a vine taking weight, the far piston.

Animals are almost absent, which is the strangest quiet of all. In a green place this warm you expect things that move. Instead the leaves are still unless a person brushes them. A moth the size of a thumb sometimes crosses a lamp and is gone. Once in a long while a pale lizard the color of fresh-cut stone is seen on a warm rail, and the children are told not to touch it, not because it is dangerous but because it is the only one. There are no flocks. There is no dawn chorus, and there is no dawn down here in any case. The ecology, if that is the word, is mostly plant and machine and the people who keep both.

The people fit the density of the place. They do not sprawl. Rooms are full; roofs are used; the gap between one household and the next is a matter of a curtain or a sheet of corrugated plate. Laundry hangs where a walkway ought to be, and the walkway has simply moved half a step sideways, as if the town were a sentence being revised in the margin. They speak little in the open, not from secrecy but from habit. Sound does not die here—it stays—and everyone has learned the manners of a shared room.

What does not fit, and will not settle no matter how long you look, is the relationship between the cut and the town. The rock is young. The town is young. The plants are already old in their occupation, as if they had been waiting for the geometry. Stand at the stair and look down and the streets seem carved rather than built, the vines following lines that were always there. Step into a lane and the opposite is true: every surface is made, recent, industrial, and the plants are only tenants. Both impressions remain. Neither corrects the other.

At the lowest point, under the last grate, there is a sump no wider than a door. The water in it does not flow. It holds a little heat and a little light from the bulb above the chapel, and on its surface the reflection of the leaves is sharper than the leaves themselves. People do not drink from it. They say it is not water in the ordinary way, though if you kneel and touch it your hand comes away wet and ordinary. Once a season, on no schedule anyone has written down, the level drops by a hand’s width and the stone around the sump shows a ring, clean and new, and then the water returns and the ring is gone. No one has seen it happen. The piston, in those hours, is quiet for longer than usual. Then it breathes again, and the Floor goes on, hot and dim and green and full, with nowhere in it that is empty and nowhere in it that hurries.
