The whole of it fits inside a walk you could finish before the light changes. A rim of poured mineral, waist-high in most places and higher where the mold overran, closes a floor no larger than a parish church stood on its side. Beyond the rim there is only the drop and a high, ordinary sky. The air up here is thin enough that a fast climb leaves a bright edge in the chest, but the enclosure itself is warm—process-warm, not sun-warm—and so wet that the warmth never dries.

Nothing underfoot is older than the work that made it. The ground is a single casting, still telling on itself: swirl-marks where the slurry was pushed, a paler skin where the surface set before the body did, hairline seams that have not yet decided whether they are joints or veins. Weather has not had time to find them. There is no grit worked loose, no rounded lip, no stain that took seasons. What looks like stone is closer to a scab. Press a thumb into a shaded face and it gives, very slightly, the way cooling slag gives, and comes away with a chalk of salts that were not there at dawn.

Water does not arrive from anywhere you can point to and then leave. It condenses out of the air onto every new surface, and it weeps from the casting wherever the interior is still hotter than the skin. Along the north rim a continuous sheet moves no faster than a watch-hand, thick as oil in the hollows, breaking into beads where a leaf has laid a wax. In the lowest third of the floor the sheet never quite becomes a pool. It stands in the pore-space of the unfinished ground, a bright dark just under the crust, and if you shift your weight the crust sighs and a ring of water rises around your boot and sinks again. Gutters were formed into the mold—clean industrial radii, too perfect—but they are already half-lost under a felt of roots, and the water prefers the roots. It climbs them. On the warm pipe that crosses the western third, a column of droplets travels upward against sense, fattening until one breaks and slaps the leaves below. The slap is the loudest thing most hours.

The plants were put in as a specification and have exceeded it. Seed mats, pressed into the formwork before the last pour, have come up through every weakness in the skin: a lawn of small ferns on the flat, then ranker things where runoff gathers—broad paddles with veins like buried cable, strap-leaves that clasp the pipe and sweat on its lee side, a thicket of jointed stems in the southeast corner tall enough to hide a person if a person were in the habit of hiding. Nothing here is ancient growth. You can still see, under the green, the grid the mats were cut to. But the grid is losing. Leaves overlap leaves until the floor is a single layered dark, and flower-brackets—small, waxy, the color of cooling metal—open and shut on a cycle shorter than a day, tied to the pulse of heat in the lines rather than to the sun. When the lines ease off, the brackets close in a soft successive click, like seed-pods, almost inaudible. When the lines come up again they open, and for a little while the thicket smells of hot sugar and wet concrete.

The lines are the only machinery you are meant to notice, and you notice them anyway. A manifold enters under the rim at the southwest and branches into runs no thicker than a wrist, lagged in a fiber that the ferns have already learned to grip. Somewhere beneath the casting an engine turns—an industrial unit, sized for a room, not a district—and its work arrives here as warmth, as a slow push of water through the buried circuit, and as a hum so low it is more a change in the way the rim feels under your hand than a sound in the air. Valves trip on a schedule. You hear them as a single soft knock, then the new route of water announcing itself by a change in which leaves drip. Vents at the four corners breathe out saturated air in turns, so that a fog-bank the size of a table forms, drifts a few paces, and is drunk back into the leaves before it can become weather. The place is busy the way a stomach is busy. Almost nothing of that business is loud.

Fauna is nearly an absence, which makes the exceptions severe. There are no birds on the rim and no tracks in the wet skin except your own, which fill and vanish. Midges sometimes lift off a warm film and settle again, too few to cloud. Once in a long while a pale mantis-like thing, the length of a finger, climbs a strap-leaf to the height of the vent cycle and waits there with its forelimbs folded, not hunting anything you can see. It may be a stray. It may have been specified and then forgotten when the order was reduced. It does not call. In the culvert where the manifold enters, something small shifts the water without breaking the surface—an eel of rubber, or a length of loose lagging, or a living muscle using the pipe as a burrow. You do not get a second look. The thicket does not rustle around it. Whatever else was meant to live here has not arrived, or has arrived and found the geology too new to trust: no soil crumbs, no old deadfall, nowhere to den that will not still be curing under them.

Light is plain daylight, let in over the rim and down through a lattice that was structural and is now also a trellis. Shadows are sharp at noon and soft by midafternoon when the vent-fog is on its round. Nothing here fluoresces. Nothing here is night-lit. The strangeness is not in the illumination. It is in the order of events. The plants are older than the stone they stand on. The water is a byproduct of manufacture and has already learned a preference. The erosion that should be writing the place—rounding, cutting, carrying away—has not started, because there is not yet anything loose enough to carry. Instead the landscape is still being added to. Each afternoon a skin of new mineral feathers out from the warm joints, white at first, then clear, locking the morning’s leaf-litter into the floor before the litter can rot. Each night the casting contracts a fraction and the seams tick, not as breakage but as continuation, the way ice ticks when it is still deciding to be ice. By morning a root has followed the new seam down, and the seam has followed the root, and neither of them was in yesterday’s drawing.

If there is a rule, it is that the enclosure finishes itself faster than it can be corrected. Crews come rarely and leave quickly; their boot-prints are the only erosion, and the water closes them before the next shift is scheduled. Tools left on the rim acquire a rind. A spare gasket, set down and forgotten, has sprouted a collar of the small ferns and is no longer a gasket in any way that matters. The work order framed on the inner face of the southwest wall—paper under a scratched pane, ink already running from condensate—still names this a curing bay, interim, to be struck when the pour tests out. The bay has declined to remain interim. It has a climate, a drainage heresy, a bloom-cycle, a single patient hunter, and a floor that is younger than its garden. Stand in the middle and the boundaries are all visible: rim, sky, the drop, the four vents breathing. Stand there long enough and the middle stops being a point in a construction and becomes a place with its own pace, wet and warm and green, assembled, unfinished, and already keeping time.
