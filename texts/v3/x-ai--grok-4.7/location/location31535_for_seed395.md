The light arrives as ordinary day and stays that way: a high, colorless brightness with no drama in it, only the steady fact of being able to see. Under it the land runs out in every direction until distance itself becomes the weather. This is not a valley you could walk the rim of. It is a basin the size of a continent, pressed low, so low that the horizon does not rise to meet you. The sky sits on it like a lid.

The ground is new. That is the first thing the feet understand, if there were feet that cared to notice. The stone has not yet learned to be old. It comes up in plates and seams still fitting themselves, pale mineral the color of bone meal and kiln dust, edges sharp where nothing has had time to round them. Cracks open and knit in the same season. A ridge that was not there at dawn may be there by the second hour of heat, not violently — the emergence is quiet — but as a slow shouldering, the crust assembling itself from whatever lies under the warmth. There are no river-cuts, no gullies speaking of long weather. Erosion has barely begun its work. The land is still arriving.

And yet almost none of that young stone can be seen.

What covers it is a city, and the city is plants, and the plants are packed until the idea of a gap becomes theoretical. They are not a forest in the old sense, with trunks and clearings and a floor you could name. They are a continuous occupation: mats, tubes, swollen columns, shelves of leaf that lock into the shelves above them, root-lattices that have become walls because there was nowhere else for a root to go except through its neighbor and then, finding purchase, to stay. The growth is lush in mass, not in wetness. Leaves are thick, waxy, sometimes the size of doors, sometimes no larger than a fingernail and numbered past counting. Sap runs but does not spill. Cut one and the wound skins over before the cut is finished, a shine of stored moisture beading and then gone, drunk back into the tissue as if the plant had been embarrassed to show it.

There is no water on the surface. No creek, no pan, no dark thread in a crease of ground. The air does not taste of rain. What moisture exists is inside — in the columns, in the animals, in the thin sealed skins of fruiting bodies that never open toward the sky. The whole basin runs on a closed account. Every drop is spoken for before it can become a drop. Dew, if it forms at all in the cool before full light, is taken off the leaf by the leaf, pores drinking their own night-sweat. Nothing pools. Nothing runs. The young stone beneath stays dry enough that you could strike it and raise only dust.

The warmth is constant and bodily, not the slap of a desert noon so much as the heat of a room that has been occupied too long. It gathers under the canopy and does not leave. By midmorning the lower galleries are close. Higher shelves, where the growth thins toward the light by a hand’s breadth only, carry a drier breath, and that difference — a few degrees, a little less saturation — is enough to organize the city. Things that need the heat live low. Things that need to dry a seed or a wing live high. The arrangement is not designed. It is what density does when it has had many generations and almost no empty space.

Call it urban because there is no better word for the order, and no worse word for the lack of tools. Nothing here was built with an instrument. There are avenues anyway: long compressions where the growth has been walked, pressed, and re-pressed until the living walls hardened into corridors a body can pass. There are chambers where a particular plant has died in place and its hollow has been kept open by the traffic of smaller lives, the walls slowly replaced by new growth so that the room persists while every part of it is replaced. There are stacks — three, five, nine levels of shelf and gallery — where the upper stories root into the lower and the lower brace the upper, a construction that would be called infrastructure if anyone had intended it. Ventilation is an accident of heat: warm air finds the seams and climbs; cooler air sinks along the bone-colored stone where a crack has not yet been sealed by roots. Bridges exist where two columns leaned, touched, and fused. Markets, if that is the word, are places where fruiting coincides, and the coincidence has become a habit of the season, and the habit has worn the ground around it into a pad of packed fiber.

No device. No metal left on purpose. No wheel, no fired brick, no cut timber. The technology, such as it is, is the body: a jaw that prunes, a secretion that glues, a generation that remembers a path by walking it until the path is a wall with a hole in it. The city is as organized as a termite mound blown up to the scale of a coast, and as innocent of plans.

It is full of animals, and you do not hear them.

The quiet is not emptiness. It is the sound of a place with no loose water to clap against stone, no wind with a long fetch, no canopy sparse enough to roar. The air moves, but through baffles. A wingbeat dies in a hand’s span of leaves. Claws on fiber make a soft tick that does not carry. What you get, standing still, is a low continuous presence just under hearing: the rasp of mandibles, the shift of a heavy body settling its weight on a shelf, the minute crackle of new mineral shouldering up somewhere beneath the root-mat, too deep and too slow to be called noise. Now and then a call — short, dry, more click than voice — and then the city takes it and does not give it back.

The fauna occupy every thickness. In the sealed dark against the stone, pale things that never see the ordinary light work the seam where root meets new crust, eating the mineral film and the dead fiber and each other. Above them, in the galleries, runners and climbers and slow browsers keep the avenues from sealing shut; their passage is a maintenance the plants endure because the alternative is suffocation. Higher, where the light actually arrives, smaller fliers work the flowers that open inward, toward the warm galleries, not outward toward a sky that has nothing to offer them. Predators are abundant and local. A hunt is a matter of one shelf and one mistake. Nothing courses for miles. There is no room to course, and no waterhole to wait beside, because there is no waterhole. Thirst is not a landscape feature. It is a private failure, inside a body that has spent its store.

The day has a pace anyway. Heat climbs. The upper leaves tighten. A scent of resin comes up, not sweet, more like warm wax and crushed stem, and with it a fine drift of spores and scales that never quite becomes dust in the air because something is always eating the drift. In the deep galleries the temperature lags, so the city inhales and exhales on a delay: morning still pooled below while noon already owns the roof. Animals move with that delay. The lower traffic thickens when the upper shelves grow too warm to hold a body still. By late light the avenues low down are as packed as the vegetation, a second density inside the first, and still almost silent — the sound of many careful passings, each one unwilling to spend moisture on a noise.

The ground does not wait for them. While they traffic, the basin continues to assemble. A plate tilts by a finger’s width over the course of an afternoon and the root-city adjusts without comment: a corridor kinks, a chamber’s floor becomes a ramp, a fused bridge takes a new load and thickens on the side that now carries weight. This is the emerging quality of the place made ordinary. The land is not finished, so the city cannot be finished. Construction is continuous and unsigned. What looks, from a little distance, like a settled urban mass — skyline of swollen towers, districts of different leaf-color where different guilds of growth have claimed the heat — is in fact a thing still being poured, still fitting its stones, still learning which of its rooms the day will allow.

Weather, at this scale, is mostly the behavior of heat over an unbroken living surface. There are no storm fronts marching in off a sea, because there is no sea. There are thermal regions: broad domes of rising air above districts that have fruited and are venting warmth, slower sinks where a span of growth has died back to the mineral and the pale stone throws the day back upward. Boundaries between these regions can be felt as a change in the closeness of the air, a seam you walk through. Sometimes, far off, a column of the city collapses under its own unsupported height where the ground shrugged, and the collapse is a long soft rush, muffled, over before the sound has decided what it is. Then the edges of the ruin are already being occupied. Bare stone does not stay bare. Within days the first mats have sealed it. Within a season the rooms have returned, not the same rooms, but rooms.

At the edge of any local view the same fact repeats: more of it. The basin does not offer a margin. Low, warm, bright with ordinary light, young underneath and ancient in the habit of its crowding, it continues until continuation is the only feature left. A traveler who somehow crossed a day’s worth of galleries would find, at evening, the same resin-smell, the same unspilled moisture locked in the same thick leaves, the same quiet abundance working its routes, and beneath all of it the mineral still rising, still raw, still becoming the floor of a city that does not know it is a city and does not need to.

Nothing here is strange if you accept the account it keeps. Water is not absent from the world; it is absent from the open. It moves as flesh and sap and the brief internal weather of a lung. The land is not barren; it is unfinished, and the unfinished part is hidden by a growth so complete that stone appears only when something breaks. The building is not invention; it is what happens when abundant lives, in a warm low place large enough to have weather, refuse to leave a gap. The silence is not peace and not death. It is the sound of a continent that has put all its noise inside its bodies, and kept the air for breathing.
