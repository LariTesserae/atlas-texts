The basin is small enough that the far wall is always in sight, and large enough that the sight does not help. A rim of new stone circles it—knife-edges, unrounded, the color of wet iron where the light catches and the color of nothing where it does not. You could walk the circuit in an hour if the circuit agreed to remain a circuit. It does not age and it does not finish. The high air here is cool enough to ache in the teeth, and thick enough that breath comes back beaded.

Nothing has been built. There is no path, no post, no soot-stain of a fire. The loudness is not a machine anyone hauled up. It is the stone. Every face is fluted as if something withdrew its fingers while the rock was still deciding to be rock, and through those flutes a pressure moves that never starts and never stops. It has the timbre of a mill: mesh, vent, stamp, the long watery hiss of a belt that is not a belt. The sound does not rise. It has no hour. Stand anywhere and it is the same loudness, a foundry heard through a shut door, except there is no door and the foundry is the distance between one edge and the next.

Water is near. Not a lake—there is not enough room, and a lake would have a level, and levels are a kind of decision. Instead the rock weeps on every plane, including planes that ought to shed. Drops gather on overhangs and do not fall; drops gather on upward edges and do not run back. The air itself condenses in sheets you can walk through. They wet the cloth and the cloth does not then dry, because drying would be a change and this place has already spent its change. Underfoot the stone is sharp and filmed. A step finds purchase and also finds that purchase was already there, as if the foot had been set down long before the body arrived to own it.

Light is low and close, a ceiling of wet slate that is not the sky and not not the sky. No sun-path. Shadows are short, blunt, and frequently disloyal: a fern throws a darkness that belongs to a thicker stem, or to no stem, and the darkness does not correct itself. In that dimness the green is almost indecent. Vegetation is lavish and widely spaced, as if the basin permits extravagance only in isolation. A single moss bolster, cathedral-soft, fills a crack and stops exactly where the crack stops. Yards of bare new rock. Then a fern taller than a person, each pinna beaded, none of them stirring. Yards again. A cushion of tiny leaves so dense they look poured, occupying a bowl of stone no wider than outstretched arms, and nothing like it anywhere else in view. The lushness is not a carpet. It is a series of permissions.

Between the permissions the animals keep their intervals.

There are many of them. That is the first wrongness, and then it becomes ordinary: abundance without crowding. A dark bird holds an angle of wing that is neither upstroke nor down, and from that angle comes the full sound of a wingbeat, continuous, unprogressive, loud enough to sit inside the mill-noise without joining it. Something low and long occupies a wet ledge and is always in the middle of crossing it. You never see it begin the ledge or end it. Its feet—or the parts that meet stone—tick against the film of water at a rate that could be walking, except the body does not advance. Elsewhere a cluster of pale moths hangs in a column of condensation, wings working, column unmoving, each moth sharp as the rock is sharp, recently emerged and incapable of having emerged, because emergence is a sequence and sequence is what the basin refuses.

They do not flock. They do not hunt in turn. Each life has a station the way each fern has a crack. The stations are far apart. Emptiness is not absence here; it is the rule that keeps the loud things from touching. Cross from one station toward another and the air between them is only cool, wet, roaring, dim. No spoor that updates. No feather that has just fallen. Feathers are either on the body or already part of the film on the stone, and both states are current.

The industrial character is not metaphor once you have stood long enough for standing to fail. The flutes do work. Water enters them and leaves them at the same instant, and in the leaving it is briefly organized—ropes of it, timed, striking the same points on the same lower lips of rock, a stamping you could set a clock by if clocks were allowed to mean difference. Mineral gathers on those strike-points in ridges like the flash on a casting, and the ridges do not thicken. Production without product. In places the condensation spins in fixed bells, small turbines of hanging weather, audible as whine inside the greater roar. Nothing is geared to anything else. There is no line, no output, no one to receive it. The basin manufactures the continuation of itself and discards the continuation, so that what remains is the manufacturing.

Scale insists on boundaries and then mistreats them. From the rim the far wall is a hundred paces off. From the middle it is also a hundred paces off, and the rim behind you has not receded. Cathedral height overhead, grove width underfoot, and both measures are local courtesies. A hand stretched toward a fern crosses less space than the eye budgeted, or more; the hand arrives anyway, because arrival here is not travel. The stone under the palm is new. You can feel the unweathered grain, the snag of crystals that erosion has not yet been permitted to dull. Young rock, high rock, wet as a low marsh and cold as the altitude it claims. All of that is true in the same moment, and the moment does not succeed itself.

There is a rule, if rule is the word for an equilibrium this complete. Nothing hunts to completion. Nothing grows past its station. Nothing dries, rounds, or falls. The drops that should leave the overhangs are the same drops. The bird is the same angle. The stamp of organized water hits the same lip with the same force, forever, and the lip rings and is not worn. Fauna is plentiful because nothing has died in a way that would reduce the count, and nothing has been born in a way that would increase it; they are simply all here, spaced through the loud dim, each one a finished motion that cannot be finished.

If you shout, the shout joins the mill-sound and is not answered and is not echoed and is not gone. It becomes one more harmonic of a place that has already included every sound it will ever make. The basin does not notice. It is high, small, sharp, soaked, green in scattered cathedrals of leaf, empty between them, roaring, cool, unbuilt, and busy with a labor that produces no after. The light does not change to show you the way out. There is no out that is later than in. There is only the rim, still in sight, still refusing to be the edge of a before or an after, and the water on your sleeve, which was there when you looked and is there when you look again, identical, loud in its silence, which is not silence at all.
