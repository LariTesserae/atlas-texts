The place is smaller than a town ought to be, and newer than its noise suggests. It sits in a shallow bowl that has only just finished rising—low ground, not a valley carved by long water, but a floor that cracked, lifted, and stopped. The edges of that floor are still sharp: ledges with unrounded lips, pale stone that flakes under a boot, seams of clay that have not yet learned to slump. Rain has visited, and will again, but it has not had centuries. It leaves films. It beads on metal. It stands in thin sheets on the flat roofs and in the joints between flagstones, then withdraws into the soil and the shade.

Within the bowl the settlement is organized and incomplete. Streets have been surveyed and partly laid. A clocked mill stands where the ground is firmest, its stack drawing a pale column that does not hurry away. Smaller sheds follow the same survey lines, each with its own engine—belt, flywheel, a low thrum that never quite dies between shifts. Power runs on poles that look recently planted, the wood still bright where the bark was stripped. There is infrastructure, but not density. Between one building and the next there is room enough for a garden to forget it is a garden. The built things are few, loud, and carefully spaced, as if the plan assumed more neighbors than ever arrived.

What fills the gaps is growth, and it is lush in a cool way. Ferns the height of a person stand in the ditches. Moss takes the north faces of brick and the undersides of loading platforms, a continuous green that does not creep so much as already be there. Willows, still young, lean over the drainage cuts, their leaves always slightly wet. Vines have found the guy-wires and treat them as trellis. The vegetation is not a jungle pressing in; it is spaced the way the buildings are spaced—thick clumps, then open ground, then another thick clump—so that you can see across the bowl and still feel surrounded. In the open ground the grass is ankle-deep and beaded. Pockets of standing water sit in the unfinished lots, not ponds, not puddles: lenses, sky-colored, with new stone showing through where the water is only a finger deep.

The air is cool without being cold. Breath shows only at the earliest hour. Condensation is the ordinary weather—on railings, on the insides of workshop windows, on the hides of the animals that have decided the place is theirs. Fauna is abundant and not shy. Starlings work the eaves in long, argumentative lines. Frogs keep to the lenses of water and call in overlapping pulses that the engines do not drown so much as ride. Swallows nest in the stack’s shadow and cut the ordinary daylight into flickering pieces. Beetles the size of thumbnails cross the warm plates of idle machinery as if the metal were stone that happened to hum. A heron stands in the largest drainage cut for so long it seems structural. None of this is frantic. The birds return to the same wires. The frogs answer on a slow meter. Even the insects seem to patrol rather than swarm.

Sound is the constant that makes the slowness strange. The mill does not roar; it occupies the air, a broad mechanical note with smaller notes inside it—chains, a stamped rhythm from a press, the wet slap of a belt. Added to that are the frogs, the starlings, the clack of a cart on uneven new paving, water ticking off a roof into a barrel, a whistle that marks the shift and then is gone. You can stand in the middle of an empty lot and still be inside the noise. It does not build toward anything. It holds.

Light is plain daylight, a little softened by the moisture in the air, strong enough to throw hard shadows from the sharp ledges and from the stack. In that light the newness of the ground shows: no polished hollows, no river-rounded cobbles, only edges, and the first dark stains where water has chosen paths it will keep. The weirdness is quiet and local. Machinery left overnight comes back filmed in green where the moss has decided the housing is north-facing earth. A drainage lens will, for an hour after rain, hold the reflection of the stack so clearly that workers walking past step around the reflection as if it were a second stack lying down. Addresses painted on shed doors bead and run, so the numbers are always slightly lower than they were meant to be. None of this is miraculous. It is what happens when a small industrial plan is set down on ground that has not yet agreed to be old, in a cool damp that favors anything willing to grow in the spaces the plan left open.

The rules, such as they are, are patient. Water arrives, stands, and leaves. Engines run their shifts and idle. Stone loses a grain, not a face. The town does not expand while you watch it; it persists, loud and green and recently made, a settlement the size of a grove with a factory’s voice, learning very slowly which of its sharp edges the rain intends to keep.
