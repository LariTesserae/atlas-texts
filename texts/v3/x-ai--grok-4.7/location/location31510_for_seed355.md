The basin does not announce itself. You are already in it when the ground stops rising underfoot and the heat, which had been a pressure against the face, becomes a property of the air itself—thick, mineral, slightly sweet, like stone that has been warm for longer than weather. There is no horizon in the ordinary sense. Darkness sits low and close, not the darkness of a closed room but of a sky that has forgotten the habit of lighting. What light there is arrives as a dull copper smear, nowhere in particular, as if the heat itself were faintly luminous and then thought better of it.

The city occupies the low ground the way a stain occupies cloth. It is not large in the way of capitals, and not small in the way of a village: a scatter of streets and courts and roofless rooms spread across a shallow valley a person could walk in a long day and still not have finished. Between the built places there is a great deal of nothing—mudflats the color of old iron, shallow pans of water no deeper than a wrist, ridges of pale crust that crack under a step and then, after a while, seal themselves again. The emptiness is not abandoned land waiting to be filled. It is part of the plan, if plan is the word for something that has been this way long enough to have worn its own edges smooth.

Nothing here was made with a tool. That is the first wrongness, and it does not go away. The walls are tufa and sinter and a darker stone that sweats. Rooms have corners, lintels, thresholds worn concave by passage, drains that still run. Staircases climb three turns and stop at a landing that opens onto sky, or onto another stair that was not there when you started. You can see, if you look at a broken section, that the masonry is not masonry. The blocks grade into one another. Seams exist, then do not. A doorway narrows by the width of a finger while you stand in it, not quickly, not with any sound you could swear to—only the sense, afterward, that your shoulders had more room on the way in. The city has the grammar of building and none of its verbs. It precipitated. It accreted. People, if there were people, lived in a shape the ground had already decided to take.

Water is everywhere and almost never deep. It sheets across the courts in films that reflect the copper non-light and then, when you turn, reflect something else: a higher wall, a street you have not walked, your own outline delayed by a fraction. It beads on the undersides of overhangs and falls in slow, regular drops onto stone that has been cupped by exactly those drops for so long the cups are polished. In the lower quarter the streets are channels. You walk on raised sills between them, and the water moves with a purpose that is not quite downhill. In one lane it runs toward you, parts around your ankles—hot, not scalding, the temperature of a bath left too long—and rejoins behind you as if you had been a temporary island in a current that knew where it was going before the lane existed. Farther out, where the buildings thin, the pans breathe. A skin of water lifts, holds, settles. Steam rises and does not dissipate so much as wander, snagging on corners, pooling in rooms whose roofs have long since become the sky.

Heat does not relent at any hour, because hour is a courtesy the place does not extend. There is no cooler edge to the dark. Stone that has stood in shadow all the while you have been walking is as warm as stone in the open. The warmth comes up through the soles and down from the air, and the water does nothing to argue with it. Rain, when it comes, is hot. It falls in brief, hard spells that ring on the crust and then vanish into it, and the city smells afterward of wet mineral and something like scorched reed, though reed is rare.

Vegetation keeps to the margins and to the places water lingers longest. A black film clings to the wet faces of walls, not moss, not quite; it has the look of a mineral that learned, imperfectly, to spread. In the outer pans, knee-high stalks rise at wide intervals, rigid, hollow, the color of char. They do not sway. When the hot rain hits them they tick, once each, and go still. Nothing eats them. Nothing nests in them. Nothing moves among them except the water and the slow rearrangement of the crust. You can walk a street for a long time and see no living thing that is not that film or those stalks, and then, at the edge of a court, a single pale shelf growing out of a windowsill like a fungus that has chosen stone instead of wood. Touch it and it is stone. Leave it and, on the way back, it may have thickened, or it may have become part of the sill, the distinction retired without comment.

There are no animals. The quiet is not the quiet of things hiding. It is the quiet of a ledger with that column empty. No insects stitch the air. No bird crosses the copper smear. The drops, the sheet-flow, the occasional soft settle of a wall giving up a scale of sinter—these are the sounds, and they do not accumulate into a din. Even the rain is local. You can stand in a dry court and hear it two streets over, a soft percussion, and walk toward it and find the street already drying, the sound now behind you, or beside you, or in a room you can see is empty. The city is active the way a shoreline is active: always in process, rarely in event. Water arrives, lingers, leaves a line. Heat drives a little more mineral out of solution. A threshold deepens. A lane that was a channel this morning is a damp path, and the channel has opened through a house that previously had no back wall, or had one, and you cannot say which memory is the later one.

The erosion is finished in the way a face is finished: not worn away, worn into itself. Corners that should have crumbled have rounded into a stable curve and stayed. Drains have found their grade and kept it for what feels like centuries, even where the grade, followed with the eye, cannot be reconciled with the slope of the land. The balance is real. Things are not collapsing. They are continuing. A tower—if the word fits a hollow stack of rooms open to the weather on one side—leans, and has leaned, and the lean is part of how it stands. Around its base the crust has built up in thin terraces, each terrace the record of a waterline that held for an age and then dropped a handspan. You can read the terraces like years. The reading does not produce a number. It produces the certainty that the lean and the terraces agreed with each other a long time ago and have not needed to renegotiate.

Scale misbehaves once you try to use it. From a rooftop—the flat crown of a building whose stair you trusted—you can see the basin's far rim, a low dark line, and the scatter of pale walls between you and it, and the shining pans. The distance looks walkable. You walk. The rim does not approach at the rate of your steps. Streets insert themselves. A court you are sure you crossed opens again on your left, identical, including the place where your footprint should be in the crust, and is, and is also, somehow, still ahead of you, filling with water. The city is not infinite. It is mid-sized and badly fitted to the space it occupies, as if a town-sized arrangement of rooms had been pressed into a valley and the valley had stretched in the middle to make room, forgetting to tell the edges. People who wanted a map would fail here for reasons that are not only practical. Two walkers who part at a junction and follow what each insists is the same left-hand lane do not meet again where the lanes should meet. Sometimes they meet where the lanes should not. Sometimes one of them finds the other's wet footprints crossing a sill that, from the other direction, was a wall.

The reality of the place does not break like glass. It fails the way a story fails when the teller loses the order of events and keeps talking. You set a hand on a stone to steady yourself and the stone is dry, then wet, then dry, under the same palm, without your having moved. A doorway frames another doorway, and through that a third, and the third opens onto the court you are standing in, seen from a height you do not occupy. The view does not include you. That is ordinary enough to be unbearable: the city rendering itself without the inconvenience of an observer, accurately, including the drop of water about to fall from the lintel you are under, which then falls, hot, on your wrist, on time. Distance folds in small ways. A voice—not a voice; there is no animal life, and nothing here speaks—would not carry. What carries instead is the occasional coincidence of sounds: your step and, a street away, a step that matches it late, as if the stone had stored the impact and returned it through a different floor. You stop. The echo stops. You were not echoed. You were preceded, or followed, by an event that used your timing because timing, here, is a material that can be reused.

At the western edge of the built ground, if west is the direction in which the copper smear sits slightly thicker, the city frays into a terrace of low walls no higher than a knee, then into isolated sills standing in open water, then into nothing but pan and crust and the rare black stalk. The water there is wider, still shallow, and it moves in slow gyres that do not match the wind, because there is no wind you can feel, only a pressure that comes and goes with the steam. Stand at that edge long enough and the gyres tighten. A patch of pan dries from the center outward, cracking in a pattern that resembles, for a moment, the plan of a court you passed an hour ago—the same alcove, the same off-center drain. Then the hot water returns from the margins and the pattern goes under, and when it surfaces again the alcove is on the other side. The city is not growing in any sense you could measure with a life. It is remembering its own layout in the places where it has not yet been built, or where it has finished being built and the memory has leaked outward into the basin like groundwater.

Night does not come. Day does not come. The dark is the climate. In it, the wet stone holds a little of the copper and gives it back reluctantly, so that edges remain legible if you do not ask them to be sharp. You can live, in the limited sense of moving and drinking and choosing a room, and the room will accept the choice for a while: a corner out of the rain, a sill that stays the height you remember, water that runs past rather than across. The acceptance is provisional. Shift your weight and the corner may be an inch farther from the wall. Sleep, if sleep is possible in the heat, and wake to find the drain now crossing the threshold, politely, leaving your dry patch intact and smaller. The city is not hostile. Hostility would require an interest in you. It is busy with its own processes—precipitation, solution, the long argument between heat and water, the slower argument between a form and the space that form believes it occupies—and you are light enough, temporary enough, to be carried along as debris is carried, not harmed, not kept.

What emerges, if you stay, is not a secret and not a history. The stalks do not conceal a grove. The walls do not conceal a mechanism. Under the oldest terraces the stone is the same stone, only denser, the cups of the drips deeper, the lean of the towers agreed-upon further back. The weirdness is not a veil over a truer city. It is the city's steady state: a low, hot, half-drowned settlement that built itself by the same patience that builds stalactites, inhabited the grammar of streets without the need for inhabitants, and now maintains itself in a dark that has no opposite, quietly, actively, with a great deal of space between its presences, each presence exactly as solid as it needs to be until you try to count it twice.
