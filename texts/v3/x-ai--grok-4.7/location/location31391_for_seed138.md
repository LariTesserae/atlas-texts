The ground is new enough that it still looks made. A low bar of silt and fill, barely above the lagoon, cut into a small grid of channels whose banks have not yet slumped. Tool marks remain in the clay. The lips of the outfalls are sharp. Water stands in every hollow and weeps from the cuts as if the water table had been waiting just under the surface and the first shovel let it out. Nothing here has had time to round.

Heat sits on it without weather. There is no clear hour when the day breaks or fails. The air is saturated, and the engines add their own water to it, so a thin steam hangs at chest and roof height and never quite lifts. Light comes through as a dull warm gray. You can see the length of a walkway, then the next building as a darker block, then nothing. Metal is hot to the rest of a hand. Concrete sweats. Boards never dry; they silver and stay slick. Asphalt in the yards softens and takes the print of boots, and by the next shift the print is gone, not because anyone smooths it but because the surface never sets.

The settlement is small enough to walk across before the steam closes again. A dozen structures, not a city: an engine hall with the proportions of a church and the manners of a shed, a row of worker rooms raised on piers, a water tower, two long fabrication bays, a pier and a crane, a substation fenced in fresh chain-link that has not yet bowed. Between them is open ground—wet fill, a service road, a canal. The plan is urban in the way a town plan is urban: graded, drained, numbered, piped, meant to be read as infrastructure. It is not dense. You cross empty yards to get from one presence to the next. The emptiness is part of the design. Heat needs room. So do the channels.

Inside the hall the sound is the place’s true climate. A pair of engines—heavy, oil-fed, coupled to pumps—run at one speed and have run at that speed since the day they were leveled on their pads. They do not cycle. Load does not rise and fall with a shift in demand; the work is the same work. Water is lifted from the lagoon, passed through condensers and wash lines, and returned, a little warmer, by a buried main whose outfall keeps a permanent boil of silt in a precise circle. The level in the channels does not change. Markers painted on the piles sit exactly at the waterline, the paint still bright because it is new and because the line never moves off it. A shift whistle blows on the hour. It is loud, and then it is gone, and the engines were there under it the whole time.

Other sounds live in the gaps and do not disturb the equilibrium. Frogs in the unused ditch at the south edge, a ditch the crew has not bothered to clear because it does not affect grade. Herons lifting off the canal with a single heavy clap. The tick of expanding sheet metal on the bays. Water slapping the piers at a height that does not vary. Midges in a thin continuous hiss wherever condensate drips off a pipe onto warm concrete. None of it builds toward anything. The loudness is steady, the way the heat is steady.

Little grows. The soil is fresh and salt-touched, and the air is too dim and too hot for a canopy to bother. A skin of algae mats the slower channels, green-black, thick enough to wrinkle when a fish turns under it. One stand of reeds holds the south ditch and stops at the maintained bank as if it had been told the measurement. Biofilm slicks the north faces of concrete, orange and gray, more scum than plant. There are no trees. Shade, such as it is, comes from steam and from the buildings.

Animals are everywhere the plants are not. The warm return water carries food—organic waste from the wash lines, settled and continuous, not a spill—and the lagoon edge answers. Mullet school in the outfall circle and hold there, a dark turning mass you can watch from the pier for as long as you like; they do not leave and they do not arrive. Egrets stalk the channel margins with the patience of things that have learned the water will be in the same place tomorrow. Swallows nest in the high louvers of the engine hall and hawk the midge columns that stand over every drip. Crabs work the fresh clay of the cuts, pincers clicking, burrows collapsing because the banks are too new to hold a hole for long; they dig again by evening. Cats sleep on the warm pads outside the hall and hunt the same routes along the piers. Dragonflies patrol the algae mats in numbers that seem wrong for so little green, their wings the brightest things in the dimness until they land and vanish into it.

People fit the same pattern. The settlement was built for a crew and has a crew, no more. They move between the rooms on the piers and the hall and the bays on raised walks, because the ground is either fill that shifts or water that does not. Shifts change; the number of people in view does not, or not in any way you could count without standing still and keeping a tally the place itself refuses to care about. Laundry hangs and dries poorly. Cooking smells stay low in the wet air and mix with hot oil. Nobody remarks on the noise. After a week you hear it the way you hear your own pulse: as the condition under which other sounds occur.

What the place enforces is simple, and it enforces it by running. Water will be at the marks. The engines will be at speed. Edges will be kept, because young ground slumps if you let it, and the crew cuts the banks back to vertical when they soften, so the landscape stays new by labor rather than by youth alone. You do not wade the channels; the bottom is slick silt over clay and the water is warm enough to be unpleasant and deep enough, in the mains, to matter. You do not expect a view. You do not expect the light to improve after a front, because fronts do not come through this pocket of air in any form that changes it. The lagoon beyond the levee is the boundary, and on most hours you know it only as a paler gray and a different smell, less oil, more mud.

At the west end of the service road the fill simply stops. A line of new piles, concrete still pale, marks where the bar ends and the lagoon bottom falls away. Standing there, the settlement is behind you as a low cluster of roofs and one tower, all of it sounding, none of it large. Steam thins for a moment off the water and you see the whole of it—the hall, the gaps, the gleam of a channel, a white bird lifting—and then the air closes and the place reduces again to heat, to the boom of the engines through the ground, and to the nearest wet surface, which is always close, and always exactly where it was.
