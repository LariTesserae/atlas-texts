The room is small enough that its far wall can be found by walking, and high enough that the walking never quite forgets the drop. It was finished recently. Nothing in it has had time to soften.

What holds you is water, and the water is made. It was mixed, clarified, heated, and pumped into a volume whose every face was already built: a nave the size of a large chapel, raised on a new pier, its floor a plate of sintered ceramic still bright at the edges, its walls a lattice of bolted trays and cast resin meant to pass for stone only if you do not look at the fasteners. There is no bedrock under it. There is no soil that was ever a hillside. The substrate is a manufactured cake—fiber, mineral, binder, perforated so that roots can be persuaded—and it was laid down after the walls, the way a floor is laid in a factory that has decided to grow things.

The water fills the nave to a line just under the lantern roof. It is warm, not hot: the warmth of a cistern left in the sun, of a bath that has been held all afternoon at one temperature by a loop you cannot see working hard. Daylight comes through the roof as ordinary daylight. The water has been stripped of the murk that would have made a depth of it. Color does not die with distance. Reds in the leaves stay red. The light does not go blue. Standing in it is like standing in air that has mass, a clear medium that presses the ribs with a patient, even hand and then forgets to vary.

Outside the glass—if the membrane can be called glass; it is a made sheet, tensioned, replaceable—the pier falls away. The elevation is not a mountain's. It is an engineering decision. The whole room was jacked. You can see, if you look down through a floor port, the fresh collars of the columns, steel still silver, paint still unscratched, the ground far enough below that weather has not yet decided what to do with the new shape. The features are sharp because nothing has worked on them. Joints meet at the angle they were drawn. Bolt heads throw hard little shadows. There is no rounding, no stain line, no moss in the sense of a thing that arrived. What grows here was installed.

It is packed. There is no aisle that stays an aisle. Trays step up the walls in a pitch too tight for a body to pass sideways without turning. Between the trays, pipe. Between the pipe, cable in looms clipped so close to the leaf that a frond will sometimes rest its weight on a conduit and stay there, because nothing here moves fast enough to shake it off. The vegetation is lush in the way of a crop that has been given everything and asked not to stop: broad leaves, layered, overlapping until the manufactured wall is only a rumor of grid behind green. Some of the leaves are the size of doors. Some are small and innumerable, a turf of engineered blades rooted in pockets that are themselves pockets in other pockets. Blossoms occur where a dosing manifold has, on its slow schedule, decided that a section should flower. They open over days. They do not fall so much as finish, and a collector arm—thin, oiled, nearly silent—takes them when the cycle says they are done.

There are no animals. No fish ribbon through the clear. No insect tests the blossoms. No bird mistakes the lantern for sky and strikes it. Whatever would have fed, pollinated, or scavenged has been designed out, and the absence is not a lack you hear; it is a rule. The flowers do not need visitors. The pumps do not need guardians. Pruning is a wheel with a soft edge that returns on a period longer than a shift and shorter than a season, and when it passes it takes only what the schedule marked.

The industry of the place is everywhere and almost inaudible. Circulation is not a rush. It is a replacement. Valves the size of a fist open by quarter-turns across hours. A heat engine somewhere in the pier—below the floor, inside the column, you cannot say—keeps the water at its one warmth by burning or by drawing, it hardly matters; what reaches the nave is a gradient so weak you feel it only if you hold still beside a return pipe and notice that one side of your hand is a degree kinder. Motors exist. They are geared down until their work is a suggestion. The loudest thing, if you call it loud, is the tick of a relay in a cabinet packed behind leaves, and even that is more touch than sound, a small mechanical fact conducted through water into the bones of the ear.

Sound, otherwise, is what water does when it has been told not to fall. There is no rain. There is no wave. A bubble, when one escapes a root mat, rises with the slowness of a decision and breaks against the underside of the lantern as a click so small it might have been imagined. Leaves rub, sometimes, when the pruning wheel's passage leaves them resettling. That is all. People who stay long enough begin to hear their own joints.

The strangeness is not a monster in the corner. It is the medium. The water behaves as architecture. It has a surface, and the surface is flat, held, a ceiling you can rise to and touch with a palm; it does not chop, does not skin over with wind, because wind is not admitted. Below that surface the liquid is breathable in the only sense that matters here: the leaves do not drown. They were selected, or built, to drink a formulated water and to treat it as both drink and sky. Roots do not rot. Stems do not lodge and collapse. A cut stem seals on a schedule, the sap replaced by a polymer bead from a nozzle no larger than a seed. You can watch a repaired stem for an hour and see nothing, then realize the bead has clouded from clear to the color of the stem, and that this was the event.

The rules are slow and total. Nothing enters that was not put on a manifest. Nothing leaves except by the collector, the drain, the sample line. Erosion is not permitted yet, and has not had time besides. The water is too clean, too still, too new to gnaw. Where a bolt might someday weep a rust tear, it is still bright. Where a tray edge might round, it is still a line. The room is early in its own life. Its age is measured in cycles of the dosing pumps, not in weather.

Change, when it comes, comes as a correction. A section of leaf yellows—not from season, there is no season in the lantern except the one the lights and the heaters counterfeit, and they counterfeit noon—and a technician's mark, left weeks ago in grease pencil on a ceramic upright, says the yellowing was expected and will be harvested on the next pass. The upright itself may be replaced before it ever stains. Panels unship. A new grid locks in with a sound like a latch in another room. For a day the new ceramic is whiter than the rest, a patch of unlived surface in a place already full, and then the leaves close over it and the difference becomes a thing you would have to remember.

If you look up, the surface of the water is a dim mirror of the green below it, and above that mirror the lantern admits a sky that is only light, not place. If you look down, through the floor port, the pier's fresh steel dwindles toward a ground that has not yet learned the room exists. The nave does not sway. It was built not to. It sits in its warmth, packed to its edges with made leaf and made pipe, a small high cistern in which growth and machinery occupy the same inches, and in which the only weather is the one the engines keep, quietly, for as long as they are told.
