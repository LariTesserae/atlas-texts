The court is smaller than the sound it holds. You come down into it by a stair that was cut, not found—treads of fired brick, each one dished in the middle where feet have passed for longer than anyone counting feet has been alive. The stair turns once, then opens, and the place declares its size all at once: a single volume, no larger than a hamlet’s common ground, roofed by nothing but the rim of the excavation and a ring of squat brick piers that once carried a cover and now carry only weather. Daylight arrives unremarked, ordinary noon-light falling straight down the throat of the court and striking the pale floor, then climbing back up the walls as a dull reflected wash. It is not a cave. Someone made the hole, and then made everything inside the hole, and then the hole learned how to be a place.

There is no ground here that was not laid. The floor is a pavement of small clay tiles, set in a lime that has gone the color of old bone, and where a tile has failed the gap has been filled not with soil but with more tile, broken and rebedded, generation after generation of the same refusal. The walls are the same material stacked and coursed, battered slightly inward so the court reads as a bowl. Halfway up, a walkway projects—stone slabs on timber that has long since been replaced by more stone, corbelled, the undersides stained dark where hands have steadied themselves. Above that, niches. Below that, more niches. The whole inner face is a honeycomb of hand-scooped cells, finger-wide to body-wide, packed so close that the partitions between them are thinner than a wrist. Nothing in the court stands apart from anything else. Ledges nearly touch. A person crossing the walkway brushes the wall with both shoulders if they do not turn sideways. Density here is not crowding of bodies so much as the decision, made once and never revised, that every surface should be a surface something else could occupy.

Warmth sits in the brick. The mass holds the day’s heat and gives it back slowly, so even when the light goes thin the air stays close, a little above the temperature of skin, never hot enough to drive anything out. Moisture is stingier. It does not flow so much as occur. A film gathers on the shaded undersides of the corbels and lets go, one drop at a time, into channels that were cut when the floor was first laid and have since been deepened by nothing but the drops themselves. The channels have found their own grades. They no longer match the masons’ falls exactly; they kink where a tile heaved, pool where a joint opened, and then, having worried at the problem for centuries, they run true again—shallow, dark, decisive. In three places the water gives up and stands: a basin no wider than a shield, a longer trough along the north curve, and a cup worn into the base of a pier where drip has drilled the same spot since the pier was raised. The rest of the court is dry to the touch and damp to the nose. After rain—and rain does come, sliding down the stair and sheeting off the rim—the basins rise a handspan and the channels speak, and within a day they have sunk back to their ordinary secretive measure.

Plants have been offered this and have mostly declined. A seam of green threads the lip where condensation never quite dries, not a mat, a stitching: small liverworts, a moss that browns at the center and stays emerald only at its advancing edge. In the north trough a single clump of sedge has taken the crack between two tiles and stayed there, sparse, blades bent by the animals that drink around it. Nothing roots deep because there is no deep. Beneath the pavement is more pavement, and beneath that the packed fill the builders put down so the court would not heave. The vegetation knows it. It lives on surface and on patience.

What the plants refused, the animals took entire. The niches are full. Swifts stitch the column of light from rim to floor and back, their turns so tight the wingtips tick the brick, and the tick is part of the noise. In the smaller cells, lizards the color of dry mortar hold so still they read as flaws in the masonry until a swift’s shadow crosses and a throat pulses. Beetles work the damp seams with a dry rasping you hear when the swifts bank away. At the basins, something smaller and wetter: frogs no bigger than a thumb, and in the longest trough a scatter of fingerling fish that have no business being here and have been here anyway, pale, quick, living on whatever the drips deliver and on each other. Higher up, where the walkway throws a permanent dusk, nests of mud and fiber plug the honeycombs three deep, and the birds that own them—not the swifts; something squatter, louder, with a call like two stones knocked—come and go on a schedule the court seems to keep better than any person does. Their guano has glazed the piers beneath the nests into a black glass that the rain polishes and never quite removes. The ecology is the architecture used as geology. Every ledge is a territory. Every drip is a resource. The boundaries are the walls, and within them the volume is saturated: wing, scale, shell, throat, all of it layered until the air itself seems occupied.

It is loud in the way a small room with hard surfaces is loud. Nothing here absorbs. A single drip arrives as a tick and leaves as a chord, the basin answering the wall answering the pier. The swifts cry on the turn, and the cry multiplies, so that six birds sound like a flock and the flock sounds like weather. The stone-knock birds answer in volleys. Feet on the walkway—when there are feet—report themselves to the whole court before the walker has come into view. Even the fish register, a faint surface-pop when one rises, amplified into something you could mistake for a finger against clay. The sound does not fade between events. It laps. The court is always in the middle of being heard.

None of this is strange in the way of marvels. The oddness is quieter and more structural: the water still obeys courses cut by hands that did not know fish, the nests are built to the module of a brick, the basins hold at levels the overflow lips still govern. The place was made with simple tools—adze, mold, fire, a string line, a willingness to lay one course and then another—and it has been kept, where it has been kept, by the same order of means. You can see the repairs. A patch of smaller brick, a pier Sistered with a pier, a channel re-cut a finger deeper where the old one silted and was not cleaned but superseded. No metal shows except a few pins, black and swollen, holding a slab that wanted to walk. The technology never rose. It didn’t need to. The court reached its form and then weathered inside that form, losing edges, gaining glaze, the way a tool loses edges and becomes more itself.

What moves, moves on several clocks at once. The drips keep the smallest. The swifts keep the daylight, absent before the light finds the floor, gone before it leaves the rim. The frogs answer rain with a noise that fills the bowl and then, as the basins fall, thins to single notes and stops. Twice a day the shadow of the rim walks the pavement in a slow blade, and everything that suns itself—lizard, beetle, the sedge—shifts to stay in it or out of it, a migration of a few handspans that nonetheless rearranges the whole visible life of the floor. Somewhere under the walkway a process slower than any of these is still at work: lime leaching, a stalactite the length of a finger growing from a corbel that was meant to be dry, dripping into a cup it is itself deepening. It will meet the floor. It has been meeting the floor for a long time. No one is hurrying it, and nothing in the court suggests it should be hurried.

Stand in the middle of the pavement, where the light is plainest and the channels all run away from you toward their three small waters, and the court feels finished in the sense that a well-used bowl feels finished: not pristine, not ruined, settled into the use that replaced the use it was built for. The builders wanted a cistern, or a gathering floor, or a tomb of air. What they got, after the weather and the wings and the drip had their centuries, is a room that breathes through its own brick, loud with tenants, warm, almost dry, green only where it must be, and entirely made—every surface a decision that the animals have counter-signed by living on it. The rim cuts the sky to a disk. Beyond the disk is whatever country surrounds the excavation, unseen from here, irrelevant. Inside the disk the rules are local and strict. Water goes where the cuts allow. Light comes from above and stops at the floor. Sound does not leave. Nothing roots that was not brought, and almost nothing was brought. What remains is occupation: dense, warm, noisy, and, in its own small circumference, complete.
