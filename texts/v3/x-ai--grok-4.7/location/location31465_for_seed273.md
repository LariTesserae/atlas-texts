The road arrives already tired. It has been climbing for so long that the last switchback feels less like a turn than a decision the mountain made years ago and then forgot. Above it, the ridge does not crest into sky. It crests into more ridge: a long constructed shoulder, pale with limewashed concrete and the finer dust that concrete becomes when wind and sun have had decades at it. Nothing here was left as found. The slopes were cut, the cuts were faced, the faces were terraced, and the terraces were filled. Whatever stone once stood in this place has been taken apart and put back as retaining walls, culvert lips, stair-nosings, and the rough aggregate that shows through where render has flaked. You can walk all afternoon and never put a foot on ground that was not first poured, packed, or laid.

The valley is open in the way a worked basin is open. From the upper parapet you can see across it — a town’s width of roofs and sheds, then the far wall of terraces rising in the same patient geometry, and beyond that a notch where the next valley begins, hazed and bright. It is not a view that ends. It simply loses detail. The scale sits at the size of a day’s errands: you could walk to the far cistern before evening if the stairs were kind, and they are not.

Warmth sits in the stone. At this height the air should bite, and sometimes, in the shade of a deep stairwell, it nearly does. But the mass of the construction holds the day. South faces throw heat back at you by midmorning. Metal roofs ping as they expand. The light is hard and high, a brightness that flattens color until your eyes adjust and then reveals it all at once: the yellow-green of leaves against gray render, the wet black of shaded concrete, the white scald of an unpainted tank. Shadows are short and sharply edged. Nothing is soft except the vegetation, and even that grows as if it has learned the angles.

Water is not a river so much as a system that occasionally remembers it was meant to be one. A thin channel runs the valley’s spine, concrete-lined, sometimes barely a slick and sometimes, after the night’s condensation has been scraped off every roof and piped down, a quick knee-deep pulse that lasts an hour and leaves the bed darker than it found it. Feeder spouts jut from terrace walls at regular intervals. Most of them drip. A few, when a tank above them overflows, speak in a steady rope of water that strikes a stone basin and keeps striking it, having done so long enough to bowl the basin deeper than the masons intended. Moss holds in those bowls and nowhere else with quite the same conviction. The larger dryness is always present — dust on the upper walks, a pale rime on unused pipes — but damp finds every joint. Mortar stays cool to the thumb. Handrails sweat on their undersides. In the early day the whole valley seems to exhale a low mist that never quite becomes fog, only a brightness with grain in it.

The planting is total, and it is not wild. Soil, where you can find it, is a made thing: dark, fibrous, held in beds whose edges are the same concrete as the roads. Yet lushness has outrun the original scheme. Whatever was set in the beds — fruiting shrubs, broad-leafed climbers, a grass that was supposed to stay in strips — has thickened until the geometry only shows at the corners. Leaves overlap leaves. A terrace that was meant to read as a line of small trees now reads as a single green load, sagging the rail beneath it, dropping spent blossom onto the walk in a slow confetti that nobody sweeps until it mats. Vines have taken the guy-wires of the power run and turned them into hedges in the air. In the deeper courts, where four walls meet and a drain keeps the footing wet, the growth is almost tubular: you pass through it rather than beside it, and the light comes down in coins.

The industry is old enough to have a gait. It does not roar in the manner of a new works. It keeps a pace. Somewhere below the middle terraces a mill or a pump-house — the local word is neither, exactly; people say the engine — turns with a low industrial regularity, a rise and fall you feel in the sternum before you hear it as sound. Belts slap. A hopper knocks when it empties, then not for a long while, then knocks again. Cable cars, or something like them, creak on a fixed incline at the valley’s east end, hauling crates of leaf and crate of rind and, on the return, sacks of the pale amendment that keeps the made soil from going tired. Electrification is present and unhidden: porcelain insulators on iron brackets, a transformer cage painted and repainted until the color is only a memory of green, conduits clipped along every wall at knee height and head height, as if the builders distrusted any path that was not also a path for current. Nothing here is smart. It is switched, geared, oiled. The smell, under the green smell, is warm metal and the faint sweetness of sap cooked against a housing.

Sound carries and piles up. The engine. The intermittent water. Birds — a great many birds, more than the planting looks able to feed until you notice the fruit and the insects and the way seed heads are left standing on purpose. Their noise is not a dawn chorus so much as a constant occupancy: sharp calls from the wires, a gabble from the cistern reeds, the tick of claws on tin. Insects add a dry high layer that the brightness seems to sharpen. Wind, when it comes, comes in a sheet along the upper walks and makes every loose sheet of roofing answer it. Under all of that, footfalls. The place is lived-in at the density of a town that never had the space to sprawl, so it stacked. Shutters. A radio two courts away, playing something with a slow beat. The clack of a gate that does not quite latch. Loud, but not chaotic. You can separate the strands if you stand still. Most people do not stand still. They walk as if the noise were weather.

Animals use the construction as if it had been built for them, which, in the practical sense, it now has. Birds nest in the gaps where render failed and was not worth fixing. A kind of small goat — short, sure-footed, collared, not quite tame — picks along the outer lips of the upper terraces, eating what leans over the drop. Their bells are few and dull. Lizards hold the bright walls until a shadow crosses, then vanish into cracks that were expansion joints. In the wetter courts, frogs keep to the basins and answer the engine without appearing to notice it. Higher up, where the terraces thin and the tanks sit in a row like a low industrial ridgeline, hawks turn in the white air, unhurried, using thermals off the concrete. At dusk, which comes fast once the sun drops behind the western cut, bats work the vines. None of this feels stocked for show. It feels accumulated. The made place fed them, sheltered them, and then was too slow to evict them, and now the contract is mutual.

Erosion here is not a wound. It is the settled argument between water, light, and a material that was never meant to last forever and has lasted anyway. Corners are rounded where drip-lines have run for lifetimes. Stair treads dish in the middle. Iron stains fan from every bolt in a pattern so regular it looks designed. A wall that lost its coping has not collapsed; it has stepped back, course by course, and something with small leaves has seated itself in each step and glued the retreat in place. The valley is in its own state. Maintenance happens — you see fresh render, a new belt, a pipe still silver — but it happens as correction, not as conquest. The form is established. Change is the change of a season: which court smells of blossom, which tank is down for cleaning, whether the channel ran this week. People measure time by which terrace is fruiting. The engine’s knock is the same knock their grandparents walked under.

The unusual thing, if you stay long enough to name it, is not a marvel. It is a proportion. There is no outside to the making. Even the scree at the base of the oldest wall turns out, when you kick it, to be crushings — leftover aggregate, spilled and never cleared, now stable under its own weeds. The sky is the only unworked surface, and at this height it looks worked too: bright, close, a little thin, as if the construction had climbed until it met the limit of what the air would still hold and then stopped, politely, and planted the last bed anyway. A single tree stands on the highest tank-roof, rooted in a square of soil boxed in timber and iron, its canopy no larger than a room. From below it reads as a gesture. From beside it, with the valley’s noise rising in layers and the damp warming off the concrete, it is simply the top of the place: one more made thing, doing what all the others do, which is persist, and leaf, and wait for the next slow turn of the light.
