The shelf is newer than the weather that works on it. You feel that before you see it: the ground under the wet still has edges. A fracture in the stone runs like a fresh cut under the green, and where a stem has split it, the break is pale, not stained the way old mountains stain. The whole place sits high enough that the air arrives already thin and already cold, and then gives up what water it has. It does not rain so much as arrive. Moisture beads on everything that is not already dripping, and most things are already dripping.

The shelf is small. From the outer rim you could walk its boundary before the light fails, if the boundary would let you. It will not. Past a certain thickness of growth the cold opens again—bare young rock, wind with nothing to catch on, a drop you cannot see because the plants have sealed the lip. Inside that rim the world is one occupied volume, cathedral-sized if a cathedral were allowed to keep growing after the builders left, and if the builders had been the stone’s own hunger for cover.

There is almost no stone left to see. What shows are teeth: a ridge of fresh fracture where two walls of growth failed to meet, a knuckle of rock weeping from a joint, sharp because nothing has had time to round it. The rest has been taken. Moss does not carpet here; it is the pavement, compressed by its own weight into something that holds a foot and then slowly forgets the print. Above that, stems have braided into partitions. They are not trees in the old sense. They are load-bearing thickets, trunks fused where they touched, bark gone glassy with cold and with the constant film of water. You can put a hand on a wall and feel it working—slow, cold, a faint give, like flesh over a rib.

The organization is what makes the place feel occupied. Growth here did not sprawl. It repeated. Passages recur at roughly the width of a body turned sideways. They bend where a main stem chose the light and the lesser stems obeyed. Floors step up in short terraces of packed root and peat, each lip sharp, unworn, beaded with water that has not yet found a lower path. In places the terraces stack three and four deep, a hamlet folded into itself: lower rooms half-flooded, middle rooms floored in living mat, upper rooms that are only the inside of a canopy, green ceilings low enough to wet your hair. No tool cut these rooms. No nail holds them. Joints are grafts. Load is taken by ribs of wood that thickened where stress asked them to. Doors are narrowings that the growth has kept, as if closing them would be more work than leaving them.

Water is the street plan. It sheets off the upper leaves—small, hard, waxy leaves, packed so tightly the canopy is a single surface from below—and falls in threads into gutters the plants have made of themselves: curled bracts, overlapping scales, a trough of fused stems that runs the length of a passage and empties, with a soft repeated sound, into a basin. The basins are leaves too, or were, grown enormous and thickened until they hold. Their water is cold enough to ache. It does not freeze solid. It skins over at the edges, a frail ice that the next drip breaks, and the skin reforms while you watch. Beneath the floors the ground is marsh, not soil: a cold soup of root, fiber, and standing water, never far down. Step off a terrace and you go in to the ankle, and the ankle comes back black-green and already stiffening in the air.

Light does not enter as light. It enters as a dimming of the green. Outside, high on the young rock, the day may be hard and white. Inside, noon is the color of deep water. You navigate by changes in that color: a paler seam where the canopy has split along a fresh fracture and not yet healed; a darker pocket where the stacking is four layers deep and the lower room has forgotten the sky. Shadows do not fall so much as pool. Wet surfaces take what little brightness there is and spend it locally, so a gutter can gleam while the passage three steps away is only shape.

Sound keeps to the water and the wood. Drip, and the smaller tick of ice-skin breaking. A creak when a stem adjusts its lean—the place is still rising with the mountain, or the mountain’s last rise is still in the roots, and the architecture answers by thickening on one side and easing on the other. Wind does not come in. It worries the outer rim and is absorbed. Sometimes a whole wall shifts with a sound like a crowd turning over in sleep, and afterward a passage is narrower than you remember, or a basin has tilted and found a new fall line. These are the motions. They are continuous and mostly quiet. Nothing here is still, and almost nothing here is loud.

Animals are an interruption, and rare. A pale moth beats once against a wet wall and settles, and the cold stills it. Something small moves in the marsh-floor—too quick to name, a flex in the fiber—and is gone under a root-mat that closes like a lid. High in a graft-joint, once, a bird the color of the dimness sat long enough to prove the room had an outside, then left without a call. The plants do the work other places give to herds and to weather. They consume the light they can get. They hold the water. They shed, and the shed material does not lie as litter; it is taken back into the floors before it can dry. Decay here is a civic service. It smells faintly sweet and cold, like a cellar that has never known fire.

There is no fire. There is no metal, no cord, no cut mark that was not made by a split or a fall. If anyone keeps this settlement, they keep it by being it. The regularity is the unusual thing—the repeated width, the gutters that do not clog, the way a closing passage seems to hesitate, as if the growth had been asked to leave a way and had agreed for now. Stand in a lower room and look up through three layers of terraces and you see not wilderness but section: plan, stack, circulation, drainage. A town drawn in stems. It is only unusual. It does not glow, or speak, or rearrange itself for a witness. It grows, and the growth has habits.

At the rim the habits end all at once. The last wall is a sheer face of interlaced stems, bearded with frost on the outer side, dripping on the inner. Beyond it the rock is new, angled, still sharp in the wind, and the air is empty enough to hurt the lungs. Moisture that condenses on that outer face freezes into feathers and then breaks off and is gone down the drop. Inside, the same water is kept, passed from leaf to gutter to basin to marsh, and lifted again by the slow thirst of the mass. The shelf breathes on a short cycle: cold in, wet held, cold out only as far as the rim. By the time you have learned one passage, another has narrowed, and a basin you drank near has skinned with ice and cleared, and the pale fracture in the floor has been half-swallowed by mat. The place is young. It does not yet know how to be ruins. It only knows how to be full.
