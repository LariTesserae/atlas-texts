The bowl is smaller than a parish and older than the idea of one. From the rim you can hold the whole of it in a turned head: a high cirque of pale, long-weathered stone, its walls steep enough to cut the wind and not steep enough to shed what the mountain keeps giving them. The floor sits well above the tree line, above even the last honest shrubs, in air so thin and cold it feels scraped. Nothing here is new. The channels, the shelves, the town, the habits of water — all of them have already arrived at the shapes they mean to keep.

Water is the grammar. It does not arrive as weather so much as seep out of the sentence of the rock. Every face of the cirque weeps. Seams darken, brighten, darken again as sheets no thicker than a thumbnail find the grooves erosion settled centuries ago and will not renegotiate. The grooves are polished to a skin. They fork, rejoin, drop a hand’s breadth, pool in basins the size of doorstones, and go on. Melt from the shaded cornices feeds them; so does a condensation that seems to belong to the light itself. In the full glare — and the glare is total, a high, hard, colorless brightness with no canopy to break it — moisture beads on the sunward side of every surface and runs. People say the bright side weeps faster. They are not wrong often enough to stop saying it.

There is almost no plant. Lichen, black and rust, plated into the stone like old scabs. A few wires of sedge in cracks that stay wet without ever thawing into soil. Nothing that could be called cover. The green, such as it is, is a stain, not a growth.

And yet the place is crowded.

The town is the bowl. There is no outskirts, no approach that is not already street. Houses of fitted rubble and pale flags stand shoulder to shoulder and often share a wall that is also a water-course: a channel cut along the bond-line, lidded with a slab you can walk, so that a neighbor’s spring arrives in your cistern already argued over. Lanes are the width of two people who have agreed not to stop. Roofs step down toward the floor of the cirque in a tight terracing, each eave dripping onto the next, so that rain — when rain bothers to fall instead of simply being present — is conducted like a tax from the highest shack to the central tanks. No metal worth the name. Stone, wood hauled up in living memory and before that, cord, bone pegs, hide, and a fired clay the color of the mountain when it is wet. Tools are what a hand can lift and a winter can spare. The masonry is the technology. It is very good.

You would expect noise. There is almost none.

The water moves constantly and does not talk. It slips. It does not drop far enough, or with enough air under it, to break into sound; where it does fall, it falls onto stone already sheeted, and the sheet takes the blow and keeps it. Voices carry a few paces and then thin out, as if the cold had somewhere to put them. Footsteps on wet flags are a soft tack. The market — and there is always a market, because nothing stays usable if it sits — is a press of bodies in the lower lanes, baskets, wet hides, pale fish, coils of cord, arguments conducted at the volume of confidence rather than of need. From the rim it looks like an anthill taken apart and spread flat. Up close it is elbows, breath, the smell of cold fish and mineral water and woodsmoke that cannot rise. The smoke itself behaves. It slides along the walls and pools in the lee of roofs, blue-white, and does not pillar.

The animals are the other crowd.

They occupy every volume the people do not, and some they do. Pale fish the length of a hand hold in every cistern, facing the inflow, so numerous the water looks grained. They are eaten, replaced, never absent; the springs seem to mint them, or the tanks have simply never been empty long enough for anyone to see the bottom unoccupied. Birds — small, dark, with a habit of standing rather than flying — line every ridge of roof and every unused peg. They shift all at once when a shadow crosses, a single soft rush of wings, then lock back into stillness. They do not call. In the weep-channels live things that are not quite insects and not quite the larvae of insects: translucent, jointed, numerous enough to make a wet wall look as if it were breathing. They travel upstream against a current that should defeat them. Children net them. They do not seem to diminish.

Stranger than the swimmers are the brief ones.

In the fullest light, in the smallest pans — a hollow in a flagstone, a hoof-print of a cistern that has overflowed and frozen at the rim — something assembles. It is not an animal you could carry away. It is a coherence: a few dozen of the translucent jointed things, a skin of algae too thin to be green, a standing wave of water that ought to collapse and does not, all held for a minute or ten in the shape of a low dome that tracks the sun by a fraction. Touch it and it becomes water, corpses, and a dark smell. Leave it and by afternoon it has walked, if walking is the word, a hand’s breadth toward the brighter stone and come apart on its own. The town has a word for them that also means a coin that spends once. People do not trouble them unless the pan is needed for something else. There are always more pans.

What is turbulent is not the plan of the place. The plan is finished. Erosion and occupation shook hands here a long time ago: every course of water has a lip worn to the angle that keeps the house beside it dry enough, every lane has a fall that will not pond, every roof stone has been turned until its bed fits the drip it was given. You could map it and the map would still be true next year. What will not hold still is the occupancy. Water switches channels inside its settled routes — left lip this hour, right lip the next — and with each switch the fish shift, the netters shift, the brief domes die on the abandoned side and kindle on the new. Goods move because damp climbs into anything left down. People move because the lanes are the only dry-enough paths and the lanes are also the spillways when a cornice lets go, which it does, quietly, in slabs of rotten ice that burst into meal against the upper walls and are gone into the weep before anyone has finished turning to look. The burst is the loudest thing in the bowl. It is still not loud. A soft thud, a white breath, and then the carts are already being walked around the wet.

By late day the brightness goes edge-on and every wet face becomes a blade of reflection. The town doubles. You see the lanes twice — once in stone, once skinned across the water-tables — and the people moving in them look more numerous than they are, which is already too many for the ground. The birds turn their breasts to the last light and become a dark stitching along the roofs. The domes, what remain, lean and fail. Smoke finds the floor of the cirque and lies there. Cold comes down off the rim not as wind but as a lowering of the whole air, and the weeping does not stop for it. Channels skin with ice at their margins and keep a black thread of movement in the center. Someone breaks the skin on a tank with a wooden maul. The sound is small. It is answered, from somewhere in the press below, by nothing you can separate from the general soft work of a place that is always, and almost silently, rearranging itself inside a shape it has already chosen.
