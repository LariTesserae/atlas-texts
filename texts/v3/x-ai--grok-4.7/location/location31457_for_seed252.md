The opening is not a mouth. It is a split in stone that has not yet learned to be a cave, a thumb-width breach under a crust still warm enough to smell of struck flint. You come in sideways. The passage does not descend so much as fail: a shelf gives, and the floor you expected is a hand's depth lower than the floor you left. Then the rock closes overhead, not as a vault but as a lid, and the place you are in is a room.

It would fit inside a small house and still leave the corners unused. The ceiling is low enough that heat collects against it in a sheet. The walls do not recede into dark. They stop, cleanly, at planes that meet at angles too crisp for any long-lived wet place. Edges catch. A ridge of glass runs the length of one wall like a fresh break in a bottle, and where your eye expects the blur of seep-water and time, the arris is still a line.

The stone is young in the way a scar is young. Basalt, pulled thin and frozen wrong, black where it is thick and a dirty honey where the roof has stretched. Nothing has rounded. A flake the size of a palm lies in the middle of the floor, not yet softened, its underside still carrying the gloss of the face it left. Beside it the rock is brighter, newer, as if the room had shrugged in the last hour and not apologized.

Water is everywhere and almost nowhere pooled. It stands in a basin no wider than a washtub, fed by a weep at the far joint, and otherwise it lives as film: on the glass ridge, in the hairline cracks, on the undersides of small shelves that have no business holding anything. The air is wet enough to bead on skin that isn't there yet. Drops travel. They do not fall where weight would put them. They run the edges, pause at the same nicks, and let go from the same three points, so that beneath those points the floor has already begun three pale nipples of sinter, soft, still growing, none of them taller than a knuckle. The basin dimples when a drop arrives. The surface skins over, breaks, skins over again.

It is hot in a way that does not flicker. Not the gusting heat of a fire, not the stored heat of sun on sand. A steady, close heat, the temperature of water you would not put a wrist into. The weep is the coolest thing in the room, and it is not cool. Steam does not billow. It exists as a slight failure of the air to be only air, a thinning you notice when a drop, falling through it, doubles for an instant and then is one drop again.

The light is the fact that will not sit with the rest.

There is no lamp, no shaft a person cut, no door back to day large enough to account for what the room is doing. The roof is stone. It is also, in one stretched panel, thin enough to be a lens, and through that panel a white noon is arriving as if the rock had agreed to be sky. The panel is no bigger than a serving plate. The room is bright past the point of comfort, brighter than the opening behind you, brighter than the color of the glass should allow. Shadows are short and pale, and they fall in more than one direction: from the roof-panel, and again, fainter, from the wet ridge, which is throwing a second light along the floor as if the water on it had been taught to keep what it was given.

Stand near the wall and the heat is in the air, not in the stone. A finger's width out, the wet glass is milder than the space in front of it. The boundary is wrong by every habit of rooms. Heat should soak inward, leak outward, equalize. Here it hangs in bands. The band above the basin is hottest. The band along the floor, where the films are thinnest, is only less hot. Nothing built is holding the split. There is no pipe, no coil, no made thing at all. The glass is doing it while it is still new, and the order of it is so plain that it does not feel like an exception. It feels like a rule that has not yet been written down because the rock has not yet had time to forget how.

Color, under that light, is mineral and few. Iron film in the weep, orange, already staining a path no wider than a string. A dust of pale sulfur on one dry shelf, untouched by the traveling drops. The black of the thicker walls. The white of the sinter, wet. No green. Nothing has rooted, and nothing is about to. There is no soil, only grit from the flake and a slime-shine on the basin lip that is not a plant — a film, mineral-fed, too thin to call a growth. It dulls where the light hits it and goes clear again when a ripple passes.

The room is sparse the way a table is sparse after someone has removed everything but three objects and not explained why. The basin. The ridge. The flake. Between them, floor, and the floor is empty enough that a sound would have room to be heard twice. Most sounds do not bother. A tick, sometimes, as the ceiling eases. The measured arrival of water into water. A low breath in a crack behind the ridge, not wind, the rock letting pressure go in a thread. Your own movement is the loudest event available, and the room does not answer it.

What moves besides water is almost not there. At the lip, where the film is breaking into the basin, a jointed thing the size of a seed works the line. The color of wet ash. It does not flee the light. It crosses the same span of stone, returns, crosses again, feeding on what the film is becoming, or on something smaller than the film that the light keeps awake. Nothing hunts it. Nothing else is in the room to hunt. Once, the water in the basin displaces by a degree that is not a drop — a shift, a small body turning over out of sight — and then the surface is only surface. Whether that was the same creature or another, the room does not repeat the evidence.

The processes do not wait on attention. While the drop-points build their nipples, the roof-panel flexes, a slow oiling of the light across the floor as the glass skin answers the heat in it. The weep strengthens for a few breaths and thins. A new bead forms on an edge that was dry, finds the ridge, and joins the traffic. The flake, drying on top, is already lighter than its shadow; underneath, the gloss is going matte. The sinter is not a deposit left by an older spring. It is happening. Press a thought against the time-scale and the place is an hour, a day, a cooling. Not a country. Not a system with a name. A pocket that split, filled with wet and white light, and began, immediately, to sort itself.

That sorting is the only engineering present, and it is not engineering. No hand set the spacing of the nicks that catch the drops. No design inverted the heat at the wall. The lattice of the young glass is simply fine enough, and regular enough, and new enough, that light stays in it longer than it should, and water follows the charge of the surface, and heat declines to cross a boundary a person could mark with a thumbnail. In any older room this would have blurred by now. Seep would have rounded the arris. A skin of life would have taken the films. The panel would have crazed and gone dark. The bands would have mixed.

They have not mixed. The room is still in the interval where its physics is naked: sharp, wet, hot, bright, quiet, and busy at a scale you can watch. It will not last in this form. Already the three nipples of sinter are dulling the points they grow from. Already the flake is a beginning of rubble. Somewhere above the lid, weather is working on a crust that was rock this morning and will be ground by the season. When the panel thickens or cracks, the second light will go, and the heat will remember how to soak, and this will be only a damp hollow under stone, unremarkable, correctly dark.

For now the hollow is exact. Water arrives. Light does not diminish. The small jointed thing keeps its line. The air holds its bands. Nothing in the room was made, and the room is making itself with a precision that does not need to be strange to be beyond what the stone, by every older law, should still know how to do.
