The place holds still under a ceiling of pale stone so thin the day comes through it as a white, unbroken wash. There is no sky, only that membrane, kilometers of it, and beneath it a valley that never saw weather. The air is hot in the way a closed room is hot: no sun on the skin, no breeze to name, only a steady heat rising from the floor and staying there.

The floor is not rock in any finished sense. It is still arriving. In places the ground is a soft, warm paste of mineral that has not yet decided to be stone, pale and slightly giving, smelling of wet clay and crushed leaf. Elsewhere it has stiffened into low ribs and bowls, their edges blunt, unweathered, as if the world stopped mid-sentence. Nothing here has been worn. There are no channels cut by old water, no smoothed faces, no dust of long collapse. Every surface looks newly issued, and yet nothing is issuing now. The unfinished ground simply remains unfinished, the same ridges in the same places, the same soft patches never hardening and never slumping further.

What fills the valley is plant. Not a cover of plants, but a single continuous mass that has taken the role of soil, wall, path, and shade. A dense braid of stems, pads, and pale-green sheaths rises from the unfinished mineral and knits itself into low halls and narrow lanes, then opens again into broader rooms where the growth thins just enough to walk. The leaves are thick, waxy, overlapping until the underlayer is a dark, damp felt. Where a stem dies it does not fall away; it is taken back into the living weave, held upright by its neighbors, so the mass never thins and never heaps. It is always the same density, always the same height in a given room, as if growth and decay had agreed on a number and kept it.

Moisture lives in pockets rather than as a system. In the bowls of the new ground, water stands finger-deep, warm, filmed with a skin of fine green. Along the undersides of the pads, condensation beads and does not fall, a permanent glitter. There is no river, no drip that can be timed, no season of wet and dry. The damp is simply part of the architecture: a sheen on the clay, a hidden wetness inside the felt of old leaf, a few still pools that neither shrink nor overflow.

Light arrives from above and does not soften much. The stone ceiling scatters it into a flat brilliance, so that even under the pads the shadows are thin and gray rather than black. Color is limited by that light: the vegetation runs from a bleached yellow-green on the upper surfaces to a deeper, almost mineral green where the layers stack. The unfinished ground is the color of bone left in sun. Nothing casts a long shadow, because there is no angle to the light; it is noon everywhere, and it has always been noon.

Sound is mostly the absence of sound. The mass of plant drinks footsteps. The pools do not lap. Now and then a pad releases its held water in a single soft tick, and the tick is so rare it seems like a mistake the place immediately corrects. There is no insect-chorus, no bird, no wind in a canopy. The heat itself seems silent.

Animals are almost a rumor. High in the weave, where the light is strongest, a few pale, slow things no larger than a thumb cling to the stems and do not travel. They might be animals. They might be a kind of fruit that never drops. Lower down, in the felt, something has left a faint trail through the damp once, a groove that has not closed, though nothing is seen to maintain it. The trail, like everything else, does not age.

Scattered through the rooms are marks that are not quite buildings. A ring of hardened mineral, waist-high, perfectly circular, with the plant growing through it and around it but not over its lip, as if the ring were a rule the vegetation respects. Farther on, a short flight of steps cut—or grown—into a rib of the new stone, leading up half a body-height and ending. No wear on the treads. No tool-marks, no metal, no fired clay. Whatever made these did it by pressure or by patience, and then stopped, and the stopping has lasted as long as the valley. There are perhaps a dozen such marks across the whole reach of the place, each isolated, each useless in the same quiet way.

The valley itself is larger than a glance can hold. Lanes in the plant-mass bend out of sight and return as other lanes. Rooms open onto lower shelves of unfinished ground, then onto broader flats where the ceiling lifts and the light goes whiter. From a rise of living stems you can see, far off, the pale ceiling dipping toward the floor until the two meet in a bright seam, and beyond that seam the suggestion of another basin, continuous with this one, under the same stone sky. The scale is that of a district, not a cave: a place you could cross in a long day and still not have left.

Nothing in it is in motion toward a different state. The soft ground does not become rock. The pools do not drain. The plant does not advance or retreat. The rings do not crumble. Even the heat does not vary between one room and the next by more than the difference of standing nearer a pool or farther from one. It is a landscape caught at the moment of being made, and the moment has been given no successor.

If there is a strangeness, it is only that. A world below the surface, bright as open day, built entirely of living mass and of stone that has not finished being stone, and arranged so completely that arrangement itself has gone still. You can walk the lanes. The pads give underhand and spring back to the same shape. The water in the bowls is warm enough to feel like the air. And when you stop, the place does not resume anything. It was already exactly what it is.
