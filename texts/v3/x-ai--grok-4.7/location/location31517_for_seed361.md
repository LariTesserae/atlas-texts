The shelf is smaller than the light suggests. From the lip it reads as a room that has forgotten it is a room: a plane of stone, high enough that the air should be thin and the growth mean, worn so long that the idea of a summit has been planed out of it. What remains is a floor with a low wall on three sides and, on the fourth, a drop you do not see until your foot finds the edge, because the stone gives no warning of its own end. Daylight falls in at a normal angle. Shadows are ordinary shadows. Nothing in the color of the sun explains the warmth.

The warmth is in the rock. Not a furnace-heat, not even the stored heat of a day — a steady, animal warmth, as if the shelf had a pulse a hand’s width under the polish. The polish is the oldest thing here. Wind and infrequent water have worked this surface past the stage of ridges and into a skin: gray-gold, fine-grained, slick where the damp lies on it, matte where it has dried. You can walk a long time and find no pebble. Whatever broke here broke so long ago that the pieces were carried off, dissolved, or pressed back into the floor. Near the drop the stone is thinner. Struck with a heel it answers, a short clear tone, as if age had tensioned it.

Moisture does not arrive as weather so much as it condenses out of a disagreement. The air above the shelf is warm; the air beyond the lip is not. Where the two meet, along the inner face of the wall and in a ragged band a few strides in from the drop, water beads and runs and is gone. Sometimes, for less than a minute, a sheet of rain stands up in the middle of the open stone with no cloud to account for it, wets a dark map, and stops. The map dries from the edges. Pockets last longer in the cracks the erosion never quite finished — finger-wide seams, a shallow basin no deeper than a bowl, the crotch where the wall meets the floor. There the damp is permanent. There the green is.

The growth does not cover the shelf. It occupies it the way a few people occupy a large hall: thick where it has chosen to stand, and then absence. Around the damp seams the vegetation is lavish and low — mats of leaf so overlapped they look poured, stems the color of old brass, flowers no bigger than a nail that open and shut on a cycle shorter than an afternoon. One island, near the wall, has climbed: broad blades, a vine with a rope-thick stem, a shrub whose new growth is red and whose old growth has gone to a bark like smoked paper. Between the islands the stone is bare for long stretches. You can see every island at once. The lushness is real and local; the emptiness is the rule the stone still enforces.

What breaks the rule sits off-center, closer to the wall than to the drop, and is easy to mistake for a stain until you are near it. It is not a building. It is a scatter: three low arcs of a material that is not metal and not glass, half-swallowed by the floor as if the shelf had tried to heal over them and failed, plus a fourth piece standing upright, no taller than a shoulder, veined with a slow interior brightness that does not cast colored light. The veins move. Not quickly. Around the scatter the damp never fully leaves, and the plants grow in slight curves, leaning not toward the sun but toward the arcs, then correcting, then leaning again. The upright piece hums at a pitch you feel in the teeth more than you hear. Whatever it is doing, the shelf’s warmth begins there and thins as you walk away. Whatever it is doing, it has been doing it longer than the polish. The stone has flowed a little around its base, the way wax accepts a wick.

The animals do not live on the shelf so much as pass through it, and the passing never stops.

They come up the drop. They come down out of the ordinary sky. They come out of the seams, which are not large enough for them and somehow are. A few stay. Most do not. At any moment the ground can look almost empty — one long-legged thing drinking at the bowl, a scatter of small dark bodies working the leaf-mats, nothing else — and the air still be full. Wings at several heights. A rasping chorus in the vine that does not resolve into single voices. Something heavy circling just outside the lip, its shadow crossing the polish and lifting off again before you have agreed it was there. The density of bodies on the stone is low. The density of attention is not. Everything that can hear is listening, and most of what is here can hear.

They are not of one kind. Near the device the insects are wrong for the height and wrong for each other: broad slow fliers that drink the condensation off the arcs, and beneath them a finer dust of life that rises and settles with the brief rains. The leaf-islands hold hoppers and a low, many-legged browser that eats only the red new growth and leaves the bark alone. On the open stone the long-legged drinker is not alone in its type; there are three or four, widely spaced, never closer than the length of the shelf’s hum will allow. They do not herd. When one lowers its head the others lift theirs. Their feet tick the thin stone and the stone answers, so that their walking is a sparse percussion under everything else.

Above them the loudness properly begins. A flight of small birds — or what read as birds until one stalls and you see the wings are too numerous — stitches the pocket from wall to drop and back, screaming a single note that the walls hold and return a fraction late. Larger callers work the lip: three voices, maybe four, never in the same place twice, throwing cries across the shelf that land on the stone and seem to continue sideways. Something in the vine answers them with a wooden clatter, rhythmic, impatient. When the brief rain stands up, every voice changes key at once, as if the water were a hand laid on the strings. Then the rain stops and the previous roar reassembles itself, not gradually.

Under the roar there is hunting, and it is not hidden. A narrow shadow detaches from the wall’s shade and crosses an island in two strides. The browsers vanish into the mats; one does not. There is a sound like cloth torn once. The shadow is already at the far side, smaller in the mouth than the space it crossed, and the long-legged drinkers have not moved except to turn their heads. They are not prey for that hunter. Something else is. It travels the lip on the outside, a bulk you mostly know by the way the callers go silent in a moving patch, and now and then a forearm or a jaw clears the edge and the stone rings. It cannot, or does not, come in. The shelf is a room with one door it refuses. The prey it wants are the things that use the drop as a throat, and it waits where the throat narrows.

The rules here are few and they hold.

Distance is slightly untrustworthy along the wall. A walk that should be thirty paces is forty, and the extra ten are always on the side away from the device, as if the scatter were a weight and the pocket had stretched to balance it. Toward the device, distance compresses; you arrive sooner than your stride promised. Neither effect is dramatic enough to make you stumble. Both are enough that a thrown stone lands where you did not aim.

Water does not pool except in the seams and the bowl. A cup poured on the open polish films, shines, and is gone into the air or into the warmth, and the device’s veins brighten a shade. Rain that is not rain falls only in the open middle, never on the islands, never on the arcs. The plants drink the condensation and the seams. They do not drink the standing sheet. If you stand in it you are wet and then, quickly, not.

Nothing nests in the open. Anything that stays more than a day stays in the green or in the shade of the wall or within a body’s length of the scatter, where the hum seems to dull the larger callers. The long-legged drinkers are the exception, and they do not lie down. When dark comes — ordinary dark, a normal slope of light off the shelf, no strange moon — they stand where they drank, and the percussion of their balance-shifts goes on at long intervals under the reduced but still loud night.

The device does not sleep. Its hum drops half a tone after sundown and the warmth contracts, so that by midnight the far lip is merely mild and the damp there turns to a cold film. That is when the outside bulk comes closest, and when the many-winged flight folds into the vine and the vine’s clatter becomes a single kept beat, like a sleeper counting. Near the arcs the small fliers continue. They orbit the upright piece in a ragged column. Some of them pass into the brightness of a vein and come out elsewhere on the arc, a gap in their path no longer than a blink. They do not seem injured. They do not seem to notice. The stone around the base has a faint new glaze each morning, as if a little more of the shelf had agreed, overnight, to be part of the machine.

By day the agreement recedes. Sun returns at the proper angle. The sheet-rain tries itself once or twice. The hunters cross. The drinkers tick their notes. The islands steam at their edges and smell of crushed green and warm mineral and, under that, a sweetness with no flower you can find — the smell of the hum, if a hum can smell. From the lip, if you lie flat and look over, there is more mountain, vastly more, pale and emptied and still being emptied, a decline of ancient stone running down into haze. No green on it. No sound rising from it. The shelf is a pocket the erosion missed, or was asked to miss, and the life in it is not a remnant of the heights. It is a visitation sustained by a scatter of work no one on the shelf is doing anymore.

The noise never resolves into a signal. That may be the point of it. In a space this small a single voice would dominate; instead the pocket is used by so many processes at once — condensation and correction, hunt and passage, the slow lean of leaves toward a light that is not light — that what you hear is the interference. Loud, continuous, intimate as a room with too many people speaking, and underneath it the stone, answering only when touched, very old, nearly flat, still keeping the one machine it could not wear away.
