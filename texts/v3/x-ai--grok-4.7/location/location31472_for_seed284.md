The air up here is thin enough that sound arrives late and incomplete, a fact the place seems to have accepted and then built around. What you stand in is not a mountain so much as a room the mountain recently agreed to hold: a cleft no wider than a street, roofed by a fresh shear of dark rock that still shows the grain of the fracture, sharp enough in places to catch a sleeve. The uplift that opened it cannot be old. Edges have not rounded. Dust has not yet learned the corners. A pale mineral bloom, dry as chalk, films the newer faces and comes off on the fingers.

There is no water. Not in the air, not in the rock, not in any cistern you can find by listening. What industry needs, it ferries. You see the evidence in the pipes—short runs, never a long aqueduct—clamped to the walls with brackets still bright at the bolt-heads, and in the small sealed drums stacked two and three deep along every passage that will take them. The drums sweat nothing. The cool here is a dry cool, the kind that lives in shade at great height, and it keeps metal honest: no rust-lace, only a fine gray oxidation where hands have not recently been.

The settlement fills the cleft the way a mechanism fills a housing. Nothing is ornamental and almost nothing is spare. Walkways of punched plate cross and recross at three heights, close enough that a person on the middle level brushes the soles of the one above if either of them is tall. Workshops open directly onto the walks—no yards, no setbacks—each one a slot of dim work-light, oil-yellow, thrown from bare bulbs and from the open mouths of small furnaces that burn something that is not wood. The light does not travel. Beyond an arm's length the cleft returns to its own dusk, a blue-gray that might be sky leaking down the shear or might only be the color of young stone in little sun. You never quite see a horizon. The place is the whole of what is visible.

Machines run, and they run hard. Lathes, stamp-presses, a line of belt-drives whose belts are hide or some close cousin of it, a compressor that cycles in a rhythm you feel in the sternum more than you hear. The thin air steals the high notes. What remains is a low, private thunder, as if the industry had been told to keep its voice down and had complied without slowing. Sparks fall and die before they reach the plate. Metal shavings curl and pile and are swept, constantly, into drums that once held something else. The sweeps never finish. A new billet is always coming off a hoist, a new housing always needing a hole. The turbulence is in the work, not in the weather: bodies and parts and decisions arriving faster than the space can settle them, a bazaar conducted in near-silence, transactions made by gesture because speech costs breath.

The animals are everywhere the machines are not quite. They were not imported as scenery. They earn their rooms. Along the upper walk a dense traffic of small, low-bodied things—fur the color of the mineral bloom, eyes large for the dim—moves in both directions with the purposive indifference of stock that has learned a route. They thread between ankles and between the legs of benches. Some wear tiny harnesses and drag trays of fasteners no larger than seeds. Others, unharnessed, simply live in the heat-shadow of the furnaces, packed shoulder to shoulder on ledges that were clearly cut for them, and rise in soft eruptions whenever a door cycles. Lower down, where the cleft narrows toward a floor you cannot see from the middle walk, larger animals stand in pens of fresh-sawn timber that still smells of resin the mountain did not grow. Their breath smokes and vanishes. They are many for the ground they have. Horns click. A hoof shifts. None of them call out. Perhaps they have learned, as the machines have, that the air will not carry it.

Nothing green answers them. No moss in the joints, no weed in a crack, no hanging garden improvised from a drum. The only growth is mineral and mechanical: the chalk-film spreading a finger's width a week across new faces, the accretion of brackets, the way a workshop that was one bay last season is now two because someone bolted a second frame to the shear and called the gap a room. Even the organic debris—shavings of hide, shed hair, the papery casings the small animals leave in corners—reads as industrial byproduct, swept or not swept, never returning as soil. There is no soil. There is plate, and young rock, and the black grit that comes off the furnace mouths.

You notice, if you stay, how recently the cleft decided to be a city. Some walls are the fracture itself, unworked, and the first pipes are clamped directly to it with a crudeness the later pipes have already outgrown. A stair ends in midair where the rock shifted again after the stair was begun; a rope ladder, new, completes it. Numbers painted on bays are low numbers. The whole settlement has the concentration of a thing that expects to be larger and has not yet been given the room, so it edits itself hourly: a bench dragged six inches, a pen enlarged by one board, a family of the small animals evicted from a ledge because a motor needs the vibration-path. No one seems alarmed by the editing. Alarm would be loud, and loud does not work here.

Stand at the middle walk and look along the cleft rather than across it. The layered construction repeats until it fuzzes—walk, slot of yellow light, walk, animal-ledge, walk—and the repetition is the weather. A press cycles. A tray scrapes. Thirty small bodies pour across an intersection and are gone into a workshop that swallows them without comment. Somewhere below, a larger animal shifts its weight and the timber answers with a single clean tick. The cool does not lift. The dust on the new shear does not settle, because someone is always passing close enough to keep it moving, and because the mountain, still sharp, still unfinished with itself, keeps offering fresh faces for it to film.

There is a smell, finally, once the thin air has been in your lungs long enough to register anything but lack: hot metal, dry hide, the faint sweet of the resin boards, and under those a mineral sharpness, like a match just struck in a room with the windows shut. No rain in it. No leaf. No standing pool. Only the industry, and the animals that service it, and the young rock that has not yet learned it is holding a town.
