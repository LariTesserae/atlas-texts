The place is smaller than the walking of it suggests, and denser than the eye is willing to admit. From the outer path you can hold the whole of it between two breaths: a low bowl, no higher than a person’s raised hand above the marsh that hems it, no wider than a hamlet that never grew past its first street. The boundary is a lip of unfinished stone, still soft at the edge, still deciding whether to be rock. Step over that lip and the bowl does not open. It folds.

Daylight is ordinary. That is the first wrongness, and the one that lasts. A pale, even light, the color of a cold afternoon with no weather in it, falls on every surface at once and casts shadows that do not agree with one another. A cornice throws its shade to the left. The window beneath it throws shade upward, onto the sky, where it hangs a moment like a stain and then thins. Nothing flickers. The light simply does not keep a single account of where things are.

Underfoot the ground is embryonic. Pavement has been poured, or grown, or both, in plates that have not yet committed to being pavement. In the joints a darker mineral sweats through, fine-grained, almost warm to look at and cold to the touch, as if the city were still being quarried from a stone that has not finished cooling out of some earlier state. Cracks do not widen. They lengthen, slowly, over days, like handwriting practicing a letter. In places the plates have not met, and what shows between them is not soil. It is a gray paste, damp, faintly luminous at the meniscus, holding the impression of footsteps for an hour and then smoothing them without wind.

Water is not a river here. It is a habit. Condensation beads on railings that have no temperature difference to explain it, gathers in the hollows of steps, stands in saucers on windowsills that face no weather. Sometimes a thin sheet appears across a lane and stays, not flowing, only present, reflecting a building that is not the one beside it. The reflections are delayed. A person crossing is still in the water after they have gone indoors. The real rain, when it comes, is brief and local: a column of drops over one intersection, dry stone ten paces away, the smell of wet metal and something sweeter, like cut stem. By morning the column has moved, or the intersection has. No one is sure which. Gutters exist, elaborate, nested, machined to tolerances that make the eye ache, and they do not always connect to anything. Water enters them and is not heard again.

Vegetation has barely been allowed. A moss the color of old nickel pins itself to north walls, though north is not stable. A few reeds stand in a cistern that sometimes contains water and sometimes contains the idea of water, their stems glassy, their seed-heads empty. One tree, if it is a tree, occupies a court near the center: a trunk of braided pale fiber, no bark, branches that end in flat discs instead of leaves. It does not grow in any season a visitor can name. It thickens. People do not sit under it. Birds do not land. There are almost no birds.

Fauna is the rarest kindness. Once in a long while a moth circles a lamp that is not lit, its wings making a sound like paper being folded very far away. Once, a pale newt was found in a drain, alive, and when lifted it left a wet print that remained wet for three days and then became a small glazed tile, perfectly square, set into the stone as if it had always been part of the work. No one has seen another. The city does not seem hostile to animals. It seems unfinished in the places animals would need: nests, burrows, the reliable dark. Those have not been specified yet.

What has been specified, densely, is building. The hamlet-bowl is a city in the only way that matters here: every volume is spoken for. Streets are narrow enough that two people must turn their shoulders, and above the streets the rooms continue, stacked, cantilevered, sleeved into one another. A stair begins in one house and arrives, without a landing that belongs to either, in a second house whose door opens onto a third street that was not visible from the first. Windows look into other windows at distances of a hand’s breadth. Curtains, where there are curtains, are thin metal meshes, too fine to be woven by any loom that obeys ordinary thread-count, and they move when no air moves. Balconies overlap so completely that rain on an upper one falls onto the lower as a sound delayed by a full breath.

The construction is beyond anything that could be reverse-engineered from the materials at hand. Joints have no fasteners. Walls meet at angles that are not in the set of possible angles, and yet they hold; load does not pass through them so much as become irrelevant at the seam. Some surfaces are ordinary brick, cold, slightly damp, honest. Adjacent surfaces are not surfaces. Touch them and the finger sinks a fraction, into a resistance like packed snow or like the meniscus of a gel, and withdraws without a mark, though the wall remembers the temperature of the hand for hours. In one lane the buildings are still arriving. You can watch it if you are patient. A lintel clarifies out of the gray paste over the course of an afternoon. By dusk it has a number stamped into it, in a typeface no one recognizes, and the number changes if you look away and back, not wildly, only by one.

Sound is mostly absent, and the absence is structural. Voices do not carry. A conversation at the end of a lane arrives as a pressure in the ear, not as words. Footsteps are audible only to the person making them. The city seems to drink vibration the way the gutters drink water. What remains is a low, slow tick, not mechanical, more like the settling of a large animal that is not there: a click in a wall, an hour later a softer click in the wall opposite, as if something were checking, at great leisure, that the geometry still agrees with itself. It does not always agree. Residents learn to sleep through the corrections. Newcomers wake when a room becomes, for a few minutes, slightly longer than it was, and the far wall is suddenly three steps farther, and the tick comes once, satisfied, and the room is itself again.

Change is patient. Nothing hurries. A crack lengthens. A reflection lingers. A moth circle closes. The tree thickens by a fiber. The outer lip of stone advances a finger’s width into the marsh in a month, and the marsh accepts it without ripples, as if the boundary were a suggestion both sides had already half-believed. Seasons, if they are seasons, are arguments about moisture and light that never become weather. Cold is constant: a dry cold in the upper rooms, a damp cold at street level, the kind that lives in the bones of the hands and does not sharpen into pain. Breath shows, faintly, and the faintness hangs longer than breath should.

People live here. That is perhaps the most ordinary fact, and it does not stabilize the rest. They keep kettles, ledgers, coats with high collars. They nod in the lanes and do not raise their voices, because raising them does nothing. They have learned which stairs will deliver them home and which will deliver them to a room that has their furniture and is not their room, identical down to the chip in the cup, except that the window looks onto a court they do not recognize and the light, still ordinary, falls from a direction their own window has never used. They wait. The tick comes. The court becomes theirs, or they become the court’s. The difference is not one they discuss.

At the center, under the disc-branched tree, there is a circle of stone that has not yet chosen a radius. Stand in it and the city is all around you, near enough to touch, layered past the point of counting, cold, damp, almost silent, lit like any other day. Step out and you are at the lip again, the marsh beyond, the bowl no larger than it was, the unfinished edge still writing itself outward by a grain at a time. Behind you, if you turn, the streets have already rearranged their agreement about which window belongs to which shadow. The light does not comment. It continues, evenly, as if this were a place that had always been finished, and the finishing were only a courtesy it had not yet extended to itself.
