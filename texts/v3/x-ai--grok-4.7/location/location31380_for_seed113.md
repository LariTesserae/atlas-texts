The chamber is not deep enough to be a mine and not wide enough to be a city, though it behaves like both. You enter it already inside: a vault of unfinished rock the size of a nave, the ceiling a low, sharp vault of planes that have not yet learned to sag. The cuts are new. Edges still throw a fine crystalline grit when the air moves. Nothing has been softened. Water found the place before weather could.

It beads on every underside. Not rain — there is no sky — but a constant, warm condensation that gathers along the ridges of the cut stone and lets go in threads. The threads do not fall straight. They drift, as if the air has a current of its own, and land on black trays set into the floor at intervals too regular to be natural. The trays never fill. Whatever arrives is taken somewhere before it can pool. You hear the taking: a low, continuous intake, wet and mechanical, running under the louder sounds the way a pulse runs under speech.

The louder sounds are the walls. They are not still. Thin sheets of something darker than the rock — not metal, not glass, a material that holds a sheen the way wet leaf holds light — stand at slight angles, floor to ceiling, and they flex. Each flex throws a report, a wet slap followed by a ringing that does not decay the way sound should in a small room. The ringing travels. It meets the next sheet and the next, so the chamber is never quiet and never one noise. Somewhere a valve opens and a rush answers it. Somewhere a sheet settles back and the ring drops a half-tone. The acoustics are wrong for the size. The place is small enough that you could cross it in a minute, and the sound behaves as if there were streets beyond the walls.

There are no streets. There is almost nothing standing in the volume. The sheets, the trays, a few pale columns that rise without bases and stop short of the ceiling, their tops open like cut stems. Between them the air is empty to a degree that feels maintained. Dust does not settle. No litter of construction remains, though the construction is plainly unfinished — or perhaps continually begun. Fresh faces of rock show where a column was recently drawn out of the wall, the fracture bright, still damp, smelling of warm mineral and something green.

The green is the only abundance. It does not grow on the floor. It grows from the columns and from the seams where sheet meets stone: mats of small, succulent leaves, warm to the touch, beaded, giving off a faint sweet vapor that the condensation carries. They are lush in the way a crop is lush, not a forest — planted, or persuaded, at exact heights. No insect worries them. Nothing crawls the wet trays. The ecology, if that is the word, is the plants and the machines and the water, and they are in motion together without anything that breathes between them. A leaf unrolls. A sheet flexes and slaps. A thread of water finds a tray and vanishes with a soft mechanical gulp. Then the cycle again, not identical.

Light comes from nowhere you can point to. A dim, amber wash, strongest near the open tops of the columns, as if something below the visible floor were burning low and the columns were chimneys for it. It does not reach the corners. In the corners the sharp new rock goes blue-black, and the condensation there is colder by a degree, and the plants thin out to single glossy rosettes clinging to the cuts.

You keep expecting a threshold, a door, a person whose absence would explain the emptiness. There is infrastructure enough for a town: the trays, the intake, the flexing sheets that may be walls or may be organs, the columns drawing heat and light from somewhere under the floor. It is organized. It is layered — you can see, where a sheet has pulled away from the stone, an older sheet behind it, and behind that a film of the same dark material already healing over a fresh cut. The place repairs itself faster than it can be weathered. That is why the edges stay sharp in all this water. Erosion has not been given time.

What the sheets are doing when they flex is not clear. Signaling, perhaps, or breathing, or keeping the volume clear. Each slap rearranges the air. Warm fog lifts off the plants and is slapped back down, and for a moment the dim amber brightens as droplets scatter, and the chamber looks briefly larger, a street of empty intervals, and then the fog closes and it is a room again.

Stand long enough and the rhythm teaches you its scale. Not weather. Not an animal’s pace. A civic pace: the intake, the flex, the unrolling leaf, the healing film, the thread of water that refuses to pool. A settlement with no inhabitants, running loud in the dark under the ground, still being built, already wet, already growing, and so new that nothing in it has yet had the chance to wear down.
