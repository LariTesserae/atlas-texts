The high ground has already finished most of its arguments with stone. What remains of the range is a broad saddle, planed by an age of weather that no longer needs drama to do its work: a cool, open shelf the size of a town and its valley together, tilting so gently that the eye keeps expecting a river and finds only the memory of one. The old watercourses are seams now, darker lines in a continuous living mat, damp to the hand and dry a pace away. Rain comes as a decision rather than a season. It beads, stalls, and is drunk before it can gather.

The ground is not soil with plants on it. The ground is plant. A pale, fibrous pavement of interlaced runners, compressed by centuries into something you can walk, cool underfoot, faintly springy, smelling of cut green and old metal. Where a boot pauses, the mat darkens with moisture it seems to have been saving. Where the mat is thin, you see no bedrock worth the name—only older fiber, pressed to the color of bone, the last shape erosion was willing to leave. Relief survives only where life insisted on it.

Across that plain the city stands sparse. Not ruins in the ordinary sense, and not a thicket. Individual structures, grown rather than raised, set so far apart that wind has a full stride between them. Each is a building only by habit of use. Towers of hollow stem, buttressed by cables of root, their walls a laminate of bark, pith, and something that was once plate and girder and has been taken up into the grain. Windows are splits that healed around light. Streets are the intervals. From a distance, in ordinary day, they read as a settlement: a loose urban scatter, roofs at different heights, a civic spacing. Come closer and the civic spacing is just the distance a trunk needs not to starve its neighbor.

Industry never left. It went inside. In the lower chambers of the larger stems, sap runs under pressure through bored channels that remember pipes. You hear pistons that are not pistons—segments of living wood flexing on a stroke, wet and regular, driving fiber looms, seed-sorters, slow wheels that grind nothing you would recognize as ore. Exhaust is a sweet, cool vapor that beads on the inner bark and falls back as feed. At night the same vapor carries a weak charge; by day, in this thin, normal light, it only shines a little, like breath on glass. No chimney. The factories exhale through lenticels. Scaled production continues at the pace of growth, which is to say it is loud and patient at once, a shift that does not end because no one remained to end it.

The loudness is the first fact that will not sit still. Wind enters the hollow towers and comes out organized: a chord for each structure, a different chord if you walk around it, as if the building had more sides than the walk allows. The industrial stroke underfoot keeps time the wind does not. Between them the mat ticks—small hydraulic corrections, runners tightening, a thousand damp closures. Sound travels farther here than it should. A loom three towers off can arrive in your ear before the tower that houses it has finished appearing. Sometimes the chord continues after you have put the structure behind a rise that isn't high enough to hide it.

Fauna is almost an absence with edges. No herds, no cries that belong to throats. Now and then a pale flake lifts off a bark-face and proves, for a second, to have wings; it does not go far, and it may only be bark that briefly remembered being an insect. Pollination, where it happens, is a function of the looms and the wind-chords. The ecology does not require animals to keep its promises. That is the loneliness of the place, and also its competence.

Cool air. Not cold enough to punish, never warm enough to loosen the damp. Shadows are ordinary shadows until they aren't. A tower's shade can lie across the mat in a direction the sun does not justify, and inside that wrong shade the fiber is a different age—greener, or already the color of the pressed bone-layer, as if the shadow were a section cut through time and laid flat. Step into it and the sound changes key. Step out and your boots are wet from a rain that is occurring only inside the discrepancy.

The rules here are local and badly kept.

Distance is a courtesy. Between two towers the walk can take the time the eye predicts, or it can deliver you to the second while the first is still occupying most of the sky, its chord already mingling with a chord you have not reached. People who used the city—if the worn thresholds and the loom-settings imply people—seem to have navigated by sound more than by sight. There are no signs. There are growth rings exposed in cross-section where a wall split, and in the rings, if you look without insisting, the plan of streets that are not the intervals you walked. The plan moves when the sap moves. A map here is a living document and therefore unreliable in the way living things are: correct about what it is doing, not about where you are.

Elevation misbehaves in pockets. The saddle is high; the air says so, the light says so, the long view to lower country—blue, ordinary, far—says so. But certain damp seams drop you, for three or four steps, into air that is thicker, greener-smelling, loud with a river that is not there. You come up again onto the same mat, heart working harder than the climb earned, cuffs wet. Other pockets lift. A stair grown into a tower's flank can deliver you to a landing whose windows open on cloud that the plateau, from outside, is not wearing. The landing is still part of the tower. The tower is still on the saddle. Both facts hold, and the holding makes a faint tearing sound, like green wood under torque.

Water obeys the seams and ignores the rest. It will not pool in the open. It will stand, trembling, in a hollow of bark at the height of your chest, and the surface of that cup will show a reflection of machinery you cannot find in the room—belts, a flywheel, a figure bent over a gauge—until you touch it, and then it is only water, cool, tasting of leaf and of the iron the plants have been mining from their own walls for longer than the walls were walls. Condensation writes on the inner faces overnight: fine branching lines, almost circuitry, almost venation, gone by the time the light is full. Some mornings a whole interior is glazed with a diagram of the city as it was when it still had a center. By noon the diagram has been metabolized. The city has no center now. It has intervals, and the intervals are the point.

What moves, besides sap and sound and the wrong weather, is the boundary between made and grown, and it moves in both directions. A gear you might swear is cast metal, lifted from a housing, shows annual rings when broken. A leaf the size of a door, laid into a façade, rings like sheet when struck, and the note belongs to the tower's chord, which adjusts to include it. Nothing here is pretending. The breakdown is not illusion laid over a real city, or a forest wearing industry as costume. The oldest stable state of this place is the confusion itself. Erosion took the stone down to a whisper; what replaced stone learned stone's job and the factory's job and the street's job, and did not keep the categories.

If you stay, the loudness becomes navigable. Each tower has a resting pitch. The mat's tick is faster where the damp seams run, slower on the bone-colored flats. You can walk the urban scatter by ear and arrive, more often than not, at the structure you intended—though "arrive" may mean standing in its shade while your body is still crossing the open, a delay the place does not apologize for. The rare winged flakes gather, sometimes, on the looms, and are woven in, and the cloth that results is a pale textile that hums if you hold it to the wind. It is not for anyone. Production continues because the channels are open and the pressure is there.

Toward the lip of the saddle the mat thins. The long view returns: lower country, a credible horizon, light behaving. Turn around and the towers have rearranged their spacing by a degree or two, as a sleeper rearranges under a blanket without waking. One of them is sounding the chord of a tower you passed an hour ago. The damp on your cuffs has dried to a map of veins that does not match your skin and will not rub off until the next rain, which may fall only on you, finely, industriously, as if the sky had narrowed its production to a single shift.

The place does not need you to resolve it. It is already its final form, and the final form is still working—cool, loud, sparsely built, entirely alive, high enough that the air should be simple, and not simple. The oldest thing on the saddle is not the pressed fiber or the taken-up iron. It is the ongoing refusal of the ground to be only one kind of thing, playing, at industrial volume, across an openness wide enough to hear itself answer.
