The ground here is newer than the habit of standing on it. You notice that before you notice the houses: a low shelf of stone, pale and angular, broken into plates that still hold the geometry of their fracture. Edges have not gone dull. A corner will catch a sleeve or a rope and fray it. Between the plates the mud is black and thin, and under the mud, if anyone were foolish enough to dig past a forearm’s depth, the water is already waiting, cold and mineral, with no patience for a hole.

The whole of it could be walked across while a pot comes to a poor boil. A rim of sharper rock closes it on three sides, not high, only definite, and beyond the rim the land gives up and becomes standing water threaded with dead reed. On the fourth side the shelf simply lowers, plate by plate, until the plates are covered and the path, if it was a path, is a matter of memory and a line of stakes. People come and go by that line, one at a time, carrying what the place cannot make.

Nothing much grows. A crust of green on the lee of a wall, a fist of sedge in a crack that stays damp but not drowned, a moss that yellows and tries again. The reed that roofs the houses was cut elsewhere and poled in, and it is rationed the way other towns ration grain. What green you see is mostly that imported thatch, silvering, and the occasional bundle lashed to a frame to dry a second time after a night of drip. The soil is too new to have learned plants, and too wet, and a little too sharp with salts that bloom white along the plate-edges by afternoon and go back into the stone when the air thickens.

The settlement takes all the continuous surface and then some. Walls touch. Eaves overlap so that rain, when it comes as rain and not as the slower business of the air, never quite reaches the slots between buildings. Those slots are the streets: a shoulder’s width, sometimes a plank, sometimes two planks with a finger of dark water winking between them. Houses are stacked stone at the base, then wattle, then a lacing of root-cord because fiber is scarce and hides are scarcer. Where a wall failed it was not torn down. Another wall was built against it, and the gap between them became a cupboard, or a sleep-slot, or a place to keep the one iron knife the lane shares. Clay never quite dries. It skins over and stays cool to the palm, and in the mornings it sweats from the inside as if the wall were breathing the water it was mixed with.

There is almost no livestock and almost no wild noise to replace it. A pale shrimp in the seep-channels, rarely; a bird that crosses the rim and does not land, because there is nowhere to land that is not a roof or a head. No dogs. No room, and nothing spare to feed a mouth that does not work. The quiet is not emptiness. It is proximity. Sound carries over the little water and dies against wet clay, and everyone is close enough to hear a pot set down two lanes away. People speak low because the place is small enough to make loudness a kind of exposure. Work is hand-work: lashing, stacking, scraping salt, turning reed so it does not rot in place. The tools are stone, bone, a little cold-beaten copper that came in on the stake-path and will not be replaced if it is lost. No wheels. Loads move on shoulders, or on sleds of plank when the planks can be spared from the walkways, which is seldom.

The light is ordinary daylight, a little thinned by moisture, good enough to judge a lashing or a face. Shadows are soft and short of drama. What is unusual is lower down. The stone still holds a warmth the air does not, a leftover from whatever darker, heavier condition it was lifted out of, and toward evening the wet air cools against it and lays a sheet of fog at the height of a knee. Heads stay clear. Thresholds disappear. People learn the town by the spacing of doorposts and by the sound of water under the planks, which changes as the sheet thickens. In that hour the reflections in the open pans between plates do not quite match the roofs above them. Not a vision, not a second town: just an extra line of thatch, a slot that should be a wall, gone when you blink and look at the thing itself. Most residents have stopped mentioning it. Newcomers mention it once.

The day moves even when no one does. The water table breathes. Not a tide from any visible sea, only a rise and fall of a handspan, reliable enough that bed-frames are built to it and unreliable enough that someone is always wrong. In the rise, the slots become mirrors and then become streams, shallow, going nowhere, and the town’s rule is simple: nothing that matters sits below the second plank. In the fall, the mud shows its cracks and the salt returns, and children—there are few—are sent to scrape it into bowls before the next breath of the ground takes it back. Condensation gathers under eaves in beads that run to carved wooden lips and into jars. That water is the drinking water. The standing water is for clay, for cooling a burn, for seeing whether the day has turned. It tastes of the inside of the rock.

Young stone keeps settling. A plate will tick in the night, a small bright sound, and by morning a hairline has lengthened across a floor that was fitted yesterday. People do not fight this. They lash, and re-lash, and keep a coil of cord in every house the way other places keep lamps. A wall that leans is propped with a fresh angle of rock, sharp side out, and the prop becomes part of the address: third lean, second prop, the house with the copper. The town is always in a minor state of repair, and the repair is the same gesture as building, because the ground has not finished arriving and the water has not finished deciding how much of the shelf it wants.

Weather crosses in short cycles. A cool rain that beads on every edge and finds every lashing. A drier hour when salt writes on the stone and the fog lifts to the waists and then is gone. Wind rarely; the rim breaks it. When wind does come it worries the reed and the town goes quieter still, listening for a roof that has begun to walk. At those times you can hear, under everything, the seep: not a stream, a presence, water moving through the young fractures with a sound like cloth drawn slow across a dry palm.

By full day the place looks almost practical. Packed, low, cool, busy with small necessary motions, boundaries visible from any doorway. A hamlet that has become a town only by refusing to leave a footprint and by building upward and inward until no gap remains. Stand on the rim and it is one object: thatch, clay, pale plates, a skin of water, a few figures in the slots no larger than tools. Step down into it and the object opens into rules—the second plank, the shared knife, the jars under the eaves, the stake-path, the refusal to dig—and into the odd, ordinary fact that the land is still being born under the floors, sharp and wet and not yet willing to soften into anything that could be called old.
