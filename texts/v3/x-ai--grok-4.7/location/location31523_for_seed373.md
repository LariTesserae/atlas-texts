The sound arrives before the place does. On the last switchback the air is only cool and thin, a high-country quiet with grit in it, and then the path tips over a lip of old rock and the quiet is gone as if a door had been shut on the world behind you. What replaces it is not wind in the ordinary sense. It is a contained roaring, the size of a hamlet and no larger, seated in a bowl you could walk the rim of in an hour if the rim would let you.

The bowl is high and finished. Its walls have the look of stone that long ago stopped arguing with whatever works on them: curves worn past jaggedness, joints opened and then healed with a pale cement of salt and mica dust, a floor that has settled into a shallow dish and intends to remain one. Nothing here is young. Nothing here is slack. The erosion has reached its own shape and holds it, the way a coastline holds a profile while every grain on it is in transit.

There is no water. Not a seep, not a stain that remembers a seep, not a sky that intends one. The floor is a salt-field in miniature, crusted and re-crusted, white grading into rust and into the green-gray of copper salts where the mineral changes. By midday the light on it is ordinary high light, hard and honest, shadows short and blue-cool, the sun doing nothing supernatural. The weirdness is not in the light. It is in the fact that this glare has nowhere wet to go. Moisture that enters—spit, breath, a cut—does not bead. It is taken. The crust brightens for a moment where you bled on it, and then the brightness is only mineral again.

Across that floor the volume is mostly empty. You can see from wall to wall. A person standing opposite would be a clear figure, not a glimpse through thicket. The emptiness is the first honesty of the place and the first lie, because the lie is lushness, and the lushness is real, and it does not fill anything.

Twelve of them, perhaps fourteen—the count slips because they move. They stand far apart, each with a radius of bare crust around it like a held breath. Call them plants only because they are sessile and they feed on light. Each is a cathedral of branching: trunks the color of dried blood-iron, limbs dividing until the outer growth is a haze of blades, filaments, and cupped scales no thicker than fingernails. Up close the lushness is obscene in its surface area, a single organism carrying the leaf-count of a grove, every scale glazed with a dry metallic film that drinks the noon and the cold together. They do not wilt. They have nothing to wilt with. Break a filament and it powders, and the powder craws away from your hand toward the light, reorganizing grain by grain along the broken edge. Growth here is a mineral habit that learned repetition. By evening a broken limb has sketched itself back in frost-white lattice, not healed so much as restated.

They are loud. Every scale is an edge in the shear, and the shear never rests. The bowl holds a standing rotor—not weather passing through, but a motion that belongs to the hollow the way a note belongs to a pipe. Air comes over the rim, is caught, and does not leave in any simple way; it circuits, climbs, falls, circuits again, sanding the walls at exactly the rate the walls can endure. That is the balance. The stone is established because the violence is old and accurate. The plants live inside that accuracy. Their lushness is a rate: built as fast as the rotor mills them, so that what you see is the steady image of a frantic negotiation. A limb thins to wire, snaps, and another has already taken its angle. You never catch the place at rest. You catch it at its average, which looks, for a second at a time, like a form.

The fauna refuses to be part of the scenery and is everywhere in the accounting. They are small, dry, and almost never still enough to count as bodies occupying space. A first glance says empty air. A second says the empty air is trafficked to a degree that feels like insult—motes with intentions, streaks with turns in them, things the size of seeds that bank in squads and reverse as if the squad had one eye. Some wear the same metallic film as the plants and vanish against them until they lift. Some are the color of the salt and live in the inch above the crust, a skating layer that boils into view when the rotor's lower fringe dips. They do not flock in the soft sense. They exchange. Clouds of them peel off a plant, cross open ground in a ribbon that does not diffuse, and enter another plant's outer haze as if into a city gate. Abundance here is a property of time and of errands. At any instant the volume is nearly void. Across a minute it has been used completely, every cubic nothing crossed and recrossed.

They are the high frequencies in the roaring. The rotor is the body of the sound, a broad howl with a flutter in it where the walls return their own voice. The plants add chords—aeolian, enormous, changing key as limbs are milled and restated. The animals add the attack: ticks, zippers, a sizzling as squads hit a standing wave and scatter. There is no conversation to be had aloud. A shout exists only as a feeling in the throat. After a short while you stop producing it, the way you stop striking a bell that will not sound inside another bell.

The smallness of the bowl makes this worse and more structured. Sound has boundaries. It stacks. Walk the inner edge and you pass through ridges of pressure that shove the chest, then through seams where the voices cancel and the world, for three steps, is only a hiss at the edge of hearing—still not quiet, but a door left ajar. In those seams the fauna becomes visible as individuals: jointed, eyeless or all-eye, wings like flakes of mica, legs trailing filaments that taste the shear. They do not flee you so much as route around the fact of you, already corrected before you finish a step. Then the seam ends and the roar takes the skull again. People who stay learn the seams the way others learn doors.

On the eastern rim, where the wall is lowest and the salt has climbed highest, there are marks that are not geology. Not many. A scatter, as the brief implied. A course of dark blocks set into the rim and mostly swallowed, their edges still too true. A fan of needles, thumb-thin, leaning out over the drop in an arc that does not match any bracing you could invent with iron; they hum in a register that finds the bones of the wrist when you lay a hand near them, not on them. Half a building, or the idea of a building: a shell the size of a chapel, doorless, its interior scoured to the same finish as the bowl, with a plinth in the center grown over by salt in leaves, like pages. Nothing on the plinth is legible. The salt has its own writing, and it is only the writing of accretion.

The needles are doing the work. You understand this slowly, which is the only speed the place allows, because understanding is not a sentence you can hear yourself finish. The rotor is not a wind that found a hollow and stayed. It is held. The needles—and others you begin to see once you know the shape to look for, some no larger than grass, some buried so only a shadow of refraction gives them away—take the ordinary high-country air and refuse it its ordinary chances. They do not spin. They select. Motion that enters their attention is biased, a little, always in the same rotational sense, and the bias has been applied for so long that the mountain has eroded into agreement with it. The technology is not a machine in the bowl. The bowl is the machine's footprint, and the machine is a kind of attention paid to air by something that does not bother to look like a turbine. Stand near the needles and the roar gains a purity, a centering, that the middle of the floor does not have. The floor is where the centered thing frays into turbulence: nested spins, salt sheets lifting and slapping down, a plant rocked to its roots and answering by flowering—if that is the word—a sudden excess of white scales that the next gust harvests completely, a bright blizzard of a few seconds that the skating animals rise into and pick apart before it lands.

Nothing accumulates except form. That is the rule you can trust. Dust is made and taken. Scales are made and taken. The animals are hatched, or divided, or whatever their dry arithmetic is, and spent, and their spending is another glittering in the shear. The walls lose a grain and do not change. Water does not join this economy. Bring it and the place will abolish it without ceremony, and the abolition is the closest thing to thirst you will see: the crust eager, then finished, then innocent.

By late light the ordinary sun goes amber and the metallic films on the plants throw it back in small true colors, copper and pale gold, not magic, only angle. The rotor does not care that the light has changed. It has not cared about evening for as long as the rim has been this rim. If you remain in a seam you can watch one plant restate a limb at a human pace, the lattice climbing into its own outline while the roar goes on outside the seam like weather against a shut house. It is the only slowness the bowl offers. Everything else is exchange, at a rate that would read as catastrophe if the forms were not so practiced at surviving their own rate.

When you climb back over the lip, the silence is violent in the other direction. The ears invent a ringing to fill what the bowl has trained them to expect. Behind you the hollow continues its work, lush and empty, deafening and bounded, cool under an honest sky, a storm that is not passing and a landscape that has already, long ago, agreed to be its bed.
