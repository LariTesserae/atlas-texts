The water is not weather that arrived and might leave. It is the air of the place: cold, heavy, and old enough that no memory of dry stone remains in the way the courses sit. What would be wind elsewhere is only a slow lean in the water, a pressure that takes a season to cross a terrace and a lifetime to cross the province beyond it. There is a surface, somewhere far above the lower floors, but it does not behave like a sky. It is a lid of colder water, rain that has never finished falling, and a skin of floating leaves so continuous that the two can hardly be told apart. Light comes through that lid the way sound comes through a shut door. Most of what stands below stands in dark.

The ground is made. Dig anywhere and the dig is a dismantling. There is no bedrock that was not cut, carried, and set. The lowest courses are blocks the size of rooms, bedded in clay that has gone the color of the water, their edges rounded until a hand cannot find the joint except by the faint change in grain. Above them sit smaller stones, fitted without mortar or with a mortar that has become stone itself. Above those, in the yards and channels and the long floors between walls, are cells of laid rubble, baskets of earth sunk as pavements, linings of puddled clay, mats of woven fiber that mineralized centuries ago and now ring like thin ceramic when a frond taps them. Tool marks survive only in sheltered lees: a chisel groove under an overhang, a row of pecked cups where a line was trued, the ghost of a cord-mark in clay. Nothing metal holds. Where copper was used it is a green stain in a joint, no thicker than paint. The whole continent of it was raised by hand, by wedge and mallet and rope, and then left until the leaving became the nature of the work.

It has reached the age where shape and ruin are the same thing. Whatever could slip has slipped. Walls have settled to the angle at which their own weight and the water’s weight agree. Corners have opened to a finger’s width and stopped. Slopes are at repose. Roofs that were vaults are now low ridges of fallen voussoirs, and the ridges have grown a skin of silt so fine it no longer moves except when a whole ridge sighs and lowers by a thumb’s breadth. The flood that covered the courses is no longer an event. It is the oldest stable water the masonry has. Solution still works, but slowly, in the cold: lime blooms in white crusts on the down-current faces, clay joints weep a thread of cloud and then clear, and a block loses a grain of itself in a year. No undercutting hurries. No storm resets the floor. The place has finished becoming what it was built to resist, and now it only persists.

Low as it is, it is not flat in the way a single floor is flat. It is flat the way a country of terraces is flat when seen from far enough, and from inside it is a stacking. Water deep enough to have weather lies over water shallow enough to count as a hall. A causeway runs for a distance that would be a day’s walk in air and is simply a direction here, its parapet worn to a rounded lip, plants rooted in every course. Beside it the floor drops by a single step, then by a stair of steps so long-settled they read as a ramp, then into a cistern whose walls are still vertical because they were built too thick to slump. Beyond that, another floor, another wall, another grid of channels no wider than outstretched arms. The channels are the densest country. Two walls, a silted invert, a canopy of fronds that meet in the middle and have braided there. You can travel a great way without ever entering open water, passing from cell to cell through doors that were cut for people carrying baskets and are now only gaps the plants have not quite closed. Open water, when it comes, is not empty. It is a deeper room in the same building, its far wall too far to see, its near wall close enough to touch.

The plants are the only life that takes up space, and they take up all the space the stone has left them. Nothing eats them. Nothing walks them down. In the cold their decay is a patience, so the mass that stands is the mass of an immense accumulation rather than of a frantic season. On the higher terraces, where the lid of the surface is nearer and a gray noon sometimes arrives, the cover is truly lush: strap-leaved plants rooted in the basket-earth, their blades the dull green of deep shade, layered until the floor is a single yielding surface; bushier growth in the joints, stiff and slow, putting on a finger of new stem in a year; curtains of finer fronds hanging from every lintel that still spans a gap. Lower, and in the rooms the floating mats have blacked out, the same kinds persist as pale versions of themselves. They are not bright. They are plentiful. Holdfasts grip the chisel-cups and the cord-marked clay. Roots follow mortar lines the way roots elsewhere follow cracks in rock, except that here every crack was drawn by a hand. In the oldest cells the growth has become structure: stems thickened to poles, peat fills raised to the sills of windows, a doorway reduced to a soft slot. The lushness is coverage and weight, not color. Most of it is the color of the dark it grows in.

The floating mats are part of the same growth, torn loose in some age and never forced back into being merely surface. They raft together, knit by their own runners, and they are thick enough in the still provinces to be a second country laid over the first. Rain falls on them and does not strike water. It soaks, and the soak drips through days later from the underside, cold threads in cold water. Where a mat thins, a column of slightly less-dark water stands down to the floor, and the plants below lean toward it by a degree no eye would catch in a year. That lean is one of the faster motions the place allows.

Sound is mostly the sound of a medium that does not want to carry it. A leaf leaves a frond and takes a long time to arrive, and its arrival is a tap, then nothing. A joint settles and the stone speaks once, a short knock carried farther than it should be and then gone. Bubbles from the leaves collect under ceilings and leave in strings when a pressure-wave, hours old, tilts the room; each bubble is a tick. Rain on the upper mats never becomes rain down here. It becomes the slow addition of water to water, a change in weight before it is a change in noise. There is no surge, no chorus, no wingbeat, no foot. In the channels the loudest ordinary thing is the brush of blade on blade when the lean comes through, and even that is a whisper stretched over an afternoon.

The water itself has provinces. It does not mix eagerly. A cold layer can lie on a slightly less cold layer for a human span of years, the boundary a faint sheet you notice only because the fronds below it and above it lean at different times. Silt falls so finely it is a darkening rather than a sediment, and it falls everywhere, and it is itself made: ground from the courses, from the clay linings, from the mineralized mats. There is no foreign mud. Visibility, where the word means anything, is an arm’s length, sometimes a body’s length in the gray columns, often less. Distance is learned by the recurrence of walls, by the grain of the laying, by the way a channel’s width repeats. The horizon is not a line. It is the point at which another course of stone becomes indistinguishable from water.

Change, when it is admitted, is a schedule of failures already almost complete. A lintel that has been cracked since before the mats closed will finish its drop, and the plants will close over the new ridge, and the channel will learn a slightly different lean. A clay cell will weep its last cloud and become a clear joint. A terrace will lose enough lime from its upstream face that the plants there thin by one layer, while downstream, where the dissolved stone resettles, a new crust will take a root and hold it. These are the seasons. There is no breeding rush, no migration, no year of insects, because there is nothing here that is an animal and nothing that needs one. Seeds and fragments travel in the slow lean. Most plants simply continue, putting a clone through a joint, filling the next cell, stopping where the dark stops them and remaining, alive, at the size they have already reached. Decay keeps pace with that continuance and does not outrun it. The equilibrium is what lush means, here, after so long: not increase, but a thickness that no longer thickens, on a floor that no longer falls.

The rules are the rules of cold water and of masonry that has become ground. Nothing burrows. Nothing hunts. A closed room stays closed until the stone gives, and the stone gives by solution and load, not by siege. Nutrient stays where the last leaf fell unless the lean carries it a terrace down. Light is a resource so scarce that the upper mats and the lower floors are different countries sharing a botany. Direction is downstream of the lean, or along the courses, which were aligned by cord and eye and still hold their lines because nothing has had a reason to erase a line that gravity has already accepted. What was built to hold water now holds it with the indifference of a thing that cannot do otherwise. What was built to be walked is walked only by roots.

Stand on a sill in one of the middle channels and the place is small: two walls, fronds meeting, a floor of peat over fitted stone, dark enough that the next doorway is a rumor. The smallness is true and it is local. Beyond the doorway the channel repeats, and the terrace beside it repeats, and the cistern beyond that holds a darkness with its own slow weather, a seiche that breathes in and out across a width no wall interrupts for a distance greater than any of the old works were meant to span. The repetition does not end in a natural shore, because there is no natural shore. It ends, if it ends, where the courses end, and the courses do not end within any reach of sense. The made floor continues, worn, planted, drowned, quiet, at the bottom of its own water, in the oldest shape it is still able to keep.
