The room is smaller than weather and larger than a person can hold in one look. Its floor plan would fit inside a parish church, if it had a floor. It does not. What you stand on is a catwalk of stamped plate, bolted to a ring-girder, and beyond the rail the volume opens and does not close again until the far wall, which is also the near wall, curved, continuous, the whole interior one chamber turned slowly on a riveted spine. People who have been here say the drop is thirty meters. The drawings, if you can still find a copy that has not been corrected by hand, say twelve. Both are true in the way distances are true where air is doing work.

It sits high. Not on a mountain so much as in the station a mountain would occupy if the mountain had been removed and the station left. Outside — and outside is only a rumor carried by seals and by the color of the light — the air is thin enough to hurt. Inside, the machines have been breathing for longer than the paint has lasted, and the breath is warm. Waste heat from the old process, whatever the process was, still moves through jackets and returns. The temperature does not climb and does not fall. It occupies a narrow band a little above the temperature of skin, and it has occupied it so long that the metal remembers. Touch a girder and it is not cold. Touch it in the same place a season later and it is the same not-cold.

Moisture is not weather here. It is a byproduct that learned manners. Condensate beads on the underside of every horizontal, gathers, hesitates, and lets go. The drops do not become rain. They become a schedule. In the lower third of the hollow, where the catwalks multiply into a loose cage, the dripping has worn the plate into soft cups, each cup the size of a palm, each holding a finger of water that never quite dries and never quite overflows. The cups are not designed. They are what established erosion looks like when the substrate is steel and the agent is patience. Nothing is being ruined. The room has arrived at the shape it means to keep.

Everything you can touch was made. There is no bedrock, no soil that was not first a shipment, no timber that was not first a specification. The vegetation does not care. It has taken the made world as if the made world were a cliff with better handholds. Vines as thick as mooring lines follow the cable trays because the trays are warm and because the trays already knew the shortest path between supports. Ferns open out of inspection ports whose covers were removed during a maintenance cycle that was never closed. Moss pads the diamond plate until the pattern is only a rumor underfoot, and then, at the edges where boots still pass, gives way cleanly, as if it understands traffic. The green is not a reclamation. It is a workforce that arrived without a contract and has been on shift since. In the cups of condensate, smaller plants float with their roots in the water and their leaves against the air, and they do not drift, because the air here has a direction and they have learned it.

Light comes in at the value of an ordinary day, which is the strangest ordinary thing in the room. There are no windows you would call windows. High in the curve, a band of laminated panels, clouded by decades of condensate and by something greener than dirt, admits a white that does not favor morning or afternoon. Shadows fall where the trusses say they should. You can read a gauge by it. You cannot tell the hour. The gauges themselves are still legible in the places the moss has not chosen. Most of them indicate states the process no longer has. A few indicate states the process never stopped having. No one adjusts them. Adjustment would be a kind of weather, and weather is not what happens here.

What happens here is sound, and the sound does not happen. It is. A low industrial note, not a whine and not a knock, occupies the hollow the way water occupies a well: completely, and without surface. It comes from below the lowest catwalk, from a housing you cannot see without climbing into the cage, and climbing into the cage is something the room permits and the sound discourages. The note is loud enough that speech at arm's length arrives as shape rather than as words. It does not rise. It does not rest. People who stay long enough stop hearing it as noise and start hearing it as the material of the air, the way a diver stops hearing the sea and starts hearing only the things that move inside it. Ears ring when you leave, if leaving is still done. The ringing is not damage. It is the absence of a thing that had become architecture.

Density, in the sense of things per volume, is nearly nothing. The chamber is a shell of made matter wrapped around a quantity of warm air that has almost no objects in it. A wrench left on a beam is an event. A person is an event. The green crowds the surfaces and leaves the middle alone, so that standing at the rail you look across a clear diameter in which nothing hangs, nothing flies for long, nothing interrupts the light except, occasionally, a body small enough to be mistaken for a flaw in the eye.

The fauna are abundant and the abundance is a matter of kinds, not of crowds. They keep to the green and to the cups and to the warm jackets, and they do not cross the hollow unless they must. There are moths the color of oxidized copper, their wings scaled so finely that in the ordinary light they look wet. There are birds no larger than a thumb, beaks adapted to the seams between plates, nesting in the mouths of dead conduits where the air is still and the sound is slightly less. There are things that might be beetles and might be fittings that learned to walk; they travel the cable trays in single file and they travel slowly, and if you watch one for the length of a shift you will see it cover a distance you could span with your hand. None of them flock. Flocking would require the middle of the room, and the middle of the room belongs to the note. They are many species in a small parish, each with a parish of its own: a port, a cup, a warm joint, a run of tray. Between parishes, the air is empty enough that a dropped glove falls straight, turns once, and is gone into the lower green, where something will eventually incorporate it or ignore it. Ignoring is more common. The room is not hungry. It is occupied.

The strangeness is not a monster and not a door. It is a rule that does not match the rules you carried up with you. Seeds here do not need soil so much as they need a surface that has already been warmed and wetted on a schedule, and the schedule is the dripping, and the dripping is the machines, and the machines are still doing a job whose product, if it ever had a product, left by a pipe that now ends in a fern. The animals do not startle at the sound. They startle at silence, and silence does not occur, so they do not startle. Tools left on beams do not rust at the rate the textbooks promise; the condensate is not quite water, or the air is not quite air, or the heat has baked the worst of the oxygen's appetite out of the metal and left it merely brown. People who sleep on the upper walk dream of the note as a corridor and wake with the taste of warm metal and the certainty that they have been walking downward for hours without losing height.

Change, when it comes, comes the way the cups came: by repetition too small to watch. A vine thickens by a thread in a month. A gauge needle, already meaningless, drifts by a hair in a year. A bird-line of thumb-sized birds abandons one conduit for the conduit beside it, and the abandoned mouth fills, slowly, with a moss that prefers the dark a fraction more than its neighbor. No one records these things. Recording would require a quieter room, or a faster one. This room has chosen its rate. It is the rate of a machine that was built to run and was never given a reason to stop, and of a garden that mistook the machine for climate, and of a climate that, having been mistaken for, agreed.

If you stay on the catwalk and do not climb down into the cage, the place will show you only what it shows anyone: green on every made surface, warm damp in the cups, ordinary light, an empty diameter of air, and a sound that is the size of the room and older than your attention. The extreme of it — the height outside, the thinness, the long fall of the world away from this single constructed volume — does not enter. It presses on the shell the way the sea presses on a hull, and the shell, weathered, balanced, long past the century of its commissioning, holds. Inside, the parish continues. A moth crosses a patch of light and is, for a second, the only moving thing in thirty meters of air. Then it reaches the far green and is gone into the abundance, and the hollow is empty again, and the note, which had not paused for the moth, does not pause for the absence either.
