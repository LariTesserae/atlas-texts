The place is small enough to walk the whole of it before the light changes, and high enough that the walk has to be taken slowly. It sits in a notch the larger ridge has not quite closed: a court of stone, perhaps a hundred paces on its long axis, walled on three sides by the mountain’s own flanks and open on the fourth only as a lip. Beyond the lip the air falls away and does not come back. You do not need the view to know the height. The sky is too near, and the breath you spend does not return in full.

What fills the court is not space but stone in close argument with itself. Fins of pale rock stand almost touching, pale enough to read as bone in the first glare and then, once the eye adjusts, as something more ordinary and more exact: a fine-grained quartzite, sugar-grained on the broken faces, smoothed on the old ones to a dull silk. Between the fins the passages are shoulder-wide, sometimes less. Shelves stack above shelves. A slab leans into another slab and has been leaning there long enough that a seam of grit has packed the contact and cemented. Nothing here is freshly born and nothing is falling apart. The forms have arrived at themselves. Edges that once were knives are now honest under the hand, still sharp enough to catch a sleeve, no longer interested in cutting. The scree at the foot of the walls has found its angle and keeps it. A stone you set down in the morning is a stone you will find in the evening, unless the day has a particular reason.

There is no plant in any of it. Not a cushion of moss in the cracks, not a stain of lichen that resolves, under looking, into a living crust. The mountain below this notch presumably has its own weather and its own green, but that country does not reach here, and the court does not seem to miss it. What grows instead is mineral, and only in the way minerals insist. On the shaded undersides of the shelves, dendritic films of manganese have branched in patterns a hurried eye would call fern. They are not ferns. They do not thicken through a season or pale in a drought. They are the record of water that arrived, hesitated, and left a drawing. Beside them, iron has wept in thin orange threads down the quartzite, and in two or three pockets a white crust of salts feathers out from a seep and then, by afternoon, is only a roughness, the water having gone back into the air. The false plants are the nearest thing the place has to an ecology, and they are not an ecology. They are the residue of a process that repeats.

Water is never a feature and never entirely absent. There is no stream. There is a thread, sometimes, in the lowest seam, darkening the grit for a few paces before the grit drinks it. There are films on the north faces that never quite dry and never quite gather into drops you could count. After a brief weather — and weather here is brief, a cloud taking the notch for twenty minutes and then the sky scouring itself clean — the shelves tick with drip, each tick placed, not hurried, and then the ticking stops. By the time the shadow of the west wall has crossed the court, the thread is gone and the dark grit is only dark grit, colder than the dry stone beside it. In the deepest pocket, under a slab that roofs it like a hand, a lens of water sometimes stands for a day or two, no wider than a basin, sky-colored when you lean to it and black when you do not. It is not a pool the place intends to keep. Wind finds it. Sun finds it. In the morning after a clear night it wears a pane of ice thin enough to powder under a knuckle, and by the time the light is full on the east fins the pane has become a skin of melt and then a dampness and then nothing you can point to. The court is damp the way a worked stone is damp: not wet, not dry, holding a little, giving it back.

The cold is of that same order. It does not announce itself with wind at first. The wind, when it comes, comes as a hush along the fins, a sound with the middle taken out of it, and it moves the grit in small, audible hisses only where a passage narrows to a slot. Most of the day the cold is simply the temperature of shade on pale rock at an altitude where shade means something. Stand in the sun and the quartzite gives heat back to your hands with a surprising readiness; step one pace into a passage and the heat is gone, not gradually, as if the stone had been waiting to be itself again. The brightness makes this easy to misread. The light is severe and almost without color at noon, a high white poured over every upper surface, throwing shadows so short and so hard they look cut rather than cast. Reflection off the fins fills even the passages. There is nowhere in the court that is properly dim, except the pocket under the roofing slab, and even that holds a blue-white rim around its dark. People squint here not because the place is vast but because every surface is doing the sun’s work twice.

Through the day the court is busy in a quiet way. Just after the light clears the east wall, rime that formed in the night lets go of the highest edges in a fine dry fall, a whisper you feel on your wrist more than you hear. The stone ticks as it takes the sun — small, spaced reports, a joint relieving itself, a plate of quartz adjusting against its neighbor. Nothing falls that would matter to a person standing clear. A grain slides. A flake the size of a fingernail, loosened by the last freeze, tips off a ledge and clicks twice on the way down and is then a flake among flakes. In the early afternoon, if a cloud comes, the whole temperature of the court drops in the time it takes to turn your head, and the bright faces go the color of tin, and the drip starts in the seams that still remember the morning’s melt. When the cloud goes, the tin burns back to white and the drip stops, one thread at a time, as if a decision were being made separately in each crack. Toward evening the wind finds the notch more honestly. It does not howl. It leans into the slots and draws a long, low note that is more pressure than pitch, and under that note the grit does its small migration, grain by grain, from the places the day warmed to the places the night will cool. By dark the court is arranged differently only to someone who had mapped every pebble. To itself, it is the same court, having spent the day being so.

Sound, apart from these things, is what the stone declines to pass along. A voice does not carry from one end of the court to the other; the fins take it and keep it. Footsteps come back as a soft scuff and then nothing. There are no birds to object to this, and no insects to stitch a noise through the bright air, which is the detail that takes longest to believe. A place this sharply lit, with water appearing and withdrawing on a daily schedule, ought to have something moving in it that is not the weather. Nothing does. The absence is not eerie so much as structural. The court has no use for anything that would need to eat, or hide, or sing. Whatever rules it runs on are physical, and they are already fully occupied.

Someone has been here, and not only in passing. The evidence is modest and has been partly taken back. Across the narrowest slot, where the west wall breaks and a draft would otherwise run the length of the court, a short wall has been fitted: unmortared, stones chosen for their faces, stacked to about the height of a knee and then stopped, as if the builder had decided the draft was sufficiently answered. The top course has slumped by a hand’s width. Grit has packed the gaps. In another generation of this weather the wall will be a line of stones that happen to lie level, and then not even that. Near the roofed pocket, the quartzite shows a scatter of peck-marks, the half-moon bites of a hard point struck at an angle, outlining a shallow niche that was never finished or was finished and has since lost its depth. On the lip, where the stone is flatter and the light is worst, three shallow cups have been ground into the surface, each no larger than a palm, each holding, on a damp morning, a spoon of water and a pinch of the white salt. A small awl of hammered copper — green at the bend, bright where a recent scrape has cut through the corrosion — lies in the second cup, as if set there to be found and never retrieved. The metal is simple. The grinding of the cups is simple. Nothing here was built by anyone who had more than a hand, a point, and time.

What the marks suggest, and the court does not contradict, is a use measured in visits rather than in residence. No one could winter in the notch. No one would need to. The tools are the tools of people who came up for a particular hour of a particular kind of day: to grind a salt, to shelter a draft, to leave a point where the water would find it. They understood the place well enough not to ask it to be larger or kinder. Their work is being returned to geology at the same rate the geology revises itself, which is the courtesy this court extends to everything.

The rules, once you have spent the day, are plain. Water arrives and is not stored. Heat arrives and is not stored. Sound arrives and is not stored. Stone stores only its own shape, and it revises that shape by increments too small to watch and too consistent to doubt. The brightness is not a mood but a condition of the altitude, and the cold is not the opposite of the brightness; they occupy the court together, divided by the width of a single fin. Nothing grows. Nothing hunts. The dendritic films will still be dendritic films when the copper has gone entirely to green and the green has gone to powder and the powder has joined the grit migrating toward the night side of the slots. If you come again, the knee-high wall will be lower by the thickness of a stone’s patience, the lens under the slab will be there or not according to a weather you did not see, and the cups on the lip will hold their spoon of sky or their spoon of nothing. The place does not wait. It is already in the middle of being what it is, and the middle is where it lives.
