The ground here is not ground. It is a pale, cool weave of living fiber, still deciding what it will be, and you walk on it the way you would walk on a floor that has only recently remembered it is a floor.

There is no water. Not in channels, not in cisterns, not as mist. The air is dry enough that the tongue goes papery after a while, and the chemistry of the place runs on something else: a slow exchange of mineral salts and a thin, almost odorless vapor that the growths pull out of the rock the way other plants pull water from soil. Where a stem has been cut — and they are cut, constantly, by the work of the place — the wound does not weep. It dusts. A fine white flour sifts out and is caught, almost at once, by smaller threads that drink it back in. The salt-fields people expected, coming down, are not fields. They are the plants themselves, crusted, crystalline along the joints, glittering under ordinary light.

Ordinary light is the first surprise, and then it stops being one. You are below the surface — far enough that the true sky is a rumor carried by the shafts — but the light is day-light, even, untheatrical. It comes down the shafts as a column and is taken up by the tissue. The larger growths are translucent at their edges. A leaf the size of a warehouse roof will pass the light through itself, cooling it, and lay it on the floor thirty meters on as a paler version of the same hour. Shadows are soft. There is no glare and almost no dark, except in the throats of unfinished tunnels where the weave has not yet reached.

The region is open. From any raised spine of root you can see a long way: a shallow valley of growth, then a rise, then another basin, the whole of it on the order of a town and its surrounding works, maybe larger if you count the shafts that drop into the next level, which is the same place again, younger. Nothing closes over you except the ceiling of older fiber, and even that is incomplete. In places the ceiling is only a lace, and through it you see the level above doing the same work at a different stage. Erosion, in the old sense, has barely begun. What you are standing in is pre-formation. The geology is embryonic. Ridges thicken while you watch if you are patient; a seam that was a wrinkle at morning is a low wall by the time the light in the shafts goes amber. Nothing here is weathered. Everything is arriving.

And yet it is a city.

The growths are not wild in the way a forest is wild. They have been persuaded — or they have persuaded themselves; the distinction is not maintained — into plans. Avenues run where the fiber has been trained into hard, pale roads, springy underfoot, wide enough for the carts. The carts are the loudest things, and they are not loud. Wooden-seeming frames, actually pressed stem, rolling on axles of the same material, drawn by animals that prefer the roads and will not step onto unfinished weave if they can help it. Between the avenues the buildings stand with a great deal of space around them. Density is low. You can walk for minutes and meet no one, and still be, unmistakably, inside a settlement: a tower of braided trunk with window-slits cut and held open by inserted ribs; a long shed whose roof is a single leaf, ventilated by pores the workers enlarge with heated irons; a stack of platforms, each one a living shelf, where goods are sorted. The architecture is the plant. There is no stone to speak of, no brick. What looks like masonry is compressed fiber, still faintly warm if you put a palm to it, still, at the joins, trying to knit.

Industry sits inside the botany without quite becoming it. In the eastern basin the mills turn. They are slow wheels, geared in the old way, driven not by water but by the contraction of great tendons that the plants throw out and draw back on a cycle of hours. The sound is a soft tick, a sigh, the occasional wooden knock when a gear tooth, which is a knuckle of hardened stem, seats wrong and is pared down by a child with a knife. Elsewhere, kilns — except they are not kilns. Chambers of thick leaf, sealed, where the dry vapor is concentrated until it carries enough mineral to plate the things hung inside them: tools, machine parts, the thin bright skins of the carts' fittings. Electrification has arrived in a modest form. Filaments of a copper-tolerant vine run along the avenue spines, and in the workshops they glow, not brightly, a steady filament-orange that supplements the shaft-light when a roof-leaf is too thick. Factories, if that is the word, are halls where dozens of people and a few of the animals work at the same process: stripping, pressing, plating, splicing. Scaled production, yes. Clamor, no. The place absorbs noise. Fiber eats it. A hammer blow arrives as a pat.

The animals are many, and mostly small, and mostly busy. A low, cool ecosystem has filled in under and around the industry. Things like silverfish, but the size of a hand, graze the salt-crust on the lower stems and leave pale tracks. Bird-analogs — flighted, quiet, with beaks adapted to pry pores open — nest in the window-ribs and feed on the insects that gather wherever the dry vapor leaks. There are the cart-beasts: long-bodied, low, with mouths that rasp fiber and somehow do not kill the road, because the road is designed to be rasped, the top layer sacrificial, always growing back. Higher up the trunks, where the light is strongest, colonies of something between a moth and a mouse hunt the birds, and the birds know the hours. You see the shift at once if you sit still. Dawn in the shafts, and the grazers come out onto the open weave. Midday, and the platers' halls exhale a sweet mineral dust, and a whole guild of small flyers rises to sieve it, and the birds follow, and for twenty minutes an avenue that was empty is a river of wings, absolutely silent except for the tick of wings against leaves. Then it is gone. Predator and prey, but conducted like a shift change.

The strangeness is not spectacle. It is the rules, which you learn by getting them slightly wrong.

Nothing rots. Dryness and the salt-chemistry see to that. What dies is taken apart by the grazers and by a class of thread-fine roots that advance only over dead tissue, never over living, a distinction the roots enforce with a chemical the workers have learned to bottle and use as a boundary paint. Mark a line with it on a living wall and the wall will not grow across. The city is drawn, in part, in that paint.

Growth is the weather. There are no storms. There is a cycle: a surge every few days when the vapor in the deep rock comes up richer, and every stem in the region puts on a measurable thickness, and doors that were cut yesterday no longer fit, and crews go out with knives and heated irons to restore the plan. People speak of the surge the way other towns speak of rain. They plan markets around it. They do not fear it. They are irritated by it, which is the sign that a force has been domesticated.

You cannot dig. The weave underfoot is the world. Beneath it, if you cut deep enough — and the crews who sink the shafts do — you meet a cooler, denser, unfinished mass, not yet fiber, not yet anything, a mineral curd that the oldest roots are still converting. It is cold to the wrist. It smells of nothing. Leave a cut open and by the next day threads have cross-hatched it, and by the third the floor is whole, and your hole is a scar that will be a road if someone paints the boundary in time, and a lump if they don't. The geology is being written. The city is the handwriting. Neither is finished, and both behave as if finishing were a process with no end-state, only a presentable stage.

Sound, mostly, is the sound of work that does not need to announce itself. Footfalls on springy fiber. The tick of the tendon-wheels. A cart's axle, knocked back into true. Somewhere far off, down a shaft, the same sounds from the level below, arriving already half-eaten. Voices carry poorly. People stand close to talk, or they use the vine-wire, which clicks in a code at the workshops and is ignored everywhere else. At the edges of the settled basins, where the plan runs out and the weave is still only a suggestion of a street, the quiet is thicker. You hear the growth itself there, if you kneel: a faint, irregular creak, the sound of tissue lengthening, of a wall deciding to be a wall.

Stand on the highest spine at the hour when the shaft-light goes amber and the filament-vines in the workshops answer it with their small orange, and the region lays itself out without drama. Sparse towers. Wide roads. The mills turning on their soft gears. Animals shifting ground in patterns you could map if you stayed a week. A ceiling of lace, and through it another town, younger, doing the same things a little less well. No river. No rain. No stone. A cool, dry, unfinished living city, still coming into being under its own light, active in every process that matters and almost silent in all of them, as if it had learned that a place this new does not need to raise its voice to be occupied.
