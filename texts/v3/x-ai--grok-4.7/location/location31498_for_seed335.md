The pocket has only just finished opening. You can tell by the edges: they are still sharp enough to cut a sleeve, and the faces of the split stone have not yet learned weather. A low place, barely above the water-table of the marsh that roofs it, and younger than the season. Something underneath heaved, or thawed wrong, or simply gave, and a room appeared where there had been only peat and cold ground. No one built it. Nothing in it was made. The walls are the walls of the rupture itself, pale mineral still dusted with the flour of its own breaking, and the floor is a shallow black pan of water that has not decided whether it is a pool or a leak.

It is small enough that you could cross it in a dozen steps if the footing allowed, and empty enough that those steps would feel like crossing a hall. Most of the volume is air. Wet air, dim air, air that keeps condensing on every new surface and falling back in threads. The light comes from one crooked slit overhead, where the marsh-mat tore and did not close. It is the color of tin left out in rain. Nothing casts a clean shadow. What grows here does not fill the room; it occupies seams. Thick moss beards the freshest fracture lines, already lush, already dripping, as if the plants had been waiting in the damp just outside the split and poured in the moment there was a ledge. Sedges lean out of cracks no wider than a finger. A few ferns, cold-stunted and violently green, stand alone on knobs of rock with yards of bare stone and bare water between them. The abundance is real and the emptiness is real. Life has arrived fast and has not yet learned to crowd.

The cold is not the clean cold of altitude. It is low, soaked, industrial in the old sense of a works that never stops. Water moves through the young rock the way a fluid moves through a machine that has not been finished and does not need to be. It finds every tube the rupture opened — hairline conduits, elbow-bends of fracture, vertical drops no longer than an arm — and it works them. Somewhere just out of sight a column of water rises and falls on a pulse you can count, a natural ram, sucking and slamming. Each slam throws a cuff of spray against the far wall and the spray freezes at the margins into scales of clear ice, then the next slam knocks the scales off and they tick across the pan like offcuts from a lathe. The rock is weeping everywhere the light reaches: not rain, not a spring with a mouth, but a continuous seepage from surfaces too new to have sealed. Droplets gather, fatten, let go. The sound of them is not delicate. In a hollow this hard and this empty, every drop arrives as a strike.

Under the pulse there is a lower register, a grinding. The pocket is still adjusting. Plates of stone no thicker than slate, sheared off in the opening and not yet fallen, flex against one another when the water-hammer hits, and the flex makes a noise like a belt slipping on a wheel. Sometimes a plate lets go entirely. It does not tumble far. It slaps the water, stands on edge for a moment as if it might become a wall, and then lies down. The edges stay sharp. Nothing here has had time to round. Even the mud, where mud exists, is only a skin over grit, and the grit is the bright, unsoftened grit of a cut made this week.

The air smells of cold iron and crushed green and the sweet rot of peat squeezed from above. Breath clouds and does not rise far; it joins the general condensation and comes back down. On the north side of the slit, where the light is weakest, the seepage has begun to plate the wall with a skin of ice so thin it looks like oil, and under that skin the rock glitters with a mineral that was never meant to see air. When a drop hits that skin it rings, a small bright note, and the note repeats itself around the hollow three or four times before the water-ram wipes it out. The place is loud the way a mill-race is loud: not chaotic, not animal-scream loud, but occupied by processes that do not pause for anything living. You raise your voice and the voice is a guest.

And yet the fauna is thick in the only way an empty room can hold it — by traffic, by number of kinds, by the insistence of small bodies using a machine they did not design. Midges stitch the dim band under the slit, a standing haze that drifts and reforms, loud if you hold still: a dry buzz riding the wet percussion. Something like a frog, but slower and more metallic in its call, answers the water-ram from a pocket of sedge, timing itself to the gaps. It is not mimicking. It is using the gaps the way a worker uses the pause between stamps. Beetles the color of wet coal cross the open floor in straight lines, as if the emptiness were a road they already know, and they do not cluster. Birds do not nest here — there is nowhere enclosed enough, and the noise would shake a cup of eggs to slurry — but they come to the lip of the slit and drop through, snatch, leave. A wren-sized thing with a pale bar on the wing works the moss beards for whatever the moss is brewing. Its feet tick on the ice-scale. When it flies, the hollow multiplies the wingbeat until it sounds like a small engine catching and failing to catch.

Lower, in the black pan, the water is not dead. It is too cold for the lushness above it to make sense, and yet the lushness drinks it, and yet things move in it. A dark back breaks the surface, long as a hand, gone. Larvae hang in the meniscus near the wall where the seepage enters warmest — warmest being a word that means only less killing — and they pulse in time with a current you cannot see, a production line of pale commas being carried from crack to pool to crack. The marsh above feeds the pocket continuously: threads of brown water, flecks of leaf, the occasional bright seed. Nothing accumulates into litter. The ram and the weep and the slow circular drift clear the floor as fast as the roof delivers. It is a works. Input, motion, output. The output is mostly water returned to the marsh by a dozen unmapped seams, and a little ice, and a mineral flour that stains the outflow the color of weak tea. Somewhere down-gradient a stain is spreading in the sedges that was not there at the start of the thaw. The pocket is manufacturing a difference in the land above it, and the land above it has not noticed yet.

There is a rule, and it is not quite natural and not quite otherwise. Sound here organizes matter. Not magically — the weirdness is only a degree past ordinary, the sort of thing you might mis-measure as echo. But stand long enough and you see that the ice-scales do not fall at random. They form where the standing notes of the hollow are strongest, along the curves where the slap of the ram returns to meet itself, and they thicken there into combs, fragile, load-bearing for an hour, then shattered and rebuilt. The beetles cross on the quiet lanes between those curves. The frog calls only in the troughs. The moss is lushest where drip-strike is most regular, as if the plants were metering themselves to a cadence. Nothing in the pocket is aware of this. It is simply what the new room does with water, cold, and a hard empty shape: it becomes a timing device. A factory with no owner and no product you would put in a crate, running at full voice in the dark under the marsh, younger than the ice on its walls.

If you wait through one full cycle — ram, flex, grind, a plate letting go, the bright rearrangement of spray — the light in the slit changes by almost nothing. Cloud, perhaps, or the marsh-grass leaning over. The cold does not lift. A fresh edge gleams where the fallen plate tore a corner off the wall, and already a thread of water has found it, and already the first bead is swelling to fall. Somewhere in the moss a seed that came down in the last hour has split. The room is not filling. It is working. The emptiness stays, and the life stays in it like workers on a floor too large for them, and the noise goes on with the particular loudness of a process that has only just begun and has no intention of becoming quiet in order to be understood.
