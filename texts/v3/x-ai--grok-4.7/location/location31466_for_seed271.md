The place is a short hollow, no longer than a nave and not much wider, cut into the foot of a low rise that itself barely lifts above the plain. From outside you would miss it. The entrance is a sag in the ground, a slope of pale grit that has already found its angle and keeps it. Step down and the sky narrows to a slot. The walls close on three sides. They are not high in any mountainous sense—twenty paces of stone, weathered to a dull ochre and a cooler gray where the grain runs—but they are high enough that the floor lives in their shade, and the shade does not move.

The rock is old sandstone gone soft at the edges. Nothing here is sharp. Corners have rounded into shoulders. Ledges that once might have broken off have instead thinned, grain by grain, until they are only a change in color. The talus at the base is a skirt of the same stone, settled, not fresh; the pieces fit the slope as if they had been placed and then forgotten by the placing. A dark stain runs down the back wall from a seam no wider than a thumb. It does not pour. It wets the face, beads where the mineral skin is smoother, and disappears into a groove that has long since stopped deepening. The groove ends in a pan the size of a table, clay-sealed, the water in it the color of weak tea. Around the pan the ground is darker for a body’s length, then pales again into silt that powders underfoot and does not crust or crack. The moisture is a fact of the hollow, not an event. It has been this damp, in these places, for longer than the shape of the walls.

Between the wet and the dry the floor is mostly empty. Space is the main material. A tuft of stiff grass stands on a low rise of grit, then nothing, then another, then a low shrub with small leaves the color of dried olive, its stems gray and rigid, holding a few seed heads that have opened and stayed. They are not stripped. Nothing comes for them. Moss occupies the perpetual damp: a thin green on the north-facing grain of the stone, blacker where it mats the lip of the pan. In the water itself a skin of algae lies unbroken, olive and still, with no ripple to tear it. Lichen freckles the drier ledges in slow coins of rust and ash. That is the vegetation. It does not fill. It marks the places where water lingers a little longer than elsewhere, and leaves the rest to stone and light.

The light is low even at the hour when it should be strongest. A high haze sits on the plain and does not lift; what comes down the slot is diffuse, the color of old paper, warm without glare. Shadows are soft-edged and short of true dark. The walls give back a mild heat. The air in the hollow is close, not stifling—a stored warmth, the kind stone keeps after years of the same weather. You feel it in the grit before you feel it on your face. There is no shimmer worth the name. The dimness takes the edge off the heat, and the heat takes the edge off the dimness, and the two have settled into each other.

Sound is almost an absence that has learned a few exceptions. The plain outside, if there is wind on it, does not reach the floor. What you hear, if you wait, is a single drop from the seam, finding the pan at intervals so even they stop registering as events. Between drops, the small noise of your own weight in the silt. No wing-beat, no tick of insect, no rustle that is not the shrub moving once and then not again, because the air does not push it. The quiet is not eerie. It is what a place sounds like when only water, stone, and a few rooted things occupy it, and they have already done all the occupying they mean to do.

Near the left wall, half sunk, a line of stones runs for a few paces and then loses itself. They are flatter than the rubble, set on edge, and the soil has risen to their tops so that they read as a darker seam rather than a wall. Lichen has taken them the same as the bedrock. Farther in, a circle of depressed clay might be a pan the seep once filled and abandoned, or a scoop someone made when the hollow was used for the little water it keeps. There is no way to tell, and nothing else to ask with: no shard, no metal, no cut mark that weathering has not returned to the general grain. If anyone was here, the hollow has finished with them. What remains is the same class of fact as the ledge and the stain—settled form, no longer an action.

Nothing in the place is arriving or leaving. The water in the pan is the water in the pan; its level meets a faint mineral line and does not cross it. The grass does not spread. The shrub holds its seeds until weather, not hunger, takes them, and the seeds that fall lie in the silt or catch in a crack and either take or do not, without altering the spacing. Grains leave the rim and join the skirt, and the skirt’s angle does not steepen. Day warms the stone a little; the haze does not break; night cools the air and not the walls. By morning the hollow is what it was. It has been in this balance long enough that balance is the only visible tense.

Stand at the entrance and the whole of it is present: the slot of paper-colored light, the wet seam, the tea-colored pan, the scattered tufts, the lost line of stones, the empty distances between them. It is a small room the ground made and then stopped making. Warm. Dim. Damp in its pockets and dry in its open floor. Quiet enough that the next drop, when it comes, is the news.
