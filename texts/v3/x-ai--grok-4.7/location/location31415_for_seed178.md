The basin does not sit still long enough to be named as one thing. From any rise—there are few, and they are new—you see a low country of black water and blacker green running out under a sky that never quite becomes day. The light is a bruise. What illumination there is comes sideways, off cloud-bellies and off the wet faces of stone, and it fails before it reaches the ground. You learn the place by sound and by the temperature of the air against your mouth.

The land itself is unfinished. Ridges of fresh rock stand up with edges still sharp enough to cut a boot: basalt ribs, gravel bars that have not yet learned to be soil, cutbanks the color of wet iron. Nothing here has had time to round. A flood took the last shape of the eastern flats sometime in the night; by the time the water dropped, a new channel had written itself across what had been a willow thicket, and the willows were traveling, root-mats and all, turning slowly in the current like rafts. The young stone weeps. Seeps open along every joint. Water stands in every hollow and runs, sheet-thin and cold, over ground that never quite dries. Rain is not an event. It is the air's ordinary condition, interrupted by harder rain.

And still the growth is almost indecent. Cold does not forbid it here; it only changes its manners. Mats of moss thick as bedding cover every surface that holds still for a week. Sedges and horsetail stand in the shallows in ranks so tight you cannot put a hand between them. On the slightly higher bars—higher meaning a handspan—low shrubs knit into a continuous pelt, leaves small, dark, waxy, built to drink what little light leaks through the overcast and to shed the rest as water. In the standing pools the water is tea-colored and full, not empty: algal films, submerged ribbons, the pale roots of things that prefer to feed from below. The young minerals in the rock feed all of it. Nothing has been leached yet. The basin is rich the way a wound is rich, and the plants know it.

Across this, life moves in numbers that make the eye refuse to settle. Flocks lift off the sedge in sheets and settle again a hundred paces on, driven by whatever the water just did. Waders work the new mud in crowds. Something heavy and low-bodied crosses a gravel tongue and vanishes into scrub without ever showing you a clean outline—only the disturbance, a wave traveling through the stems. In the channels, fish or fish-shaped things boil at the surface whenever a bank slumps and dumps insects and silt together. You do not get individuals. You get weather made of animals. They are ordinary animals, as far as you can tell: no extra limbs, no light of their own. They are simply everywhere, and they are loud, and they do not pause for you.

The sound is the true climate. Water on water, water on fresh rock, wind in a canopy that never opens, the slap of wings, the tear of a cutbank letting go, the thin high cries of whatever is nesting in the scrub and the lower, gut-deep calls from the pools. None of it takes a turn. It stacks. At the open center of the basin—there is a center, a broader reach of floodplain maybe a morning's walk across—the noise becomes a pressure in the teeth. Speech is a gesture. You point.

Scattered through that reach, never crowded, never absent for long, are the works. They are not ruins and they are not towns. A pylon like a needle driven into a bar, no higher than the shrubs once the mist closes around it. A low disk seated in a pool, its rim flush with the water, the pool around it clearing and clouding on a rhythm that is not the rain's. A tracery of filament, almost invisible until it loads with droplets, strung from ridge to ridge where a channel is trying to jump its banks; when the filament takes the strain you hear a tone under the rest of the noise, clean, not mechanical in any way you have a word for, and the bank holds a little longer than it should. None of these things look tended. None of them look abandoned. They are doing something to the water table, to the load the young stone can bear, to the way silt settles and lifts. You cannot see the mechanism. You can see the argument they are having with the basin, and the basin is winning often enough that the works have to be many, and loud in their own register: a subsonic correcting, a hiss of pressure bled off, a crack like lake-ice when a filament takes a shock the rock was about to take instead.

Walk and the scale opens without ever emptying. The scrub does not thin. The flocks do not thin. Another needle, another disk, another reach of black water braiding around a rib of stone that was not there, you are almost sure, when you looked up an hour ago—or was, and the light was too poor to separate it from its reflection. The horizon is a rumor. What you trust is nearer: the suck of mud, the cold climbing your legs, the green so dense it has a temperature of its own, a half-degree warmer than the air where the mats are thickest, and the continuous fact of water moving under, through, and over everything that claims to be ground.

Nothing here is uncanny. That is the part that takes longest to accept. The dark is weather. The cold is latitude, or season, or both. The machines are machines, however far past your own shop they were built. The stone is young because something—uplift, a shift in the river's allegiance, a shore that decided to become inland—happened recently, and the basin has not finished answering. It is answering now, all at once, at the top of its voice: banks failing, bars being born, plants throwing themselves at every new surface, animals following the failure because failure is food, and the scattered works tightening, easing, tightening again, trying to keep a valley-sized sheet of wet country from tearing itself into a different valley before whatever they were set here to hold can be held.

You stop on a rib of rock no wider than a path. Water runs over your boots and does not care. Downstream a whole mat of shrubs detaches with a sound like cloth ripping underwater and begins its slow turn in the current. Upstream the tone of the filaments drops a note, and a cutbank that had been bright with fresh clay stops shedding, for a minute, maybe two. The flocks go up. The rain thickens. The place rearranges itself around the fact of you and does not record it. By the time you look back, the way you came has already started to mean something else.
