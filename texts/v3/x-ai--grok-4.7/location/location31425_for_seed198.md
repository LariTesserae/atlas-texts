The valley was made before anyone living can remember the making, and it has spent the time since settling into that fact. From the upper lip of the rim road you see the whole of it: a long highland basin, open enough that weather crosses it in one motion, packed so tightly that the motion has almost nowhere to land. Cool air. A sky the color of wet slate, low enough that the far ridges blur into it. No sun to speak of. Light arrives already used, a thin gray that makes distances honest and colors quieter than they are.

Everything underfoot is built. There is no bare ground left in the basin, and if there ever was, the work covered it so thoroughly and so long ago that the covering has become the ground. Concrete terraces step down the old slopes in broad, weathered flights. Between them run service roads of poured stone, their edges rounded by decades of tires and rain. Retaining walls of dressed block, stained tea-color where water has been finding the same joints since the walls were young. Metal walkways bolted over channels. Rooflines of low factories, glasshouses, pump halls, and the long sheds where something is sorted, dried, packed, and sent on. The materials have stopped looking new. They look like what they are: a landscape that has been rained on, cooled, and walked until the construction and the climate agreed on a shape.

Water is everywhere and never dramatic. It does not fall in spectacle so much as refuse to leave. A film on every horizontal surface. Seepage at the foot of every wall, darkening the lowest course of block and then the course above it. Gutters run even when the sky is only threatening. In the channels between terraces the water moves with a low, constant purpose, brown-clear, carrying bits of leaf and the fine grit that concrete gives up when it has been wet long enough. On the north faces of the larger halls, moisture beads and runs in slow threads. Standing near a pump house you can hear it: not a roar, a ticking, the sound of drip finding drip. Higher up, where the rim catches cloud, the air itself is wet enough that railings sweat and your sleeves darken without rain having properly begun.

The vegetation is not wild in the old sense, because there is nowhere wild left to be. It is lush anyway. It grows in the only places the built world left it, and those places turned out to be many. Planter troughs along every terrace, originally for windbreak and drainage control, now solid with cool-climate growth: thick grasses, broad dock-like leaves, shrubs that have been cut back so often they have learned to be dense rather than tall. Ferns in the joints of walls. Moss so established it reads as a surface, not a guest, greening the shaded sides of cisterns and the undersides of walkways. Climbing stems on guy-wires and downspouts, kept from the working faces of buildings and allowed everywhere else. In the glasshouses — and there are rows of them, fogged, ribbed, slightly sagging in their frames — the green is heavier, warmer by a few degrees, leaves pressed to the panes from inside. Outside, the palette stays subdued: deep greens, olive, the black-green of wet bark, the occasional pale flower that looks accidental and probably is not. Nothing here is ornamental in a careless way. The plants are infrastructure that forgot, slightly, to stay only infrastructure. They transpire. They hold the soil that was trucked in and then became soil. They slow the water just enough that the channels do not scour.

It is cool in the way high country is cool when the sun cannot get through. Not bitter. A temperature that lives in metal and in stone longer than in air, so that a handrail in the afternoon is no warmer than it was at dawn, and breath shows faintly when you stop walking. The elevation is enough that the basin feels above the weather of the lowlands and inside its own: cloud caught on the rim, a steady coolness, air that tastes of wet concrete and cut green stems. People dress for it without thinking. Collars up. Gloves in pockets even when they are not worn.

Animals are scarce, and the scarcity feels structural rather than tragic. There is little for them that has not already been assigned. You might see a bird on a ridge vent, gone before you are sure of the species. Insects enough to keep the flowers honest, not enough to make a sound of their own. No flocks. No scampering in the walls. The ecology that thrives is photosynthetic and human. Everything else passes through or does not come.

What moves is the work, and it moves constantly.

From the rim the basin can look still, because the stillness is visual distance and because almost none of the motion makes a large noise. Come down one terrace and the quiet turns out to be a choice the place made, or had made for it: electric drives instead of combustion, belts under housings, water doing the heavy carrying, walls and mist swallowing what sound remains. Close to a shed door you hear a hum, a clank spaced far apart, voices kept low because the lanes are narrow and everyone is already where they need to be. Further along, a sluice gate lifts and the channel beside you deepens by a handspan without announcement. Steam from a vent walks sideways in the damp air and is gone against a glasshouse roof. A shift ends in one hall and begins in the next; the foot traffic on the connecting walk does not surge so much as rethread, the same density rearranged.

That is the turbulence, and it does not stop. Gates, levels, routes, which house is venting, which terrace is taking runoff after the cloud thickens, which line is running and which is being cleared. Carts on the mid-level roads, never empty long, never loud. Workers in the planters cutting back growth that would close a drain by evening if left. A crane servicing a roof two districts over, its movement small against the scale of the basin and continuous if you watch. Water redirected from one holding tank to another because a glasshouse row is heavy with condensate and the north channels are already high. The place does not rest between these adjustments. It is always mid-correction. An anthill's busyness, except the hill is a valley, and the ants built the hill, and then the rain and the plants and the decades finished the surfaces until construction and weather could no longer be cleanly told apart.

Walk it and the openness and the packing stop contradicting each other. The basin is large — you can see across to the far industrial terraces, a second town's worth of roofs and glazing dissolved in mist, a rail line stitched along the lower contour, stacks that do not smoke so much as breathe a paler gray into gray. That is the scale: regional, several kinds of work visible at once, weather shared. But the path you are on has no spare width. A wall. A channel. A trough of leaves brushing your sleeve. Another wall. Someone coming the other way steps into a recess made for that purpose, because the recess was planned when the path was poured. Above you a walkway. Below you another, and the sound of water you cannot see. Every view along the terrace is blocked at twenty paces by a corner, a tank, a stand of shrubbery trained against a frame. The horizon exists. The immediate world does not offer any of it. You move through a fully occupied section of a larger fully occupied section, and the only unoccupied thing is the sky, which is occupied by cloud.

Nothing here is strange. The rules are ordinary ones, obeyed at length. Water runs downhill and is caught. Concrete weathers and is not replaced until it must be. Plants grow where water and a crack allow, and are cut where a crack cannot be afforded. People keep the machines running because the machines keep the water, the heat in the glasshouses, the roads, and the wages in order. Light stays poor because the elevation holds cloud and the buildings hold shade. Quiet stays, not because nothing is happening, but because what is happening was built not to shout, and the wet surfaces finish the job.

By late day the gray thins without ever becoming sun. Condensation on the north walls slows. A gate somewhere upstream changes position and, minutes later, the channel at your feet changes its note by a fraction. In the glasshouses the lamps some rows use — sparse, practical, not enough to push light out into the basin — come on as small warm rectangles, then haze over again as the panes fog. On the rim road a vehicle passes, tires on damp stone, and is gone. The terraces keep their levels. The leaves keep their water. The work keeps moving through a place that has already, long since, become the only ground there is.
