The city sits on a shelf of the world that has already finished becoming itself. Whatever mountains once stood here were ground down so long ago that the plateau no longer remembers them as forms; it remembers them as a grain. The ground is a warm, fine mineral, the color of old iron where any light finds it, and elsewhere only a temperature underfoot: not hot enough to blister, not cool enough to promise night. It has been this temperature for longer than the buildings. The air does not move in weather. It settles, a dry weight, and stays.

There is no water and no memory of water that the stone will admit. What would have been riverbeds are shallow, parallel troughs, planed until their walls are only a change in the pitch of the walking. Salt has bloomed in them and then been abraded back into the same dust as everything else. In places the dust has sintered into plates, glassy at the edges, where some older heat passed through and left. You can follow those plates for a long time. They do not lead to basins. They lead to more plate.

Above the shelf the dark is not a sky so much as an absence that has thickness. Nothing falls out of it. Stars, if they are there, do not declare themselves; the eye gives up and begins to invent faint seams where the dark is merely less convinced of itself. Down on the ground, industry supplies what light there is, and it supplies almost none. A furnace-glow leaking under a door. A filament in a tower that has burned at the same dull orange since before anyone now living was assigned to watch it. The light does not travel. It pools, and a few paces off it is gone, and the warm dark resumes.

The city is real, and large, and mostly empty of closeness. Streets were laid on a grid that the erosion has not quite erased, though it has softened every corner into a radius. Blocks open onto blocks. Between them the distances are civic rather than intimate: you can see, when a filament allows it, the next mass as a shoulder of black against a blacker field, and know it is a hall, a stack, a foundry shed, and know also that reaching it will take the time of a walk across open ground. The buildings are low. Height was tried, once, and the wind that used to live here took the upper stories down to the same plane as the plateau, so what remains is the part that was always willing to lie flat. Walls of fired mineral, of sintered dust, of metal that has oxidized into a skin continuous with the ground. Roofs are lids. Doors are set flush. Nothing casts a long shadow because nothing rises far and because there is almost nothing to cast it with.

Inside the works the machines have reached the condition the plateau already knew. They run. They have always been running, in the sense that matters: not cycling, not starting and stopping, but held at a single rate that does not accumulate or decay. Belts move at the speed of cooling metal. Presses descend and rise on a period so long it does not read as rhythm unless you stay. What they make is not stored in heaps. Output and input have been balanced until the factories are less producers than organs, keeping a chemistry that is not water's. Vats hold oils that never were alive, solvents the color of the dark, powders that drink heat and give back a slower heat. Pipes sweat nothing. Valves are welded at the aperture they settled into. The sound of all this, heard from the street, is a pressure more than a noise: a low, even presence in the jaw, gone when you step into a trough and the ground takes it.

No green thing has ever taken here, or if it did, the record was milled away with the mountains. There is no soil in the sense that holds a seed. There is only the dust, and under the dust the plates, and under the plates a warmth that comes up without steam. Roots would find no purchase and no drink. What passes for growth is accretion: a lintel thickened by a century of the same warm air laying mineral on mineral until the opening is narrower than the builders cut it. No one chips it back. The narrowing is the correct width now.

Life is not absent. It is rare enough that meeting it rearranges the walk. In the lee of a foundry wall, where the glow under the door makes a small climate, something the size of a hand will sometimes be fixed to the metal, not climbing, not feeding in any way that looks like feeding. It is a crust with a slower pulse than the machines, a animal only by the fact that if you wait long enough it is not where it was, having shifted by the width of itself toward a warmer seam. There are others, fewer, higher up on the stacks: pale hooks that drink the dry air and do nothing else visible. They do not call. They do not flock. A city of this size might hold a few hundred of them, scattered so widely that most streets have none, and a person can cross a district and believe the place is only mineral and work.

People, or whatever keeps the assignments, live in the same sparsity. Quarters are cut into the thicker walls, one lamp to a room, the lamp allowed because a body still needs to find its own hands. Food, if that is the word, comes from the vats: a paste that never dries in the mouth and never wets it either, measured in the same unchanging portions. No market gathers. No bell marks a shift, because the shift does not turn over. Someone walks the grid because the grid is there to be walked, checking that a filament still burns, that a belt has not learned a new speed. They do not hurry. Hurry would imply a state other than this one.

The strange part is not a monster at the edge. It is the way the city agrees with the plateau too well. Stand in an intersection and the four streets decline at angles that are not quite the angles of a made thing; they are the angles of a surface that has been settling toward flat since before the first course of brick, and the streets have settled with it, so that a block which was square is now a shallow dish, and the dish does not drain because there is nothing to drain. Compass needles, where anyone still carries one, rest. They do not spin. They rest at a heading that is local and sufficient. Time behaves the same way. A clock on a foundry wall, glass long gone, shows a hand that has not been seen to move, and the workers do not wind it, and the work continues at the rate the unwound clock would have described if it had been designed to describe a single hour forever.

Farther out, past the last lid-roof, the shelf continues. The grid fades into troughs. The troughs fade into plates. The plates go on until the dark takes the distinction between ground and not-ground, and still the warmth is underfoot, the same warmth, as if the city were not an exception on the highland but the highland's way of having joints and doors. Nothing out there changes into weather. Nothing grows up to meet you. If you walk until the last filament is only a rumor in the side of your eye, the sound in the jaw fades with it, and what remains is the soft tick of dust against a boot, regular as a machine that has finally become indistinguishable from the ground it stands on.

You can turn around. The city will be where you left it, at the same glow, at the same rate, the rare crusts shifted by a finger's width toward seams that have not cooled. It will not have missed you. It does not have a mode for missing. It has the mode it reached when the mountains finished lying down, and the factories, arriving late, had the courtesy to match it.
