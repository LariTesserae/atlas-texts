The way in is not a door. High on the outer mass, where the built ridge has been weather long enough to pass for a hill, a seam has opened along a joint the masons never meant to leave. You have to turn sideways. The stone—if it is stone—takes the warmth of a hand and gives a little, the way dense foam does, then firms again. Beyond the turn the passage drops its shoulder and you are in the room.

It is not large. A person could cross it in six steps if the floor were clear, which it is not. The ceiling comes down in a shallow vault, close enough that the warm air pools against it and condenses before it can rise away. Elevation has done what elevation does: outside, the wind is thin and the light is a hard high glare off pale constructed slopes. In here the pressure feels wrong for the height, heavier, wetter, as if the room were holding a borrowed climate against the mountain’s wish. Something in the walls is doing that work. You cannot see the mechanism. You can feel the slight, continuous give of it under the noise, a pulse too slow to be a pump and too regular to be weather.

The noise is the first fact and the last. It does not arrive from a point. It occupies the volume the way water occupies a cup, already there when you enter, already against the teeth. At first it reads as rain on a metal roof, then as a mill, then as a rookery. None of those hold. What you are hearing is a great many small bodies striking, drinking, and answering one another inside a space too tight to let any sound die. The walls return it. The water film on every surface returns it. After a minute the separate clicks and sips blur into a single pressure, and speech, if you tried it, would be a shape in the mouth and nothing in the air.

Light is poor and local. No window. A few seams in the vault glow the color of old resin, not bright enough to throw a shadow across the room, only enough to pick out wet edges. Most of what you know, you know by the change in the sound when something moves between you and a wall, and by the warmth, which is steady and close, never quite hot. The air smells of mineral and of a sweet, thin rot, the smell of film rather than of soil. There is no soil. Every surface was made: plates laminated and sintered, ribs poured, a floor of overlapping tongues whose joints have been worried open by water until they look like the bedding of a soft rock. The makers’ geometry is still in it—repeats, radii, a preference for the curve that sheds rather than the angle that catches—but water has had the longer vote. Channels run where channels wanted to run. A lip that was meant to throw drip clear of a corner now feeds a dark crescent pool no deeper than a wrist. The pool has been there so long its rim is polished, and the things that use it have worn a pale road along one side.

They are everywhere, and they are small. Nothing in the room is large enough to be mistaken for a person. They crowd the wet plates in layers: a lower film of near-flat bodies the color of wet slate, almost indistinguishable from the water until one lifts and shows a pale underside; above them, on the vertical, a denser stipple of creatures no bigger than a thumb, clinging, sipping, dropping back into the runnels; in the air itself, for a hand’s breadth above the floor and again just under the vault, moving sheets of still smaller ones, wingless or nearly, riding the updraft off the warm plates and falling out of it in soft dark curtains. They do not scatter from you so much as re-form around you. Step, and the floor-layer parts with a sound like dry seed poured into a tin. Stand still, and it closes to the edge of your boots. They do not bite. They investigate moisture. A wet cuff is a resource. They mouth it, find no film worth keeping, and leave it.

Vegetation is almost an absence, which makes the few plants more legible than a forest would be. In three joints, and only three, pale fans have seated themselves: not leaves exactly, more a radiating mat of flattened tubes, yellow-white, no taller than a spread hand. They do not cover ground. They occupy cracks the way a signature occupies a corner of a page. Water beads on them and does not sink in; whatever they take, they take from the joint beneath, from whatever slow nutrient the walls still bleed after all this time. The animals do not graze them. They avoid the fans by a margin, a small bare ring in the otherwise continuous carpet, as if the plants were either poison or a rival machine. One fan has a torn edge, old, scarred over in the same yellow-white. Nothing here is young in the way a garden is young. The plants look installed and then forgotten, and then not forgotten by the water.

The water’s habit is weeping, not flooding. It comes out of the plates themselves. Stand near a wall and you can watch a bead form in a pore too fine to see except by the shine, swell, and start down a track shinier than the stone around it. The tracks are established. They do not wander. A new drip follows the path of the last ten thousand, even where a cleaner route seems obvious. At the vault, the condensation is faster. Drops gather on the shallow curve and fall in a warm, irregular rain onto the animals, which flare and drink and flare again. The rain is part of the noise. So are the bodies. So, underneath both, is a low tonal component you only separate out when you press a palm to the wall and feel it in the bone: the room singing in a register the animals are answering, or driving, or both.

It is hard to say which is servant. The abundance is too exact to be accident and too messy to be a display. There are dead among the living—husks in the crescent pool, pale and hollow, nudged to the downstream lip and held there by the current until they soften and go out through a gap at floor level, a drain that was cut as a circle and has become a soft oval. Nothing piles up. The film on the plates is consumed about as fast as it forms. The warmth never spikes. If you watch long enough you see a slow exchange: a patch of wall dulls as its weep falters, the animals thin there, and somewhere else a seam brightens and the crowd thickens over it, drinking the new surplus, their combined small impacts ticking louder on that plate until the tone under your hand shifts by a fraction and the first patch wets again. A load being moved. A balance the builders may have intended and that time has since edited, the way a river edits a channel and then becomes unable to leave it.

The unusual thing is not that the room is alive. It is that the life is so nearly a finish on the architecture. No burrows. No nests that last. The animals carry nothing, store nothing, mark nothing except by wear. Their abundance is a surface condition, dense as lichen, loud as a falls, and when one lifts from the wall there is always another under it, and under that the plate, still made, still warm, still deciding to be wet. You begin to understand the dimness as a choice that has lasted: light enough for the resin-seams to show a person the way back to the seam, not enough to dry the vault or to drive the upper sheets of animals into some other habit. Outside, on the high constructed slope, the day is loud in the ordinary way of wind. In here the loudness has a job. Stop your ears with your fingers and the tone in the wall does not stop. It leans, faintly, as if missing the upper register of strikes, and a bead that was about to fall hesitates on the vault, swells past its usual size, and breaks late.

You should not stay long enough to learn whether that hesitation matters. The room does not threaten. It includes. Warmth, wet film, the polite mouthing of any damp cloth, the press of sound that turns your own pulse into a rumor. The way out is the same sideways seam, now marked by a thread of cooler, thinner air that the animals will not cross. They pile at that threshold in a soft dark lip, drinking what condenses where the two climates touch, and they do not follow. Behind you the noise remains entire, undiminished by your leaving, as if the room had never needed a listener and had only, for a while, contained one.
