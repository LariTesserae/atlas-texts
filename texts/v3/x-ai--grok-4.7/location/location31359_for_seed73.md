The first thing the height does is fail to thin the air. You come up through the last of the cloud-sea—an ocean of slow white, laid out below the rim for as far as the eye will go—and the shelf above it should be a place of cold, spare breathing, of wind that strips the mouth. It is not. The air on the benches is thick, sweet-heavy, and hot in the way of a closed room after rain: it sits against the skin and does not hurry off. Walking it, you sweat at once. The light is ordinary daylight, a little honeyed by the moisture, with real shadows and a sun you can find. Nothing about the brightness explains the heat. The heat is in the ground, and in the weight of what you breathe.

The plateau is not a peak. It is a region: a run of hanging shelves and shallow basins, open enough that you can see the next shoulder and the one after, a town’s-worth of country laid out without a town, maybe a long day’s walk from the near rim to where the land folds down into a farther trough you cannot quite read. The forms are old and settled. Cliffs hold a scalloped profile, as if they agreed on it centuries ago and then stopped arguing. Talus has greened over and stayed. Streams have found their beds and kept them. Nothing here is in the act of becoming a ruin of itself. It is already the shape it means to be.

Water is everywhere and nowhere deep. It does not carve. It weeps. Black rock faces wear a constant film, beading, gathering, letting go in threads no thicker than a finger, and the threads never quite dry between showers. The showers come as a habit rather than a weather-event—brief, warm, already expected by the leaves. Between them the air itself condenses. On the shaded side of a boulder you can watch a skin of water form out of nothing, brighten, and slide. Shallow basins hold tea-colored marsh a hand deep, warm as bathwater, their bottoms sealed by something paler than mud: a mineral crust, faintly iridescent where a foot breaks it, knitting shut again behind you with a patience you would miss if you watched. Drainage is a courtesy the land extends, not a necessity. The water stays near the surface because the surface has been taught, long ago, not to swallow.

The vegetation takes that refusal and spends it. Lush does not mean jungle-dark. The shelves are open to the sky, and the growth is layered rather than vaulted: knee-high sedges in the wet, then waist-high broadleaf that smells of pepper and wet copper when crushed, then islands of trees with pale, spongy bark and leaves the size of platters, angled to shed the condensate in a steady tick. On the weeping walls a second forest grows sideways—moss in blankets, then straps of fern, then shrubs rooted in cracks that should not hold a shrub, their roots braided into the film of water as if drinking from a tablecloth. Growth is slow. A snapped stem beads sap and does not rush to scar. Rings in a cut trunk are hairline-fine, packed past counting. The abundance is the abundance of things that have not been asked to start over.

Life is loud in it, and crowded, and unhurried. The marsh shelves click and pipe and thrum. Finger-long waders the color of wet clay stalk the sedge and do not flush until you are near enough to see the double lid on the eye. Something colonial—part insect, part floating lace—lifts off the water in sheets when the light shifts, drifts a few spans, and settles again without panic. In the pepper-leaf, flocks keep up a bright, overlapping chatter that carries farther than it should; you hear a flock you cannot see, and then, a little late, the same phrase from a flock you can. Herds of low, heat-sleek grazers move across the drier shoulders in lines, cropping, pausing, cropping. They are not scarce. They also do not run. The whole register is of a place with many mouths and no emergency. Even the loudness has a settled quality: the same calls, the same water-tick, the same soft clap of platter-leaves tipping their drink, hour after hour, as if the score were written and the musicians faithful.

What keeps the air thick is not visible as a machine, and then, here and there, it is. The works are scattered, never a district of them—faint marks, partial remains, the occasional thing that is still doing its job without announcing the fact. A ridge-spine of dark glass, no higher than a person, half-swallowed by sedge, sweats harder than the rock around it; stand near it and the damp on your arms cools a fraction, as if something were drawing heat off you and folding it back into the bench. In one basin a ring of pale posts, waist-high, weathered to the same scallop as the cliffs, and inside the ring the marsh is an hand deeper and the plants a shade larger. No inscription. A single tower on a far shoulder, narrow, slightly wrong in its shadow: the shadow arrives a blink after the light would prefer, and lies a little aside from the sun. Birds nest in its upper courses and do not mind. Lower, where a path might once have met it, the stone has been eased by roots into the same vocabulary as the talus. Whatever raised this country, or weighed its air, or sealed its floors against the thirst of height, has been here long enough to be geology. The engineering that current accounts cannot quite name has finished its argument with the weather and entered the balance. You do not find a control. You find a place that still obeys an instruction too old to be read as one.

There are rules, once you have walked enough shelves to feel them repeat. Water may climb a vein of the pale crust for a while—thin, uphill, purposeful—and then it must fall, and it does, politely, into the next basin. Breath stays easy no matter how far above the cloud-sea you are; the weight in the lungs is local, and it ends, sharply, if you climb down through the rim into the white. Nothing large hurries. A grazer startled will trot, then remember itself, then graze. Erosion has a floor: the scalloped cliffs shed grains, and the grains do not accumulate into a new shape; they are taken back into the film, into the crust, into the slow stitch. Night, when it comes, is a normal night—stars, a moon if the season has one, the heat easing but not leaving, the condensate thickening until every leaf carries a lens. The sounds do not stop. They thin, and overlap, and continue, the way a held note continues in a room that has agreed to hold it.

At the scale of the region the features declare themselves without drama. A marsh shelf wide enough to lose a person in the sedge, loud with the sheet-insects and the double-lidded waders. A weeping wall you can walk beside for an hour, the sideways forest ticking onto your shoulder. An open shoulder of shorter grass where the herds line out and the air moves at last, hot still, but moving, and from which you can see three basins, the glass spine, the wrong-shadowed tower, and the cloud-sea’s far bright edge. A trough beyond that, greener, narrower, where the platter-trees knit into something almost continuous and the sound changes register—deeper, more echo, still patient. No road. A few worn lines where hooves and feet have agreed, over a great many slow years, on the driest thread between wet places.

If you stay, the strangeness stops presenting itself as an exception and becomes the manner of the country. Heat at a height that should starve it. Water that will not dig. Forests that drink from walls and from the air. Animals in numbers, calling across distances the humidity both softens and extends. A handful of remnants still weighting the sky, sealing the floor, lagging their own shadows, and otherwise letting the lichens have them. The light remains the light you know. The day proceeds. The place does not perform. It has already arrived at itself, and it is in no hurry to be anything further—only wet, and green, and loud, and high, and warm, and old.
