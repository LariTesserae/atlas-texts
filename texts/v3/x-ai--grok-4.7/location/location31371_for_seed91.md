The cut is newer than the cold in it. You come down off the original ground by a ramp whose edges are still angular, drill-lines and blast scars unrounded, and the air changes before the light does: damper, heavier, and already loud. The hollow is not a cave in the old sense. It is a valley that has been lowered—benches, headings, and one long open floor sunk below the country around it—so that the surface you left is now a rim, and everything you walk is under that rim’s shadow even at midday.

The noise is the first complete fact. It does not rise and fall with weather. It is a steady industrial roar, close to deafening, seated somewhere past the next bench and multiplied by the young walls. Pumps. A ventilation set. The particular metal shout of water being lifted and dropped again through pipe. Speech is possible only if you put your mouth near an ear, and even then you give up after a sentence. The place has learned to be described by gesture.

Underfoot is not spoil and not pavement. It is a living sheet: a cold mat of moss, compacted sedge, and a thin peat that has been laid, or has laid itself, across the fresh floor and up the toes of the cuts. It gives slightly. It holds damp in lenses—standing film in a hollow, a darker seam where seepage pearls out of a joint and vanishes again into the mat—while the broader ground stays only wet to the touch, never a river, never dry. The water is intermittent and local. You can go a long while between pockets, then find a black pool no wider than a cart, skimmed with cold, reflecting nothing because the light is too poor and the surface too still.

The plants are the architecture, and they are not crowded. Great columnar growths stand at wide intervals across the open floor and along the benches: cold-country stems, thick at the base, pale bark or no bark worth the name, branches beginning high and carrying a sparse, dark leafage that never knits into canopy. Between them the space is empty enough to see across—town-width, then valley-width—gallery after gallery of the same sunk ground, the same spaced trunks, the same sharp rock lips where the excavation is still younger than the growth. Roots have been encouraged, or have simply taken the chance, to bind the new faces. In places a wall is more root-weave and hung moss than stone, but the stone’s geometry remains: recent, planar, unsoftened. Where a trunk meets the mat, the living mass thickens into a low plinth. You do not walk on soil with plants in it. You walk on plant, and the mineral world shows only where the machines have cut faster than the mat can follow.

Light is dim without being night. The rim eclipses most of the sky. What comes down is gray, flat, and a little blue with cold. Lamps exist, but they are few and industrial—caged bulbs on short standards, a sodium wash at a pump gallery—and they do not try to illuminate the hollow. They mark work. Between them the spaced trunks read as darker verticals in an already dark field, and the damp pockets take what little light there is and keep it.

The built things are scattered and partial. A pump house of corrugated metal, one wall buckled, its intake lost under the mat. A run of conveyor that ends in air, rollers still, framework orange with a rust that has not yet had decades. Cable trays along a bench, some live, ticking faintly if you set a hand on them, though you cannot hear the tick. A concrete pad with bolt-holes and no machine. Farther off, where the roar clarifies into separate sources, a low works: engines in a shed half-sunk against the cut, ducting disappearing into headings, a stack that does not smoke so much as exhale a wet warmth that dies within a few meters in the cold. No town. No roads worthy of the name—only the ramp, a service track pressed into the mat, and the faint geometry of older survey marks painted on stone the plants have not yet taken.

Fauna is almost an absence you notice by contrast. The noise and the cold and the newness have left little room. Once, a small dark bird crosses a gap between trunks and is gone, too quick to count as a population. In a seep, a few larvae dimple the film. On a metal flange, a scatter of pale moth-wings, settled and not flying. Nothing herds, nothing calls in a way that could compete with the machines. The ecology you can actually read is plant and water and machine: the mat drinking the seep, the pumps drinking the sumps, the spaced trunks using the dimness and the damp and ignoring the roar because they have no ears.

Change, when you look for it, is slow. The engines hold one note. The seeps migrate by centimeters across a season, not by floods. Frost needles the mat at the margins and lets go without drama; the cold here is the condition, not an event—a steady chill that lives in the below-grade air and in the metal and in the black pools, never warming into weather you could wait out. The sharp lips of the cuts will round, but not while you watch. A new heading shows raw stone, tool-fresh, and beside it a heading from the previous campaign already wearing a first skin of green. The difference between them is the only clock the place offers that is not the clock of maintenance: someone still comes, rarely, to keep a pump from seizing, to clear a intake the mat is trying to close. Their marks are small—a cleared square, a propped duct, a boot-path that the moss is already narrowing.

At the scale of the hollow you stop thinking of rooms. The floor runs on past the works, past another cluster of trunks, toward a far bench that is only a change in the gray. Side headings open and do not announce themselves. Above, on the rim you can no longer see from the lowest floor, the original country continues—colder wind, ordinary sparse cover, a world that does not know it is loud down here. The rules below are simple and physical. Sound does not rest. Water does not travel far before it is taken up, by mat or by pipe. Stone stays sharp until the plants are given years. Nothing hunts you; nothing guides you. If you stop, the cold settles into your clothes and the roar settles into your skull, and the place continues its long, patient work of becoming a forest of spaced columns on a living floor, inside a valley that was cut, and has not yet begun to forget the cutting.
