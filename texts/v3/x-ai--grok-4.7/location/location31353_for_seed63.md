The height arrives first as a thinning, not as a view. Air has less to say for itself. Sound thins with it. What you take, at the shoulder of the rise, for the ordinary cold of altitude lasts only as long as the wind can reach you—and the wind stops at a lip of old glass, as if it has learned the boundary and keeps it.

Beyond the lip the cold is a lie told by the sky.

The place is small. You could walk its long wall in a few hundred paces if the wall would hold you and the floor would let you, and you cannot see the far side well enough to want to try. An overhung rim circles it, a slumped oval of welded stone, cathedral in the vertical and hamlet in the plan: tall enough that the dark has room to stand up in, closed enough that the dark is the room. The sky is a slit, hard and small and useless. Light comes down as a rumor and dies in the first fathom of air. What illuminates nothing still shows you, if you look back, how high you are—the slit is the color of altitude, thin blue gone nearly black, and the heat under it is the color of nothing.

The stone has been this shape for a long time. Nothing here is fresh. The glass is not the glass of a new wound; it is clouded, pitted, peeled in scales that have peeled before. Edges that once were sharp have gone to a grained roundness, and then stopped, as if rounding had reached the angle the place prefers and kept it. Salt has bloomed and cracked and bloomed again in the same seams, a white that reads gray in the dark, never deep, never wet. There is no water. There is the memory of water only in the way certain crusts mimic what water does to stone, and even that memory has been revised so many times it no longer refers to anything. A drop, if one were brought here, would not lie long enough to be seen. The salt and the heat would take it into mineral before it found a low place. The low places are already full.

Nothing grows. What first resembles a lichen-map on the walls is a chemistry, not a plant: a dry film, rust-dark and sulfur-pale, bound into the crust and fed by what the fractures breathe. The fractures do not glow. They exhale a metal gas, alkali and brimstone, hot enough to lift the film and never wet enough to fog a surface. You smell it before you understand the shape of the basin—worked iron, struck flint, a sweetness like heated bone that is not sweet. The film is the only plenty the stone offers. Everything else that lives here lives on that, or on what lives on that, or on the heat itself, which the film drinks as other lives drink light.

The floor is not floor.

It is bodies, and then bodies under those, and the stone only by implication, the way a riverbed is implied by the river. They are packed past the idea of gaps. Plate-backed, low, dusted in the same silica that dulls the glass, they overlap in a continuous scale, three and four deep where the basin settles, higher where they have climbed one another along the warmer seams. A hand set down would not find ground. It would find the slow give of a living surface that does not cry out and does not scatter. They do not scatter. There is nowhere to scatter to. The walls are dressed in them too, hanging clusters thick as moss would be if moss could endure this, and the clusters are not moss. Brush one and it returns, not by intent you can see, but by the pressure of the mass behind it. The dark is not empty dark. It is occupied dark, saturated, a volume in which every hand's-breadth has already been taken.

They are quiet because the place is built to eat sound and because they have no need to spend it. The glass is porous under its glaze. The bodies are dry and close-fitted. A thousand small shifts, a change of grip, the adjustment of a plate over a plate, all of it vanishes into the mass the way a word vanishes into cloth. What you hear, if you wait, is not them. A scale of stone lets go somewhere on the rim with a tick no louder than a cooling stove. The gas finds a new thread in a seam and the pitch of the smell changes before any hiss does. Your own pulse becomes the loudest citizen. Under that, if you are still enough, a lower thing: not quite sound, a slow shove in the soles, the basin breathing on a cycle longer than a breath and shorter than a watch.

They move with the breath.

The heat is not even, and it does not stay. It pulses from the seams on a rhythm the stone has kept so long the life has shaped itself to it. When a fracture warms, the mass leans. Not a stampede—there is no room for a stampede, and no sound for one. A displacement. Bodies on the hot line go rigid and climb into temporary columns, stacked, mineral-skinned, so still they pass for dripstone until the pulse peaks and the column unknots from the top down, each animal taking the warmth and passing the excess sideways. When the seam cools, the columns slump and the tide leans toward the next waking fracture, a slow rotation around the basin's hottest throat, clockwise for hours, then—not randomly, but by a rule you would need days to trust—reversed. Individuals join the wheel and leave it. The wheel does not notice. It is not a mind. It is a balance that has already been found, erosion and accretion, grazing and being grazed, arrival and replacement, all settled into a form that no longer hurries to become something else.

They do not drink. There is nothing to drink, and their bodies are finished with the idea. Moisture is a wound in the chemistry of the air. What water they carry is bound, locked, not for spending. They graze the film with mouthparts that are almost combs, almost files, lifting the mineral bloom and the life in it, and where the film has been shaved the stone shows paler for a little while and then clouds back, the breath of the seams painting it on again. In the deeper stacks, where light never mattered and heat is a rumor from above, others do not graze. They wait inside the mass the way larger fish wait in murk. You know them by the partings. The scale-layer opens along a line no wider than a wrist, closes, opens elsewhere. Something low and longer moves through, not fast, and the packed bodies seal behind it without a rustle, already replacing what was taken. Predator and prey are not a drama here. They are a pace. The numbers hold. They have held.

The strange part is not their number. It is what they treat as direction. Eyes, where you can make one out in the slit-glow—a dull bead, a pinhole—do not search the dark. The whole animal searches heat. A cool place is an event. Stand on the mass and you are a cold well in a hot room; they do not flee it so much as revise themselves around it. Some press to the boundary of your chill to work the gradient, as they press to the seams. Some will not cross the patch of stone your shadow would have cooled, if your shadow could reach the floor. Lie still and the wheel deforms around you, a slow eddy, and resumes its older circle once you are no warmer and no colder than the crust. They were never looking at you. Looking is not the sense this world is organized by.

There are marks that do not join the wheel.

They are few, and faint, and the place has not bothered to finish them or to erase them. On the western wall, just above the highest regular climb of the clusters, three stones have been set on edge in a short arc, fitted, knee-high, older than the salt bloom that has tried them and failed to topple them. The bodies flow to the arc and part around it. They do not mount it. Nothing in their traffic requires the avoidance—the arc holds no special heat, no special film—and yet the avoidance is worn into a pale track, scoured to the same depth on both sides, a path kept by habit after the reason has gone to powder. Farther along, on a face of cleaner glass, a run of scratches catches the slit-light if you bring your own eye close: too even for cooling joints, too worn to be read, a tally or a sentence or a map of seams, half-swallowed by later pitting. Near the hottest throat, a ring of collapsed pebbles, no metal in it, no ash that is not the basin's own, a hearth-shape for a fire the rock was already making. No tool remains. No device. If something arranged these, it arranged them with its hands and then left, or changed into what does not arrange. The basin did not take the lesson. It kept the stones because keeping and discarding have come to the same balance, and a fitted arc erodes, here, on a scale of time that makes the wheel look quick.

Weather, if that is the word, does not fall. It circulates. The gas thickens and thins. The film fattens after a strong pulse and is mown back. Sheets of glass exfoliate on the rim and tick down into the mass and are gone under plates before they can echo. Once in a long interval the whole basin seems to draw in; the columns rise together, every seam awake at once, the wheel stalled at full lean, the dark glittering with ten thousand minute shifts of dust—and then it releases, and the ordinary rotation resumes, and the quiet, which had not really broken, reseats itself. You could live a season beside the rim and call the place still. You would be describing your ears.

What the height contributes is the last tightness. Breath is work. The heat is not the heat of a fire you can step back from; it is radiant, stored, a kiln-dark that meets you at the skin and again in the lungs. Sweat starts and is finished in the same moment, a brief flare of salt and iron in air that has no habit of water. That flare is the only fresh chemistry you bring, and the nearest plates turn toward it—not toward you—combing the place where a drop failed to land. Then that, too, is mineral, and they forget, and the wheel has you as a temperature and nothing else.

It has been itself for longer than the arc of stones. The erosion is done becoming. The life is not exhausted and not becoming abundant; it is already as abundant as the volume allows, and the volume is spoken for. No green thing will find a foothold. No stream will cut a new bed. The slit will go on failing to light the nave. In the dark below it the wheel turns, reverses, turns, and the fitted stones keep their small incorrect law, and the gas goes on painting a film no foot will ever call grass, and the only sound that lasts is the one you carried in.
