The ground is newer than the heat.

That is the first fact of the place, and it takes a long time to believe, because the plain is already so large that the horizon behaves like a coastline: a soft ruled line, slightly higher in the shimmer than the eye expects, and never any nearer. You can walk toward it through a whole afternoon and the only change is in the pavement underfoot — a paler band, a seam, a place where last night's dew has not yet finished leaving. The distance does not arrive. The land is simply continuing, low and made, and the sky is occupied with the work of a surface that broad.

Nothing here was found. There is no older stone to break through to. Wherever the crust splits — and it does, in hairline checks no deeper than a finger — the walls of the split are the same substance as the top: a fine mineral pavement, laid in sheets, each sheet a little different in grain from the one beneath it. Some layers are chalky and drink the light. Some are tight and faintly glassy, as if a film of water dried too quickly and sealed what it covered. Between them run seams of a duller material, raised a millimeter or flush, like the joins in a floor that was poured in successive days and never meant to be one pour. Dig, if you had anything to dig with, and you would only reach earlier days of the same work. The making goes down out of sight. The world has no substrate that was not put there.

It is still being put there.

That is harder to see than the heat, because the making is slow and the heat is immediate. By midmorning the pavement has already given up the night's cool. The air a hand's-breadth above it quivers. Bare skin would not stay bare for long. The light itself is ordinary — a high white sun, a blue that thins toward the horizon into the color of hot metal — but the ground spends that light as warmth, and the warmth has nowhere to go except up. There are no hills to break it, no forests to drink it, no towns. The plain is its own stove, and because it is continental in extent it is also its own weather. The morning begins windless. By the time the seams are too hot to follow with a bare look, thin columns of air have begun to lift off the paler sheets, and far out, where the eye loses the texture of the ground, a line of cloud street forms, perfectly spaced, as if the sky had found the rhythm of the paving. The clouds are small. They do not stack. They travel. Sometimes one thickens enough to drop a brief, hard rain onto a patch of crust no wider than a dry lake, and the rain walks with the cloud until the cloud spends itself and the wet patch is left behind, dark, steaming, already shrinking from the edges.

Those wet patches are the water of the place. There are no rivers, because there is not yet a slope long enough, or an old enough weakness, to gather water and teach it a direction. What falls sits where it falls, in the shallow pans that the laying-down left behind — construction depressions, not basins carved by anything. A pan may hold a finger of water through the hot part of a day and be only a darker stain by evening. At night the pavement cools fast, faster than the air, and a dew comes out of nothing and stands in a haze so fine it does not fall so much as appear, beading along the seams, slicking the glassy sheets, collecting in the checks. By dawn the low places have a skin of moisture again. By the time the first thermal lifts, most of it is gone, and the rest is already warm enough to taste of the mineral it has been sitting on. The damp is intermittent, local, and faithful to the clock. It does not accumulate into a hydrology. It wets, it cures something, it leaves.

What it leaves is the beginning of wear, and almost nothing else. The land is too young for gullies. A seam that has taken dew for a season may show a faint rounding at its lip, a powder along its base where a grain let go. A pan that has held water often enough develops a pale rim, a tide-line of salts, and inside the rim the surface is a shade softer, as if the sheet there is still deciding whether to set. These are not landforms yet. They are the first sentences of landforms. Stand at the edge of such a pan in the late light and you can see the future of a drainage only as a suggestion: a slightly lower path where three pans nearly touch, a darker thread where water has crept from one to the next twice and dissolved a little of the join. Give it centuries and there might be a creek. Give it the age of an ordinary continent and there might be a coast. For now the suggestion is all there is, and the rest of the plain is raw in the way a fresh pour is raw — sharp at the lips of its own seams, unsoftened, not yet taught by weather what shape it prefers.

The growth of the pavement itself is the other motion, and it is quieter than the rain. In places the surface is finished: tight, pale, ringing if a stone were dropped on it, though there are no loose stones, only flakes where a sheet has checked and lifted at a corner. In other places, always at a distance from one another, the crust is incomplete. A front of newer material advances across an older sheet in a low, lobed edge, no higher than the side of a foot, matte where the finished pavement is bright. Touch is not required to know it is less cured. It holds the dew longer. It takes a footprint, if anything heavy crosses it, and the print remains until the next laying, or until the heat bakes the mark into a shallow scar that the following sheet will roof over and keep. These fronts are scattered. Most of the plain is done enough to walk. The unfinished work appears as islands and long irregular borders, as if whatever lays the ground works in patches and rests, and the rests are measured in seasons rather than in hours. Nothing attends the work. There is no apparatus, no cut, no course of blocks. The sheets arrive as sheets: precipitated, bound, cured by the same sun that will later try to crack them. The making is biological only in the way a reef is biological when the reef-builders are not animals you could name — a living chemistry at a scale below notice, generation after generation, until the chemistry has become a country.

On that country the vegetation is an afterthought, and a sparse one. The heat and the youth of the ground do not favor a cover. What grows, grows where the damp repeats: in the checks that hold dew past midmorning, along the inner rims of pans, in the soft zone just behind an active front where the sheet has not yet sealed. It is not grass in any familiar sense. Low crusts, gray-green, tightly bound to the mineral, looking at a distance like stains that refused to dry. Occasional tufts, stiff, the color of dried reed, rising a hand's height and no more, each tuft alone enough that you could walk a long time and count them. Between them, distances. The eye travels over empty pavement for so long that a single tuft becomes an event, and then the event is passed, and the pavement resumes its argument with the light. Nothing grazes the tufts. Nothing nests in them. They stand, they whiten at the tips in the worst of the afternoon, they green a little after a walking rain, and they are the whole of the visible life. Whatever else lives here lives in the sheets and the dew film and does not show itself as a body.

There are no bodies. No tracks except the ones the unfinished sheets will take and keep. No calls. The silence is not the silence of a held breath; it is the silence of a process that does not use throats. Wind, when it comes, is the afternoon's answering draft — air sliding back in low to replace what the thermals took — and over a surface this flat it does not roar. It arrives as a change in temperature on the shins, a faint dry tick as it crosses a seam, a soft hollowing in the ear. Rain, when a cloud street spends itself nearby, is the loudest thing the plain produces, and even that is brief: a hard stitching on the pavement, a hiss while the water is still water, then the quieter work of steam. Afterward the quiet returns larger than before, because the wet patch is visible and the sound of it is already over. At night, if there is sound at all, it is the small mineral tick of a sheet cooling, a check extending by a grain, and then nothing. The stars are ordinary. The dew arrives without announcement.

What moves, moves on the scale of the day and the scale of the laying. The cloud streets form and unform. The wet patches migrate with the showers and die in place. The dew draws its map and erases it. An unfinished front, if you could mark it and return after a long absence, would have advanced by a margin you could measure with a span of fingers, and the sheet behind it would have tightened, paled, joined the finished ground. A proto-thread between two pans would be a shade deeper, or would have sealed over and ceased to be a suggestion. These are the predators and the prey: heat against moisture, moisture against the uncured edge, the uncured edge against the sun that both builds it and begins, very slowly, to take it apart. Nothing flees. The activity is total and unhurried, a continental metabolism conducted in mineral and vapor.

Rules, if they can be called that, are only the habits of the materials. Water does not run until a path has been used often enough to exist, and almost no path has. Shade does not occur except in the brief dark under a spending cloud, and that shade moves. The pavement will take a mark only where it is not yet itself. Finished ground forgets footsteps; unfinished ground remembers them until it is finished over them. Night returns the only reliable water, and the day spends it. Distance is not an obstacle so much as a condition: the place does not thicken as you go. It remains sparse, low, hot, and made, hour after hour, until the idea of an edge becomes theoretical. Somewhere, if the making has a margin, there must be a place where the newest sheet ends and there is not yet ground. No one walking the interior will reach it. The interior is too wide, and the weather it breeds — the disciplined clouds, the walking rains, the dew — belongs to the width. Leave the width and you would leave the climate that keeps the crust curing and cracking and wet in its small faithful ways.

By late light the finished sheets go the color of bone, and the active fronts stay matte, and the pans hold their last dark ovals like eyes that will not last the night. The cloud streets collapse into scraps. The thermals die. Heat lifts off the ground in one last sheet and is gone, and the quiet, which never really left, becomes the only climate left above the mineral. Somewhere a check extends. Somewhere a film of dew begins, invisible, faithful, on a surface that did not exist, as ground, for very long before it. The plain continues. It is still becoming the thing it already, enormously, is.
