The height has forgotten it is high. What remains of the range is a planation so complete that the horizon does not rise to meet you; it simply fails, somewhere out past the wet, in a dimness that has no weather-line and no farther ridge. The cold sits on the skin like a film of metal. Breath condenses before it leaves the mouth and then does not fall. Droplets hang, revise themselves, and are taken back into the air as if the atmosphere were a lung that had not decided on exhalation.

Underfoot there is no stone you can trust as stone. The oldest rock of the plateau was worn to a film so long ago that the film became food, and the food became pavement. What you walk is a living substrate: a continuous, cold, slow body of plant-matter fused across the whole open reach—peat that has forgotten it was peat, lichen raised to the thickness of architecture, mats of alpine grass whose roots have anastomosed until the distinction between one plant and the next is a courtesy. It gives, slightly, like the hide of something asleep and very large. Water is never far. It weeps from the mat in thin sheets, stands in lens-shaped hollows no deeper than a wrist, beads along every raised vein of the living ground. Post-rain is the permanent tense. There is no storm you can point to as the cause; condensation is the climate’s industry.

And it is an industry. Nothing here was built. There are no walls, no stacks, no roads, no hand in any of it. Still the plateau manufactures. The mat runs processes you can hear before you can parse them: a piston-rhythm in the sap of buried culms, a belt-whine where a long pale runner of stem tightens and pays out across a mile of open ground, hauling nutrients the way a works hauls ore. In places the vegetation has organized itself into low engines—knotted organs the size of sheds, half-submerged, venting warm wet breath into the cold. They are not factories. They are also not not-factories. Steam lifts off them in columns that shear sideways and refuse to disperse, hanging as pale machinery in the dim. Now and then a spark jumps between two raised ribs of the mat, blue and brief, and the air smells of ozone and crushed resin. Electrified, mechanized, scaled to the region: production without a producer, an industrial ecology that grew its own plant and then became the plant.

The light never commits. A gray that might be dawn or the underside of a permanent cloud, except there is often no cloud, only a thickening of the air until seeing becomes a kind of wading. Distances misreport themselves. A dark tuft that reads as a stone’s throw away proves, after a long walk, to be a ridge of the mat no higher than your knee—or, on another day, a standing wave of vegetation as tall as a barn that was not there when you looked away. The plateau is open in the sense of a valley-country, many features, room to be lost, but openness here is not stable measure. The ground’s ancient flatness is real. The space above it is not finished deciding how large it is.

Between presences there is a great deal of nothing, and the nothing is loud. Wind across the wet mat makes a torn-silk roar. The engines in the vegetation tick and surge. Water, with almost no gradient left to follow, still moves—sheet to hollow, hollow to vein—with a sound like cloth being dragged through a mill. Far off, something percussion-heavy repeats, not quite regular, as if weather were stamping sheet metal against the horizon and missing. You can stand in what feels like emptiness and be unable to hear your own step.

The fauna are many and far apart, which is how abundance lives on a sparse plain. A herd of low, wool-dark grazers works a vein of sweeter mat a half-mile off, heads down, unstartled, their jaws making a wet industrial click. Between you and them the ground is empty for a long time, then not: a bird the color of the dim lifts from a seep and is, for a moment, three birds in three slightly wrong positions, and then one again, farther than the flight could account for. Things here do not always agree with the moment that produced them. You will see an animal and its absence sharing the same hollow, the absence drinking. You will watch a grazer put its head down and come up with the wrong season in its mouth—a mouthful of green that cannot grow at this cold, already browning as the jaw works, the plateau correcting the error without apology.

Rules surface the way water does, near the surface, never posted.

Weight is negotiable where the mat is thinnest. A footfall sinks and then, an hour later, the print rises into a low welt, healed past the point of healing into a small new organ. Do not sleep on the engines. Their breath is warm and that warmth is a lure; people-shaped depressions in the stories of this place are only stories, because there are no people, but the mat keeps the shape of anything that rested too long, and some of those shapes still breathe on a cycle of days. Water may be walked. It may also be listened to until it is behind you without your having turned. Uphill and downhill are retained as words and frequently reversed in practice: a seep will climb the subtle swell of the mat for an afternoon, then forget, and sheet back the way physics prefers, leaving a dark lick that smells of iron and crushed leaf.

The turbulence is not erosion. Erosion finished. This is the oldest stable state, near-flattened, settled into a final form that nonetheless will not hold still inside that form. Creation and destruction run as a paired process over the living pavement the way production and scrap run through a works. A stand of blade-grass assembles itself out of the mat in the time it takes to cross a seep—stalks locking, a small architecture, a shade—and by the time you reach it the stand is already unmaking, fibers paying back into the substrate with a sound like thread pulled from a seam. Weather-cells drop out of the dim, spin a few hundred paces of white water and flying culms into a brief tower, and are reabsorbed. Nothing is added to the height of the land. Nothing is subtracted. The plateau has reached the end of wearing-down and continues, hyperactive, inside that end: an anthill the size of a region, a bazaar with no merchants, only transactions of matter.

At the edge of hearing, past the last trustworthy hollow, the percussion resolves for a moment into something like pistons the size of hills, and then into birds, and then into the mat itself clearing its throat. The dim does not lift. The cold does not let up. Wet film on every surface. Between one step and the next the open ground shows you a second version of itself, slightly older, already flattened further, already alive, and then takes it back, and the roar of the wind across the living stone goes on without having paused for the revision.
