The city is inside a single stem.

From the basin you would not call it a city. You would not call it anything. The ground here is new — a cold low bowl that split open not long ago, its rims still sharp, the torn edges of soil and stone not yet rounded by weather. Water lies just under the skin of the earth and comes up in thin sheets wherever a foot presses. The only thing that has had time to become large is the plant: one organism, risen in the split, a pale column no taller than a house and not much wider, its surface already sweating in the dark air. People walk past it. Animals do not. Animals go in.

There is no door worth the name. Low on the windward side a seam has opened where two ridges of living rind failed to knit, and the seam stays open because traffic keeps it open. The rind is cold to the touch, not frozen, and it gives a little, like wet bark over something softer. Step through and the outside scale falls away. What contains you is a pocket, an interior no larger than a granary, and yet it is organized as a town: floors, not continuous, but lent and stacked; passages that are streets because they are kept clear; rooms that are rooms because something has decided they should hold still.

The decision is only partly human.

Light does not enter. The rind is too thick, and the basin above is already dim, a place where cold air pools and the sky stays a rumor. Inside, what light there is comes off the walls themselves in a faint, uneven sweat-gleam, green-black, never enough to show a whole corridor at once. You learn the place by hand and by the small warm spots where your own breath hangs and then is taken. The air is wet enough that it beads on eyelashes and on the edges of tools. It condenses on every new surface the stem grows, and the stem is always growing something new. Chambers appear as splits: crisp, recent, their lips still sharp, not tumbled into the soft maze an older hollow would be. A passage you used yesterday may have a fresher wall today, the cut of it bright with sap that smells faintly of iron and crushed leaf.

People live here with almost nothing made of fire or metal. What they have are bone awls, stone flakes no bigger than thumbnails, cords of inner fiber pulled when the stem is willing, and a grammar of cuts so light they hardly scar. They do not fell. They persuade. A sleeping-platform is a shelf of thickened pith, encouraged by a season of careful scoring until the tissue swelled and flattened. A cistern is a cupped node where the stem already wanted to store water; the town only lined its lip with packed fiber so the water would not all weep back into the walls. There are stories of levels — three, some say four — but level is the wrong word. The town is a scatter of holdings inside a volume of living mass. Between one holding and the next there is often nothing but plant: a long darkness of moist wall, a floor that is not a floor but the curved inside of a vein, wide enough for one body and empty for long stretches. Density, if that is the word, is low. You can walk and meet no one. The city is real anyway, because the paths are maintained, the nodes are named, and what moves through the emptiness has a schedule older than the names.

What moves is everything else.

The fauna are the overwhelming fact, and they are almost silent. They do not flock in the open way of birds over a field. They use the stem the way blood uses a body: numerous beyond counting, spaced so that any single stretch of passage holds only a few, and those few do not announce themselves. Moths the size of hands travel the upper seams, wings dry, the only sound a soft tick when one misjudges and touches rind. Lower, in the water-films, things like woodlice the color of the sap itself patrol in loose lines, thousands over a day, never a crowd in any one moment. Between the walls, in pockets of still air, hang colonies of something nearer to seed than to insect — pale, slow, pulsing once every few breaths — and when they pulse the nearby tissue loosens, drinks, and reseals. Larger animals come through on longer circuits: lean, fur-damp climbers that use the human shelves without appearing to notice them; blind runners no higher than an ankle that follow the freshest splits because the newest tissue is sweetest; and, rarely, a heavy shape in the dark that does not climb at all but presses, and the stem opens a temporary gallery around it, and closes again after it has passed. You hear almost none of this. The quiet is not emptiness. It is the sound of many soft bodies on wet plant, which is nearly the sound of nothing.

The processes do not pause for the quiet.

Water moves constantly, not as streams but as a slow redistribution. It enters from the basin through roots that are also, in places, the floors of the lowest holdings. It rises in the walls, beads, runs two finger-widths, is drunk by tissue or by the small patrols, and falls again as cold drip into the nodes people call cisterns. After rain outside — and it rains often, a fine cold rain that never quite becomes weather you can watch — the whole interior sweats harder, and for a day the streets are louder by a degree: more drip, more ticking of wings, more of the climbers shaking themselves on the shelves. Then the stem takes the surplus down into the new splits, and the city returns to its ordinary near-silence.

The plant itself is in motion on a slower clock and a faster one at once. Slow: the rind thickens, the column leans a finger's width toward the wetter side of the basin, a chamber that was head-high becomes stoop-high over a month as tissue swells into it from above. Fast: a fresh split can open while you are standing in it, a clean pale wound with sharp lips, and within hours the small runners have found it, and within a day someone from the upper holdings will have come with a flake and a cord to decide whether the new room is to be kept or allowed to seal. The keeping is never certain. The stem has its own civic order. Rooms it wants, it holds open. Rooms it is finished with, it narrows, gently, until a person must turn sideways, and then until only the moths fit, and then not even them.

There is weather inside, of a kind. Cold gathers in the lower third, where the water is nearest the surface of the earth and the air hardly moves; sleepers there wake with their outer clothes damp and stiff. Higher, near the youngest growth, the air is still cold but drier by a fraction, and the walls tick as fibers tighten and loosen — the only rhythm some people use to mark anything like hours. Now and then a pressure change outside pulls a long, low movement through the whole stem, not a sound so much as a displacement: every hanging colony pulses at once, every water-film shivers, and for a moment you understand the city as one animal with thousands of smaller animals inside it, and yourself as a tolerated irregularity in the circulation.

The strangeness is not spectacle. It is the ordinary fact that the infrastructure is alive and not especially interested. A street may be closed by growth overnight, and the detour will already be in use, found first by the runners, then by the climbers, and only then by the people, who follow the damp tracks and call the new way by the old name, because names here attach to routes of traffic, not to walls. Disputes are about nodes: who may score a shelf, who may drink from a cistern before the patrols have passed, whether a fresh split should be encouraged into a market — a widened place where, twice in a dim cycle, people stand farther apart than the passages allow and trade fiber, flakes, and news of openings. The market is the densest the city ever gets, and even then you could stretch your arms and touch no one if you chose the wrong moment. Overwhelming numbers belong to the other citizens, who do not trade, and who do not stop.

No one has built a monument. There is nothing to build one of, and no light to see it by. What stands for memory is a scar that refused to heal: a gallery near the center, older than the others by a little, its lips gone from sharp to merely firm, where the stem once tried to seal and the traffic would not permit it. People leave cords there, knotted in a pattern you can read with your fingers. The cords say who was lost in a sealing, which splits drowned after a hard rain, which family of climbers changed its circuit and took three shelves with it. The moths rest on the cords sometimes. They do not disturb the knots.

Outside, the basin continues its short life. The rims will soften. The water under the soil will find longer paths. The column may thicken, or split into two, or be what it is until cold and age bring it down — a young landform, still all edges. None of that is visible from within. From within there is only the wet dark, the spaced holdings, the endless soft passage of bodies, and the stem deciding, moment by moment, which emptiness to keep and which to fill. The city persists the way a vein persists: not by being large, and not by being loud, but by being used, continuously, by more lives than it can hold in any single dark room, and by never once becoming still enough to close.
