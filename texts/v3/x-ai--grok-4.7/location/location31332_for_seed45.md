The height does not announce itself as a summit. There is no lower country in view to measure against, only a floor of dark mineral that has not finished deciding what it is, and a ceiling so close and so black it might be rock that never quite arrived. The air is warm. It moves in slow bodies, not wind so much as heat shifting its weight, and the warmth has nowhere wet to go. Nothing sweats. Nothing shines with moisture. The dryness is not a desert's dryness, which still remembers rain; this is the dryness of a place that has not yet been given water, or has refused it.

What lies underfoot is new. Sheets of glass, still faintly plastic where the last heat left them, overlap plates of grit that have not learned to be sand. In places the ground is only a skin, and beneath it something continues to sort itself: a dull press, a settling, a soft internal click that does not carry. Erosion has barely begun. Edges are raw, not worn. A ridge may stand for an hour with the profile of a spill frozen mid-pour, then slump along a seam no one could have predicted, the new face steaming a little in the dark, already cooling into a different stone. The land is making itself in front of you, and unmaking the version it tried a moment ago.

The scale is regional and open, though the dark cheats the eye. You can walk a long time between events. A pale fan of fresh mineral. A trough where the floor split and welded wrong, still warmer than the rest. Far off, a low wall of uplift that was not there when you started, or was, and has simply become more itself. Between these, emptiness enough that a single standing stone reads as architecture.

There are a few such marks. Not buildings, not tools: alignments. Three slabs set on edge where the ground is firmest, their faces still glassy, their bases already being overgrown by the next upwelling of rock, so that they look half-swallowed, half-deliberate. Elsewhere a shallow ring of darker clasts, placed, not fallen — you can tell because the ring is too even and the ground inside it has been kept clear of the newest spill. No hand is here. No device. Whatever arranged them has gone, or was never an animal, or was the land practicing a shape and then losing interest. The rings hold heat differently. Stand in one and the air against your shins is a degree warmer, and very still.

Sound is scarce. The turbulence is not a storm you can hear. It is the continual revision of the ground: a far slab detaching with a noise like cloth, a pressure change that pops in the ears, the almost-silent crawl of a cooling crust contracting. Now and then a whole terrace gives, somewhere out in the dark, and the report arrives late and small, already finished. Between those events the quiet is thick. Warm air carries little. Your own steps are the loudest thing, and they sound wrong — too crisp on glass, then muffled where the mineral is still mealy and uncompacted.

There is no plant life and no animal life, and the absence is not barren in the familiar way. Nothing has died here. Nothing has tried to live. The only ecology is mineral: sequences of cooling, fracturing, welding, a chemistry of dry heat sorting elements into thin bands you can see only when a fresh face splits and the interior shows, briefly, a dull rose before the dark takes it. Some of those bands continue to move after the rock has seemed solid — a slow brightening along a vein, a dimming, as if heat were being passed hand to hand under the crust. That is the unusual part, and it does not explain itself. The veins do not glow enough to light the way. They only prove the dark is not empty of event.

Overhead, if it is overhead, there is no sky you can separate from the high dark. Sometimes a shelf of warmer air passes, and the black seems to lift; sometimes the ceiling lowers until the tallest new ridge nearly meets it, and the gap between them ticks with small static discharges, soundless, a faint mineral smell. The whole region is in a storm-state that has no weather: creation and collapse running together, sparse, warm, waterless, mostly quiet, and unfinished on every side. If you wait, the slab you are standing on will decide, eventually, whether it means to remain a floor.
