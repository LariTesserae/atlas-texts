The world is a room, and the room is full.

There is no waterline. Nothing to rise toward, no skin of air, no place where a wave could form and spend itself. The volume is closed and liquid from the lens overhead to the shallow dish of the floor, and it has been closed long enough that the idea of a surface has worn out of it. Warmth is even, almost domestic, a little above the warmth of blood. Light comes through in an ordinary way: you can judge color, you can see the far wall, shadows have soft edges where the water scatters them and a second, harder edge where they fall from the work-lamps still burning in a ring around the lens. Two lights, two shadows. Neither is dramatic. Together they read as day.

The room is not large. A body could cross it in a few strokes and would be near a wall the whole time. What makes it a landscape rather than a tank is the time in the surfaces, and the amount of unoccupied water, and the fact that everything in it is doing something on a schedule older than the lives using it.

Every surface was made. There is no stone under the crust, no sand that was ever a beach. If the pale rind is flaked away, what shows is older rind, and under that a sintered layer, and under that an alloy whose grain has been relaxed by heat and water until it no longer looks like a decision. Corners have given up. Bolt circles remain as faint rings, the bolts themselves reduced to nubs the color of old teeth. Pipe mouths open in the walls at irregular heights, but the irregularity is only the last stage of a regular layout: each mouth has been rounded by flow into a short throat, lipped with mineral, and the throats are the only caves this world will ever have. The floor is a dish, gentle, gathered toward a valve at the lowest point. The valve is the size of a torso. It is shut. It has been shut for the entire age in which the room became what it is. Through it the room withholds itself from the drop.

That is the height, and it is not visible. Outside the shell, above the lens, the air is thin and hard and loud against metal; the light that enters has the too-clear quality of a summit, white at noon, quick to leave. One wall runs cooler than the others by a fraction — the wall that faces the night side of the open sky — and the difference is a geography. Life arranges itself against it. Below the valve, beyond the world, the works continue downward: shaft, bulkhead, shaft, a manufactured fall so long that the outlet pipe sings with the head of it even while sealed. The room is a pocket at the top of that fall. Extreme elevation here is not a view. It is a load the valve carries, a note in the metal, a cold leak on one face, and the knowledge, available to anything that has lived long enough to inherit it, that the room is high because the rest of the building is a depth.

Nothing is freshly broken. The erosion has gone all the way to a stopping place. Cycles still move sediment, still round a lip by the width of a grain, still feed a little dissolved metal into the water, but the form does not advance. Come back after another long stillness and the dish will be the dish, the nubs will be the nubs, the lens will still be crazed in the same map and still clear enough. This is the oldest stable state of a made thing: not ruin in progress, but the shape a machine settles into when weather, heat, and water have finished their argument with design. The argument ended in a draw. The room keeps its volume. The details have all been forgiven.

The water carries a faint sweet-metal taste and a slipperiness that is not oil so much as the long extract of seals and crust. It is clear. Across the open middle you can see through to the opposite embayment, and most of what you see is nothing. Density never recovered from the original engineering, or the equilibrium prefers space. Presences stand apart. Between them the warm volume is simply volume, lightly moved, holding light.

The plants are lush in the body and scarce in the plan. They root where sealant failed along the old mounting grid, so their arrangement still remembers a schematic: rows that waver, gaps where a seam healed instead of opening, a crowd of three where a joint collected silt. Each plant is fat and long. Ribbons of even cultivar green trail with the flow, broad blades hinge at a mineral socket, a few holdfasts have become trunks the width of a wrist, ringed like the bolt circles they grew against. They are not wild stock that found a way in. Their green is too uniform, their spacing too nearly regular, the way they flex too precisely tuned to a current that has not changed its angle in an age. No one tends them. The grid tends them. Nutrients arrive as the walls surrender, particle by particle, and as a darker pulse that enters through one throat on the warm side — a feed line, still valved, still on its ancient timer, delivering a broth brewed somewhere else in the works or leached from them. The plants thicken after the pulse. For a little while the water between them is less empty. Then the outlet on the far side takes its share, the middle clears, and the room returns to its spare green.

The animals are many for a room and few for a sea. Abundance here means you are rarely without a life in view, and rarely with two of the same kind close enough to confuse. In the open water, slim holders park themselves like buoys, each with a column and a patience, spaced so that their territories tile the middle without filling it. They face the motors. When the ring of work-lamps flickers at the top of a cycle they correct their heading by a degree, as if the lamps were a reef. Along the bolt-rings, small bright clingers speckle the metal, abundant only at that scale: a constellation on a seam, then a long blank of crust, then another seam. In the dish, larger grazers move with a rasp that fits old threading. Their mouths were not designed for these nubs and yet have become exact. They crop the pale threads that bloom on the cool wall, and they crop them incompletely, so the cool wall is always partly written-on, a slow script of growth and erasure that is the nearest thing the room has to a season you can see.

In the pipe mouths, one lurker to an embayment, sometimes none. They come forward when the room inhales.

The inhale is the pump. It does not live in the room; it lives in the wall, and the wall is continuous with it, so the distinction fails at the ear. A low industrial presence, engines and pressure and the particular loudness of machinery in a small hard space, never distant. Then the valve-work speaks — not the great valve under the dish, which stays shut, but the lesser valves on the feed, the outlet, the recirculation jet. They open in a fixed order. The jet crosses the upper third of the room and is, for its minute, a weather system: ribbons lie flat, sediment lifts out of the dish in a pale veil, the holders tilt and feed, the lurkers slide to the lip. A slam follows, loud enough to be the largest object in the world, a single mechanical report with no echo worth the name because every surface is close and already ringing. In the slack after the slam the larvae appear, a brief milk in one corner of the volume, never the whole. The holders and the small bright clingers take them. The outlet opens and the milk is gone. The grazers resume. The ribbons lift off the horizontal and write their slow cursive again.

This is the activity of the place, and it is real weather: multiple, timed, biological, mechanical, repeating. It does not develop. The room is not heading toward a clearer water or a thicker green or a failure. Predator and prey are tuned to the duty cycle the way other creatures are tuned to dusk. The slam covers the sound of an approach; the lurkers that hunt well hunt in the noise, and the holders that survive have learned the half-second before the slam as a kind of dawn. Between events the loudness does not stop. It only changes register — crust ticking as it cools by a shade on the night wall, the impeller-animals near the jet housing clicking as they turn (they are animals; they only look like leftover hardware, a likeness the water has been selecting for so long that likeness and descent are the same fact), the hull answering the high wind outside with a note so steady it could be mistaken for the pump and is not. Inside a submerged room, loudness is not distance. It is how completely the world is present.

The strangeness is ordinary once you have waited out a cycle. No air. Two shadows. Animals that navigate by motors. Plants still obeying a mounting diagram. A summit you cannot see, recorded only as light, cold, and the singing of a sealed drop. A feed line whose other end is not part of this world and does not need to be. Nothing here is supernatural. It is what happens when an industrial volume is left at the top of itself, flooded, warmed by its own remaining heat and by the broth it still receives, populated by stock that was installed and then never collected, and given enough time to reach the end of form. The stock is abundant because the room is kind and closed and fed. The stock is sparse because the room is small and the open water is the larger habitat, and crowding would foul the jet, and the equilibrium has already punished crowding, long ago, until space became a feature.

There are rules, and they are simple because the place has had time to simplify.

There is no earth. The building is the soil, and it feeds by disappearing, slowly, inside a form that no longer changes.

There is no surface. Rising is a direction toward the lens and the lamps, not an escape. Everything that lives here lives wet, and has, since before the present shapes of its mouths.

There is no season but the cycle. Slack, pulse, jet, slam, slack. The cool wall writes and is erased. The high light beyond the lens brightens and thins — a true day, summit-clear, passing over a world that does not open to meet it.

Nothing new is built. The lamps burn because their supply is the pump’s supply, and the pump runs because the valves still answer, and the valves answer because the head on the great seal has not yet found a flaw. Maintenance is a property the system has, not a labor anyone arrives to perform.

The middle is supposed to be empty. Life at the seams, life in the dish, life in the mouths, a few holders keeping station: that is plenty. The unoccupied water is not a lack. It is the room’s remaining wilderness, the part of the design that time agreed to keep.

If the great valve under the dish ever failed, the landscape would become a brief, loud, descending event, and then it would be gone into the height it has spent its age refusing. It has not failed. In the warm, ordinary light, a grazer fits its rasp to a nub that used to be a bolt, a ribbon lifts, the jet prepares itself again in the wall, and the small bright clingers on the nearest ring catch both lights at once — daylight and lamp — so that each of them throws two shadows, precise and harmless, across manufactured crust that will look the same for as long as the seal holds.
