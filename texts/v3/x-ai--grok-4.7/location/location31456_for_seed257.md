The water begins at the lip and does not end inside the place. What holds it is a basin the size of a small church, set on a height so old the height itself has been planed almost away: a last shelf of made ground, its outer edges rounded to the same curve as the inner ones, as if weather and the slow creep of the fill had been at the same work for longer than either remembers. There is no shore worth the name. The rim is a thickened wall of fired clay, shell-mortar, and stone dressed with hand tools—chisel marks still legible in the few stretches the water has not polished into anonymity—and beyond the rim the world drops, but the drop is not the subject. The subject is the volume.

It is warm. Not hot; a body-warmth held without a source you can point to, as if the basin had learned the temperature of whatever once tended it and then outlasted the tending. The water is clear only in the sense that nothing clouds it on purpose. In the dark it might as well be solid. Light does not really enter. A stain of day lies on the surface like a film that never quite mixes, gray-brown and thin, and a hand’s length down it is already gone. Below that the basin is night with a floor.

The floor is not bedrock. Nothing here is substrate in the old sense. Every surface was put there: courses of flat stone, beds of crushed shell, plugs of clay thumbed into joints and then fired, or left to harden in the warmth until firing and time did the same job. Ages of settling have pressed the courses into one another until the courses are a suggestion. Steps exist, but they have become ramps. Niches exist, but their mouths have softened to ovals. A low colonnade crosses one side—posts no taller than a person, set too close, the gaps between them narrower than a shoulder—and the posts have worn until they look poured rather than cut. Whatever roof there was is gone, or was never more than the sky the rim cuts into a square. The makers worked without metal worth mentioning, or with metal so simple it has since become stain. You can still find, if you know to feel for it, a groove where a rope ran, a socket where a pole stood, a lintel whose underside was adzed and then forgotten. The building has arrived at the shape it will keep.

Nothing green grows in it, and nothing ever will while the water stays. No film of weed on the courses, no root in the mortar, no drifting scrap of leaf. The basin is mineral and animal and made, and the animals have the whole of it.

They are many, and they are close. In the upper dark, just under the failed light, a layer of small swimmers turns in a slow wheel, each almost touching the next, the wheel itself barely moving relative to the walls. They do not flash. Their bodies are the color of the clay. Lower, against the colonnade, larger bodies hang in the gaps between posts as if the gaps had been measured for them: pale, soft-sided, holding still for so long that stillness starts to look like architecture. Along the floor a carpet of shelled things clicks only when one shifts, and the click dies inside a hand’s breadth. Nothing hunts in a hurry. Mouths work on the slow snow of the basin itself—the finest grit off the worn courses, the castings of the layer above, whatever the warmth loosens from old mortar. They are abundant the way sediment is abundant. You could not extend an arm without meeting one.

The unusual thing is not their number. It is that they seem to have taken the building’s measurements as their own. The wheel above matches the square of the rim. The still ones occupy only the made gaps, never the open water where a gap was not cut. A few, near the deepest corner where three walls meet and the floor has sagged into a shallow, have grown shells that repeat the oval of the nearest niche, as if shape here were contagious over a long enough quiet. None of this is dramatic. It is the mild wrongness of a tool marks still visible on a creature that was never a tool.

Sound is almost an absence. The surface does not break; there is no wind in the square of sky strong enough to put a wrinkle on water this held. What you hear, if you put an ear to the rim, is a low grainy hush, the sum of many small mouths and the minute slip of shell on stone. No calls. No splash. The basin keeps its own counsel.

Change, when it comes, comes as a redistribution. Over a season the wheel drifts a quarter-turn and stops. A post, already more suggestion than post, loses another grain and the animal in its gap adjusts by the same grain. The water level neither rises nor falls in any way a visitor would notice; if it moves, it moves with the height itself, which is finished moving. Warmth stays. Dark stays. The rim, planed and planed again by nothing more violent than air and the occasional hand of rain that never quite becomes weather, continues its long arrival at a final outline: a small, high, handmade sea, crowded, plantless, and nearly still, holding a dark that has outlasted the people who taught the stone how to keep it.
