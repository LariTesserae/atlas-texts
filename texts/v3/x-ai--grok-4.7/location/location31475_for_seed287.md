The chamber is smaller than the heat suggests. You could walk its long axis in a few minutes if the floor would let you, and you can see, dimly, that it does end: a curve of cut stone closing on both sides, the roof not much higher than a tall nave, beaded with condensate that never quite becomes weather. Nothing here is weather. The air is only the chamber’s own breath, held and reheated until it feels older than the walls.

Heat sits low. It comes up through the flagging in slow pulses, not a furnace-roar at the skin but a weight that makes the damp cling. Sweat does not cool you. It joins the film already on the stone. Somewhere under the floor a line still runs—pipe, flue, buried artery—and whatever passes through it has been passing through it long enough that the stone has learned the rhythm. The pulses are patient. You could count them against a slow walk and lose the count.

Water is not a feature so much as a habit. Pockets in the low joints. A dark tongue along one wall where seepage has worn a lip and then stopped, as if the rock and the leak agreed on a level centuries ago. Now and then a drop falls from the root-mat overhead and strikes metal, and the sound is larger than the drop. The chamber is loud in that way: not chaotic, not many voices, but a few sounds amplified by the close stone until they occupy the whole volume. A tick of condensate. A long metallic settling, like a hull working in a calm. A wet hiss where heat meets a film of water on a casing half-swallowed by the floor. Between them, the vegetable sound—fibers taking strain, a slow creak that might be growth or might only be the plants shifting their weight as the pulses come and go.

The growth is thick. It is not a forest. It is a room that has been occupied by green until the room’s original volumes are only guesses. Vines mat the springing of the vault. Broad leaves overlap in layers so close they touch, their surfaces dull with mineral dust and always a little wet on the underside. Stems thicker than a wrist have split the joints between blocks and then, finding no further give, thickened sideways, shouldering the masonry into a new shape that both parties seem to accept. There is no animal life to move among them. No wings. No small claws on the casings. The stillness of that absence makes the plant-movement more audible, and stranger: everything that shifts does so without a creature’s intention, only pressure, heat, and time.

What was built here was never a city. A single installation, perhaps, or the surviving cell of one: scattered housings, a gantry whose far end has slumped into the root-mass and been held there, a row of bolted plates along the west curve whose bolts have bloomed into orange crust and whose faces are lettered in a script the damp has half-eaten. The machines are not ruins in the picturesque sense. They are in their own state. Erosion has finished its argument with them and moved on to maintenance. Edges are rounded where hands or water once worried them; interiors are sealed by scale and by a pale crust that may be mineral, may be something the plants exude when the pulses peak. A flywheel, if that is what the buried disc is, no longer turns. It has become a table for moss that is not quite moss—leafier, oil-dark, rooted in the grease that outlasted the bearings.

Light is a rumor. It enters, if it enters, from a seam high on the north curve, a crack no wider than a hand, and does not travel far. What you see by is mostly reflection: wet leaf, wet casing, the occasional pale flange of fungus-like growth that is nonetheless a plant, flowering in a color too close to the color of the metal to be comfortable. In that light the chamber looks nearer than it is. Distances compress. A housing across the floor seems an arm’s length away until you try to reach it and find three strides of uneven root and stone, each stride loud, each footfall answered by the hiss and the tick as if the room were keeping a ledger.

The strangeness is not a single marvel. It is the fit. The plants have not overrun a dead factory; the factory has not poisoned a cave. They have arrived at a shared posture. Leaves cup the drips and deliver them, by a logic of veins and slight tilts, onto the same casings every time, so that certain plates are always bright and certain plates are always dry and chalky. Roots follow the old cable-runs as if the runs were beds prepared for them. Where a pipe has burst and healed itself with mineral, a trunk has split around the scar and rejoined above it, a living collar on a dead repair. Nothing hurries. A new leaf unrolls across what might be a week. A flake of scale loosens, hangs, and does not fall until the next long pulse from below lifts the whole floor by a hair.

If there are rules, they are the rules of a closed agreement. Do not expect animals; the niche has been taken entirely by green and by machine-remnant, and whatever might have hunted or pollinated here has been absent long enough that the flowers set no seed you can recognize—they thicken, instead, into plates, into flanges, into things that look like they were milled. Do not expect the water to rise. It has its pockets and its lip and its disciplined drip, and the heat recycles it without ever granting a pool large enough to drown a boot. Do not expect silence; the chamber spends its energy as sound, and the sound is the proof that the agreement still holds. Do not expect an exit that announces itself. The seam of light is not a door. The long axis ends in stone that has been cut and then grown over until cut and grown are the same gesture.

Stand long enough and the pulses enter the chest. The leaves tick against one another in a sequence you almost parse. A drop finds the same bolt it has been finding since before the lettering failed. The heat does not lessen. The room, small as it is, continues—settled, loud, dim, green to the point of crowding, and in no hurry to become anything other than the thing it has already, patiently, become.
