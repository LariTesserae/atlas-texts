The plateau does not announce itself as high. There is no rim in the ordinary sense, no sudden drop that would let a body understand the distance beneath it. The ground simply continues, and continues, until the idea of a lower world becomes a rumor carried in the weight of the air. What you stand on has been standing so long that weather and stone have finished their argument. The ridges are rounded to the same patience as the hollows. Talus has settled into slopes that no longer shed. Even the cracks have a finished look, as if erosion reached a treaty with the rock and both sides have kept it.

It is warm. Not the warmth of a sun you can find—there is almost no sun to find—but a low, even heat that comes up through the soles of things and never quite leaves the air. At this height the sky ought to be thin and cold. Instead the dark presses down like a lid, and under the lid the heat stays. Breath does not smoke. Stone, if you put a hand to it in the long intervals between damp and damp, is only a little cooler than skin. Somewhere far under the weathered crust, old engines of the planet are still doing their work: not fire you could see, but pressure, mineral change, the slow spending of heat that a continent this large can hoard. The plateau has weather of its own because it is large enough to make weather, and the weather is mostly a rearrangement of that stored warmth against whatever moisture manages to arrive.

Water is not a system so much as a habit that keeps almost failing. There are no great rivers. There are threads. A seep that wets a shelf of stone for a season and then forgets. Condensation that beads along the undersides of overhangs where the warm air meets a slightly colder face of rock, then gathers, then falls in single drops into pockets no wider than a bowl. Occasional rain, when it comes, does not fall as a front you can watch approaching. The dark thickens by a degree. The air takes on a smell of hot dust briefly wetted. Then a sparse rain reaches the ground and is gone into cracks before it can make a sheet. What remains are dark stains, temporary mirrors the size of a hand, and a few longer-lived pools in stone basins that the old erosion carved and then stopped enlarging. Around those pools the ground stays damp for a while. Everywhere else returns to a dryness that is not desert, only the ordinary condition between drinks.

Vegetation keeps to those drinks. It is not absent, and it is not a cover. A low mat of something fibrous will colonize the rim of a basin and stop where the damp stops, so that from any distance the growth looks like a dark ring drawn on paler stone. In the cracks, where condensation collects and does not quite evaporate, there are wiry stems, gray-green if there were light enough to judge color, sparse enough that you can count individuals rather than perceive a meadow. Nothing here grows tall. Height would be a waste in a place where the valuable thing is a film of moisture and the enemy is space. Roots run sideways, hunting the next pocket, sometimes bridging bare rock with a cord no thicker than a finger. Seeds, if that is what the hard capsules are, wait in the mats for a rain that may not come in any given year. The plateau can carry whole weather systems and still offer a plant only a puddle.

What the eye cannot spend on plants it might spend on distance, except that the dark spends the distance first. Light is not gone. It is reduced to a rumor: a slight lifting of black into charcoal along a ridge when some high haze catches whatever source exists above the lid; a dull sheen on wet stone; the faint heat-shimmer that is not light at all but a bending, noticeable only when a silhouette of rock ought to be steady and is not. You learn the ground by temperature and texture more than by sight. A slope that has held sunless warmth all day—if day is even the right word—feels different underfoot from a hollow where last month's rain still cools the grit. Far off, sometimes, a pale vein opens in the cloud deck and closes again before any landscape can be read through it. The dark is not a night that expects morning. It is the climate.

And yet the place is not empty of life. It is empty of crowding. Between one presence and the next there is an enormous amount of nothing: bare saddle, gravel flat, a mile of stone the color of extinguished coal, wind that moves and does not speak loudly. Then a pocket. In the pocket, abundance.

They gather where the water gathers, and they travel the long emptiness between gatherings with a discipline that looks, at first, like the movement of machines. Herds—if herd is the word for bodies that do not press together—cross the high flats in files so widely spaced that each animal is its own weather. They are not large, most of them. Size would cost water. What they have instead is number, and a metabolism tuned to industrial patience. Along the damp margins, colonies of smaller things encrust the stone in tiers, not built, grown: mineral crusts that are also bodies, bodies that sort grains, extract salts, lay down waste that the next layer reads as food. In the basins, swimmers the length of a finger turn the still water over and over, cloudy, then clear, then cloudy, processing what the rain brought down from the dark air. On the open plateau, the wide-spaced walkers crop the rare mats and, in their gut, grind stone dust they swallowed with the fiber, cracking it, concentrating metals and phosphates, dropping them miles later in small, precise piles that other organisms will find and finish. Nothing here smelts. Nothing here houses a gear cut by a hand. There are no walls, no roads, no lights of a yard. The technology is the arrangement: extraction, transport, refinement, deposition, performed at a continental scale by living processes that have had time to become as settled as the landforms. You can watch a line of walkers for an hour and understand that you are watching a factory whose sheds are the distances between them.

They are quiet about it. Hooves, or whatever meets the stone, are padded or spaced so that the sound dies in the gravel. The basin-swimmers make a soft granular tick, like sand settling, and only if you are at the lip. Wind crosses the plateau in broad sheets and, because there is so little to catch it—no forest, no tower, no wire—it becomes a pressure against the ear more than a voice. Now and then a call goes out, low and short, from one walker to another a long way off, and the answer comes late, thinned by distance into something you might mistake for the stone cooling. Predators exist in the same economy of space. They do not crash through cover; there is no cover to crash through. A shape detaches from a ridge that was only ridge, crosses an open flat at a speed that seems unhurried until you notice how much ground it has taken, and meets a walker that had been one of the wide file. The meeting is brief. Afterward the file adjusts its spacing by a body's width and continues. The plateau absorbs the event the way it absorbs rain: locally, then not at all. Somewhere else, in another basin, the same transaction is occurring, and in another, and the sum of them is a system in motion that never rises to a roar.

Other motions run underneath the biological one, slower and as continuous. The warm air pools by day—by whatever interval passes for day—and drains at night into the old erosion hollows, so that a fog, thin as dust, forms over the basins and then lifts without ever becoming weather you could name from far off. Electrical charge builds along the high ridges where the dark air moves fastest. Sometimes it spends itself in a silent sheet, a brightness so brief and so low that it illuminates nothing but the fact of its own happening: a horizon-edge gone pale, then black, and a smell of hot mineral left behind. The walkers pause when that happens. Not in fear, or not only. The charge is useful to them. Certain crusts in the damp cracks grow faster after a discharge, the way a mill runs better when the wheel is fed. The animals that tend those crusts—small, numerous, almost invisible against the stone—shift toward the ridge bases in the hours after, harvesting what the sky just refined.

Nothing stands that was put there. If there were ever makers, the plateau has weathered them down into the same treaty as the talus. A straight edge, if you found one, would be a joint in the rock. A hollow that seems planned is a basin that stopped enlarging ten thousand seasons ago and now holds water when water consents to be held. The industrial fact of the place is not an occupation laid over a landscape. It is the landscape's own production, running at a scale that would, in another world, require engines and sheds and a labor force counted in cities. Here the labor force is scattered across a darkness wide enough to have climate. The engines are guts, crusts, charge, and time. The sheds are the miles of empty stone between one damp ring of vegetation and the next.

Stand long enough and the quiet acquires structure. There is the grainy tick of a basin working. There is the late, thin answer of a walker. There is the soft collapse of a fog as the warm drain reverses and the hollow gives its air back to the plateau. There is, under all of it, a sound so broad and so low that it registers as balance rather than noise: the settled stone, no longer falling, still exchanging heat with the dark, still feeding the slow industry that lives on its patience. The place does not brighten. It does not fill in. It keeps its distances, its pockets, its warmth without a sun, and the work goes on inside them, abundant and almost silent, as if a continent had learned to manufacture what it needs without ever disturbing the dark.
