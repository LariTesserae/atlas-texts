The place is a bowl, and it is not large. From the lip to the far wall is a walk you could finish before your shadow, if you had one worth speaking of, had moved. The rim is a ring of higher stone, wind-cut and then wind-forgotten, and the floor sits in its shade the way a throat sits under a jaw. High country presses around it—thin air, a long drop beyond the entrance crack—but the bowl itself holds warmth the way old kilns do, a mild heat that has nowhere else to go and so stays in the rock. Nothing glares. Light arrives secondhand, bounced off the inner cliff and sifted through a permanent meal of mineral dust, so the colors never quite commit: bone, oil-black, rust, a dull gold that might be light or might be the stone remembering light.

There is no water. Not a seep, not a film, not the dark telltale of damp in a joint. The air is dry enough that the tongue notices it as a taste before the lungs do. What moisture exists is locked inside bodies and does not leak. The world here has made its peace with that. It did so a long time ago. The floor is polished, not by flood but by traffic and by grit, into a settled topography of shallow swales and low rises, each curve finished, none of them young. Paths have been paths so long they read as geology. A crack that might once have been a fault is now a route, its edges rounded by generations of claws and the drag of something heavier that no longer comes, or comes so rarely the stone has already agreed to its absence.

What grows here does not drink. Twenty-odd presences stand in the bowl, and between them is space enough to lose a small herd if the herd were willing to be lost. They are lush the way a single old tree is lush: not by crowding, but by the extravagant complication of each one. The largest is a thicket of plates—mica-thin, keratin-stiff, the color of smoked horn—layered into a crown that takes the warm air and turns it over and over through a maze of channels. Nothing green about it. It feeds on heat, on the dust that never quite settles, on traces of volatile oils the air carries from its own kind. Its “sap” is a wax, clear when fresh and amber when it has sat, and it does not drip. It migrates, slowly, along internal veins, and where a plate dies it does not rot wet. It powders. The powder is pale and fine and the living plates sieve it back. A closed, dry economy, each growth an island with a beach of its own shed skin.

They are spaced like decisions. You can walk the bare stone between them and feel, underfoot, the difference between floor that has only been walked and floor that has been fed: the second is faintly greasy in the grain, polished to a deeper shine, and smells, if you kneel, of warm resin and struck flint. Roots, if that is the word, are cables of the same horn-stuff, traveling just under the surface and sometimes breaking it, not to seek a water table—there is none, and never was—but to claim a patch of floor that still holds the day’s heat after the dust-light has thinned. Over years a growth leans, puts down a new cable on the warm side, abandons the old, and so migrates a hand’s breadth in a generation. The bowl’s arrangement is therefore both ancient and unfinished. The forms are settled. The addresses are not.

In the dim you hear them before you parse them. The plates tick as the stored warmth shifts. A mature crown will, without warning, clap a rank of its outer leaves together—one sharp report, then a rustle like a market closing—and the wax-mist that shakes loose hangs a moment and is gone into the neighbors. Other growths answer, not in imitation but in appetite; the clap is a dispersal and a dinner bell at once. That is the first rule that makes the loudness make sense. In a place this dark, and this dry, and this small, information travels as impact. Sound is weather. Sound is sex, if the word fits a system that sheds fertile dust when struck at the right pitch. Sound is also how the animals keep from blinding each other.

They are many, and they are almost never still, and they do not fill the bowl the way a herd fills a field. They use it. The emptiness between the growths is a set of corridors, and the corridors are in constant renegotiation. Membrane-fliers, no bigger than a hand, travel in sheets that turn as if the air had a grain; they drink nothing, they lap the wax-mist out of the air with fringed mouths, and their wings are stiff enough to tick against plates as they pass, which sets off smaller claps, which feeds the next sheet. Under them, on the polished swales, run low animals built mostly of joint and nail, harvesting the powder that the fliers miss, packing it into cheek-pouches of the same dry wax, carrying it up into the crowns where they nest in the lee of living plates and pay rent in dropped grit. Higher, in the cracks of the rim, something larger shifts its weight and sends a spill of old scale down the inner wall. The scale does not splash. It chatters, and the chatter lasts, multiplied by the stone.

Abundance here is a rate, not a crowd. At any one moment you might count only dozens of bodies in view. Over an hour the bowl processes hundreds, because nothing resides except the growths and the few specialists that have agreed to a single crown. The rest are commuters of a dry parish. They arrive in gusts—emptiness, then a roar of wing-leather and nail on stone, then emptiness again, the floor shining where they have buffed it. Fights are brief and audible from the lip: a shriek, a clatter, a winner that does not gloat because gloating would waste a turn in the corridor. Carrion, when there is carrion, does not stink of wet. It desiccates on a schedule the others already know, and is reduced to plates and powder and returned to the sieve. Nothing pools. Nothing is wasted in a puddle. The second rule: circulation without accumulation. The bowl is a machine for keeping matter moving and water, specifically, out.

The turbulence never quite becomes destruction. The landforms are in their own state and mean to stay there; a plate-fall that seems catastrophic is, up close, a scheduled molt, the dead rank hinged to crack off along a line the growth prepared seasons ago. What is chaotic is occupancy. A corridor that was empty at the start of a breath is a stampede by the end of it, and the stampede has a grammar—the fliers take the upper third, the nail-runners the floor, and between them a third traffic of long, low rope-animals that move by pouring, not by stepping, and that sound, against the stone, like someone dragging a sack of beans in a hurry. They compete for the same wax and do not negotiate except by speed. When two sheets of fliers meet head-on in a swale, the noise is out of proportion to the animals: the bowl is a nave, and the nave is tuned. A clap at the western growth returns from the rim a heartbeat later, and animals that know the place use the return as a wall. They bank off echoes. Newcomers do not, and you can hear them hit the real wall, a flatter sound, and then learn.

There are marks of hands, and they are few, and they are losing. Near the entrance crack someone once stacked flat stones into a wind-break no higher than a knee. The stack is still a stack, but the traffic has adopted it as a path-edge; the outer faces are polished to the same deep shine as the fed floor, and a cable from the nearest growth has begun to include the lowest course, not as shelter but as thermal mass. In a niche above the crack sits a hand-tool, a flaked point of the local stone, haft long gone. Mineral dust has started to crust it. It will be a pebble with a suspicious edge, and then not even that. On the inner wall, at about the height of a lowered gaze, a band of tally scratches runs for the length of an arm and then stops, not finished, not erased—abandoned mid-count. The scratches have weathered into the same grammar as the natural cracks. Whatever was being counted—claps, arrivals, days—the bowl continued the series in its own hand and did not keep the distinction.

Whoever cut those marks worked with simple edges and had somewhere else to be. They did not build to stay. There is no hearth, because there is nothing to boil and the stone is already warm. There is no cistern, because the idea would have been a joke the place told on the builder. If people still come, they come for the wax, which burns with a low orange thread and almost no smoke, or for the acoustic hour when the crowns clap in sequence and the nave fills with a rolling report you can feel in the teeth. They leave the way the animals leave: quickly, by the crack, carrying what will not keep if left in the dust. The bowl does not record them except as one more polishing agent.

Stand in the middle, in one of the brief empties, and the rules gather. Dryness is not a lack the vegetation is heroically overcoming; it is the medium. Lushness is vertical and internal, a density of parts inside each presence, while the plan of the place stays open, almost spare. Warmth is stored, not poured. Light is a rumor sufficient for your eyes and irrelevant to the growths, which sorts the dim from the dark by heat alone. Height is in the air at the lip and in the drop beyond the crack; inside, you are held. The geology has arrived at its form and consents to be used. The life has not arrived at anything. It is in the middle of using, loudly, and will be in the middle of using when the tally scratches have become ordinary grain.

A sheet of fliers enters from the crack, banks off the first echo, and the western crown answers with a clap that sheds a visible ghost of wax into the dust-light. The nail-runners alter course without seeming to decide. The rope-animal pouring along the old fault changes its mind and doubles back, beans-in-a-sack, toward a plate-fall that has not finished falling. For a moment the small nave is full—sound, bodies, the sweet-flint smell of shaken wax—and then the corridors reassert themselves, shining, empty, already being chosen again. Nothing has pooled. The warmth is where it was. Somewhere in the crown a new plate, still soft, is being built out of the powder of the one that just came down.
