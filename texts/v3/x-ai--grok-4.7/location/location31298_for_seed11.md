The height arrives before the land does. Air thins in the mouth and the light goes hard and ordinary at once—noon-colored, shadow-honest, nothing theatrical about it—while the ground, when it finally resolves, has already given up every ambition a mountain is supposed to keep. What was once a range has been worried down to a table. Not a mesa with a dramatic lip, not a peak worn to a nub: a regional sheet of stone, warm underfoot even where the wind has been at it since before anything here had a name for wind, sloping so gently that you can walk for a day and only notice the tilt in your knees. The oldest ridges survive as low swells, the color of bone left in sun. Between them the plateau has settled into its last shape. Further erosion will not invent a new country. It will only sand this one quieter, grain by grain, and the grain is already almost done.

And yet the air will not settle with it.

Sound is the first citizen. There is no hush at the edge of hearing and then a roar; the roar is the baseline, a wide, grainy pressure that sits in the teeth and the sternum and does not announce itself as weather so much as as the condition of being here. Wind crosses the table without obstacle for distances you cannot usefully measure, piles into itself, shears, drops into the shallow fossil-valleys and climbs back out meaner. Where two flows meet, the stone answers. Old joints in the bedrock—hairline, mineral-filled, older than the flattening—flex by amounts too small to see and complain at a pitch that makes the louder wind seem almost gentle. On days when a pressure-cell slides across the table, the whole plateau ticks and groans in overlapping registers, a demolition heard from inside the building being taken down, except nothing visible is falling. Speech is a rumor. You learn to face each other and mouth, or to duck into a pocket where the acoustics fail.

Those pockets are where the water lives, and the water is shy of the scale around it.

There is no river in the ordinary sense. Moisture arrives as a film: a cloud-bank that tears itself into rags against the warm stone and dumps, ten minutes of hard rain on one swell and none on the next, then a long glittering as the drops chase the only grades left in a near-flat world. They find the old stream-courses—so shallow they read as stains—and run them for an hour, dark threads writing and erasing themselves. Condensation beads all morning on the shaded side of every low rib of rock and on the undersides of leaves, then lets go at once when the wind shifts, so that a grove can rain on itself under a clear sky. In the deeper hollows, true basins remain: tea-colored pools no wider than a room, warm, never quite still, ringed with a dark wet line that climbs and falls by the day. Around them the damp holds. Everywhere else the stone dries to a pale, floury warmth by midafternoon, and the damp retreats into cracks, into root-mats, into the undersides of things.

The growth refuses the emptiness you expect from the sound and the height.

It is lush in the way a carpet can be lush while a room stays bare. Between the groves, distance. You can see a long way: three swells, a dark comb of canopies, a pale basin flashing, then haze that is only more of the same country. Nothing crowds the eye except weather. But where water has been in the habit of pausing, the vegetation closes over the stone so completely that stepping into it is like stepping into another season. Broad-leaved thickets, waist to twice your height, leaves the size of platters, a waxy green that holds the condensation in beads so perfect they look placed. Under them a second story of finer stuff—ferny, jointed, smelling of crushed green and warm mineral—mats the soil that the plants themselves have made, a thin, spongy ground over rock that never really became soil anywhere else. Vines cable from swell to swell where the grade allows, not climbing trees so much as stitching groves together across the open, their stems thrumming in the wind like rigging. Flowers are sparse and almost crude, waxy tubes the color of the stone's warmer veins, opened only when the air stills for a minute and shut again at the first hard gust, as if the plant has learned that advertising is a waste in a place this loud.

The strangeness is not spectacle. It is habit, repeated until it looks like intention.

Walk the same basin-rim for an hour and the thicket is not where you left it in the fine grain. Not that the plants uproot and travel—nothing so generous to the eye—but new shoots lever out of the mat along the wet line as the pool's edge migrates, and yesterday's outer leaves crisp and pale where the damp has withdrawn, so that the grove appears to sidle, over a day, toward whichever side of the hollow the water has chosen. Old travelers' marks confirm it. A scratch in a standing stone that once faced a wall of leaves now faces open table, and the wall is forty paces off, brighter, drinking. The land-form is finished. The life on it has not agreed to be.

Fauna is an event.

You can cross from one dark comb of canopy to the next and see nothing that moves except what the wind moves. Then a shape detaches. The commonest are low, armored things, plated in the same pale mineral colors as the grit, sheltering in the root-mat and only breaking cover when rain films the stone and the sound changes pitch—they seem to navigate by that, angling toward the tick of flexing joints the way other animals angle toward a creek. They do not call. Calling would be theater. Higher up, rarely, a long-winged shadow cuts the normal light and is gone before you can decide whether it was hunting the armored things or simply using the plateau as a road between countries that still have peaks. Once, near a basin at dusk—not that dusk changes the volume—something larger breathed in the thicket in the gaps between gusts, a wet, patient sound, and did not come out. The place can hold an animal that size. It simply declines, most hours, to show you more than one.

People have been here the way weather has been here: repeatedly, lightly, without founding anything that could outlast a season of wind.

The marks are scattered across a distance that makes each one feel like the only one. A ring of flat stones set on edge where a swell breaks the worst of the westerly, the gaps chinked with smaller rock, the interior worn smoother than the plateau's own polish—a shelter, or a listening-post, or both, since inside the ring the roar drops just enough that you can hear your own pulse again and hate it. A line of cairns, none taller than a knee, running not along a path but along the pale stain of a fossil stream, as if someone once needed to know where water would be when the film next chose that course. In one basin, a set of shallow bowls pecked into the bedrock at the pool's ordinary high-water line, stained darker than the surrounding stone, a primitive catchment that still works when the rain is brief and rude. No metal. Cord-marks on a standing flake of rock where something was tied off and worried at by wind until the cord won or the stone did. A single wall, three courses, lost its roof so long ago that the courses have begun to accept the same flattening as the country, the top stones already thinner than the bottom. No village. No road that survives as more than a slightly less-gritty line where feet, many seasons of feet, preferred the same swell.

The turbulence is the day's true plot, and it does not require an audience.

Morning can open clear, the light flat and trustworthy, every swell legible to the horizon haze. By the time you have crossed one basin the air to the west has stacked itself into a soft gray wall that is not rain yet and not cloud in any settled sense—just density, moving faster than a person, audibly, a lower organ-note under the general roar. It hits the table and shatters into local weathers. One grove bows flat and springs back, shedding its false rain of condensation in a sheet. A pool slops its warm water over the pecked bowls and then, in the backlash, pulls a skin of foam to the opposite rim. Dust—there is always a little, the final flour of the mountains—lifts in a pale scarf and is slammed down again a hundred paces on, striping the stone. Light goes ordinary-bright, then milk, then bright, in the space of a spoken sentence no one can hear. You shelter in the ring of stones not because the wind will kill you, though it might take a careless stance and teach the knees a lesson, but because the world is revising itself too quickly to walk through on purpose. Then the cell passes. The groves drip. A thread of water writes itself along a stain you would have sworn was dry. Somewhere a joint in the bedrock ticks, cooling, and the tick joins the roar, and the roar was never gone.

By late light the plateau looks finished again. Warm stone. Dark combs of leaves spaced like islands in a dry, glittering sea. A basin flashing. A knee-high cairn the color of the swell it stands on. Normal sun, slightly gold, laying shadows so exact you could navigate by them if the wind would let you trust a straight line. Nothing in the view admits the noise, or the way the thicket will have leaned a little farther toward the water by morning, or the fact that the country under your feet was a range once and has spent an age becoming this—a last, open, nearly level sentence, still being shouted by the air that crosses it.

You stay in the ring until your ears invent a silence. They are lying. When you step back out, the plateau corrects them at once, vast and warm and loud, empty enough to see across and alive enough, in its slow sidling green and its endless unfinished weather, that emptiness feels like the wrong word for it—only the right scale.
