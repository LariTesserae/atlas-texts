The floor is not stone. It gave up being stone so long ago that the word only survives in the smell—a mineral ghost under warm leaf-rot—whenever a seam splits and something mineral briefly shows. What you walk on is root-plate: fused, callused, sanded by centuries of feet and by the slow rasp of the place against itself. It holds. It has held. The chamber is not large. From one sweating wall to the other you could count your steps and not lose the number, and yet the ceiling is lost. Not high in the way of a nave. Lost the way a throat is lost when you look up it and the light fails before the opening.

There is almost no light. What there is comes in threads, green-gold and tired, from splits in the overburden where the world above still remembers sun. The threads do not reach the floor. They die in the vapor a body’s-length overhead, so the lower half of the room is always dusk and the upper half is a dim aquarium of hanging color. Warmth lives down here without a source you can point to. It is body-warmth scaled up: the heat of metabolism, of wet wood working, of machines that have been running longer than anyone’s calendar. Sweat starts at the collarbone and never quite dries.

The walls are orchards that forgot to stop. Trunks have grown into one another until the distinction between tree and masonry is a courtesy. Galleries run through them—cut, then healed over, then cut again—so that a corridor is also a living beam, and a doorway is a place where two boles agreed, long ago, to leave a gap. The growth is total, but it is not thick in the way of a jungle press. Between the great presences there is room. You can see across. The air is full of space, and the space is full of animals, and the animals do not crowd the architecture so much as occupy every pause in it.

They are not one kind. That is the first wrongness, and it does not resolve. On the root-plate, pale crabs the size of hands conduct a traffic that has rules: they yield at the seams, they stack when the floor flexes, they abandon a lane the moment a drip begins. Above them, in the mid-air where the light dies, moths the color of engine oil circle in columns so dense they throw a shadow on the vapor. The columns move. They are not random. Each column tracks a warm updraft off a housing or a vent, and when the housing changes pitch the column shears and reforms downwind of the new heat. Higher still, in the lost ceiling, something larger turns. You hear the wingbeat before you admit you hear it. A slow, leather slap, regular as a piston, never landing in the visible room.

Sound is the true weather. Nothing here is quiet, and nothing here shouts once and stops. The baseline is a layered industrial hum that has been weathered into the key of the chamber: not a single engine but a choir of them, buried in the plant-mass, their housings skinned over with bark until only grilles and oil-dark mouths remain. They do not seem tended. They seem incorporated. Sap beads on casings and cooks to a sugar crust. Condensation runs down flywheels and is thrown off as a fine warm rain that never quite becomes weather—just a local, repeating wet, a pocket of water that exists for the length of a gear-cycle and then is flung back into air. Between pulses the floor drinks it. The root-plate is damp in lanes and dry on the rises, a map that redraws itself every few minutes.

When a machine loads, the pitch drops and the crabs freeze. When it unloads, a shiver goes through the nearer trunks and a rain of small fruit—or what passes for fruit, hard green knuckles—patters onto the plate and is taken. Not eaten in any mouth you can see. Taken: rolled into seams, handed off, vanished into the living floor as if the floor had indentures to fill. The transaction is old. The machines and the trees have had time to learn each other’s tempos. Erosion here is not ruin. It is fluency. Edges that once cut have been rounded by growth and by use until a valve-wheel is also a burl, and a catwalk is a path worn into the top of a horizontal limb, railed with living suckers that tighten when weight comes onto them and slack when it leaves.

There is a settlement in it, if you accept the word at this scale. Not streets. Stations. A dozen clearings in the root-plate, each no bigger than a room, each with its own machine-throat and its own animal office. In one, the moth-columns are thickest and the air tastes of hot metal and nectar. In another, the crabs are absent and a low herd of something like salamanders—glass-bodied, lantern-organed, too many legs—pulses in a circuit around a dripping coupling, drinking the condensate and leaving phosphorescent tracks that fade before the circuit completes. In a third, the wingbeats from the ceiling come down close enough that the vapor dents, and whatever is up there feeds without descending: a tongue, or a tube, or a root that learned the trick of a tongue, lowers, takes a measure of the glass-herd’s light, and withdraws. The herd does not scatter. This is a tithe, not a hunt. It has been paid long enough that panic would be the strange behavior.

Water never gathers into a stream you could name. It argues with itself. A seep on the left wall builds a skin across a metal lip, fails, and sheets down into a crotch of roots that hold it for an hour as a dark mirror. In the mirror the moth-columns look like smoke going the wrong direction. Then the roots drink, or a change in engine pitch shakes the sheet apart, and the mirror is gone, and the damp migrates three steps to the right where a housing weeps. Occasional true rain arrives from above—you hear it first as a change in the ceiling’s rhythm, a softer percussion joining the leather slap—and for a little while the threads of light thicken, and every surface gives off the smell of warmed resin. Then the overburden closes its splits again, or the rain was never more than a passing cell, and the chamber returns to its own water: mechanical, biological, brief.

The weirdness is not a creature at the edge. It is the grammar. The place has rules, and the rules are consistent, and they are not the rules of a cave or a factory or a grove. Load and unload. Freeze and traffic. Tithe and circuit. Growth sealing over what it has decided to keep. Animals in numbers that should be a plague, arranged instead into offices, loud with wing and claw and gear, and still the sightlines open, the floor readable, the distances short. You are never far from a wall. You are never without a witness. Something in the lost ceiling keeps time with the largest engine, and the largest engine keeps time with a pulse in the root-plate, and the root-plate’s pulse is the combined step of everything that walks it.

If you stay, your own breathing joins the choir whether you mean it to or not. The warm air is already full. There is room for you the way there is room between the trunks: not empty, only unoccupied for the moment, a pause in a mass that has been arranging itself longer than the cut of any tool. The tool-marks are still there, under the callus. Someone built this, or began it. The chamber finished the work, and then kept finishing it, and the finishing is what you are standing in—small, dim, loud, damp at the seams, warm as a held breath, and alive in every direction you have a sense for.
