The place does not begin at its edge. It begins where your hand is, and then notices it has always been that hand.

You find it by missing a step that should have been stone. The mountain continues around the miss—wind, pale rock, a drop that does not bother to finish—but the miss itself is occupied. A pocket. A seam. A room that was cut, or rather packed, into the crown of something too high to have a crown, and then filled until filling became the only architecture. There is no threshold worth naming. One moment the air is thin enough to sting the teeth; the next it is thick, hot, and close, and the thinness has not left. Both airs occupy the same mouthful. You breathe summit and you breathe bathhouse. The lungs do not agree on which report to send.

It is dark in the way of a closed hand held up to a lamp. Light comes, but it arrives already used—filtered through layers of woven matting, through wet clay daub, through leaves that have been tied, not grown in any open sense, into every gap the weave allowed. What light remains is the color of old brass left in steam. It does not travel. It sits on surfaces and makes them glisten, then gives up. You learn the place by touch and by noise before you learn it by sight.

Everything you touch has been made.

That is the first rule, and it does not soften. There is no native bedrock here, though the room insists it is a mountain. The floor is a pavement of hand-split slabs, edges still sharp, still pale where the stone was shocked apart, fitted so tightly that a fingernail finds no seam except where a maker wanted water to move. The walls are courses of the same stone, chinked with fiber and mud and strips of bark hammered soft, then plastered over with a clay that has not yet decided to dry. In places the plaster is newer than your arrival: a thumb-drag still holds its ridge, and the ridge is sweating. The ceiling is a close basket of poles lashed with root and twisted grass, so low that standing is a negotiation, and over the basket a skin of oiled leaf, and over that more clay, and over that—if “over” means anything—the mountain’s weather, which never quite becomes this room’s weather and never quite stays outside it.

No surface has been worn. Corners are embryonic. Nothing here has a past long enough to round a lip or undercut a course. The geology is in the act of being invented with stone tools and wet hands. Fresh breaks glitter. Fresh bindings creak as they tighten in the heat. A slab near your knee is only half-set: it lifts a fraction if you lean, and underneath it something else is being laid, a course that should not have room to exist, stone sliding into a gap that was not a gap until the stone needed it. You hear the work more often than you see the worker. A knock of rock on rock. The dry scuff of a cord being pulled. The soft, repeated slap of mud. Sometimes a breath that is not yours, close, patient, already gone when you turn. Fauna, if that breath belongs to anything living, is almost not here. What passes for life is mostly the place itself, and a few stubborn tenants.

Water is everywhere the making is unfinished, which is everywhere. It does not fall so much as appear. Joints weep. The undersides of leaves grow beads so quickly that the beads seem to be the leaves’ idea of edge. A warm film covers the stone and makes every step a small decision. Somewhere under the pavement a channel has been thumbed into the clay, and water moves through it with the hurry of a thing that has just learned downhill. You can hear it change its mind. It goes left, then the left becomes up, and a thread of it breaks through a wall-course at chest height and runs sideways along a lashing before dropping into a cup of leaf that was not a cup a moment ago. The air is past the point of holding. It condenses on your arms, on the ceiling poles, on the insides of sounds. Heat sits in it the way heat sits in a kiln after the fire has been banked but not forgotten—heavy, biological, not the clean cold of altitude and not willing to admit the contradiction.

The plants are a crowd. They have been installed, not discovered: bundles of reed pushed into clay pockets, mats of moss pressed into the mortar while it was still alive, vines trained along the lashings and already trying the knots. Fern-forms, or things that have agreed to look like ferns for now, uncurl against the dimness in a packed green that leaves no honest interval. A leaf touches a leaf touches a wet stone touches your sleeve, and none of them yields the space. Where a gap tries to open, a new shoot occupies it, or a hand—or the memory of a hand—packs more fiber in. The lushness is not wilderness. It is saturation as a craft. Sap smears. Cut stems still bleed. Some of the growth is so new it has no color yet, only a translucence, like the place is extruding its own shade.

And still it is loud.

The loudness is not a single weather. It is several processes refusing to take turns. Water ticks and runs and suddenly pours, a cup’s worth, from a ceiling joint into a basin that is also a folded leaf and also, if you look too long, a course of stone that has not been cut yet. The lashings tick as they shrink and swell. Stone answers stone with small, bright reports, as if the room is being tapped into tune. Leaves slap. Steam sighs off the hotter faces of the clay. Under that, a deeper sound, not quite geological: the compression of too much material in too little room, a packed creak, the noise of a sack filled past sense. Occasionally the altitude intrudes—a high, thin whistle, the outside wind finding a pinprick in the leaf-skin and arriving as a blade of cold that dies within a hand’s breadth, boiled off, turned into another bead. The cold and the heat meet and the meeting is audible, a soft continual crackle, like green wood thinking about fire.

Scale will not settle. Crouch, and the room is a den, an intimate worked hollow, everything within arm’s reach because everything was placed by arms. Stand in the way the ceiling allows, and the same slabs recede into a slope that has weather on it. A fern that was brushing your wrist is, without moving, a stand of growth on a wet face far enough away that its detail should blur, and it does not blur; it simply is both distances. Your foot, set on a fresh-split paver, is also set on a ridge. The drop you missed outside is inside the basin of leaf-water, a depth of a thumb and a depth that would take a body. Neither measurement wins. The place keeps both, the way a knot keeps every turn of the cord.

This is where the world frays, not as spectacle but as labor. The makers—if they are makers, and not the room’s way of remembering hands—are building a mountain inside the fact of a mountain, at a size that should only hold a sleeper, with tools that leave tooth-marks: stone hammers, antler wedges, cords of twisted grass, clay mixed with spit and ash and leaf-fiber. They are not repairing. There is nothing old enough to repair. They are causing the substrate. A new stone appears in a course you had already counted. Its break-face is brighter than the light. For a moment it is not stone. It is the idea of resistance, still warm from being decided, and then it is stone, and water finds it, and a moss-hand presses green into its joint before the joint has cooled.

Gravity is local and recent. In one corner it pulls toward the floor. Two steps on, it pulls toward the wall, so that the planted reeds grow sideways with perfect conviction, and the water obeys them, sheeting along the vertical as if vertical had applied for the job of down and been accepted. Loose grit—there is almost no loose grit; erosion has not been hired—does not fall. A chip struck from a slab hangs, then drifts toward the newest surface, eager to belong to it. Dropped things are adopted. The room is hungry to be continuous.

Time is worse. The plaster by your shoulder dries, cracks in a fine net, and is wet again, not from rain but because the drying has been unscheduled. A leaf yellows at the tip and the yellow retreats, ashamed, back into green. You hear a cord being tied, and the knot is already old in the fiber, and then the cord is still a loose end in someone’s absence. Construction and weather and growth run concurrently, not in sequence, so the room is post-rain and mid-pour and not-yet-rained-on in the same glance. Shadows do not match their objects. Your own shadow sometimes arrives on the clay before you lean. Sometimes it stays on a stone you have left, keeping your shape as a stain of less-light, until a new course is laid through it and the stain becomes part of the joint, a darker mud, structural.

Smell is a body. Hot green. Wet mineral. The sweet rot of fiber just at the edge of going, arrested there, not allowed to become soil because soil would imply a before. Crushed leaf. The faint iron of struck stone. No smoke, or only the ghost of a fire that was used to harden a clay lip and then folded back into not-having-happened. Breathing is work. The air is too full. It carries pollen that has no flowers yet, and the pollen sticks, and in the dimness the motes do not float so much as crowd, a visible packing of the volume, each speck bright only when it crosses the brass remainder of the light.

What lives here is rare and looks accidental, though nothing in this room is accidental except the accidents the making requires. A pale insect, almost the color of fresh stone, walks the underside of a leaf and is the only animal you are sure of—then it steps onto a surface that is still becoming surface, and it is a crumb of mineral, and it was never an insect, and the leaf ticks as if relieved. Farther in, where the channel-water gathers in a thumb-deep pool that is also a tarn, something moves under the film: a pulse, a muscle of water, not a fish. It keeps the pool from closing. When it stills, the water begins to set like the clay, a skin, and the pulse moves again and the skin splits with a small sound like a seed. You do not see a second creature. You begin to suspect the room counts its own motions as fauna so it will not be alone at this height.

Rules, if you stay long enough to collect them:

The room is finished only in the sense that it is full. Emptiness is the one material that will not take. Any gap is a task.

Water seeks the newest joint. Heat seeks the newest water. Growth seeks whatever the water has just claimed.

Nothing erodes. Change happens by addition, by the intrusion of a fresh face, by a course laid through an older course that was never allowed to age. Wear is refused. Even your footprints mist over and are pressed out by the slow flex of the pavement, which is still settling into the idea of bearing weight.

Outside remains outside only by courtesy. A whistle of high wind, a bleaching of light at a pinhole, the knowledge—bodily, unpleasant—that the true summit is a knife of cold air a palm’s thickness away, through clay and leaf and basket. The pocket does not leak upward. The mountain does not invade. They overlap, and the overlap is this heat, this packed green, this unfinished stone.

Sound outruns its cause. You will hear the slap of mud before the place where mud will be. Do not move toward the sound expecting a maker. The maker is the interval between the sound and the wet new surface, and the interval is shorter each time.

If you close your eyes, the scale rushes. The pocket becomes a country of dripping shelves, and you are a mite in the mortar. Open them, and a leaf is on your mouth, and the country is the width of a sleeping body, and both accounts are filed as true. The dizziness is not a failure of balance. Balance has not been built yet. It is next, perhaps. A wedge is waiting in the clay, pale, unused, already warm.

Stay, and the room continues its work around you and, with a craftsman’s lack of sentiment, through the space you occupy. A cord brushes your wrist and tightens on something you cannot see, including you among the lashings for a moment, then thinks better of it—or worse, and leaves the pressure, a bracelet of twisted grass, new, unfrayed, humming faintly with the water in its fibers. The light does not improve. The heat does not break. Somewhere under the slabs the channel finds a stone that did not exist when the channel started, and the water shouts, a bright small shout, glad of the obstacle, and turns, and begins again, uphill, toward a joint that is only now learning it was always meant to weep.
