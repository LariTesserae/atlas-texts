The rim is ordinary country, and that is what makes the drop feel wrong. Up here the stone has already decided what it is: a pale, wind-scoured bench, warm under a normal sky, with a few dark seams where last night’s rain still clings in the grain. There is no green. There has not been green. The bench gives way without ceremony—no cliff dressed by weather, only a fresh scarp, raw as a split log, and beyond it the land has sagged into a basin wide enough that the far wall is a heat-shimmer rather than a line.

You go down on stone that has not yet learned to be a path. The faces are sharp. Corners still hold the angle of their breaking. Pale dust lifts and does not travel far; the air below the rim is heavier, warmer, and already damp against the wrists. Daylight comes straight down. Nothing shades it. The basin is open to the same sun as the bench, only sunk, so the light arrives unsoftened and then loses itself among a thousand small surfaces instead of one.

The floor is not a floor. It is a region still arriving. In one reach the rock is a skin of volcanic glass, thin enough that a footfall stars it, and under the skin a dull red-brown clay breathes. In another the ground is a pavement of plates that have not quite separated, each plate warm, each joint a dark line of moisture. Farther on, a low swell of mineral curd has pushed up overnight and already begun to craze. Nothing here has been worn round. Erosion has barely been introduced to the place. What looks like age is only the speed of setting: mud skins, salt rims, a glaze where water stood at dawn and was gone by the time the shadow of the scarp had moved a hand’s width.

Water does not flow so much as occur. A pocket in the lee of a fresh block holds a warm, tea-colored sump, insects writing on it. Ten paces away the same weather has left only a dark map on the clay, already shrinking. Condensation pearls along the undersides of overhangs and falls when the stone ticks. Somewhere toward the middle of the basin a longer seep has found a weakness and is worrying it into a thread of a channel, but the thread keeps being interrupted—by a plate that rose this morning, by a slurry fan, by the simple fact that downhill has not finished being decided. Rain, when it comes, is brief and local. You can stand in it on one side of a glass ridge and watch the other side stay pale. The wet places steam a little. The dry places drink and forget.

There are no plants. Not a crust of moss, not a pioneer weed in a crack, not the ghost of a root. Whatever film feeds the basin is not a thing you would call vegetation: a mineral slick, a warm scum at the margins of the sumps, a faint iridescence on damp clay that smells of metal and yeast. It is enough. It is more than enough. The basin is crowded past the point where you can hold the crowd as a view.

The air over the warm pockets is a continuous grain of wings. Midges rise in columns that bend when you walk through them and reform before your foot has left the print. Beetles the color of wet slag work the edges of every sump, so many that the margins seem to crawl of their own accord. Larger things hunt the grain. Lizards the length of a hand hold the new ledges in such numbers that the ledges look furred, and when one glass plate ticks in the heat they all shift at once, a single dry whisper. Birds come and go from the scarps in sheets—not flocks passing over, but a local population that treats the unfinished walls as a city of niches. Their shadows stripe the floor and lift and stripe it again. In the shallows, something between a frog and a flat stone blinks by the hundred, throats pulsing, each one stationed on its own coin of warm water. Higher on the rubble, denser still: a pale scurrying that might be mammals small enough to live between plates, visible mostly as motion and as the constant small collapse of dust. You cannot find an empty surface large enough to lay a hand without first clearing it. Near-touching is the ordinary distance. Layered is the ordinary arrangement—fliers over climbers over waders over whatever works the film underneath the film.

The noise is not weather and not quite animal, though both are in it. The stone is still speaking. Plates cool and answer each other with ticks that carry across the open floor. A slurry tongue somewhere out of sight slumps with a wet clap. Glass spalls. Under that, and louder, the living: the high seethe of wings, the knock of beaks on fresh edges, the racket of throats at every sump, a dry corporate hiss when a ledge of lizards relocates. Sound is how you map the place before your eyes do. A bright patch of noise means water that has not yet sunk. A sudden drop in the seethe means a plate is about to move, and the grain of wings has already lifted clear. The basin is turbulent in the way a market is turbulent, or a nest disturbed and not yet re-settled—creation and small destruction running on the same clock, nothing holding still long enough to become scenery.

People have been here, or something with hands and a short patience for tools. Not a town. Not a ruin in the old sense. Scattered, and already being argued with by the ground. On a stable-looking bench halfway down the near scarp someone has knocked a ring of slabs into a windbreak one body wide; two of the slabs have tilted since, because the bench they trusted is still deciding whether to be a bench. Inside, a scatter of chipped flakes, a core the size of a fist, a cord of twisted fiber left in a crack where damp will ruin it. Farther out, on the floor itself, a line of scratches on a glass plate—tallies, or a map of sumps that moved the next day and made the map a lie. A single leaning shelter of three rocks and a hide, the hide already stiff, the rocks already walking apart by a finger’s width as the clay beneath them swells. No metal. No mortar. The work is early: strike, bind, stack, leave. Whoever made these marks seems to use the basin the way the birds do, in bursts, and not to trust it with anything they mean to find again.

The rules, if the place has rules, are still being written in front of you, and they do not match the country above the rim.

Nothing roots, because nothing holds its shape long enough to be a soil. Food is film and flesh and the warm margins of water that will not be there by evening. The animals have not waited for a proper world. They breed in the interval between a seep and its disappearance, nest on ledges that spall, follow the noise of setting stone the way other animals follow green-up. Density is not a sign of richness so much as of a narrow living: everything pressed into the wet places and the new edges, everything eating everything else at a speed that matches the speed of the ground.

Downhill is provisional. A channel begun at noon may be a ridge by dusk if a plate rises under it. Rain does not make rivers here; it makes events, and the events are fought over before they soak away. Shade does not exist except as the moving shadow of wings and of your own body. Light is plain daylight, and that plainness is part of the exposure—no canopy, no depth of cave, only a sunk country lying open under the same sun, steaming where it is wet, ticking where it is dry.

What is built does not outlast the mood of the clay. A shelter is a wager. A scratched map is a record of a basin that has already ceased to be the basin it records. Hands come down the fresh scarp with simple tools, take flies and flat-stone frogs and the warm mineral slick if they can stomach it, and go up again before the ledge they climbed has finished cracking. The ones that stay too long leave only flakes.

Stand long enough and the basin changes while you are using it as a landmark. A sump skins over. A bird-city on the far wall lifts all at once for no reason you can see, wheels, and resettles thirty paces to the left, where a new warm seep has declared itself by noise alone. A plate near your feet gives a single clean tick and drops a thumb’s width, and the lizards on it do not even break their stance. The air smells of wet stone, salt, and the sweet-metal reek of too many small bodies in a warm enclosed weather. Above, on the rim, the ordinary bench waits, finished and empty. Below, the country is still being born, already loud, already full, already refusing to stay the shape you just described.
