The room is smaller than the noise suggests. You come into it through a door that seals behind you with a soft hydraulic kiss you cannot hear, and for a moment the place seems only a wet box of iron: a gallery no wider than a corridor, a ceiling you could touch if you stood on the lower run of pipe, walls of riveted plate still bright at the edges where the mill scale has not yet been worn away. Nothing here has had time to round. The welds are proud. Bolt heads throw hard little shadows. The floor plates have not been polished by boots; their raised diamond pattern is still sharp enough to catch a sole.

Then the water declares itself, and the box opens.

It does not open outward. It opens into itself. Along the left wall a bank of copper coils weeps continuously, not from a leak so much as from a decision: chilled water inside, cool air outside, and the air cannot hold what it is given. Beads form, merge, and run in bright threads down the fins into a trough that was cut into the plate and finished with the same care as the rest of the work. The trough carries the water to a sump the size of a bath, set into the floor at the far end. The sump never fills and never empties. A pump the color of old oil lifts it and sends it back up through a header you can follow with your eye the whole length of the gallery, a single straight run of pipe no thicker than your wrist, jointed every few paces with flanges still silver at the edges.

This is the whole landscape. There is no view beyond it. The high place it belongs to exists only as pressure in the ears and as a thinness in the breath: the plant sits above the weather, and this gallery is one of the rooms that makes that possible, a pocket cut into the service spine where the cooling circuit turns a corner and must be watched. Outside, if there is an outside, would be wind and a long drop. In here the elevation is only the fact that water wants, very slightly, to leave the air, and that the machines are built to argue with that want without end.

The light is a row of caged bulbs, half of them dark, the rest the color of weak tea. They do not illuminate so much as locate. Under them the wet metal takes what it can: a dull coin-shine on the trough, a colder gleam on the copper, and then long stretches where the plate drinks the light and gives nothing back. Steam from a vent near the ceiling — not a failure, a designed bleed — drifts and thins and never quite becomes fog. It beads on the bulbs and on your sleeves. The air is cool enough that the wet feels colder than it is. Your breath does not show, but your hands do, after a minute, in the way the skin tightens.

Sound is the climate. It does not come from one engine. It comes from the agreement of many small ones, each doing a simple thing at a rate that will not slow while the plant is alive. The pump in the sump knocks a steady double beat, a mechanical heart with no variation you can detect. Above it, somewhere in the header, water hammers at every reversal of a valve you cannot see: a blow like a fist on a beam, then the ringing after, then the blow again. Air moves through a louver at the ceiling in a roar that would be wind if wind had been machined to a tolerance. Droplets tick the trough in thousands, and the trough answers with a thin continuous pour into the sump, and the sump slaps the intake. None of these sounds yields to any other. They occupy the room the way furniture would, except there is no furniture, and no corner quiet enough to stand in and think. After a while you stop trying to separate them. The gallery has one voice, and it is loud enough that the door, the walls, the idea of a larger building outside, all recede. The pocket becomes the world because nothing else can be heard through it.

Between the presences there is more room than the noise admits. The coils occupy one wall. The header occupies the upper third. The sump occupies the end. Everything else is plate and space and the shine of water moving across plate. You can walk the length without brushing anything if you keep to the middle, and the middle is empty on purpose: a maintenance path, painted once in a yellow that has already begun to blush rust at the edges, though the paint is new enough that the brush marks still show. Young corrosion. Young everything. The place has not learned its own stains yet. Where a weep has been running only weeks, the copper below it is green in a narrow honest line; elsewhere the metal is still the color it left the shop. A scuff on a flange. A chalk number, half wiped, on the sump rim. No moss has taken the joints. No litter has collected in the corners, because the corners are still being watched, or because the water is too clean and too restless to let anything settle.

What vegetation there is does not belong to a plan so much as to a patience. In the seam where the trough meets the wall, a dark green film has begun — not a plant you could name from a path, a slick of algae no thicker than paint, living on the drip and the dimness and whatever mineral the pipes give up. A second patch, smaller, has taken the lip of the sump on the side away from the intake, where the water laps but does not scour. That is all. No stems. No leaves casting shadow. The green is a stain that breathes, and it is scattered, two presences in a room of iron, with all the unclaimed metal between them shining wet and bare. Nothing eats it. Nothing walks it. If anything alive moves here besides that film, it is too small and too quiet to register against the pump, and the gallery does not seem to require it. The processes are mechanical and hydrological, and they are sufficient.

They do not rest. A valve above the header cycles on a timer you could almost learn: a hiss, a shudder in the pipe, the hammer-blow, a brief higher note as pressure equalizes, then the return to the knock of the pump and the roar of the louver. The weep on the coils never pauses, but it changes — thicker when the bleed-vent sighs, thinner when a draft from the louver shears the droplets sideways so they strike the fins instead of falling clean. The sump's surface is never still. It carries concentric shivers from the pour, and a slower bulge when the pump takes a gulp, and an occasional oil-bright skin that tears and reforms. Condensation on the ceiling plate gathers, fails, and falls in heavy drops that explode on the floor path and are gone into the seams before you can watch them travel. The room is always in the middle of several weathers at once, all of them indoor, all of them made.

There is no natural ground under any of it. Lift a floor plate — they are meant to lift — and you find more plate, a lower tray pitched to a drain, and under that the structural steel of the spine, painted, bolted, numbered. The numbers match a drawing somewhere. The wet that escapes the trough is not soaking a mountainside; it is being collected, measured by the simple fact of the pump's labor, and returned to the circuit. Even the algae is an accident inside a specification. The high cold outside has been reduced, in this pocket, to a temperature the machines prefer and a humidity they cannot quite refuse. They answer it with copper, with troughs, with a sump that has no mud, with flanges sharp enough to cut if you drag a hand wrong.

Stand long enough and the deafening becomes a kind of walls. You stop flinching at the hammer. You begin to notice the small deviations that mean the system is not a recording: a slightly longer hiss, a drop that falls early, the pump catching a bubble and knocking once out of time before it finds the beat again. The gallery is new, and it is already at work, and the work is the same work it will be doing when the green film has climbed a hand's breadth farther up the seam and the yellow paint has gone. For now the edges are still crisp, the path still mostly empty, the light still too little, the water still everywhere it was invited and in a few places it was not. The door is behind you. You could not hear it if it opened. The landscape continues, at the scale of a room, as long as the header runs and the sump answers and the coils keep giving the air back as rain.
