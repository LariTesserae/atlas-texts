The shelf is smaller than the weather that feeds it. From the ridge above you can miss it entirely: a crease in the high rock, no wider than a shout carries, hung on the warm face of the mountain where the day’s air first thickens and fails. Step down through the lip and the world closes. The far wall is close enough to read the seams in it. The sky is a slot.

The stone has been here long enough to stop arguing with water. It is pale, grainy, already half soil in the places hands would want to hold, and it weeps without drama. Seams darken, then shine, then let go a thread. The threads never hurry. They find the same paths they found last season and the season before that, darkening a grammar of stains that no longer changes. Where a thread meets a flatter place it spreads into a skin, warm as the air, and the skin slides off a low lip with a sound much larger than the water that makes it. That is the first loudness: not a fall, a continuous tearing, as if cloth were being pulled forever just out of sight.

Underfoot the ground is not mud and not pavement. It is a mat of old root and newer moss, springy, holding water the way a sponge holds it after you have already squeezed. Press and a clear thread runs out between your toes and rejoins a crease and is gone toward the lip. Nothing stands. The slope is the memory of a slope. Erosion finished its large work ages ago and now only keeps the books: a grain moved, a grain replaced, the same hollows in the same places, brimming and never quite overflowing into a stream you could name.

The plants are few and they are lavish. You can walk between them without bending. Each one takes a room of air for itself and fills that room completely. The common one is a shrub-tree with a trunk no thicker than a wrist and a crown like a tent of oiled leather, leaves broad, entire, dark on top and copper where the light comes through the edge. They do not touch. Between one crown and the next there is open air and the bright rock and the moving skin of water, so the lushness is never a thicket. It is a series of full stops. Under each crown the moss is deeper, and the dripping from the leaves is a second loudness, higher, irregular, a handful of clocks that do not agree. When the wind comes up the slot it does not roar in branches. It slaps the leather leaves one by one, and each slap is distinct, and then the grove is briefly a crowd of flags.

Nothing here seems to have been cut, piled, or set. There are no hearths, no chipped edges that read as tools, no trails worn by feet that return. If anyone has ever come, they came the way water comes, and left the same way. The only working is the mountain’s. Still, some of the plants look tended by a rule you cannot see. A ring of the leather-crowns always surrounds the loudest seep, none of them rooted in the shine itself, all of them leaning a little inward, as if the wet air at the center were a thing with weight. Their lower leaves are lacquered with a mineral film, pale green going to rust, and the film never flakes. It thickens, season by slow season, until a leaf is too heavy and folds under and is received by the moss without a tear.

The light is ordinary daylight and somehow local. It comes down the slot clean, not colored, and then it stalls in the wet. Surfaces give it back late. The wet rock is brighter than the sky it reflects. In the middle of the day the crowns throw hard small shadows, and between the shadows the moss burns a yellow-green that hurts if you look at it long. Toward evening the slot goes blue first, while the shelf still holds the last warm white on every wet face, so for a while the place is lit from its own floor.

Animals are almost an absence you can hear. No birds work the crowns. No insects stitch the air in any number you can count. Now and then a single click from the rock, a beetle or something like one, crossing a dry patch between seeps and gone. Once, if you wait, a narrow track in the moss where something low-bellied passed in the night and did not return; by afternoon the track has drunk itself shut. The loudness, then, is all water and leaf and the slow stone. It does not crest and it does not rest. Stand in the middle and it is around you at the volume of a river heard through trees, though the water in sight would not fill a bucket quickly. The emptiness of other lives makes the sound feel occupied, as if the dripping had taken the place of inhabitants and was not going to give it back.

Warmth sits low. The rock holds the day. Mist does not boil up in banks; it condenses in a continual fine sweat on anything that has been still, on the underside of a leaf, on the fine hairs of your arm, on the north side of each trunk. Breathe and the air is thick enough to notice, sweet with crushed moss and a cleaner smell like wet iron. Nothing rots in a hurry. Fallen leaves bronze and stay. A crown that died—there is one, near the back wall, its tent collapsed into a gray coracle—has been collapsing for what must be years, and the moss has climbed it without burying it. The pace of the shelf is the pace of that climb.

There is a strangeness, and it is not dramatic. It is that the water does not run away as much as it should. Follow any thread and it reaches the lip and the lip is loud and the thread continues over—and if you go to the edge and look down the outside face, the face is dry within a body’s length. The loudness falls into the notch and is not explained by what leaves. People who think in watersheds will stand there too long, trying to find the missing creek in the talus below, and they will not find it. The shelf keeps more than it spends. The crowns drink from the air and from the film on their own leaves, and what they drink does not reappear as sap you could tap or fruit you could name. In the cupped center of one leather leaf, if you lift it, the water sitting there is warmer than the air and very slightly thick, and it does not wet the finger so much as film it. Set the leaf down. The film is gone from the skin before you have walked to the next tree, and you will not be sure you felt it.

At the back, where the wall is closest, a single seam weeps harder than the others. The rock around it is scalloped, old water-work, settled into a shape it will still have when the crowns have replaced themselves many times. No pool. The water arrives and is taken into the mat at once. Put an ear near the seam and the loudness changes register: a low, grainy rush inside the stone, patient, not the sound of a cavity but of a great many small passages that have agreed, for a very long time, to be one passage. It does not vary while you listen. It will not vary while you sleep, if you slept here, which the place does not invite. There is nowhere level that is also dry. There is no windbreak that is not a living crown, and the crowns slap when the air moves. Night comes as a dimming of the slot, not as a shutting. The dripping goes on at the same rate. The iron-sweet smell strengthens. Whatever clicks in the day does not click in the dark; whatever moved in the moss does not move where you can hear it. The shelf continues its slow accounting in the open, loudly, with almost nothing alive to answer, holding its water against the dry face of the mountain as if that were the whole of the law, and had been, and would be.
