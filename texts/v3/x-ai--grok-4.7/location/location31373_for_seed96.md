The whole valley is a made thing, and it has not yet learned to look old.

From the rim you can see across it in one glance and still not see an end to the work. The light is high and hard, a cool white that finds every joint. There is no soil left, no parent rock showing through, no weed-line, no animal track. What holds the water is what was stacked, tamped, fired, and fitted by hand, and it fills the basin the way a kiln fills a yard: terrace against terrace, wall against wall, basin lipped to basin, no spare ground between one presence and the next.

The elevation is enough that the air thins the color out of distance. The far side of the valley is the same construction, only paler, a grid of pale shelves and dark water-slots climbing until the ridgeline itself is a parapet. Nothing has been allowed to weather into softness. Edges are still crisp. Corners still cut. The clay faces still carry the drag of the float that finished them. Where a course of stone was set yesterday or a century ago is hard to tell, because the slow work of water has only just begun to stain the joints, not to round them.

Water is everywhere and almost still. It does not fall in any voice you would call a stream. It stands in rectangular pools no wider than a body turned sideways, and it sheets, finger-thin, down faces of rammed earth sealed with ash and fat. It beads on the undersides of lintels. It collects in thumb-bowls cut into every coping, and from those bowls it is led, by a groove no deeper than a fingernail, into the next pool, and the next. The whole valley is a single slow argument about level. A film of water crosses a tread, pauses at a raised seam, finds the nick that was left for it, and continues. If you stand long enough you can watch one bright thread advance the length of a hand. That is the speed of the place.

The materials are few and repeated until repetition becomes weather. Fired brick, pale where the sun strikes and dark where the weep keeps it cold. Rubble set in clay mortar, the stones chosen small enough to lift, their faces left rough on purpose so the water will cling instead of sheet away. Tamped earth walls, layered in handsbreadths, each layer a slightly different grit, so that when they weep they weep in stripes. Cord lashings at the corners of timber frames—timber that must have been carried up, because nothing grows here—gone silver and tight, not yet slack. Ceramic pipes no longer than a forearm, bell-and-spigot, stacked in runs along the inner faces of walls, their joints packed with fiber that has not rotted so much as mineralized. Lime wash. Salt crust. A black smear where iron-bearing grit in the clay has wept and dried and wept again.

There is no plant life. The wet does not green. What you might mistake, at a distance, for moss is a mineral fur: a pale crust of salts and sintered clay-dust that blooms where seepage meets air and then hardens into a lace no thicker than paper. It breaks under a finger into glittering meal. In the pools the water is clear enough to show the floors, which are paved, every one of them, with shards set on edge in pitch and clay, a mosaic with no picture, only the instruction to keep the water from soaking out the bottom. Nothing swims. Nothing skims. A bubble rises sometimes from a joint and takes a long time to decide to break.

The density is the strangest ordinary fact. You cannot find a place to put a foot that was not placed for a foot. Treads, coombs, notches, little landings no larger than a kneeling-mat, all of them occupied by the next necessity: a jar braced in a niche, a wooden plug, a stack of spare bricks kept dry under a cantilever of stone, a coil of cord, a scraping-board, a bowl of the same pale meal that grows on the walls, gathered and not yet used. The builders’ things are still in the work, as if the work had paused mid-gesture. No one is here. The pause has lasted long enough that dust has settled in the dry lee of every upright, and not long enough for any of those tools to have been taken for something else.

Brightness does not mean warmth. The stone drinks the sun and gives back a clean cold. Shade, where a wall overlaps a wall, is blue and immediate. Your breath shows if you stand in it. Out on the open treads the light is almost metallic, glancing off every water-skin so that the valley, seen from above, is a field of narrow mirrors, none of them large, all of them aligned. When a thin cloud crosses, the mirrors go dull together, and the place becomes what it is without the glare: a packed instrument, hand-made, for holding water at a height and letting it down by patience.

Sound is mostly the absence of the sounds you expect from water. No birds. No wind with anything to move except a single cord-end that ticks, sometimes, against a brick. The water itself speaks only when a sheet breaks and reforms: a soft tick, a brief run of drops along a groove, then quiet again. Stand inside one of the narrower slots, where two walls nearly meet and the sky is a bright seam overhead, and you hear the seep as a continuous small friction, like cloth drawn slowly over cloth. That is the loudest room in the valley.

The rules are visible because nothing has overgrown them. Water may only descend by the nicks. A pool may not be deeper than the brick that lips it. Every surface that can hold a film must be given a way to pass that film on, or the wall will soften, and the walls have not softened. Shade is kept for the jars and the spare courses. Sun is kept for the drying-ledges, where tiles stand on edge in ranks so close you could not slide a hand between them, each tile still bright, still ringing if you tap it, not yet bowed by years. Nothing is ornamental that is not also a measure. The slight weirdness of the place is only this: the measuring has continued past the presence of anyone to read it. In one terrace a line of bowls has been filled to the same hairline, and the hairline is still the same in every bowl, as if the valley were still being taught its own level, one patient inch at a time.

Change, when it comes, will come as stain, as the lengthening crust, as a cord that one day does not tick. Not yet. The features are young. The joints are sharp. The water is near the surface of everything, and everything is a surface that was made.
