The heat arrives before the geometry does. You feel it as a weight on the tongue, a sweet metallic damp that has no wind to carry it, only the slow exchange of air through a throat of brick no wider than a cart. Below that throat the place opens—not into a cavern, not into a hall, but into a room the size of a parish church that has been filled, almost to the point of collision, with the body of a town.

Walls stand an arm’s length from other walls. A stair of poured stone rises three steps and dies against a pipe-run; the pipe-run itself is young, its flanges still bright where the mill-scale has not yet browned, bolts seated so recently that the washers have not loosened their first grit. Everything here was cut or cast not long ago. The rock around the rooms has not had time to forget the tools. Tool-marks remain as pale strokes on the ceiling, parallel and a little reckless, as if the excavation had been a single season’s decision rather than a century’s habit. Corners are sharp. Lintels have not sagged. Dust has not yet found the ledges it will eventually own.

It is hot the way a kiln is hot when the fire has been banked but not put out: a steady, enclosed temperature that does not spike and does not fall. Condensation gathers where the rock is cooler than the air, which is almost nowhere, and so the moisture lives in pockets—a black shine in a floor-joint, a bead-row along the underside of a warm pipe, a shallow pan of water under a valve that weeps on a schedule no one has posted. The water is not a river. It is intermittent and local, appearing where metal meets stone and vanishing into a drain that was cut square and has not yet rounded. Sometimes, far up the throat, a brief rain finds the surface and sends a thin thread down; you hear it arrive as a change in the pitch of one drip, then nothing.

Vegetation is an accident of that moisture. A few pale threads of rootless growth—something between mold and a plant that never learned leaves—cling in the wettest joint and nowhere else. They do not spread. The heat and the dark and the salt of the seepage keep them honest. There is no green that a person would name. There is no animal life at all: no scurry in the wall-voids, no moth at the lamp, no track in the damp flour of drillings that still lies in the corners. The place moves without creatures.

What moves are the machines, and they move as if taught to be nearly silent. A small engine somewhere behind a double thickness of brick turns at a patient rate; you know it by a tremor in the sole more than by a sound. Belts pass through slots cut for them and do not slap. A press, or something that behaves like a press, completes a cycle and resets; the reset is a soft metallic settling, the kind of noise a heavy object makes when it accepts its place. Steam, or the cousin of steam that this heat produces, travels in lagged lines and exhales at a vent no larger than a fist, fogging a patch of wall and then ceasing. Production happens. You can smell warm oil, hot stone, a faint sweetness like overheated insulation. You cannot see the full line. The rooms are too close, the sightlines too short. Industry here is a set of adjacent facts rather than a vista.

Light is what the builders could spare. A few bulbs behind wire cages throw a honey-colored dim that does not reach the ceiling’s center. Elsewhere the illumination is borrowed: the glow of a gauge, the dull orange of a pipe that runs hotter than its neighbors, a reflection off standing water that makes the floor seem deeper than it is. Shadows do not pool so much as stack. Between two nearly touching facades—one of new brick, one of the living rock left as a party wall—there is a slot of dark wide enough for a shoulder and tall enough for the whole height of the excavation. Things are stored in that slot. You infer crates, coils, a ladder. You do not confirm them.

The density is the true climate. Doorframes almost kiss. A balcony of iron grating, installed for access to an upper run of pipe, passes so near the opposite roof that a person on it would have to duck the eaves. Windows, where they exist, look into other windows across gaps you could span with your hands. This is a settlement in the strict sense: rooms for work, a narrower room with a bench and a tin basin, a stair that implies sleeping somewhere the heat is fractionally less, a painted number on a door that has no reason to be numbered unless someone expects to find it again. The organization is real. The generosity of space is not. Whoever cut this place decided the town would fit inside the heat pocket, and then made it fit.

The unusual thing is not a monster or a glowing fissure. It is the age. The rock is old; the wound in it is not. Fresh faces of stone still shed a grit as fine as flour when a pipe expands against them. Mortar is pale, not yet stained by the perpetual damp. A chalk mark on a bulkhead—an arrow, a depth, a name half-wiped—has not been rained on enough times to die. The town feels inhabited by process more than by people, and by a process that began recently and has not yet taught the stone its manners. You stand in a place that is already crowded, already working, already hot enough to slow the blood, and yet still in its first weather: drips finding their permanent routes, bricks giving up their kiln-water, machines learning the clearance they were given.

If you wait, the cycle declares itself without raising its voice. The weep at the valve starts. The press completes. A thread of surface-rain, if the hour is right, arrives and is gone. The gauge-glow does not flicker. Somewhere above the throat the world is larger and, presumably, lit; down here the boundaries are the cut itself, a cathedral of near-collisions, sharp-edged and damp and quiet, busy in the way a heart is busy when you are not listening for it.
