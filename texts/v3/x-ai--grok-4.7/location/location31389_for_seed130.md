The first fact of the place is not a shape. It is the noise, and the noise has no edge.

It arrives as pressure in the teeth before it arrives as hearing. A low, continuous grinding, industrial and hydraulic at once, as if a city-sized gearbox were turning under a shallow inland sea. Closer, it sorts itself: the cough of a pump that has not stopped in an age, the metallic tick of cooling pipe, the slap of water finding a lower plate and striking it. Farther off, those separate themselves into weather. A condenser bank on the eastern rise cycles every few minutes and the whole basin seems to inhale. When it exhales, steam piles against the dark and comes back down as a fine, cold rain that never quite commits to falling. You stand in it and are wet without having been rained on. The air is always on the point of becoming water.

There is no soil. That absence takes a while to believe, because the ground looks, at a distance, like a tidal flat at dusk: dark, level, glistening, broken by low ribs. Underfoot it is plate. Slag poured and then planed. Concrete that has recrystallized into something denser than the concrete that was mixed. Ceramic tile the size of fields, the grout gone, the edges rounded by water until one sheet passes into the next without a joint you can trust. In places the surface is metal, not bright, a skin of oxide the color of wet tea, flexed over whatever was laid beneath it so long ago that the flex has become the topography. Nothing here was ever bedrock. The bedrock is the making. Erosion has not uncovered an older world underneath. Erosion is what this world does to itself, and it has been doing it long enough to arrive at a kind of peace: nearly flat, nearly finished, still not quite done.

The elevation is an argument you lose. Walking, you feel you are on a plain. Looking back after an hour, the place you left is neither above you nor below you. The whole basin sits in a subsidence it made for itself, a shallow dish a few tens of meters deep at most and many kilometers across, the rim so gentle it reads as haze rather than hill. Water likes this. It does not run so much as decide. A film covers the plates after every exhalation of the condensers. Where the film finds a depression left by a settled footing or a collapsed culvert, it stays, black and still, a marsh measured in centimeters. Reeds do not grow there. Nothing animal moves in them. They reflect the steam and the few far lights and otherwise hold the dark the way a closed eye holds it.

The lights are not for seeing by. They are process-lights: a dull orange at the throat of a stack that still draws, a white-blue stutter from an arc somewhere in the western yards, the sick green of an indicator that has forgotten what it indicates and simply continues. Between them the darkness is the actual climate. The sky, if it is a sky, is a ceiling of vapor and particulate, low enough that the taller structures lose their heads in it. Cool air. Not winter. The cool of metal that has been losing heat for centuries and of water that never warms. Breath shows, and then the breath is taken up into the general condensate and is no longer yours.

What stands up out of the flat is sparse, and the sparseness is the scale. You can walk a long time on the ringing plate and meet nothing taller than a valve wheel. Then a thing arrives at the edge of the dark and is larger than the walk suggested. A cooling tower, its hyperbolic shell stained in vertical weeps, water crawling down the concrete in slow bright threads and vanishing into the apron. It is not ruined. Ruin would be quieter. From inside it comes a roar so complete that near the inlet the smaller sounds are subtracted and you are left with a single note, vast and toneless, the sound of air being asked to become weather and refusing and becoming it anyway. Half a kilometer on, a gantry, its rails still true, a crane cab dark and empty, the hook hanging over a flooded bay where ingots or blocks or whatever was once worth lifting have become a shoal of rectangles under a hand’s depth of water. The shoal clicks. Temperature, or the vibration coming up through the piles, shifts the blocks against each other. It is one of the few sounds you can locate. Everything else is regional.

Farther, the pumping district: a scatter of engine houses, brick and plate and later plate over the brick, each house a small island in its own seep. They do not all run at once. That is the motion of the place, its only real weather besides the wet. A house starts. The basin’s note changes key. Water, which had been lying, begins to travel in a channel whose sides are poured and whose bed is the same plate as the plain, only lower by the thickness of an old decision. The channel does not go to a river. There is no river. It goes to a sump, or to another house, or out toward the rim where the land, still made, rises by a meter over a mile and the water is lifted again and sent back. A cycle with no outside. You can watch a stick—no, there are no sticks—a flake of scale ride the channel and, if you are absurd enough to wait, imagine it returning. People do not seem to be part of the waiting. There are walkways, catwalks, railings thinned to wire by the wet. There are stairs. There are doorways without doors, and inside them the heat of a bearing and the smell of hot oil cut with metal and with the sweet, mineral stink of condensate that has passed through the same pipes since before the pipes had names. No one tends them. The tending, if it happens, happens at a scale you cannot see: a governor, a float, a gate that opens because the level says so. The industry has become the drainage. The drainage has become the climate. The climate has been running so long it has worn the industry down to the shape climate prefers, which is low, and broad, and always a little wet.

Vegetation is an embarrassment to the surfaces, and there is not much of it. In the grout that remains, and in the oxide pits, and along the lip where warm discharge meets cold plate, a dark crust grows, not quite moss, not quite mineral, something that drinks the perpetual film and does not seem to mind the noise or the lack of sun. Here and there a pale, jointed stem, no taller than a hand, leafless or nearly, rising from a crack that has been a crack long enough to hold a teaspoon of silt—and the silt itself is only the ground, powdered. These plants do not appear to flower in any way that would require a visitor. They shed a dust of themselves into the water, and the water carries it, and somewhere a crack receives it. That is the whole ecology. Nothing eats them. Nothing walks between them. There are no insects in the film, no birds on the gantries, no rats in the engine houses, though the houses are full of the kind of heat and scrap a rat would have thanked. The absence is not a recent dying. It is structural. Whatever rules assembled this basin did not include animals, or included them once and then settled, as it has settled everything else, into a state that no longer needs them. The sound is loud enough to have replaced every call. There is no silence in which a small life could announce itself, and so no small life does.

The strangeness is not a creature and not a violation of the materials. It is the way the materials behave after this much time. Plates that should have fatigued have instead work-hardened into a continuous armor, and where a seam must move it moves all at once, a slow tidal flex you feel in the knees when the big condenser draws. Water on vertical faces does not sheet evenly. It finds paths and keeps them, so that every tower and stack is written with bright permanent tears, and the tears hum, each a tiny string, and together they are a part of the roar you cannot subtract. In the dark, distances misreport. A light that seems near is an hour away; a knock that seems at your feet is a dropped scale-flake in a sump you will not reach before the cycle changes. Sound does not fade here so much as change employment. Near a running house it is violence. In the open plate it becomes a floor, a thing you stand on, and you notice its absence only when a machine stops—and when one stops, the stopping is an event. The basin rings with the lack. Water that was being lifted slumps back. A channel reverses for a minute with a sound like cloth torn slowly. Then another house, far off, takes the load, and the note returns, altered by a fraction, and the fraction is how you know the place is still deciding what its final form will be.

It has been deciding for a very long time. You can read the decisions in section, where a collapsed apron has exposed the edge of the made ground. Layers. Not strata in the old sense. Courses. A bed of rubble-concrete. A pour of something glassy and black. A mattress of corroded mesh. Another pour. A skin. Another skin. Each course is the repair of the last, and the repairs have become the geology, and the geology has been planed by water and by vibration until the top is the smoothest thing in the basin and the bottom, however deep, is only more of the same. There is no natural substrate waiting under the last layer. The last layer is a rumor. Dig, and you are still in the work.

By what passes for day—a slight thinning of the vapor, a mean rise in the number of process-lights reflected in the films—the basin shows its full reach and still withholds its end. Engine house, tower, canal, gantry, a low switchyard humming so hard the puddles on its gravel (and the gravel is crushed insulator, crushed brick, crushed plate) stand in ripples without wind. Then open plate again, a kilometer of it, dark as a shut foundry floor. Then another district, smaller, where a row of stacks bleed a white fume that never rises far and comes back as the cold rain. The scale is a valley’s scale, or a town’s, if a town were only its machines and had forgotten its rooms. You could cross it in a day and not be out of it. The rim, when you think you have found it, is only a service berm, and beyond the berm the plate resumes, lower, wetter, the sound a little different because the pumps there are older and larger and turn more slowly.

Nothing hunts. Nothing flocks. The active processes are all of water, heat, and metal, and they do not pause for observation. A bank of louvers opens because a temperature somewhere has crossed a line drawn into a mechanism before the mechanism’s housing was cast. Steam leaves. Rain returns. A sump fills, trips a float the size of a door, and a pump the size of a house takes the water and throws it sideways into a header that runs, buried and then not buried, toward the towers. The header knocks. The knock travels. In the western yards the arc shutters and starts again, throwing a brief hard light in which every wet surface becomes a mirror and the basin, for a second, is all reflection and no depth. Then the dark comes back, and the reflection was only water on made ground, and the ground is still flexing, still shedding scale into itself, still being exactly as finished as an unfinished thing can be.

If there is a rule, it is this: the world has already been built, and building was the last natural act, and since then there has been only maintenance performed by the maintained. Water is the tool. Darkness is the hour, and the hour does not end. Sound is the population. The plants in the cracks are not a return of the wild; they are a tolerance, a thin greenish-black permission in a system that has room, barely, for anything that does not move and does not cry out. Stand long enough and the roar stops seeming like damage. It is the stable state. It is what this much industry sounds like when it has outlived the need for anything else to live in it, and has not yet, and perhaps never will, outlive the need to keep the water moving.
