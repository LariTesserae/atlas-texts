The highland does not rise so much as continue. From far enough out it is only a change in the color of heat: a paler, higher country laid across the continent like a second skin, its edge lost in its own shimmer. Climb onto it and the air thins without cooling. The sun is an ordinary sun. What is wrong is the ground.

Underfoot the stone has given up. What you walk is a crust of old growth, weathered into pavement—plates of hardened fiber, gray-green and bone-pale, fitted together by centuries of swelling and shrinking until the joints look deliberate. They are not. Wind and weight and the slow appetite of the thing itself have planed it into this. In the seams, where a boot breaks the glaze, the interior is still damp, sweet, and faintly warm, as if the highland were running a low fever it no longer notices. Press an ear to a wide plate at midday and the heat is in it, not merely on it. The organism, if it is one organism, has reached the age where it no longer builds toward a shape. It maintains.

The living architecture stands up out of that pavement at long intervals. Not a forest. A forest would fill the eye. Here the eye travels. A rib, a buttress, a hollow column flared at the base like the foot of a hill and open at the top like a chimney; then half a morning of bare crust; then another, smaller, already dying back into the plate it grew from. Some of the columns are tall enough to make their own shade-weather. Clouds snag on them and come apart. Between them the distances are continental in feeling if not always in measure: you can walk all day toward a pylon and find, when you arrive, that it was only a spur of a greater one whose root you have been walking on since dawn. The vegetation is total because there is nothing else for the land to be. It is sparse because the land, having become plant, no longer needs to crowd itself. It has already won.

Water does not run here in any way a valley would recognize. At night the high air sheds a film. By first light the plates shine, not wet enough to drink from a hand, wet enough to darken the fiber and wake whatever lives in the hairline cracks. The film is gone before the shadows shorten. What remains is a dampness held inside: sap standing in the chimneys, condensation beading on the undersides of flanges, pockets where a collapsed rib has formed a basin and the basin has learned to stay a little cool. Drink from those and the water tastes of the plant—green, slightly salt, already going warm. After a rare rain the crust drinks faster than the sky can give. For an hour there are streams, bright and brief, threading the joints and vanishing into throats that close behind them. Then the highland is dry again, except in its private places, and the heat returns as if the rain had been a rumor.

The heat is the true climate. It sits on the plates and rises in slow columns. Where two great ribs stand far apart, the air between them organizes. You can watch a storm begin as a darkening of the gap, a local decision, not weather arriving from elsewhere. It rains on one side of a pylon and not the other. The plant takes what falls and spends the afternoon breathing it back out as a haze that smells of cut stem and hot dust. Nothing erodes quickly anymore. A fallen column lies where it fell for longer than the memory of the people who notch its flank, and the crust grows over its leeward edge in thin, annual lips, the way a scar closes. Balance, not stillness.

The sound is the part that does not match the view.

The pavement looks empty. The distances look empty. The sky, for long stretches, looks empty. And yet the highland is never quiet, and at most hours it is not merely loud. It is occupied by noise the way a surf-coast is occupied by surf: a continuous fact, varying in register, never in presence. The chimneys are the instruments. Each standing rib is hollow for some fraction of its height, and the hollows are inhabited—not by a few singers, by nations of them. They are small. You almost never see the body that makes a given cry. What you see, if you look up a shaft at the right hour, is a shivering of the interior dark, a traffic of bodies too quick to count, using the plant as both house and horn.

Their voices do not resemble birds in any lasting way, though the first morning you will try the comparison and keep it for an hour. Some tones are dry and mechanical, a clicking that multiplies until it becomes a hiss. Some are long and nearly musical, and those are the ones that carry. A single chimney, caught by the wind at its mouth, can throw a note across a reach of crust that takes a person two days to cross. When many chimneys speak at once—and they do, on some schedule of their own, not dawn and not dusk but a third time the highland keeps—the notes interfere. The air between the pylons fills with beats, slow pulses you feel in the teeth. Conversation is a matter of leaning close and shouting, or of waiting for a trough. The troughs come. They are brief. In them you hear, suddenly, the small sounds: your own blood, the tick of cooling fiber, a far crack as some dead flange finally lets go.

The animals are not only in the chimneys. On the open plates, in the heat, larger bodies move at a distance that preserves the emptiness. You see them as interruptions in the shimmer: a low herd drifting along a joint-line, grazing the living glaze without seeming to lessen it; a solitary shape stalled in the shadow of a buttress, too still for too long, then gone between one blink and the next. Overhead, at the height where the local clouds form, there are others, broad and slow, riding the thermals the crust feeds them. They cast shadows the size of roofs. They do not call. Their silence is the only quiet the highland offers, and it is private to them. Everything else seems to have been given a voice and an obligation to use it.

What they eat, mostly, is the plant, or each other, or the film of night-water and the life that breeds in it before the sun returns. The ecology is closed in a way that feels older than food-chains drawn in a margin. The crust grows. The grazers take the soft lip of new fiber. The chimney-nations take the grazers' parasites, the winged scavengers, the condensation-flies that rise in brief silver weather after a pocket is disturbed. Something in the deep flanges takes the dead and returns them as warmth and sweetness to the sap. You do not see that part. You smell it when the wind drops: a richness under the green, not rot exactly, more like a kiln and a kitchen sharing a wall.

There are rules, and they are simple because the place is simple at the scale that matters.

Do not sleep in a chimney mouth. The night voices are louder, and the bodies that make them descend with the cool air. A person who wedges into a flange for shelter wakes, if they wake, with their skin patterned by small mouths that were only curious.

Do not cut the living plate for water. The cut weeps, then seals, and the seal is poorer ground for a season. The basins already exist. They are where the plant has agreed, for its own reasons, to hold a little drink. The people who cross this country know the basins the way other people know fords. They are not marked. Or they are marked so lightly you must already know.

The marks are the only built things, and at this scale they are nearly a kind of pollen. A line of seed-plates set on edge along a safe route between basins, each plate no larger than a shield, already being swallowed by the crust at its base. A ladder of lashed fiber up the lee of a chimney, not to the top—never to the top—but to a gallery where the roar is muffled enough to sleep. A shelter cut into a section where the fiber has gone gray and soundless, the organism withdrawn, the dead wood honest. Stone tools. Bone needles. Resin boiled in a hollow and smeared on cord to make it hold in the damp-that-isn't. No metal you would trust, or if there is metal it came from somewhere lower and is already wearing back into ore under the night film. The shelters are far apart. From one you cannot see the next. Some have been empty so long the crust has begun to learn their corners, pushing a soft lip across the threshold, not hostile, only patient.

The people, when you meet them, speak by signs until a trough in the sound arrives, and then they speak quickly, with the economy of those who rent their sentences from the weather. They do not claim the highland. They use the dead parts and the basins and the routes the herds have already argued into the glaze. Their stories are mostly about sound: which chimney has gone silent this year, which new one has opened and what that means for the herds, which note, if you hear it under the others, means a flange is about to fall and you should not be in its shadow when it does. A fall, when it comes, is the loudest thing, and then for a little while the loudest thing is the quiet after, a wound in the chorus, already healing as neighboring shafts take up the missing pitch and redistribute it. Within a season the fallen column is a long hill of fiber, and within a longer one it is pavement again, and the highland has not changed its mind about what it is.

Walk it in the normal light of afternoon and the strangeness is not in the sky or the sun. It is in the proportion. A country made entirely of one living patience, worn into equilibrium, hot at an altitude that ought to have cooled it, damp only in secret, so open you can see a storm deciding itself a day's walk away—and so full of animals that the openness is a lie told to the eye. The ear knows better. The ear has known since you came up onto the crust. Under the heat, under the long distances, the highland is a single held note with a billion throats in it, and the note does not finish. You are small enough to cross a little of it. You are not large enough to be in the way. The plates warm your feet. Far ahead a chimney takes the wind and speaks, and another answers, and the space between them, which looked like nothing, fills.
