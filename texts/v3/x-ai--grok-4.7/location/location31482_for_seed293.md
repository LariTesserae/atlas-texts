The place is smaller than its weather. From the rim you can see all of it: a shallow saucer of ground, barely lower than the country around it, no wider than a town’s oldest quarter and no taller, anywhere, than a cathedral nave. The rim is not a wall. It is the last hesitation in a plain that finished eroding so long ago the word hill has nothing left to attach to. Stone here has been planed to a skin. What stands up does so only because something living insists.

Warmth sits on the skin without drama. The air is the temperature of a room someone has just left, of breath, of water that never quite decides to be cold. Light is ordinary daylight, a little thick, as if it has passed through wet cloth. Shadows are short and green at the edges. Nothing is hidden by darkness. Things are hidden by being the same substance as what is next to them.

Underfoot there is no soil in the old sense. Soil was an interim. What remains is a felt of roots, sheaths, and a pale cork that drinks and does not rot. Press it and water rises in the print of your hand, not from a puddle but from the material itself, the way sap rises when a stem is nicked. Every vertical face weeps. Condensation beads on the undersides of leaves and does not fall so much as travel, tracking veins until it finds a lip and returns to the felt. The saucer holds a continuous shallow sheet—ankle to calf, never a lake—broken by low lawns of a moss that is also a paving, firm enough to walk if you accept that walking means being slightly inside the ground.

The vegetation is not a covering. It is the substrate, the architecture, and the only civic material. Between the standing forms the space is open, almost sparse: you can see across standing water to the next presence, and the next, a dozen of them scattered like furnishings in a room too large for its furniture. Each presence is total. A tower is a single organism that has agreed, for a while, to be a tower. Its walls are leaves fused edge to edge, thinner than they should be, holding floors that have no right to their own weight. Stair-helixes are petioles thickened into treads. Windows are stomata taught to stay open at the size of doors. The streets are not between the plants. The streets are the plants lying down, and when they tire of being streets they rise a hand’s breadth and become hedges, and the water, which had been using them as curbs, finds a new grade and goes there instead.

This is a settlement, and it is organized, though the organization does not keep a fixed map. There are addresses, of a kind: a particular bitterness in the air marks the place where exchanges happen; a sweeter resin marks sleep; a metallic green, almost the color of wet copper, marks the halls where the specifications are kept. Those halls are the strangest quiet in a loud city. Inside them the light is still daylight, only delayed. Morning moves through the tissue and comes out in the afternoon, stored in paths that are not optical in any way a lens would recognize. You can stand in noon and be lit by dawn. The delay is engineered. So is the water that climbs the inner walls against every gradient the stone remembers. The plants keep an older slope in their grain—a slope that belonged to hills that no longer exist—and they spend a continuous small effort making capillary law agree with that memory. Where the effort is local and intense, other laws lean. A floor can be walked that is, for three paces, not quite as heavy as it should be. A seed no larger than a fingernail unfolds overnight into a cistern whose walls are one cell thick and do not burst, because inside the cistern viscosity has been asked, politely and with great precision, to be otherwise.

No one here builds in the sense of cutting and joining dead matter. They specify. The citizens—warm, bipedal, no taller than the door-stomata, numerous enough that you are never the only animal and rare enough that the open water between structures stays open—carry seeds the way other towns carry keys. A seed is a document that refers to constants and then amends them inside its own radius. The amendments do not spread. Step out of a cistern and weight returns. The surrounding plain, ancient and finished, is never recruited. The saucer is the only jurisdiction, and it has been the only jurisdiction for so long that the amendments read as weather.

The weather does not settle. That is the other law, and it is as old as the flatness. The landform is done changing; the life upon it is not permitted to finish a gesture. What you see at any hour is mid-revision. A colonnade you noted on entering is, by the time you reach it, a canal with the same ribs, water pouring through gaps that were shadows. A market platform—broad, leaf-laminated, scattered with bowls grown rather than thrown—tilts as the organism under it thickens on one side, and the bowls skate, and people laugh the short laugh of those who price this into the day, and catch what they can. Walls thicken and thin. A room inhales and becomes a gallery; exhales and is a thicket with a person still inside it, unharmed, irritated, already negotiating with the stem for a door. Nothing collapses in the catastrophic sense. The turbulence is metabolic. Creation and unmaking run at the same speed, and have run at that speed since before the plain lost its last ridge. Stability, here, is the refusal to complete.

Because of that, the place is loud. Not with voices alone, though voices carry—the exchange-quarter has the bright clatter of a bazaar, arguments about specifications, the tap of seed-cases. Under and over that is the city itself: fibers parting with a sound like wet rope, reknitting with a tick; water changing its mind in slurps and sudden sheets; the creak of a tower redistributing a floor. Animals add the rest. They are abundant and never a mass. A scatter, the way the structures are a scatter. Amphibians the color of the felt sit in the weep-lines and sing in overlapping pairs, each pair claiming a few meters and no more. Things like herons, but with feet that adhere and release in time with the city’s revisions, stalk the open water and lift when a lawn decides to become a ridge. Insect-clouds orbit the resinous sleep-halls and the bitter exchange, pollinating transactions as much as flowers; their wings make a dry high shimmer that does not stop. Smaller mammals, bright-eyed, use the hollow petioles as streets of their own, and their traffic is a soft thunder inside the architecture, audible if you put a hand to a wall. Nothing hunts in a way that clears a zone. Appetite is constant and local. The ecology is a pure-plant economy with animals as couriers, editors, and accidental priests. What dies becomes felt before it becomes memory. What is born is often a revision of a structure that was, an hour ago, a dwelling.

You learn the manners quickly because the place teaches them in the body. Do not trust a path that has been a path since morning; it is showing off, and it will spend the afternoon as water. Do not sleep against a wall that smells of copper-green unless you have asked; those tissues are busy keeping a constant bent, and they run warm, and they sometimes fold a sleeper into a specification by mistake—never fatal, always inconvenient, a person waking with a map of a cistern branching under their skin like a bruise that fades by evening. Drink from the weep, not from the standing sheet; the sheet is everyone’s road, and the weep is the plant’s own surplus, clean, slightly sweet, warm as the air. If you must cross where a structure is revising, cross during the inhale. The exhale is when gaps close. The citizens will not stop you. They are busy. A child on a petiole-rail—there are children; the place is a town—points at your feet, because you are still walking as if stone were the authority.

At the rim, if you are patient, the whole argument becomes visible at once. The dozen structures brighten and dim as stored light arrives out of order. Water writes and erases silver lines across the felt. A tower that was a spire kneels into a long hall and the heron-things resettle on its new ridge without comment, having seen this kneel before, or a kneel enough like it. Sound rolls up the non-slope and thins: the bazaar, the tick of reknitting, the paired singing, the inner thunder of small feet. Beyond the rim the plain continues, the same ancient flatness, empty of amendment, silent, dry enough that the contrast feels like a held breath. Nothing out there is unfinished. Everything in here is unfinished on purpose.

Stand long enough and the saucer seems less like a city planted in a marsh than like a single instrument left running after its players have become part of the tune. The low ground will not rise again. The plants will not agree to be only plants. The water will not consent to lie still. Between those refusals, in a space you could walk across in an hour if the hour would hold still, a town keeps the oldest stable state available to it: continuous, audible, wet, and alive in every direction, including the ones that used to be reserved for stone.
