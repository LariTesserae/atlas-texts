The floor is the whole of it. There is almost no rise left to stand against—only a shallow, pale depression no larger than a room, sunk into stone that has been wearing down for longer than anything here can remember. The walls, if they were ever walls, have slumped into low shoulders of grit. What remains of the rim is a lip of cooled mineral, barely higher than a hand, and beyond it the world simply stops: a sealed edge, as if the place were cut out of a larger dark and set down by itself.

Nothing grows. The ground is a skin of dust and salt-crystal and fused glass, cool under any touch, dry enough that the air has no weight of moisture in it. Light comes in ordinary and even, from somewhere above the rim that cannot be seen clearly—daylight of a plain kind, no drama in it, laying soft shadows under every small ridge the erosion failed to finish. The color of the place is the color of old mineral: bone, ash, a faint iron blush where something once oxidized and then stopped.

And the floor is not empty. It is crowded.

They cover it in layers that shift against one another without quite leaving a gap. Small bodies, hard-shelled and matte, the size of knuckles and coins, packed until their edges click. Where one layer pauses, another is already moving underneath it—narrower, paler, working the same dust. They do not browse leaves or sip at pools. There is nothing of that kind. They rasp the mineral itself, and the salt crust, and the thin films of oxide that bloom and flake on the old metal scattered through the basin. Their mouths leave tracks no wider than a thread. The tracks overlap until the surface looks braided, then the braid is eaten too, and the floor is smooth again for a moment before the next pass.

The sound is the first thing that fills the space past its edges. A continuous dry rattle, many small impacts at once, like grain poured over tin and never stopping. When a denser knot of them turns, the pitch rises; when they spread, it drops into a hiss. There is no wind to carry it. The noise stays inside the rim and beats against the low stone until the pocket seems louder than its size should allow.

Here and there the swarm parts around something that is not stone. A housing, half-buried, its bolts gone to powder. A length of pipe no longer connected to anything, lying so long that the basin has tried to swallow it and only mostly succeeded. A toothed wheel, industrial in its cut, its teeth rounded by the same patience that flattened the walls. These are not ruins of a city. They are few, and small, and they do not form a plan—scattered tools of some mechanized work that ended, or was abandoned, or simply outlasted its purpose and sank into the mineral the way everything else has. The creatures treat them as terrain. They climb the pipe in a living sleeve. They fill the housing until it ticks from the inside. On the wheel, they walk the gaps between teeth as if the teeth were a ridge-line of their own.

Nothing hunts in a way that clears the ground. What feeds, feeds while being walked on. A larger form—still small, still cool-skinned, jointed like the rest but slower—moves beneath the upper layer and the upper layer does not flee so much as rearrange, a brief hole that seals behind it. The exchange is constant. Dust lifted by their feet hangs a finger’s width above the floor and settles again. Salt grains hop. A flake of rust detaches, is taken, is gone. The basin breathes only in this sense: the rise and fall of bodies, the turn of the wheel when enough of them weight one side, the soft collapse of a crust that has been thinned past holding.

It is an old stillness wearing the costume of motion. The stone has already arrived at the shape it will keep. The machines will not be repaired. No green thing is waiting in a crack. What remains active is the crowd itself, generation against generation, loud in a place with no water and no shade worth the name, wearing the last topography down by another useless fraction and calling that a day. Close your eyes and the rattle could be weather. Open them and there is only the floor, the low rim, the ordinary light, and the living density that has made this pocket its entire world.
