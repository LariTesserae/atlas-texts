The height should have thinned the air. It did something else instead.

You come up onto the basin already cold, and the cold is not the clean cold of a summit. It is a damp cold, the kind that finds the seams in any covering and stands in them. Breath leaves the mouth and does not go. It draws out into a pale thread, drifts sideways, and joins other threads already hung across the glare — old breath, or the air practicing breath, none of it dispersing. The light has no disk. It is simply present, high and hard, thrown back by every wet face until shade becomes a rumor and the eyes ache from surfaces that should have been dark.

The basin is open enough to be crossed by sight: a high hollow the size of a small town’s fields, ringed by walls that have been planed for so long they no longer look quarried by catastrophe. They look agreed upon. Benches, shoulders, a far rim like a cut lip. Between you and that rim the ground is not empty. It is crowded. Plates of stone lie fitted against one another, near-touching, their joints full of black water. Among the plates stand low stools and combs of mineral, grown by deposition — branching, almost, but only the way frost branches, or slag. Nothing here is green. Nothing here was green and died. The color is wet gray, iron-rust, glass-black, and the white scurf that precipitates out of the air and settles like rime without ever having been ice.

Water is the commonest thing after stone, and it does not keep to low places. It films every surface. It stands in the joints in sheets thin enough to show the stone and loud enough to tick, continuously, as if a fine rain were striking a roof that is not there. On the sunward walls — though there is no sun — the film thickens, beads, and runs upward. The beads travel with a visible patience into a high sheet of mist that never quite becomes cloud, and vanish into the brightness. Lower down, in the lee of the benches, the same water lies flat and wide, marsh-shallow over pavement, trembling. There is no reed-bed. The edge of the wet is only a change in sound: from tick to slap to a long hiss where the film is shedding itself back into the air. The channels that drain these sheets are old. Their lips are rounded, their beds polished, their courses settled into the balance of the place. Several of them run uphill. The stone has consented to this for a long time. You can see the consent in the polish.

Walk, and the walking is louder than it should be. The plates click at their edges when the glare strengthens, a dry tick under the wet one. Cavities in the walls hold a note — not wind in the ordinary sense, though the air moves — a bowl-tone, many of them, poorly tuned to each other, so the basin is never in a single key. Now and then a clear strike rings out, the sound of a hand-tool on stone. Nothing is striking. Often the strike arrives first, and only afterward a copper pin in a crack is seen to be shivering, or a grain lets go from a wall and clicks down the bench, late, as if the stone had remembered an impact and then performed it. Speech, if anyone tried it, would not carry as words. The place is already speaking at the volume of a workshop.

The constructions are few, and all of them small, and all of them older than their makers could have planned for. A course of unmortared plates set on edge, waist-high, running a dozen paces and then ending in the middle of nothing. A lean of two slabs that might have been a shelter and has since been taken halfway into the pavement, the way a sleeper is taken into a riverbed, except that the pavement is not a river and the taking is mineral. Hand-smooth lips on the rock where rope worried, or knees, or the same gesture repeated until the gesture became weather. Pins of soft metal — copper, or something as soft — driven into cracks, green and brown with oxide, their heads mushroomed by hammering. A scatter of flakes, struck, not natural. None of it is a town. It is the residue of people who had hand-tools and a need to mark, to pin, to keep a roof of sorts between themselves and a sky that is only light. They did not master the basin. The basin weathered them into its own state.

Some of the marks cannot have been made under the rules that would let a person stand where the marks are. A line of pins climbs a wall along a traverse where the hold is glass and the fall is the whole hollow. The oxidation on those pins is the same age as the oxidation on the pins you could reach. A low wall stands in the middle of an uphill channel, and the channel has flowed — upward — around it long enough to have carved a civil little island of polish. The wall was not built against the flow as a dam. The flow agreed to the wall, or the wall agreed to the flow, and both have been true together long enough that arguing precedence is a visitor’s habit.

Distance is the first courtesy to fail, and it fails politely. A cairn of flat stones sits on a mid-basin bench, clear in the glare, small with range. You walk toward it across the clicking plates and it remains the same size for too long. The middle of the walk is missing, or was never offered. Then you are beside it. The stones of the cairn are beaded with the same film as everything else, and one face is dark — not shaded, dark — a pocket of night about the size of a crouch, sitting in full brightness with edges as sharp as a cut in cloth. Step near it and the note in the nearest cavity drops. The darkness is not empty in the way a hole is empty. It is a vacancy with a temperature, cooler by a little, and a sound of its own, a soft grainy hush, the hiss of the white scurf coming out of air that has forgotten light. These vacancies are scattered through the basin. They drift. Not quickly, and not as animals drift: nothing here has an animal’s way of moving. They migrate the way a weather migrates, a few paces in a cycle, and where they have rested the pavement is a shade smoother, as if glare itself were an erosive tool and its absence a preservation.

Across the hollow, on the far wall, the near wall is visible. Not a reflection in the ordinary sense — the water-film mirrors, but it mirrors wrong by a few degrees, showing you a sky with a seam in it, showing the pins on a shelf you have not found yet. The far wall shows the basin’s own near side, delayed. If you have walked, you can find the walk still happening over there: a small interruption in the brightness, the size of a person, arriving at a cairn you are already standing beside. When the delayed arrival completes, the tone in the cavities shifts, all of them, a chord changing because a sequence has been allowed to catch up with itself. Then the films thicken. Fine water falls out of the clear glare for a little while, loud on the plates, and the upward beads hurry, and a little more of the white scurf lays itself down, and the vacancies inch along the benches like slow lids. The cycle is short enough to watch through twice in a standing. It has been running long enough to have made the shape of the land.

At the margins, stone and water are not fully decided. The lip of a shallow sheet will take a print — a heel, a pin’s shadow, the square end of a flake set down and lifted — and the print remains when the lip has gone hard again, a dimple in pavement that rings, faintly, if the strike-sound passes near it. Leave a hand in the film at such a margin and the cold is instructive: not freezing, not safe, a mineral cold, as if the water were a solvent for edges. Nothing bites. There is nothing here to bite. The solvent only reminds the hand that surfaces in this place are a negotiation.

Nothing roots. Nothing burrows, calls, or crosses the glare on legs. The density is all mineral, acoustic, and built — layers of mist at the ankle and at the shoulder and a third sheet overhead that does not move with the same air as the lower two; combs of sinter close enough that you turn sideways; walls and pins and vacancies; water in too many orientations. The openness is real and it is only horizontal. Across, you see another bench, another wrong channel, another small hardness of night, another cairn that may be this cairn seen late. Down and up are full. The sky is bright and near and has no birds to prove it deep.

What the basin is, once you have stayed through a cycle, is not a ruin and not a storm. It is a finished argument that remains in session. Erosion has accepted the upward streams, the self-seeing walls, the strike that precedes the blow. The light is not a time of day; it is the climate. The loudness is not an event; it is the tool, as much as water is, by which the plates are kept fitted and the pins kept shivering and the white scurf kept falling on a world that has never needed a leaf. The people who pinned and walled and sheltered here worked in simple metal and struck stone, and the place kept their work on its own terms, duplicating a pin where no hand went, drowning a wall in a flow that climbs, shelving a shelter into the pavement grain by grain. It is not hostile. Hostility would require an interest in visitors. It is occupied — densely, loudly, brightly — by its own processes, cool and wet at an altitude that should have been neither, and balanced in a way no lower world would countersign.
