The chamber is smaller than the sound suggests. You could cross it in a slow swim if the current would let you: a single volume, about the height of a church nave and not much longer, closed on all sides by work that was finished and then left to the water. There is no shore inside it, no ledge that stays dry. The water occupies the whole of the made space, cool enough that your hands ache after a minute, clear in the near distance and milk-brown farther off where the lamps fail.

Everything you can touch was built. The walls are courses of engineering brick under a skin of pitch and iron plate, the plate riveted in long vertical seams that the water has worn until the rivet heads are low domes and the seams are only darker lines. The floor is a poured slab, then a second pour over the first where something failed, then a grating of bar-stock welded into squares wide enough for a body to pass. Under the grating the slab continues, stained and smooth, with no stone, no clay, no natural bed showing anywhere. Even the corners were designed: chamfered, then caulked, then caulked again. The oldest caulk has gone soft and black and does not hold; thin threads of water move through it in one direction and not the other, as if the chamber is breathing through its own repairs.

The roof is a shallow vault of the same plate, bowed a little from a century of load, with a single circular hatch near the north end that no longer opens. Light comes from four industrial lamps fixed to the springing of the vault, glass globes thick as bottles, two of them still lit. They throw a dirty amber that does not travel. Between the lamps the water is the color of weak tea held up to a window at dusk. A third source, weaker, enters through a row of bull’s-eye ports along the east wall—daylight from somewhere above, broken into coins, moving when something passes between the ports and the sun. You cannot see what that something is. The ports themselves are green with a film no thicker than paint.

There is almost no vegetation. A few straps of dark weed have taken the horizontal members of the grating, and a moss of brown filaments clings where the lamps warm the plate by a degree or two. Nothing grows tall. Nothing flowers. The water is too cool, the light too poor, the surfaces too often scoured. What lives here lives on what the machines and the current bring in, and on each other.

The fauna is the real population. Schools of small pale fish hold in the lamp-glow in loose, slow wheels, breaking and re-forming when the current pulses. Larger fish—gray, blunt-headed, the size of a forearm—cruise the mid-water on long diagonals, never hurrying, never still. Along the brick, where the pitch has cracked, mussels have built low reefs, their shells worn as smooth as the rivets, packed so tightly that in places the wall is shell rather than masonry. Eels occupy the gaps under the grating. You see them as motion more than as bodies: a flex, a disappearance, the grating ticking once as a tail strikes a bar. Near the south wall a cloud of tiny translucent animals hangs in a column that rises and falls with the pulse of the pumps, a living thermometer of the current. Nothing here is strange in kind. These are the ordinary tenants of a cold, enclosed, fed water. There are simply more of them than the room looks able to hold, spaced out through the volume so that you are never crowded and never alone.

The sound is industrial and continuous, and it is what makes the smallness hard to believe. Somewhere below the slab, or inside the thickness of the west wall, a machine is still turning. You feel it in the grating before you hear it: a low, even shudder, then the sound itself, a wet roar with a metallic edge, as if a turbine or a great impeller is driving water through a throat that was never meant to stay open this long. Every few breaths the pitch drops and recovers. Valves knock—three hard reports, then a hiss, then the roar again. Air trapped in the old pipework burps out through a split coupling near the floor and climbs the wall in a silver rope that breaks apart before it reaches the vault. The fish ignore all of it. The mussels do not close. This has been the weather of the room for longer than any of them have been alive.

The current is the other constant. It enters through a square culvert low in the south wall, a mouth of plate and brick worn oval by the water’s patience, and it leaves through a round throat high on the north side, just under the dead hatch. Between those two openings the water does not simply sit. It organizes. A slow spiral occupies the center of the chamber, wide enough that you can hang in its eye and watch the walls turn past you. Along the floor the flow is faster, combing the weed flat, lifting silt in brief veils that the lamps catch and then lose. Along the roof it is almost still, and that is where the warmest water gathers, a thin layer you can feel with the back of your hand, a degree or two above the rest, smelling faintly of metal and of the oil that still weeps from the old bearings.

Age here is not ruin in the dramatic sense. Nothing has collapsed. The room has settled into the shape it will keep. Edges that were sharp are round. Corners that were square hold a soft fillet of lime and iron oxide. Stamped numbers on a bulkhead near the culvert—capacity, date, a maker’s name—are legible only as shallow dishes in the metal, the letters emptied out by the same slow sanding that has polished the rivets. A ladder runs up the north wall toward the hatch. Most of its rungs remain. The ones that are gone have left oval scars, and the scars are as smooth as the rungs that survive. Someone built this to be entered dry. The water came later, or the water was always the point and the dry phase was only construction. Either way, the dry phase ended, and the room did not die of it. It became this: a finished machine, flooded, still working at a fraction of its design, loud in a small space, cool, dim, and fully occupied by animals that treat the roar as tide.

If you stay long enough the rhythm teaches you the schedule. The turbine surges. The valves knock. The silver rope of air climbs and breaks. The school wheels, scatters, wheels again. Silt lifts, hangs, settles onto the grating in a film so fine it will take another year to matter. The bull’s-eye ports dim and brighten as whatever passes above them passes. Nothing in the chamber is waiting to be rescued or restored. The place has already arrived at the form it knows how to keep, and it is keeping it, noisily, in the dark amber under the lamps, with the fish turning in their slow wheels and the made walls holding, worn, and entire.
