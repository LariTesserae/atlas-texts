The basin sits low enough that the sky has nowhere to go. Cool air pools in it and does not leave. What light arrives has already been spent once, on cloud, on vapor, on the wet faces of things that rise close together and then again beyond them, so that the place is both open and packed: a valley you could walk across in a day if the ground would let you, and a thickness you cannot see through for more than a few body-lengths at a time.

Nothing here has been worn down into kindness. The rock is young in the only way rock can be young—recently broken, recently lifted, edges still decisive. Shelves step out of the floor at angles that have not yet learned to slump. Spines of dark glass stand in rows, near enough that their shadows touch even when the light is too thin to cast a proper shadow. Between them the ground is not soil. It is a skin of mineral, slick, cold, and only just coherent, as if the last cooling had happened within memory, though nothing here moves on the scale of memory.

Water is not a feature of the basin. It is the basin’s habit. It stands in thin sheets on every flat, finds every joint, and comes out of the rock itself in slow weeping lines that never quite become streams. The air is heavy with it without being warm: a cool condensate that beads on any surface left still, including the inside of a sleeve, including the fine hairs of an ear. After rain—and it is always after rain, or during, or in the long pause that is neither—the glass shelves shine like they have just been made. Fog does not drift so much as occupy. It fills the gaps between the near-touching towers and then opens, farther out, into a pale corridor where the same forms repeat until they gray out.

There are no plants. Not a moss-thread, not a film of green on the wet. What grows is precipitate. Where water has been leaving the rock for a long time—and long, here, is the only speed available—it has built curtains. Pale, brittle drapes hang from overhangs, layered so tightly they look woven, ringing if a sleeve brushes them. Knobs crowd the lips of every seep, grape-sized, then fist-sized, then fused into ridges that narrow the passages until two of them almost meet overhead. In the dim they read as bone, or salt, or the inside of a shell. Up close they are neither soft nor alive. They are the water’s patience made solid: a slow writing of mineral across a surface that is itself still sharp.

The sound is the first thing that refuses the eye. The eye says slow, says dim, says nothing hurries. The ear says the valley is full. Every sheet of water has a voice, and because the sheets are everywhere and the rock is close, the voices do not fade; they stack. A high tick from a curtain. A lower pour where a shelf spills into a pocket no deeper than a hand. A hiss, almost too even to notice until you stop, from the vapor rising off stone that is only a little warmer than the air. Farther off, something larger—an entire face of the basin, perhaps—lets go a sheet all at once, and the clap comes back from three directions because the spires are good at returning what they are given. Under that, a faint mineral ringing, as if the young glass is still settling by thicknesses too small to see, each shift a tone. The loudness is not weather. It does not crest and pass. It is the ordinary condition of a place where water, rock, and echo have nowhere else to be.

Walking is a negotiation with density. Paths exist only as the slightly lower films between ribs of stone, and they pinch, widen, pinch again. You are always near a surface: a wet wall at the shoulder, a hanging drape at the brow, a floor that gives a little and then rings. Scale announces itself only when a passage opens sideways and you see the basin continue—same shelves, same seeps, same pallor—out to a far rim that is itself only another rank of spines, and beyond that a suggestion of more basin, cooling into the same dim. The place is a valley the way a lung is a room: enclosed, repetitive, larger than the breath currently in it.

Life is rare enough that the first sign of it feels like a mistake in the light. Something small crosses a wet shelf and the water closes behind it with a sound cleaner than a footstep. It is the color of the precipitate, or else it carries the precipitate on its back, a little moving crust. It does not graze; there is nothing to graze. It pauses where two seeps meet, and the pause is long, and then it is gone into a joint too narrow for a hand. Later, much later, a second one, or the same one, sounds from inside a hollow spine: a dry click against glass, answered by the glass, not by any other creature. If there is a population, it is thin as the water-film, and it does not sing. The singing is all stone.

Scattered through this, never in a pattern you can trust, are marks that are not quite the same as the curtains and the knobs. A course of flat pieces set on edge where no seep would have stood them. A shallow cup ground into a shelf, too regular, holding a darker water that does not film over as fast as the rest. A line of scratches on a young face, already being skinned by new mineral, so that the marks are both fresh and being erased by the same slow habit that builds the drapes. None of it is a device. None of it hums or measures or remembers in any way a tool would. They are faint remains, or faint intentions, or the basin imitating the idea of a made thing because repetition, given enough time and enough close surfaces, starts to look like laying courses. You can pass three of them in an hour and then none for the rest of the day, and the absence is as loud as the water, because the water never absents itself.

What is strange is not a single prodigy. It is the way the rules almost hold and then do not. Light pools in the cups of the shelves as if it had weight, a little brighter in the hollows than on the rims, which is backward, and which you stop noticing until a drape swings—no wind, only the release of a drop—and the pooled light does not swing with it. Sound arrives before the event that should have made it, by a heartbeat, from the direction of the far rim, as if the basin had already echoed something that is only now deciding to fall. The precipitate curtains, in the dimmest pockets, show a faint interior grain, like growth rings, except the rings are not closed. They spiral out and stop at the edge, and the next curtain over spirals the other way. Touch one and it dusts your fingers with a cool grit that tastes, if you are foolish, of metal and rain, and that grit will not rinse off in the standing sheets. It wants the drier air of a higher place, and there is no higher place nearby.

Change, when you wait for it, refuses the waiting. A shelf does not crack while you watch. A seep does not find a new mouth between one breath and the next. The loudness is a surface over a very slow body. What alters is the inventory of the day, perceptible only if you leave and come back by a measure longer than weather: a passage narrowed by a finger’s width of new knobs; a cup gone cloudy, then clear, then cloudy in a different pattern; a spine that rang yesterday in one pitch and today in a pitch a shade lower, as if it had accepted a little more water into itself and meant to keep it. The basin is still being born. It is simply being born at the speed of mineral, which is also the speed at which it is already full.

Toward the middle, if middle is a word that applies, the shelves sink until the films join into a single dark floor, cool, shallow, loud with the rain of drips from every drape that overhangs it. Nothing floats. Nothing roots. The reflection is poor because the light is poor; what you see instead is the underside of the near surface, a second architecture hanging down to meet the one you walk, so close in places that the two almost kiss, water to mineral, mineral to water, and the sound there is the loudest of all—not a roar, a braid of small strikes, thousands, none of them in a hurry, none of them finished. Stand long enough and the cool gets into the joints. The basin does not mind. It has been this temperature since it rose, and it will be this temperature while the edges are still sharp, and after, when some later age finally teaches them to round. That later age is not visible from here. From here there is only the wet, the close, the dim, the ongoing clatter of a young world that has not yet thought to grow anything but itself.
