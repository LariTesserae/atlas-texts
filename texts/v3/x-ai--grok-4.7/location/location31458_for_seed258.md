The basin sits high enough that the air should thin, and does not. Warmth holds in the hollow as if the land were exhaling into its own cup. From the rim you can see across it for a long way—ridges, courts, towers, channels—yet nothing crowds. The structures stand apart from one another with the patience of things that have never needed to close a gap. Between them the distances are real: walking-distances, shouting-distances, sometimes a full silence of open floor before the next wall begins.

There is no stone that was not grown, and no growth that is not also a building.

What passes for ground is a pale, unfinished mineral, still deciding its grain. It beads where the plants have only lately laid it down—soft plates, not yet joined, the color of wet bone and new lime. You can press a thumb into the younger seams and feel them give, warm, faintly sweet-smelling, like sap gone to chalk. Older stretches have stiffened into broad floors, but even those show the seam-lines of secretion: ring on ring, as if the basin were still being poured, endlessly, and had simply agreed to pause in the shape it has. Nothing here has been worn down. There are no gullies cut by weather, no talus, no polished channels of long runoff. The land is pre-ruin. It is arriving.

The plants are the masons. They do not carpet the place so much as raise it. A tower is a single trunk risen past any ordinary proportion, its bark plated into courses, its interior hollowed not by rot but by a deliberate absence of wood—rooms stacked on rooms, floors of compressed pith, windows that are only places the cambium declined to close. A wall is a hedge that forgot hedges and became masonry: stems fused side to side, leaves shed long ago, the joints caulked with resin the color of honey held to ordinary daylight. Bridges are roots that left the soil, walked a span of air, and set down again, thickening until a person could cross without thinking of falling. Roofs are canopies trained flat, tiled in living scales that drink the light and pass a green, ordinary brightness down into the rooms beneath.

And still the basin is sparse. Most of what you see, standing in one court, is not plant. It is interval. Air. Water. The unfinished mineral between one living structure and the next. The vegetation is total in the sense that nothing else builds, and nothing unbuilt remains—but the architecture does not fill its own city. It leaves plazas. It leaves long aisles of pale floor where only a thread of root shows, like a foundation abandoned before the walls were called for. You can walk a long time and meet no trunk. Then a colonnade begins, and you understand the whole valley has been designed by growth that prefers its own company at a distance.

Water is everywhere and never in a hurry. It stands in the courts in sheets so thin they look like varnish, warm against the ankles, never deepening, never draining away. It weeps from the joints of the living walls in steady threads, each thread in the same place it has always been, darkening the resin to amber and then falling into the sheet below without splash enough to change the level. Higher up, on the inner faces of the towers, moisture beads and runs in the same paths, polishing nothing, because there is no time here in which polishing could accumulate. The air itself condenses. On a windless stretch of floor you can watch a mist lift from the warm mineral and settle again, a breath the basin takes and returns, identical each time. No streams leave the hollow. No storms cross it. The wet is a condition, not an event.

The light is daylight, unremarkable and complete. It comes from above, finds the open courts, enters the leaf-roofs as a softer green, and lies on the water without glare enough to hide the floor beneath. Shadows are honest. A tower's shadow is a tower. Nothing flickers. Nothing suggests a cloud is about to revise the hour.

Sound does not match the stillness of the picture. The basin is loud, and the loudness does not vary. Water threading from a thousand joints makes a bright, continuous stitch of noise. Inside the hollow towers the sound changes register: a low hydraulic murmur, as if sap were moving in volumes large enough to hear, which it is. The flat roofs tick—not randomly, but in a dense, overlapping pattern, each living tile drinking and releasing, the ticks merging into something like heavy rain on a vast roof when no rain is falling. Across open floor the sound thins but does not die. You carry it with you. Stand in the middle of the widest court and the murmur comes from all sides at once, equal, without a source you could walk toward and find louder. It has no rise and no fade. It is the voice of an equilibrium that has already been reached and sees no reason to stop announcing itself.

Animals are almost an error in the accounting. Now and then a pale moth-sized thing lifts from a resin joint and settles again, indistinguishable from a flake of bark until it moves. In one tower, high, something small and dark keeps to a single ledge and does not call. Tracks do not last on the wet mineral; the sheet of water closes over them as soon as the foot is lifted. Whatever else lives here lives as the plants do: fixed, or so nearly fixed that a visitor cannot tell the difference. There are no flocks, no herds, no argument of birds at dawn, because there is no dawn that differs from the hour you are in.

The strangeness is not a monster in a clearing. It is the grammar of the place. Doors exist, but they are apertures the trunk left open while it thickened, and they do not shut. Stairs are compressions of fiber, rising in a spiral that was never measured, each step the same height because the plant has no other height to offer. In some rooms the floor is still forming: a skin of mineral sweating up through the pith, warm, incomplete, so that one side of the chamber is walkable and the other is a shallow pool of stone not yet convinced it is stone. On certain outer walls, leaves have returned where leaves should not—flat against the fused stems, green, and each leaf the size and exact outline of a window in the tower opposite, as if the plants were remembering one another's plans. If you look long enough you notice the courts are not empty of intention. Their proportions repeat. A wide court and a narrow one alternate down the basin in a ratio you could pace out. No one is there to have preferred it. The preference is older than preference. It is how this growth fills a valley when it has decided that filling means leaving most of the valley alone.

Nothing changes while you watch, and nothing would change if you watched for a season. The threads of water occupy their grooves. The sap-murmur holds its pitch. The mineral skin in the unfinished room advances so slowly that a day does not give you an edge you could mark. Warmth stays. Light stays. The towers do not lean, leaf, or fruit in any way that would alter their outlines. Seeds, if there are seeds, do not travel; new structures arise only where an old root has already chosen a site, and those sites have all been chosen. The basin is not paused. It is finished in the manner of a chord that does not need a next note—fully built, fully wet, fully loud, still being born in its floors and joints, and born already into the only shape it will ever hold.

If you stay, the loudness becomes the quiet. The warmth becomes the temperature of your own skin. The wide spaces between the living buildings begin to feel like rooms of a larger building whose walls are only the rim of the highland, and whose architect is the habit of plants that construct, withdraw, and construct again, always in the same places, always leaving the same water to speak for them, under an ordinary sky that has nothing left to do but shine.
