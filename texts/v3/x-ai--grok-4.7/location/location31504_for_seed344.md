The ground gives way a little earlier than the eye expects. What looked, from the service track, like a shallow stain in the fill turns out to be a rim: a lip of pale, unsalted concrete no higher than a knee, broken on one side where something heavier than a boot has already stepped through it. Beyond the lip the floor drops only a few body-lengths, but the drop is abrupt, and the air that rises out of it is thicker than the air above. Hot. Wet in the mouth. It smells of iron pipe and warm stone, not of rot.

The pocket is smaller than a room and larger than a cistern. You can cross it in thirty slow steps if you mind where the floor has gone soft. The walls are not old. They still show the clean planes of a cut: a blade or a bucket took this earth out recently, and the faces have not had time to round. Fresh mineral crust feathers the ledges in thin white shelves, sharp enough to catch a sleeve. Where the cut met something harder, the rock stands in angles that look unfinished, as if the place were still deciding its corners.

Water is everywhere and almost nowhere deep. It beads on the underside of a pipe that crosses the upper third of the hollow and falls, one heavy drop at a time, onto a plate of bright new stone. It sheets down the north face in a skin so thin it looks painted, then gathers in a thread along a construction joint and disappears into a crack no wider than a finger. A single pool sits off-center, no deeper than an ankle at its middle, the color of weak tea where the light strikes it and nearly black where the wall overhangs. The surface does not ripple from wind. There is no wind down here. It moves only when a drop arrives, a slow coin of disturbance that dies before it reaches the far edge.

The heat is not the heat of sun on open ground, though the light itself is ordinary: noon-colored, even, falling straight down through the open top and laying a hard rectangle across the pool and the near wall. The warmth comes up. It lives in the stone. Stand with a palm near the weeping face and the skin tightens. The water is warm enough that the drops, when they hit, sound softer than rain should. Steam does not billow. It exists as a faint, patient thickening a hand's width off the wet rock, visible only when you look along the wall rather than at it, a slow breath the hollow keeps taking and never quite lets out.

Almost nothing grows. On the rim, where the cut meets the fill, a few stiff tufts of a pale grass have taken the loose spoil, each clump separated from the next by a stride of bare grit. Lower, where the seep keeps the stone damp, there are stains more than plants: a green film in the joint, a rust-colored smear that might be mineral and might be the first insistence of something living, and one small pad of moss no bigger than a coin, lodged where two fresh fractures meet and hold a spoon of water. Nothing casts a real shadow except the pipe and the rim. The floor between these few presences is empty. You can see all the way across. The emptiness is part of the shape of the place.

No wings cross the rectangle of light. No track mars the soft margin of the pool except the one you are making, and even that fills, slowly, as water returns to the print. Whatever might have lived here has not arrived, or has already decided against it. The hollow keeps its own company.

What was built is scattered and half-swallowed. The pipe is the clearest thing: galvanized, industrial, still bright on the upper curve, already orange where the drip has worried it. It enters through a rough sleeve in the west wall and leaves through another, purpose unclear from inside the pocket. A flange hangs open on the near side, bolts missing, the gasket gone to a black curl. Below it, half-buried in the spoil that slid when the cut was new, a short run of cable tray lies on its side, empty. Someone stenciled a number on the concrete lip and the number has already lost its last digit to the seep. A single machine-bolt, large as a fist, sits in the pool like a deliberate offering. It is not deliberate. The place is too young for offerings. Things were left because the job moved on.

The sound does not match the stillness. It is loud, and it does not change. Somewhere beyond the west sleeve a pump turns without hurry and without pause, a low mechanical stroke that arrives through the pipe as a pressure in the ear more than a melody. Each drop from the flange lands with a clean, amplified tick, and because the walls are close and hard and new, the tick does not fade so much as relocate, climbing the cut face and coming back thinner. Under that, a hush that is not quiet: water moving inside the rock, a continuous pour just past the range where you could point to it. The factory, if that is what the pump belongs to, never shows itself. Only its effort arrives, steady as a climate. Stand long enough and the loudness becomes the walls. You stop hearing it as an event. It is simply how this air is occupied.

Nothing here is in a rush. The crust on the ledges thickens by a grain. The orange on the pipe advances by a thumb's width in a season you will not stay to measure. The pool's edge creeps when the seep strengthens and retreats when it does not, a tide measured in weeks. The moss holds its coin of water and does not spread. Even the heat is patient: it does not flare, does not slacken while you watch. The hollow is still becoming. The cut is too sharp, the concrete too clean in the dry places, the mineral too thin. Give it years and the angles will soften, the pipe will finish its long rust, the film on the wall may decide to be a plant. For now it is a new mouth in low ground, hot, wet, almost empty, speaking in one industrial voice that does not intend to stop.

If you climb back to the rim, the track looks ordinary again. Dust. A stain. The number missing its digit. The loudness thins but does not leave; it was never only inside. Below your boots the pocket keeps its rectangle of normal light, its one pool, its slow drops, its heat rising into a day that does not know the day is standing on a small, unfinished room.
