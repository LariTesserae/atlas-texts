The hollow is smaller than a parish and lower than the country around it, a shallow stone bowl you can walk the rim of in an afternoon and still see, from almost anywhere on that rim, the far lip and the few roofs inside. Nothing grows on the lip. The rock there has been weather for so long it has forgotten edges: a grey sinter, cool to the hand, filmed with the same damp that never quite becomes rain and never quite dries. Ordinary daylight lies on it without drama. You can read a gauge by it. You can see the color of iron.

Down in the bowl the town keeps to itself in a scatter. There is infrastructure enough for a place that once meant to be a city and then stopped meaning anything new: elevated walks on thin legs, a ring of pipe the diameter of a culvert, rail set into the yard in curves too tight for any car you can imagine leaving, valve-houses no bigger than chapels, and one engine hall whose nave would hold a congregation if anyone had thought to put benches in it. Between these things the ground is mostly empty. Yards of stained concrete. A lane of packed fines that shines where feet have polished it and dulls where they have not. Space enough, always, to see the next structure before you reach it. The town is urban in its bones and sparse in its body, as if a full grid had been drawn and then most of the blocks left unbuilt on purpose, and the purpose had been forgotten so long ago the absence itself weathered into the plan.

Nothing green has ever taken here, or if it did the taking was so ancient it left no peat, no stain, no seam of old roots in the cut banks. What lives does not root. It clings, or it flies, or it stands in the wet the way rust stands.

The water is not a river in any honest sense. A thread of it moves through the lowest yards, black and slow, never higher, never lower, taking the temperature of the stone and giving it back. Elsewhere the damp keeps to pockets: sumps under the gantries, a rectangular cistern with a skin of mineral ice at the rim that is not ice, only the place where dissolved metal has chosen to be solid. Condensation beads on every north face of pipe and never falls in any pattern you can count. The air is cool enough that your breath shows if you stand still in the hall, and not so cold that the machines mind. They do not mind anything. They have been running at the same load since before the paint on their housings had a name.

The sound is the first thing that is too much, and it does not arrive. It is already there when you step off the rim, a standing roar with no swell and no rest: fans in the hall, a gear train somewhere under the yard that has worn itself perfectly round, the slap of a belt that has not slipped in living memory, and under all of that the animals.

They are not a visitation. They are the census. In the upper dark of the engine hall they hang in sheets, small and pale, wing-membranes the color of old paper, each one no larger than a hand and together enough to hide the trusses. They do not flush when you enter. A few peel off, circle, and settle back into the same density, the same altitude, the same dull ticking of claws on iron that is simply part of the roar. Outside, the yards carry another kind: low, many-legged, the color of the sinter, moving over the damp concrete in lanes so fixed you could paint them and find the paint still accurate at the end of a year. They do not graze. There is nothing to graze. They work the film of moisture and the thin bright crust where metal has wept, and they do it at a rate that matches, exactly, the rate at which the film returns. Along the thread of black water a third population stands in the shallows on stiff legs, heads down, unmoving for so long you take them for fixtures, until one shifts its weight and the whole line adjusts by the width of a thumb and freezes again. Their throats make a wet wooden sound, constant, no call and answer, only the sound of throats that have forgotten how to be quiet and never learned how to vary.

Nothing hunts in any way that changes the numbers. Nothing breeds in any way you can watch. The populations hold. If a body fails it is taken down into the sumps by the low things, and somewhere else, out of sight, in a rhythm you will not be present long enough to confirm, another body is present instead, already the right size, already in the right lane. The books, if there were books, would show a flat line. There are no books. The gauges in the valve-houses show flat lines of another kind: pressure, temperature, flow, each needle parked a hair off a painted mark, not drifting.

The weirdness is not a monster in a corner. It is the fit. The animals are shaped to the machines and the machines are shaped to the animals, and neither side looks as if it came second. Valve wheels have been turned so many times by something without hands that the rims are scalloped to a grip that is not human. The pale sheets in the rafters roost only where the updraft from the hall's own heat keeps their membranes from chilling, and the hall's louvers have seized at exactly the angle that keeps that updraft true. The many-legged things avoid, by ancient habit, the one strip of yard where a cable tray sheds a faint warmth, and the tray has sagged over centuries into a shallow arch that sheds its warmth in precisely that strip. You can walk the whole hollow and find no tool marks younger than the weather, and no weather younger than the balance. The place is not ruined. Ruin would be a change. This is a settlement that reached its own state and then declined to have a next one.

By late in the ordinary day the light goes thin and copper on the west rim and the east rim stays grey. The animals do not alter their noise for it. The thread of water does not flash. Lamps in two of the valve-houses are lit, have always been lit, small industrial bulbs behind glass gone milky, throwing the same puddles of yellow onto the same patches of concrete. No one walks out to tend them. If there are people, they are not a feature of the hollow as it holds. Footprints in the polished lane exist, old and overlapping, worn into a single shallow trough, and they do not increase while you watch.

Stand long enough in the nave of the hall and the sheets overhead shift as one, a slow turning, like a page, and settle, and the roar dips by nothing you could measure and returns to itself. The cool damp beads on your sleeve at the same rate it beads on the pipe beside you. Somewhere under the yard the round gear keeps its perfect mesh. On the black thread the stiff-legged line adjusts by the width of a thumb, or does not, and the difference is smaller than the sound. The rim is still there when you look up. The far lip. The few roofs. A town the size of a cathedral close, loud as a works, empty between its machines, and so finished with changing that even the overwhelming life in it has become a kind of weather that does not move.
