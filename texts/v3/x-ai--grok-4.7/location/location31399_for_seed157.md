The place is a bowl no wider than a shout would carry, if anyone shouted, cut into the shoulder of a high ridge and then left alone so long the cutting has forgotten it was ever a wound. The walls stand close and almost meet overhead. They are old rock, gray going to black where it stays wet, and every face of them has been worried by water until the edges are soft and the cracks have become throats. Nothing falls here anymore in any way that changes the shape. What the frost once pried loose has already fallen. What the seep could dissolve, it dissolved centuries ago, and now the stone and the water have agreed on a form and keep it.

Light does not quite arrive. The sky is a slot, and the slot faces away from the sun for all the hours that matter, so the bowl lives in a permanent dusk the color of wet slate. You can see, but only as far as the next surface, and every surface is near. There is no horizon. There is no long view. The far wall is a few dozen paces off and already lost in the dim, not from mist alone but from the simple fact that the air between is full.

Water is everywhere and almost nowhere in a channel. It comes out of the rock in slow sheets, a glaze moving down the stone no faster than the eye can doubt. It beads on moss and does not fall. It stands in the air as a cool film that never quite becomes fog and never quite clears, so that breath and weather are the same temperature and the same weight. Underfoot the ground is not flooded. It is soaked to the skin of things: a cold sponge of peat and root and black leaf-mold, water sitting just beneath the living surface, rising into any print and then, given time enough, erasing it. Pools exist, but they are shallow lenses between roots, and their levels do not vary. What weeps in is what the plants drink and what the cold air takes back. The bowl neither fills nor drains.

The green is not tall. It cannot be; there is no room and not enough light for trees to make a canopy worth the name. Instead everything that can grow has grown into the gaps left by everything else. Moss in thick pads on the rock, and on the moss a fur of liverwort, and through that the stiff combs of fern, and between the ferns a low thicket of shrubs with small hard leaves that hold the damp and do not drop it. Lichen hangs where a dead stem still stands, and the dead stem is not bare for long. Even the weeping walls carry gardens: cushions in every niche, veils where the seep is thinnest, a green so continuous that stone shows only as a darker decision underneath. Nothing is in flower in any way that announces itself. Bloom here is a small pale event, constant, never a season.

It is cool the way a cave is cool, not the way a wind is. The air does not move enough to be called a breeze. The walls kill weather before it enters. Cold comes up from the water and down from the slot and meets in the middle, and stays. Nothing freezes hard enough to heave the ground. Nothing warms enough to lift a true mist-column or to dry a leaf. Wet cold, held.

What startles, if anything can, is how occupied it is, and how little of that occupation announces itself. The bowl is full of animals the way a reef is full, except quieter, and smaller, and pressed into a room instead of a sea. The moss is not a surface. It is a city of minute things moving at the speed of feeding: mites, springtails, the pale threads of worms, snails no bigger than seeds working the same stone their parents worked. Under the pads, salamanders lie in the exact wet they require, so many that a hand set down gently would find one, and they do not flee because nothing in the arrangement of the place has ever taught them a reason to. Frogs rest in the lenses of water with their throats still. They have voices. They do not use them. The calling that would thicken a marsh at dusk has no purchase here, because dusk does not come and go, and the need that makes a call does not rise above the need that is already met.

Higher, in the shrubs, birds sit in numbers that would be a flock if they ever left. They do not leave. They are small, plain, the color of wet bark, and they shift their weight and preen and sometimes pass a stem from beak to claw the way a bird will when a stem is useful and a claw is not. That is as close as the place comes to a tool: a twig braced, a snail-shell tapped once against a stone that was already there, a pellet of moss tucked into a fork that has held the same tuck, in one body or another, for as long as the fork has stood. No one has built anything. There is no wall that is not the mountain, no path worn below the level of the moss, no cut, no pile, no mark that intends to last. Whatever is made is made of what is already living and is unmade again by use, and the unmaking feeds the next making, and the count of twigs and shells and bodies does not change.

Insects are present in a standing cloud that somehow makes no sound you would name as buzzing. Their wings are small and the air is heavy, and what noise they produce is taken by the wet surfaces before it can gather. A beetle crosses a fern rib. Another is already where it was going. The exchange is perfect enough that the fern looks unvisited.

The quiet is not emptiness. It is the sound of a great many small processes that have worn themselves down to the same low note: seep against stone, the minute click of a snail, the soft adjustment of a wing, water finding a lower fiber in the peat and stopping there. If you stand still long enough the drip seems to stop, not because it stops, but because it has no rhythm left to notice. It has always been dripping at that interval. The interval is the silence.

Nothing in the bowl is waiting. That is the strangeness, and it is a quiet one. Elsewhere a landscape is a set of disagreements—season against root, flood against bank, hunger against numbers—and the disagreements are what a visitor reads as time. Here the disagreements have been settled. Growth equals rot. Birth equals death, distributed so evenly through the dark that no generation is visible as a generation. The water does not seek a lower valley; it is already where it will be. The ferns do not advance on the moss or surrender to it. A leaf yellows only as another leaf finishes its green, and the yellow does not accumulate. Even the erosion has finished its argument with the stone. The walls will be this shape on any day you mean by day.

There is no hour at which the slot brightens into morning. There is no dry week. There is no year when the frogs fail or the pads of moss thin out and show the peat. Come back, if the word means anything in a place that does not record arrivals, and the same salamander will seem to lie in the same throat of water, and whether it is the same body is a question the bowl does not ask and does not need answered. The animals are overwhelming only when you try to count them. While you do not, they are simply the condition of the ground, the shrubs, the air: packed, cool, wet, and still in the way a held breath is still, except that no one is holding it, and it will not be let go.
