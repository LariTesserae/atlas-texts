The ice is not weather. It is the ceiling, and it has been the ceiling long enough that the brick below has forgotten any other lid.

You come to it as a low place: a basin no wider than a parish, cut into ground that never rose much above the old river and then was asked to hold more water than a river. The walls are worked stone and later iron, a ring you could walk in an afternoon if there were air to walk in and light to walk by. There is neither. The surface sealed in a cold year and stayed. Snow and rime thickened it. Melt never finished. What hangs overhead now is a pane of gray-green ice, cathedral-high and no higher, flexed by pressure into a shallow dome. Under it the whole hamlet stands submerged, streets and all, in water so cold it seems to have given up being liquid and become a slow glass that still, somehow, moves.

The town was built for this, or finished becoming a town that could bear it. Not a drowning. A level. The flood marks on the walls are not scars; they are the watermark the engineers cut the sills to, and the water has kept the appointment for so long that the stone has rounded into the current the way a tooth rounds into a bite. Erosion here is finished work. Iron has gone to a black scale that no longer sheds. Mortar has retreated into a sandy skin and then stopped, the chemistry of the basin having reached whatever truce cold brine and old lime were willing to sign. Corners are soft. Valves are blunt. Nothing is collapsing. The place is in its shape.

And the shape is crowded. Scale is small; occupancy is not. Warehouses stand almost hull-to-hull, their loading docks parted by lanes a body could side through and not much more. Catwalks cross the lanes at every story, railed in pipe, so that the vertical is as furnished as the street. Cranes have folded down over their own yards and rest there, booms nested among hoists, chains hanging in slack catenaries that brush the next chain. Barges sit where they were moored when the lid closed, gunwales a handspan from the quay, holds open, filled with the same water as the street, so that inside and outside differ only by a thickness of plate. You do not see distance here. You see the next surface, and the one behind it, and the one threaded through both: conduit, belt-housing, the rib of a stair, a lamp-standard bent by some old shock and left, because straightening it would have meant moving three other things.

Light is mostly the idea of light. The ice passes a rumor of day, a bruised pallor that dies within a few fathoms and never reaches the lower streets. Deeper, the dark is brown before it is black, stained by iron and by the fine silt the currents keep permanently aloft. Here and there a gauge-glass still holds a phosphor charge, a sick green coin in the murk, bright enough to show the near edge of a wheel and not the wheel's far side. In one upper room, trapped under a sound roof, a pocket of air has never been exchanged. A work-lamp burns in it, or burns down: a filament the color of a coal in ash, lighting dry brick, a chair, a coat on a peg, and then meeting the window and stopping. Beyond the glass the lamp becomes threads, and the threads become nothing. No one has been in that room to turn it off. The room is a rule as much as a place: air stays where the seals hold, and the seals hold because the cold swelled every joint shut and the pressure outside leans on them the correct way.

Sound has nowhere to go and no strength to go there. The basin is not silent in the sense of stopped. It is silent in the sense of thick. A bearing turns in a turbine house at the basin's low point, and if you put a hand to the housing you feel the turn as a change in temperature and a faint granular shiver; a foot away it is already gone. Chains do not clink. They adjust. Silt does not hiss. It arrives. When a sluice in the ring wall opens on its schedule—and it does open, on a clock older than the ice—the event is a softening of the dark, a lean in the water, a pop behind the ears. The exchange lasts as long as the old timers were set to last. Then the gate seats, and the seating is a sigh you notice only because your body was braced for noise and received pressure instead.

What moves is industrial, and it moves because the water was recruited as staff. The streets were cut as flumes. Curbs are vanes. The parish was a works before it was an address, and the works kept the addresses as housing for keepers who are absent now, or so rarely present that the fauna count does not include them. Intakes at the uphill wall drink the slow seep from the ground around the basin; turbines at the foot pay that drink out as rotation; rotation keeps a line of pumps lifting a measured share back up to bleed-points under the ice, so the lid does not thicken without limit and the basin does not stagnate into a single dead cold. It is a small engine, hamlet-sized, running on the fact of being low and full. You can learn its gait if you stay. There is a long inhale through the west lanes, where the current is channeled between a ropewalk and a boiler shed and runs quick enough to comb the silt into parallel roads. There is a stall at the square, where five streets meet and cancel, and objects that have come loose—spanners, a glove, a pane of mica, the pale husk of something that might once have been a crate—orbit one another in a lazy school and are not allowed to settle. There is an exhale through the turbine throat, colder than the inhale, because the machines take their due in heat as well as in head.

Vegetation has little to hold and less to spend. On the lee side of brick, where the current is tired, a mineral fur grows, cream and rust, not quite a plant: a precipitation that has learned, over a long stillness, the habit of filaments. True growth is stingier. Near the bearings—only near them—friction keeps a skin of water a few degrees less murderous, and there a dark green thread clings, sparse as wiring errors, never forming a bed. It browses nothing. It is browsed, perhaps, by what little lives, and then it thins again until the next slight warmth.

Living things are exceptions the machines have not filed. In the warm film around a working shaft, a scatter of pale flecks hangs, each no larger than a seed, rising and falling with the micro-current of the turn. They do not school in any committed way. They endure the neighborhood of motion. Once, in the square's stall, something larger passes through the orbiting junk: a flat shape, the color of the water, visible only as a brief disagreement in the silt. It does not hunt. It corrects its angle against a current that tried to pin it to a wall, and is gone between a barge and a wall of chain. Whether it is fish or an old float torn free and taught by years to flex is not a distinction the dark will settle for you. The basin does not support a food web so much as a rumor of appetite. Most of the volume is only occupied by what was built.

The strangeness is not visitation. It is procedure that has outlived its clerks. Traffic still obeys the one-way plates, because the plates were also flow-directors, and water cannot read but can be steered. Doors that were hung to swing inward now stand ajar at the angle where pressure and hinge agreed to stop arguing; they do not drift. Clocks in dry pockets, where any remain, are wrong in the human sense and right in the plant's: they divide the sluice cycle, not the day. There is no day under the ice worth dividing. Night is the medium. The weirdest courtesy is reserved for the air rooms. Enter one—if you found a lock that still opened, if you passed through the temperature wall that stands in the doorway like a pane of harder water—and the noise of your own body arrives all at once, indecent, the tick of a buckle louder than the town. The room smells of cold metal and old wool. The window shows the parish as a brown nothing with a green coin in it. Leave, and the silence takes your ears back with a pressure that feels like a rule being reinstated: in the basin, announcement is a failure of design. The works were built to be heard by gauges.

Weather, such as remains, happens on the lid and in the grain of the water. The ice ticks. Not often. A seam propagates a hand's width and stops, having reached a bolt. Rime on the upper surface sheds, somewhere above, and the shock comes down as a broad dull change in the dome, a dimming, a brightening, meaningless to anything below except as a slight reordering of the pallor in the topmost fathoms. Inside, the silt-weather is more honest. After each sluice-cycle a fine snow of particles lifts off ledges and walks the lanes and lies down again in new drifts against the same corners, because the corners have been teaching the current where to drop its load for longer than the lintels have had sharp edges. The drifts are small. Everything is small, and packed, and cold enough that a bare hand on a railing is a negotiation you lose.

If the place has a law readable from inside it, it is this: nothing is wasted and nothing is hurried, and the boundary is the wall you can touch if you follow any street to its end. Beyond the ring, earth. Above, ice that has become geology with a maintenance schedule. Below the lowest intake, stone the drills stopped at, smooth now, uninterested. Within those limits the parish continues its shift. Turbines take the seep. Pumps answer. Chains adjust by a link. A glove completes another circuit of the square. The lamp in the dry room shortens its coal by a degree no one attends. The water, which is nearly all there is of this world, holds the town the way a mold holds a casting—dense, close-fitting, quiet—and does not rise, and does not leave.
