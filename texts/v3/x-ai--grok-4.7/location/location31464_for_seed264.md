The light is ordinary daylight, and that is the first thing that keeps you from understanding the place. It has the color of a late morning that will not commit to noon: clear enough to throw a hard shadow, warm enough that the shadow is a relief and not a cold. The sun sits where it sits. It does not travel. The shadows on the ground are old in the way furniture is old—worn at the edges by nothing you can catch moving, simply present, and correct, and finished.

The country is low. Not a pit, not a coast, not the floor of anything dramatic. A broad reach of mineral ground, open to the length of a small valley and then a little past that, with the far side visible and unimpressive: a long, shallow rise the color of a dry kiln shelf, and beyond it more of the same country, smaller with distance, never becoming mountains. You can walk it in a day and not leave it. You cannot see out of it into another kind of land. The air sits heavy the way air sits in low warm places, but it is not wet air everywhere. Most of it is only warm, and dry in the mouth, with a faint metallic brightness, like the smell near a hot railing after rain has been and gone.

There is no green. The eye looks for it out of habit and fails, and after a while the failure becomes the color of the place. What reads, at a distance, as pale grass is a flour of silica and dried mineral skin, broken into plates the size of a hand, the size of a yard, the size of a room. Between the plates the ground is a warm iron pan, dark where it holds yesterday’s damp and the yesterday before that—except there is no yesterday. The damp is where the damp is. It does not advance. In the hollows a skin of water stands finger-deep, tea-colored, warm if you put a hand in, not hot. On the undersides of the low overhangs—crusts that have curled and set, little shelves of cemented dust—condensation beads and does not fall and does not dry. Touch one and it is always the same bead, or the bead that replaced it without a change in the count.

The ground has been worked on for a long time and has arrived at the shape it means to keep. Nothing is fresh. The glass in it is not a flow; it is pebbles, dull, tumbled down from some earlier hardness and then left, because there is nowhere lower for them to go and no pulse in the drainage to move them. The drainage itself is a set of shallow threads, mineral-white at the rims, carrying a trickle that neither rises nor fails. They do not meet in a river. They wander, lose themselves in a pool, begin again on the far side as if the pool were a joint in a machine and the water a coolant on a closed loop. Scaled across the reach, the pools repeat: not many, not a marsh, a scatter. Much of the walking is dry. The wet places are events you come to, and then leave, and the space between them is the real size of the country.

What the place is doing, and has always been doing, is a kind of manufacture without a maker. In the balance it has reached, whatever the ground lays down, the ground takes back at the same rate, so the forms do not grow and do not diminish. You can see the production if you stand still long enough to trust your eyes. A pale apron of sinter below a low vent has the thickness it had when you arrived, and the thickness it will have. The lip of it is soft-looking and hard to the touch, layered like wasters stacked at the edge of a works. Warm vapor leans out of the vent in a sheet, not a gust. It smells of wet stone and a hot pipe. It sounds, more than anything else you will compare it to, like a boiler holding pressure.

The sound is the industrial fact, and it is everywhere, and it does not change. There is a basin-wide hum at the edge of hearing and inside it, a louder life spaced out across the flats the way sheds would be spaced in a yard: one roaring stack of mineral, hollow, cemented by its own fallout, breathing on a period you could time with a watch; then a long quiet-not-quiet of open pan; then a field of small cracks that tick and tick and tick at the same interval, stone expanding and not expanding, a drive-belt of stress with the temperature locked so the belt never speeds and never stops. Farther on, a seam spits a fine electrical crackle. The iron-rich crust there will bite a wet finger—not a wound, a taste of current, ozone thin in the warm air. Nothing built the seam. Nothing wires it onward. The current is a property of the damp and the metal and the unchanging stress, and it runs where it runs, steady as a line that was never switched on because it was never off.

Stand in the middle of one of the dry intervals and the loudness is still loud. It comes across the open ground without wind to carry it. There is no weather-wind. The air moves only as the works move it: steady outflows from the vents, a push along the ground that does not gust, does not reverse, does not smell of anywhere else. Speech, if there were another person, would be raised. There is not another person. There are no structures to suggest there was. No posts, no cuts that are cuts rather than joints, no slag that is slag rather than the same sinter the ground makes for itself. The resemblance to a factory is all in the behavior: scaled, repetitive, electrified in places, mechanized in the ear, productive in the slow laying-down of the same material in the same gauges. The gauges are the strange part, and only that strange. The vents are not random. The pools sit at intervals your feet learn. The plates of crust break along lines that repeat like the lines in a poured floor. It is unusual the way a natural thing is unusual when it has found a regularity and kept it so long the regularity has become the form. It is not a message. It does not look at you.

Under the fixed sun the permanent shadows make the regularity easier to see and harder to doubt. A ridge of cemented pebbles throws the same dark bar across the same pan, and the bar has a softer edge on one side where vapor from a distant vent has been touching it for as long as the ridge has stood. That softness does not deepen while you watch. Erosion here is not a story with a direction. It is the other half of the work, matched. If you broke a plate of crust—and you could; they lift, sometimes, with a dry click, warm underneath, dark-damp in a shape that fits the plate exactly—you would be introducing the only change the reach has room for, and the reach would begin, at its own rate, to make the plate’s absence into the next correct surface. You do not see that happen. The rate is the rate of the balance, which is to say it is not an event.

Life, if that is the word, is rarer than the water. There is no film of green even in the warm pools, or if there is a film it is the color of the water and does not announce itself. In one pool in several, something the size of a hand rests against the bottom, the same pale as the sinter, so still it reads as a stone that settled wrong. It is not stone. It has a margin that is a body, and a slower margin that might be feeding, pressed to the mineral skin as if the skin were the whole of the pasture. It does not flee. There is no schedule here on which fleeing would be rewarded, and nothing in the open that hunts by crossing ground. Most crossings you will see none of them. Their numbers, where they have numbers, do not rise. They are part of the level, the way the current in the seam is part of the level: a rare term in an equation that has already been solved.

No birds cross the fixed light. There is nowhere they would be going that is not this. Seeds do not arrive because whatever the country is, it does not offer them a next stage; the damp pockets do not become marshes, the marshes do not become stands of anything. The manufacture does not include soil. It includes grit, crust, warm water in closed courses, vapor, a low and constant electrical weather that never becomes a storm, and the sound of engines that are only the ground being itself at a volume the ground has settled on.

If you stay, the staying teaches the rule better than the walking does. The light does not move off your shoulder. The roar does not develop a new note. A bead of condensation on the underside of a shelf gathers to the same weight and does not fall, or falls and is replaced by the gathering of the same weight, and you cannot tell which, and the difference is not available to you. The place is not paused. Paused things are waiting. This reach is not waiting. It is in its state, loud, warm, low, open, damp in its pockets and dry in its distances, producing and unmaking at one speed, under an ordinary sun that has given it one set of shadows to keep.
