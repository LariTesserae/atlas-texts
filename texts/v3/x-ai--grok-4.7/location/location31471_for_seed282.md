The pocket has no sky and no floor that was ever ground. It sits inside a thickness of made things, a cavity that was left when the pour cooled too fast and the crews moved on, and it has been finishing itself ever since. You find it by the change in the air more than by any door: a step down a service stair that was never meant to be used twice, a bulkhead whose bolts have already grown a skin of white scale, and then a space no wider than a small room and no taller than a person can stand with the head tipped. Both walls are near enough to touch at once if you open your arms. The ceiling sags in a shallow weld-belly. Nothing here was quarried. The substrate is plate, cast block, extruded channel, felted insulation gone hard, and a slurry of mineral binder that was pumped in and abandoned mid-set. Even the grit underfoot is machined — filings, ceramic dust, the shed skin of gaskets — not soil.

It is cold in the way a closed machine is cold when its heaters have been told to idle. Breath shows, then thins. Metal takes the heat out of a palm in a few seconds and gives nothing back. The cold is not weather. It is a setting, held by pipes that run through the walls and do not quite belong to this room: a return line, a jacket, a coil that sweats because something farther along the works is warmer than the pocket and the difference has to go somewhere.

Moisture arrives the same way, intermittent and local. There is no stream. There are seams. Along the north wall — if north still means anything under this much mass — a hairline joint weeps once every few minutes, a bead that travels the length of a channel and vanishes into a slot cut for a cable that was never pulled. Condensation pearls on the underside of a tray and lets go only when the bead is heavy enough to remember gravity. In the lowest corner a shallow pan has formed itself out of drips and sintered dust, no deeper than a thumbnail, and its surface skins over with ice-film, breaks, skins again. The rest of the pocket stays merely damp: a film on bolts, a darkening in the felt, a smell of cold iron and the sweet chemical edge of coolant that has traveled a long way and lost its name.

The room is still being made, and that is the strangest ordinary fact about it. Joints have not closed. A run of angle-iron along the east wall ends in mid-air, the last cut bright where a saw stopped, and from that bright edge a rind of new metal has begun to creep — not rust, not quite weld. It looks deposited, as if vapor found the unfinished cut and decided to continue the drawing. In places the walls are only lattice: open frames, hangers, the ghosts of panels that were measured and not yet hung. Elsewhere the skin has already thickened past usefulness, layer on layer of scale and binder, so that a valve wheel is half swallowed and can no longer turn, though the stem behind it still ticks, very softly, on a cycle you cannot see. Erosion here is not wearing-away. It is the opposite hesitation: matter arriving faster than the plan, the plan itself unfinished, the pocket embryonic inside a building that believes it is already complete.

Everything is close. Density is not crowd; there are no bodies. Density is the number of made presences sharing a volume that was never granted enough volume. Pipes cross pipes. A cable tray sags onto a duct. Brackets bite brackets. A pressure gauge the size of a fist is bolted where a hand would want to pass, its glass clouded, its needle resting a hair off zero and shivering when the seam weeps. Labels have been stamped, painted, and then overgrown by the same white scale that took the bulkhead; a few letters survive — a K, a fragment of a unit, an arrow pointing at a wall that has since grown a rib across the arrow’s path. You cannot take a step without your sleeve finding something. The intimacy is industrial, not domestic. The room was sized for a function that shrunk, or for a worker who never came back, and the machinery kept being fed into it anyway.

Light is an afterthought the pocket has not been granted cleanly. A single indicator, set into a box near the ceiling, glows the color of a coal almost out: red-brown, steady, not bright enough to throw a shadow you could trust. Where the weep-seam runs, the damp catches that glow and draws a thin line, so the wall seems to have a vein. Elsewhere the dark is not empty. Machined edges return a dull grain. A patch of insulation, once yellow, has gone the color of old teeth. In the pan, the ice-film holds a little of the indicator and looks, for a moment, like a window onto something lit from below. It is not. It is only water remembering a lamp.

There is almost no green, and what there is does not behave like a plant that knows the sun. In three places — the lip of the pan, a wet bolt on the unfinished angle, a felt seam that never quite dries — a pale filament has taken hold, no thicker than thread, branching once or twice and then stopping. It is the color of salt. It feeds on the film and on whatever mineral the binder gives up, and it does not spread. Touch it and it powders. It is vegetation only by the habit of growing where water pauses. Nothing moves on it. Nothing eats it. The pocket has no animal life: no insect on the warm side of a pipe, no moth at the indicator, no sound of small feet in the grit. The processes here are not ecological in that sense. They are mechanical, thermal, chemical, and they proceed without witnesses.

They do proceed. That is the activity under the quiet. A relay somewhere in the wall cycles on a long interval — you hear it as a single dry click, then a long nothing, then the click again, as if the building were taking a pulse it does not need to share. The valve stem ticks. The bead on the seam forms, travels, is gone. Far off, not in this room, a pump starts and the coil in the wall answers with a faint tightening, a change in the sweat, a new cold. Then the pump stops and the pocket settles back into its own small weather: drip, skin, break, deposit. Dust shifts when the relay clicks, a glitter too fine to be called a fall. The unfinished cut on the angle-iron is measurably longer in its rind than it was when you entered, though you would not swear to it. Growth here is a kind of patience. The room is busy the way a closed oven is busy, the way a mold is busy, all of the work happening at the scale of molecules and tolerances.

Sound has been designed against, or has simply failed to accumulate. The felt eats footsteps. The mass overhead swallows anything from the works above, so that what reaches you is only what originates inside the pocket or in the pipes that trespass through it. Mostly there is the hush of a place with no air exchange worth the name — a pressure in the ears, a sense that the room is holding its breath between clicks. When the bead falls into the pan it is a bright, private sound, gone at once. When the rind on the cut advances you hear nothing at all. The quiet is not peace. It is insulation. Beyond it, you can believe, the larger machine is loud; this pocket was either forgotten by the noise or built to be deaf to it.

The strangeness is not a violation you can point at. It is a set of small disagreements with how a room ought to behave. The floor is newer than the ceiling: the slurry skin under the grit is still faintly tacky in the cold, while the ceiling plates are already chalked with old condensate and stamped with inspection marks from a decade the pocket does not otherwise admit. Distances are unstable in the way only a half-built tolerance can make them. Reach for the gauge and your knuckles meet it sooner than your eye promised, because a bracket has crept. Look away from the unfinished angle and back, and the bright cut seems to have acquired another millimeter of rind, as if attention were a condition of deposition and neglect a faster one. The arrow on the drowned label points into a rib that did not exist when the arrow was painted, and the rib is hollow — you can hear that if you tap it, a short, contained note — as if the wall were growing a duct for a flow that has not been specified. Rules, such as they are: water moves only along made seams, never across open faces; cold is delivered, not fallen; new matter prefers unfinished edges and the undersides of things; nothing living with a pulse is admitted; and the pocket does not complete itself on anyone’s schedule but its own, which is the schedule of vapor, binder, and the long idle of a heater set too low.

If you stay, the place does not welcome you and does not expel you. It registers you as a thermal error. The indicator does not change. The nearest pipe, sharing its cold, draws a little warmth from your sleeve and answers with a fresh, narrow fog that lasts a breath and then freezes into a lace along the insulation, a lace that will be scale by the time the relay clicks again. Your footprints in the grit are already being silted by the same fine shed that feeds the pan. The room is not a ruin. A ruin has finished being what it was. This is a chamber still arriving at its own drawing, dense with the organs of a factory, hidden inside the factory’s thickness, damp and dim and almost silent, and active in every seam — a landscape with no horizon, only surfaces, all of them made, none of them done.
