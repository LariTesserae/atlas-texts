The valley does not rest on stone. If stone is under it, it has not mattered for longer than any living thing here can show. What you walk on is made, and has been made again over the made thing beneath it, until the ground is a depth of work: mud carried in mouths, wood chewed to pulp and set, silk stiffened with rain, feathers and hair and husk packed until they take a weight. The paths are not cleared. They are worn, and the wearing is maintained, because everything that uses them also repairs them. A bare place does not stay bare. Something roofs it, floors it, walls it, or fills it before the season turns.

It sits high. The air should be thinner and colder than it is, and in the first minutes it feels as if you have climbed into weather that forgot its elevation. Warmth holds in the basin the way water holds in a cup. Above the rim the wind is quicker and the light, such as it is, comes from a sky you cannot see from inside. Down here the warmth is body-warm, leaf-warm, the stored heat of so many lives pressed into a small volume of air. It does not spike. It does not drop at dusk in any way you can feel, because dusk is not an event on the floor. Dusk is a rumor from the upper works.

The upper works are a roof, and the roof is alive in the ordinary sense: branches trained and re-trained by the things that nest in them, thatch of abandoned nests left in place because new nests are built against them, mats of vine that were pulled across gaps and then grew there because the gap stayed damp. Light comes through in a grade so low it does not throw shadows so much as fail to arrive. What reaches you is brown, green-black, the color of wet bark seen with your eyes half-closed. Higher up, where the canopy architecture thins toward the rim, there must be day. Down in the galleries you learn the day by other signs: a shift in the calls, a warming of the condensate, the way certain fliers rise and do not come back until the upper sound changes.

Water is not a river in the old sense, though something like a river moves through the middle of the basin, slow enough that its surface holds flecks of down and winged seeds until a body breaks them. Its bed is paved. Shells, packed guano gone mineral-hard, rounds of chewed wood set edge to edge by animals that do not plan a pavement and nonetheless produce one, generation after generation, because a soft bed floods and a hard bed keeps the channel where their dams and weirs already expect it. Off the channel the water lives in pockets. Old basins that were ponds when the dams were newer, now shallow pans filmed with algae. Cups in the walls where seep collects. The undersides of the woven ceilings, always beaded, letting go a drop at a time onto whatever is built below. Rain, when it comes, does not fall on you. It arrives as a thickening: the ceilings darken, the drips multiply, the pans rise a finger's width, and the whole manufactured ground drinks what it is given and holds it. Dryness is real between these events, a mild dryness, the dryness of something that never quite finishes drying. Your hands stay faintly damp. So does the mud. So does the fur of anything you are unwise enough to touch.

Nothing here is new, and nothing here is ruined. The forms have settled into what they can keep. A wall of worked earth slumps on one face and is buttressed on the other by roots that were not planted so much as permitted, then depended upon. A gallery that once ran straight now bends where a collapse was built over rather than cleared. Terraces step down toward the channel in grades so weathered you read them as landform before you read them as labor. The labor is still happening. It is slow. A season adds a lip to a pool. A decade rounds a corner that used to catch feet. You could live a life in one district and believe the place does not change, and you would be nearly right, and wrong in the way that matters: the balance is a rate, not a stillness.

Vegetation is not a layer on top of this. It is threaded through it. Every ledge holds a mat. Every seam has a tongue of leaf. Ferns occupy the drip-lines as if the drip-lines were made for them, which, in the long accounting, they were. Vines use the galleries as trellises and are used in turn as highways and as food. The green is lush without being jungle-wild in the unworked sense; it is lush the way a tended wet field is lush, except that the tending is beaks and teeth and the blind habit of insects, and the field is the size of a town and then of the valley beyond the town. Soil, where you can call it soil, is a manufactured crumb: leaf packed by feet, droppings, the dust of old silk, the rot of nests that were allowed to fail. Plants root in that, and their roots become the next course of the construction. Pull at a bank and you do not find earth under the world. You find more world.

The openness is not a view. The roof denies that. You know the scale because the place does not end when the path turns. Districts differ. Near the channel the air is wetter and the sound is water plus the things that live by water. A terrace-width away the water-sound drops behind a wall of calls, and the ground underfoot changes from slick pavement to a springy mat of husk. Farther, toward what must be the basin's far side, the roof lifts slightly — you feel it in the air's movement more than you see it — and a second kind of warmth arrives, drier by a little, carrying the smell of sunned grass you cannot find. There are features you can walk between in an hour and features that take the day. None of them open onto a horizon. The horizon is acoustic. A fall of sound tells you a gallery has widened. A brightening of one particular chatter, taken up and passed along, tells you the rim is nearer than the dark suggests. You could be lost for a long time and still know you had not left the valley, because the valley is the only sound there is.

The sound is total. It is not a backdrop. It is the medium, as water is the medium for the things in the pans. At first you try to separate it: a rattle of beaks, a seethe of wings in a shaft you cannot see, the slap of something heavy entering a pool, the high continuous sawing of insects in the thatch, a lower boom that might be a throat and might be air in a hollow of the roof. Then the separations fail. There are too many sources, and they are too near. Bodies pass close enough to stir the hair on your arm, and each one arrives already calling or already chewing or already striking the woven floor with a foot or a wing. The roof answers. The walls answer. Even the water, which ought to be the quiet part, talks where it falls from ceiling to cup, cup to pan, pan to channel. You stop hearing your own breath. You stop trusting your ears to tell you distance. A call that seems beside your head is sometimes a call from the next terrace, thrown along a gallery that was built, accidentally, as a horn. A call that seems far is sometimes a mouth at your knee, small, and busy with something that is not you.

They are ordinary animals. That is the fact that takes longest to accept, because the quantity feels like a different order of thing. Birds in the numbers that usually mean a roost at the edge of a field, except the roost is the field, and the field is roofed, and the roof is also roosts, and under the roosts are mammals in the runs, and in the runs are the insects the mammals are there for, and in the wet the frogs, and on the frogs the flies, and over all of it the traffic of things that nest in colonies because colonial nesting is what their kind does. No species here requires a new name. A weaver finch is a weaver finch. A termite is a termite. A rodent carrying a strip of leaf is doing what rodents do. What is unusual is that their works have met and overlapped until no unworked surface remains, and that this has been true long enough for the meeting to look like terrain. You do not find a nest in a landscape. You find that the landscape is the part of the nesting that did not fall down.

They do not treat you as an event. Most of them cannot spare the attention. The rules, such as they are, are rules of traffic. Low runs are for things that keep to the floor; if you stand in one you are climbed, or you are gone around with a small violence of bodies that would rather not stop. Mid-level galleries belong to climbers and to whatever nests in the walls; a hand on a wall is a hand on somebody's entrance, and the response is immediate, local, and finished — a strike, a mobbing, a spray of alarm that travels twenty body-lengths and dies. The channel edge is contested in a slow way, measured in seasons: dams extended, dams notched, pools silted and re-excavated by feet and snouts. Nothing hunts you as a category. Plenty of things will take a finger if a finger is offered into a crevice at the wrong depth. The danger is density, not malice. There is very little empty volume, and empty volume is where mistakes go.

At night — if the dimming of the upper sound can be called night — the composition changes and the volume does not. Day-callers fold into the thatch and become part of the smell: warm feather, ammonia, the sweet rot of fruit carried up and dropped. Night-callers take the same galleries without any sense that they are borrowing them. Wings of a different cut. Feet with a different grip. Eyes, where you catch them, are ordinary eyes. The condensate continues. The channel continues its patient shift from pan to pan. Somewhere a section of roof, years past its strength, settles by a thumb's width, and a thousand small architectures adjust around the settlement before morning, which is not a light but a returning of the first calls to the places they left.

You could map it, if you had years and no need to hear yourself think. The central channel and its string of held pools. The terraces, older than the dams that feed them. The raised districts where the mat is deep and the sound runs hollow underneath, proof of older galleries still in use below the ones you walk. The rim, where construction thins not because the animals stop but because wind and a truer night-cold limit what a nest can hold, and the made ground gives way — finally, reluctantly — to something that might be the mountain. From inside, that edge is theoretical. What is actual is the warmth, the damp that never quite declares itself rain, the green in every seam, the dark that is structural rather than seasonal, and the noise of a valley that built itself by living in itself, and is still, slowly, not finished.
