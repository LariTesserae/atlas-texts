The ground begins as a finished argument. High enough that the air has already given up most of what it could carry, the land has worn itself down past drama into a long table of shelves, shallow basins, and ridges planed by weather that no longer has much left to do. Nothing here is young in the way a peak is young. The stone has arrived at the shapes it means to keep: broad, low, slightly darker where old water once insisted, pale where wind polished the same face for an age and then mostly stopped. You can walk a day and feel you have not changed rooms. The horizon does not rise to meet you. It simply continues, dim, and slightly colder than the place where you are standing.

Light arrives already tired. A deck of cloud, continental in its own right, hangs with the patience of something that is part of the ground rather than a visitor to it. It is not a storm and not a clearing waiting to happen. It is the sky this elevation keeps for itself, fed by whatever moisture the stone can spare and by the slow breath of everything that lives on it. Under it, noon is a gray-brown noon. Shadows are soft-edged and short of conviction. Distances flatten. A ridge two hours off and a ridge two days off share the same lack of contrast, so scale has to be learned with the feet.

Water is an event, not a feature. Most of the year the rock is only damp in the sense that a palm pressed to it at the change of temperature comes away filmed and cold. Then, on no schedule a traveler could trust, the cloud thickens by a shade and lets go. Rain is brief, fine, and strangely local: one basin darkens while the next remains the color of old bone. The water does not run far. It finds the hollows erosion finished centuries ago, stands in them as dark lenses with ice already knitting the rims, and is gone again by the time the cloud remembers its usual height. In the lee of certain ridges, condensation beads every morning-equivalent along mineral seams and along the windward faces of the animals, then freezes into rime that the next weak gust powders back into the air. Rivers, if the word still applies, are hypotheses: a darker thread in a basin that appears once in a long cycle, occupies its bed without hurry, and leaves a stain the lichens will take a human lifetime to colonize. Drinkable water is a pocket. You learn the pockets by the slight greening around them, and by the way the cold sits differently there, wetter, as if the air had been given one small thing to hold.

What grows, grows as if apologizing for taking up space. Lichen is the true vegetation: crusts the color of rust, sulfur, and wet slate, mapped onto the stone in countries of their own. Moss occurs only where seepage outlasts the rain that caused it, packed into cracks no wider than a finger, black-green and brittle at the edge. Once in a great while a basin holds a stand of stiff, knee-high stems, pale as dried reed, clicking faintly if a hand is drawn across them and otherwise contributing nothing to the quiet. There are no trees. Wood would be a rumor from lower country, if lower country were a place this plateau consented to know. The green, such as it is, never gathers into a view. It is a texture you notice only when you have stopped looking for landscape and started looking at the ground between your boots.

Between presences there is mostly nothing, and the nothing is the larger fact. A hour’s walk can be uninhabited in any ordinary sense: stone, lichen, a frozen cup of last week’s rain, the soft collapse of your own breath. The sparseness is not emptiness of life so much as emptiness of encounter. Life here has learned to be rare in the horizontal and enormous in the accounting.

You understand the animals first as errors in the light. A ridge that should have been continuous carries a darker mass, too regular to be a remaining knob of bedrock, too still to be weather. Another, far to the side, the same. They do not cluster. The distance between one and the next is a distance you could live in. Yet once the eye has agreed to them, they are everywhere the plateau is, a census too large for the emptiness to excuse. The biomass is the scandal of the place. Plants are a stain. These are the standing crop.

They are not fast, and they are not loud, and at first they are barely legible as animals at all. Each is long as a barn and heavier than it looks, the body slung low between limbs that end not in hooves exactly but in broad, ridged pads suited to shelves of stone. The hide is the color of the local rock, because the hide is partly the local rock: a lifetime of mineral dust worked into the skin until the surface reads as masonry, plates lifting and overlapping along the spine, paler at the joints where flexion keeps the crust thin. Up close — and up close is a choice, since they do not flee and do not approach — the plates are not stone. They are grown. A fingernail drawn across one raises a dry mineral smell and a trace of warmth the air has no business holding.

They make almost no sound. A footfall is a pressure you feel through the soles before you hear it, and often you do not hear it. The pads are shaped to set weight down without scrape. Breath, even from a chest that could house a room, leaves the nostrils as a visible thickening and little else; the cold takes the volume out of it. They do not call. Nothing here does. Whatever smaller life persists — pale beetles in the moss-cracks, a kind of wingless moth the color of rime, things like slow shrews that occupy the drip-line under a standing animal and nowhere else — conducts itself without stridulation, without cry, without the tick of hard parts that would carry. The air is thin and the distances are insulting and the habit of silence is older than either. A person who shouts hears the shout taken apart within a few body-lengths, not by echo, by refusal. After a day you stop producing noise you do not need. After longer, you notice that your own pulse has started to seem indiscreet.

What they do, they do at the speed of weather. Grazing is the wrong word and also the only one. The mouth is a wide, lipless mill. It takes lichen, the grit the lichen bound, the occasional clump of stiff stems, and a steady ration of the stone-flour that erosion still supplies in its small settled way. Inside, the work is audible if you are pressed to the flank and willing to wait through a minute of nothing: a low, infrequent knock, like a millstone giving one thoughtful turn. Gizzard, furnace, gut — the distinctions blur. The body runs warmer than the day by a margin you can measure with a bare wrist. Along the belly, where the plates gap, the skin is damp and faintly metallic to the smell, copper and wet iron, as if the animal were a slow reduction works that had never been designed, only arrived at. What leaves them, hours or days later, is not scat in any barnyard sense. It is a friable, pale casting, already half mineral, set down in a line so straight it looks surveyed. Rain, when it comes, sorts these castings. Finer clays wash a hand’s breadth and stop. Heavier grains — dark, sometimes greenish, sometimes a dull brass — remain as a thread across the shelf. Over a long enough quiet, the threads become seams. The plateau’s chemistry has been combed by mouths.

No one built this. There is no wall, no track, no post, no rusted frame of a thing that once had a purpose and lost it. The industrial fact is entirely animal and entirely old. Each body is an engine in the plain sense: it takes feedstock, applies heat and mechanical work, and yields a sorted product the weather can use. Multiplied across a continent and slowed past the patience of any single watcher, the engines are a manufactory without a roof. In a few of them, if you are beneath the belly when the light is least, a weak prickle rises in the fillings of the teeth or the fine hairs of the wrist — galvanic, not magical, the leftover voltage of a gut that moves charged slurries past membranes all day and all the decades of its life. The animals do not seem to use it. It is exhaust, the way heat is exhaust. On the driest weeks the rime on their windward plates grows a little faster on one side of the spine than the other, as if the charge were combing frost out of the air. That is as close as the place comes to a machine you could point to.

They are not alone in kind, only in scale. The shrew-things under the drip-line are warm and almost odorless and freeze into stillness so complete that a boot can pass over them and register only stone. They drink the condensate the great bodies pull out of the wind, and they pick through the margins of the castings for whatever organic fraction the mill did not finish. Beetles work the same margins on a slower clock, their shells matte, their legs too cold to tick. Near the rare stands of stiff stems, a low web of pale threads — fungal, or something that only resembles fungus — binds stem to grit and is grazed in passing, a single mouthful in a day’s intake. None of this small life would overwhelm anything. The overwhelming is the sum: the spaced hulks, the dependent minute, the seams of sorted mineral running out of sight, the fact that if the great animals stopped, the plateau’s slow balance would eventually notice. Sediment would linger unsorted. Lichen would thicken past the crusts the stone can hold. The cloud, robbed of the moisture their breath and their warm flanks return to it, would thin, and the dimness — this particular dimness — would fail.

That is the loop, and it is not quick. A crossing from one water-pocket to the next may take one of the great animals the better part of a year, not because the distance is extreme for its stride but because the stride is a decision. It feeds as it goes. It may stand a month on a shelf where the lichen is thick and the casting-seam beneath it is already old, adding one new thread and then not moving until some internal measure, illegible from outside, is met. Seasons, if they exist, are gradients in the cloud’s thickness and in the frequency of the brief rains, not a theater of blossom and die-back. There is almost nothing to die back. Ice thickens on the lenses, then thins. Rime comes and powders away. A ridge-line shifts by the width of a palm in the time it takes a sapling, in some other world, to become a tree. The animals’ own lives are longer than the usefulness of counting them. You can find, on the oldest shelves, the faint oval polish where a body lay season after season until the stone accepted the shape, and the animal is still out there somewhere, or its descendant is, continuing a rest no one declared.

Weather belongs to the plateau and not to any passing front from elsewhere. The cloud deck is generated here: breath, warm flanks, the small violent evaporations when a rare sun-thinning heats a dark lens of water, the long recycling of rime. Storms, when they occur, are wide and unsurprised — a lowering, a day of finer rain, a wind that is more a change in pressure than a voice. Lightning is uncommon and, when it comes, strangely quiet at a distance, the thunder already defeated by the air. Snow falls as a dry dust that does not drift into drifts so much as into a new surface on the old one, indistinguishable by evening. The high cold is stable. It does not plunge and recover. It occupies.

Rules, then, if a place this uninsistent can be said to have them. Sound does not accumulate; spend it and it is gone, and the inhabitants have declined to spend it. Water is a loan, recalled without notice, and every drink is taken from a pocket that may not be a pocket next cycle. Warmth is produced, not received, and it is produced inside living engines for the sake of their own milling, with the cloud and the frost as incidental customers. Nothing is constructed, because construction would be a faster solution than the ground is interested in, and the ground has already solved its shapes. Distance is real and deceitful: the light will not rank it for you, and the next dark mass on the ridge may be an afternoon away or a week. You do not hurry the animals. They will not hurry you. If you stand in one’s path, the path bends, over the course of a day, by the small angle required. There is no threat in it and no welcome. You are not feedstock. You are not weather. You are a brief warm shape the plateau’s census has not yet decided to count.

At the end of a long dim day the castings catch what little light there is and read, for a moment, as roads. They are not roads. They are the residue of appetite arranged by a continent that has agreed with itself. Far off, one of the engines lifts its head — the only quickness you will see, and it is not quick — and the head is small for the body, eyed, unadorned, regarding a basin where water stood and does not stand. Then it lowers, and the mill resumes, one knock, then none, and the quiet, which had not really been broken, continues at the scale it prefers: larger than weather, older than the need for anyone to listen.
