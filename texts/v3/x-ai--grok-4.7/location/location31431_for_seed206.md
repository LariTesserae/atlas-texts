The chamber is no larger than a room you could cross in six steps, and it has no door. You arrive by pushing through a thickness of leaf that gives, then seals. The air on the other side is warm and already wet on the skin, the warmth of a body that has been breathing in one place for a very long time.

Nothing here was built. The floor is a mat of roots grown so long in the same shape that they have taken on the look of worn stone: pale where water has polished them, darker in the seams, every edge rounded by a slow that never hurried and never stopped. The walls are the same living mass turned upright. Stems as thick as wrists rise and branch and fuse until there is no mineral left to see, only plant performing the work of architecture, and doing it without joints, without nails, without any memory of a hand. Overhead the ceiling is a shallow dome of overlapping blades, green going to gold where the light comes through. The light is ordinary daylight. It does not dramatize anything. It simply arrives, thinned by leaf, and lies on the water.

Water is everywhere and nowhere deep. It stands in a sheet no higher than the ankle across most of the floor, so clear you can count the root-ridges under it, and it moves only because something underneath is always pushing. From the walls it weeps. Not in falls. In a constant fine issuance, as if the tissue itself cannot hold what it draws. Drops gather on every downward curve, swell, let go, and strike the sheet with a sound bigger than their size. In a space this small the strikes do not fade before the next ones come. They stack. They become a machinery.

That is the other fact of the place, and it does not resolve into metaphor if you stay long enough. The plants are doing industrial work. Low in the walls, swollen bladders fill and empty on a period you can time with a breath: a soft hydraulic knock, then a hiss of released vapor, then the knock again, regular as a piston that has never missed its stroke. Higher up, fibrous columns vibrate at a pitch just under hearing and just inside the teeth. Warm air moves through them in pulses. Condensate beads, runs, is taken back. Nothing is manufactured for anyone. There is no product leaving the chamber, no intake from outside except what the living mass itself draws and returns. The system has closed on its own usefulness and kept the motion. It has been in this balance so long that erosion and growth have agreed on a single outline and no longer argue. The hollow does not widen. The roots do not advance. The water level does not rise. What looks like process is a held note.

Between these presences there is a great deal of room. The chamber is not crowded. A stem here, a kneeling root there, a span of open water wide enough to turn around in without brushing anything. The abundance is not of objects pressed together but of lives distributed through a small volume with care, or with the indifference that looks like care when it has lasted. On the far root-bench, three birds the color of wet bark sit with their bodies touching and their heads turned different ways, and they call without pausing, a bright industrial chitter that locks to the rhythm of the bladders and does not wander off it. In the sheet of water, finger-length fish hold in the same places they held when you entered, pectoral fins ticking, each one stationed over its own patch of polished root as if the patches were assigned. Something larger turns once under the surface near the left wall and does not turn again. A column of midges occupies a shaft of ordinary light and does not drift out of it. Their sound is a thin high sewing, continuous, sewn into the louder knock and hiss until you cannot say which came first.

Along the weeping wall, moths the size of a thumb rest with wings open and do not startle. Their bodies are warm to look at. When one lifts, another has already settled in the same spot, so the pattern on the wall does not change. Beetles work the seams where root meets root, and the click of their shells is another gear in the same machine. None of them are many in any one reach of the eye. Step toward a bird and the space around it stays space. The fauna is abundant the way a well-run floor is abundant: every station filled, none doubled, the intervals between them as much a part of the design as the bodies.

The smell is green sap and warm metal that is not metal, the smell of wet voltage in leaf, of sugars being moved under pressure. Breath fogs and clears and fogs at the same rate. Nothing drips faster because you are here. Nothing calls less. The equilibrium does not include you, and it does not need to exclude you either. You are a temperature it can absorb.

If you put a hand to the wall, the wall is alive in the plain sense: firm, fibrous, faintly pulsing on the same period as the bladders. No bark has died back to bare wood. No stem has hollowed into ruin. Weathering here is a finish, not a loss. Every surface has been touched by water for so long that touch has become the surface's own manner. The low elevation is in the way the water sits, unwilling to run off, and in the way the light falls flat, without the blue of height. Somewhere outside this pocket there must be more plant, because the chamber is embedded, a pause inside a greater mass. You cannot see that mass. You can hear it only as a deeper octave under the knock, a slow compressive sound, as of vast tissues leaning on one another and not giving way.

Stand in the middle, where the water is shallowest and the intervals are widest, and the loudness is complete. It does not swell. It does not rest. Drops, bladders, birds, midges, the subsonic columns, the click of beetles in the seams: a single production, scaled to a room, running at the rate it has always run. The light stays ordinary. The warmth stays. A fish holds its station. A drop gathers on a downward curve of living wall, swells to the size it always swells to, and lets go, and the sound it makes is the sound it made before there was anyone to cross six steps and hear it.
