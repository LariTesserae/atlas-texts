The land has already finished the work of becoming flat. Whatever stood here once—ridges, deltas, the last shoulders of hills—has been carried off grain by grain until the horizon is a ruled line and the ground itself is only a slight argument with that line. Relief is a matter of ankles. A person walking would rise and fall by less than a hand’s breadth in an hour, and the change would be felt as wetter or less wet, not as climb. The country runs on past the eye: a regional sheet of pale silt, old water, and sky, big enough that weather arrives as a change in the whole air rather than as an event in one place.

It is warm without being fierce. Heat sits low and steady, the kind that has been here so long it no longer has a season’s edge. By midmorning the mud gives off a soft vapor, and by afternoon the light—ordinary daylight, neither glare nor gloom—comes through a thin milk of humidity. Shadows are short and weak. Surfaces do not sparkle so much as they dull the sun into a wide, even brightness. At the far edge of sight the plain simply thins into that brightness and stops being distinguishable from the sky.

Water is not a feature of the place. It is the place’s habit. It lies in every slack, films every crust, beads on the undersides of stones that are no longer stones but rounded remnants the size of fists, half-sunk and weeping. After what passes for rain—slow, warm, without thunder—the sheet spreads until walking would mean wading, and then it draws back by seepage rather than by drama, leaving the silt shining and the air thick. In the deeper pans, no deeper than a knee, the water is tea-colored over a bottom you cannot see, and it moves only when something moves in it or when a long wind crosses and combs it into low, patient ripples. There is no river worth the name. What flow exists is a drift: water finding the next inch of lower ground, pausing, warming, and going on. The smell is mineral and sweet-rot, not foul—old organic film, warm mud, the clean edge of standing water.

The ground itself is the record of that patience. It is not soil in any generous sense. It is a paste of silt and the finest sand, gray-tan where it has dried to a skin, blue-black where a foot would break through into the permanent wet beneath. Cracks open in the skins and close again. In places a harder pavement shows: iron-stained crust, a few inches thick, the last concentration of whatever minerals the ages did not carry away. These pavements are warm to the touch even in the morning, and water beads on them rather than soaking in, so they become brief mirrors, then brief drying-boards for whatever the water leaves. Nothing stacks. Nothing overhangs. Erosion here is not a wound. It is the finished sentence.

Plants do not cover this. They punctuate it, and even that word is generous. A rush stands alone, then another thirty paces on, each clump a dark vertical against the horizontal world, roots gripping a slightly higher rib of silt that the sheet-water does not quite drown. Between them the ground is bare or filmed with a living scum—olive, rust, sometimes a pale green so thin it looks like tarnish. That film is the real vegetation: algal mats breathing in the warmth, drying at the edges into curled flakes that the next water will soften again. Farther out, where a pan holds water longer, a few broader leaves float, sparse enough that you can count them. No canopy. No thicket. The eye travels. What green there is reads as accent, not as cover, and it does not soften the flatness. It measures it.

Life is louder than the plants suggest, and it is everywhere the water is. The abundance is not of large bodies. It is of small ones, many, and of the birds that live on them. The mud is written over with tracks that do not last a day: the stitch of shorebirds, the drag of something low and many-legged, the clean oval where a fish turned in water no deeper than a wrist. Crabs—or what serves their office here, small, pale, quick—work the margins, raising tiny towers of pellets that the next sheet will erase. In the warmer shallows, fingerlings flick and vanish. Midges rise in loose columns where the film is richest, and the columns drift rather than swarm, as if even the insects have learned the country’s tempo.

The sound comes from them and from the water, and it is constant without being violent. A dry, endless ticking of small lives in the silt. The slap and suck of a bird’s bill. Frog-voices from the fresher pockets—low, spaced, not a chorus so much as a relay passed across a mile of flat. Wings. The thin tear of air when a flock lifts all at once and the whole plain seems to exhale. Under that, always, the minute sound of water adjusting itself: a seep finding a crack, a pan lipping over into the next pan, bubbles loosening from the warm mud with a soft, irregular tick. Wind, when it comes, has nothing to catch on. It crosses unimpeded and turns the open water into a low hiss that can be heard farther than it can be seen. Standing still, you would not call it silence at any hour of the day. The place is occupied, and it says so, at a volume that carries because there is nothing to absorb it.

The animals keep their distance from one another the way the rushes do. A heron occupies a pan and the next heron is a long walk off, each statue-still until the strike, which is the only fast thing in view. Flocks are loose, not packed; they feed and lift and settle again a few hundred paces on, as if density itself were a mistake the land has already corrected. Nothing denser than that thrives. Cover is too poor for anything that needs to hide in vegetation, and the water is too shallow and too warm and too shiftless for anything that needs a channel. What lives here lives in the open, on the film, on the invertebrates in the film, on the small fish in the pans, and on the patience to cross a great deal of almost-nothing between one feeding and the next.

Nothing has been built. There is no line that is not a track or a crack or the edge of a pan. No heap that is not a crab’s morning work. If anything like a path exists, it is only the slightly firmer rib where generations of wading birds have preferred to stand, and even that the water rewrites. The rules are old and few. Water wins the low ground and keeps it. What cannot root in a shifting inch of silt does not root. What cannot feed on the film, or on what feeds on the film, does not stay. Speed is wasted; the gradients are too weak to reward it. Change happens the way the flatness happened: by the removal of a grain, the spreading of a sheet, the drying of a skin, the return of the same birds to the same kind of edge. A year would look like a day if you were not counting the minute advance of a mat, or the way a pavement, once buried, takes another century to show again.

By late light the ordinary sun goes amber in the vapor and every remaining skin of water becomes a strip of metal laid on the silt. The rushes turn black. The frogs take the middle distance. The birds settle into the pans they have already measured, scattered, each one a small dark weight on an immense level. Nothing rises. Nothing falls. The country continues, warm and wet and nearly finished, sounding of itself, past the place where the eye gives up and the flatness goes on without a witness.
