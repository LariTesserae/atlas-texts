The light arrives before anything else, and it does not soften. At this height the air has given up most of its thickness, so the sun stands in the sky as a hard white disc and the stone gives the brightness back without warmth. Shadows are short, blue-black, and sharp enough to cut a hand on. The cold is not a weather; it is the temperature the rock has agreed to keep. Breath, if anything here breathed as you do, would show and then be taken.

The land is a table, but not a flat one. Long ago — long enough that the cutting is finished — wind and grit planed a highland into broad pavements of pale stone, separated by low swells and by joints the water still knows. Nothing is being made anymore and nothing is being unmade in any way you could watch. The ridges have the look of things that have reached their own shape and stopped. Flutes run with the wind, parallel, finger-wide to body-wide, polished on their inner curves. In places the stone has been bored clear through, not by hands, into short throats and slotted combs. The wind has used the same paths for so long that the paths have become instruments, and the instruments hold one note.

That note is the first sound and it does not rest. It is not a gust and not a storm. It is a continuous roar pitched just above the range where a voice could answer it, full in the chest, grainy with the hiss of the finest sand still traveling an inch above the pavement. Where the bored stone stands in rows the roar splits into overtones, a chord that does not resolve because nothing in the air ever changes enough to retune it. You can walk a long way and the chord changes only by which throat is nearest. It does not rise. It does not fall. There is no evening lull. The dark, when it comes, is only a change in the light; the sound remains at the same pressure, as if the plateau were a single standing wave and the stone its body.

Water is present the way memory is present in a dry mouth. It does not run as rivers. On the north sides of the low swells a film gathers from the air itself and never quite dries, darkening the grain so that those faces look oiled. In the deepest joints a seep moves at the speed of a slow pulse, always the same volume, wearing a groove no wider than a thumb and never flooding it. Here and there a hollow the wind cannot fully scour holds a tarn the color of ink, rimmed with a white crust where the water has climbed and retreated by the same small daily measure forever. Rain, when it happens, is brief and almost vertical, a few hard minutes that bead on the stone and are gone into the joints before the chord has time to notice. The tarns do not grow. The seeps do not fail.

Between these waters the ground is empty in the strict sense: pavement, then a long reach of pavement, then a swell, then pavement again. You can see a half-day's walk in every direction and count the features on one hand — a comb of bored stone, a tarn, a darker seep-line, the far rim where the table breaks toward lower country you cannot see from here. Nothing stands up. Nothing has been stood up. There are no walls, no cairns, no hearths. If anything with hands has crossed, it carried what it needed and left the stone as it found it, or the wind has since scoured the evidence down to the same polish as everything else. The only working is what the wind does, and what a few animals have learned to do with what the wind drops.

The plants are a scatter, not a cover. Lichen maps the damp faces in slow plates of rust, sulfur, and a black that looks charred and is only pigment. In the lee of a flute, where the sand cannot quite reach, a cushion of gray-green leaves clamps itself to a crack and flowers once in a tight white button that does not nod, because nodding would require a wind that varies. There are no trees. There is no grass in any continuous sense — only wiry tufts where a seep goes underground and the roots can drink without being planed away. From a distance the plateau reads as bare. Up close it is inscribed, thinly, and the inscription does not spread.

What the eye calls empty, the ear does not. The fauna are many, and they live inside the sound rather than against it. Along the seep-lines and the tarn rims, colonies of small dark birds — short-winged, dense-feathered, feet adapted to wet stone — stand in ranks that do not shuffle. They call in the gaps of the chord, or rather in a register the chord does not occupy, a metallic ticking so numerous that it becomes a second continuous layer, bright and dry, never a chorus that starts and ends. They do not flush when you approach until you are inside the distance of a single body-length; then the nearest rank lifts, circles once in the hard light, and sets down in the same order. Their eggs, if they have a season, are not in evidence as a season. Nests of wind-sorted grit sit in the flutes year-round, some occupied, some not, in a ratio that does not appear to shift.

Higher, in the air that the roar combs flat, larger birds hang without seeming to work. They have learned the standing wave. A wing set at one angle holds them over the same pavement for as long as you watch; a change of a feather's worth slides them to the next swell. They drop to the tarns and rise again, and the sound of their wings is lost inside the stone's note, so that feeding looks silent, a mime. Their shadows are the only things on the pavement that move with any speed, and even those repeat: the same birds over the same water, the same long ellipses.

In the joints where the seep runs, something smaller ticks and clicks — not insects of the lowland kind, but armored, slow, the color of the wet stone, harvesting the film of growth the damp leaves behind. They are abundant enough that a hand set on a north face comes up bristling, and then they return to the film as if the hand had been weather. A few of them carry flakes. The wind-bored stone sheds edges sharp enough to cut, and these animals wedge a flake against a tuft or a cushion-plant stem and saw, poorly, persistently, the way a creature does that has found a trick and has no reason to improve it. It is the only fabrication on the plateau, and it builds nothing. The cut stems lie where they fall and are sanded away. The flakes return to the joints. No midden accumulates. No shelter rises. The trick is old and it is sufficient, and sufficiency here does not lead anywhere.

At the rims the table ends in scarps that have already fallen as far as they will fall. Talus lies in aprons whose stones are locked, not sliding; lichen has joined them. From the rim the lower country is a rumor of haze and a drop in the pitch of the wind, still loud, no longer deafening. Up here the chord does not reach down to meet it. The plateau keeps its own air, its own water, its own animals, and does not exchange them on any schedule you could call a season. Dawn brightens the same faces. Noon shortens the same shadows to the same lengths. The tarns climb their crust and slip back by the width of a fingernail. The birds reset their ranks. The note holds.

If there is anything unusual, it is only this completeness. Most high places are passages — weather crossing, animals crossing, stone still deciding what it will be. This one has decided. The erosion is not a process you are witnessing; it is a result you are standing in, and the result includes the sound, the damp, the thin life, the unbuilt distance. Walk until the light goes iron-colored and the disc sits on a swell, and you will not have found the edge of the equilibrium, only another pavement, another throat of stone pouring the same roar into the thin bright air, another line of small birds ticking inside it, another seep moving at the speed of a pulse that has never once hurried.
