The floor lies under the country rather than in it. From the rims, where the ordinary fields still hold their color, the land simply ends in a long irregular lip of unsettled clay, and beyond that lip the ground has gone down—not as a gorge with walls and a view, but as a broad sag, a town-sized hollow whose far side is only a paler band of the same lip, hours of walking away through wet air. In places a skin of the old surface still hangs: thin plates of soil and caliche, cracked, sagging, not yet fallen. You pass under them. The light that comes through the splits is already tired, a gray-amber that never quite becomes day. Elsewhere the roof has dropped and the hollow is open to weather, but even there the sky sits low, steamed, and the brightness does not travel. Everything you can see is close, and everything close is occupied.

The stone is new in the way raw dough is new. Faces that should have been cut by a season of rain are still smooth, still bleeding. Water does not run here so much as leave. It beads out of hairline partings, films down warm rock, gathers in thumb-deep sheets that have no channel and no name, and stands. The warmth is not heat; it is the temperature of a room someone has just left, held by the mass of the ground and by water that never quite cools. Breath fogs, then doesn’t. Condensation rebuilds itself on every pipe, every rail, every underside of broken crust. Touch a wall and your hand comes away filmed. Stand still and the cuffs of your trousers darken from the ground up, as if the basin were wicking you into itself.

There is almost no plant cover, and that absence is what makes the occupation visible. A few reed clumps have taken the older spoil heaps—knots of green, already browning at the tips, roots gripping gravel that was dumped last year and has not yet decided what it is. Algal film colors the standing water in dull greens and rusts, but it is a skin, not a growth you can walk among. The rest of the floor is mineral: slick clay, fresh fracture, white crust where water has crept out and left its salts, black mud where machines have churned the same path until it holds. Pioneer weeds show on the drier berms beside the pipe runs, knee-high at most, spaced like survivors. Nothing here has had time to become a thicket. The land is still arriving.

What fills it is everything else, packed until the idea of an empty patch stops making sense.

The water itself is a crowd. Sheets overlap sheets. Pools no wider than a stride share edges with pools you could not cross without wading, and between them the ground is only a darker, slower water pretending to be earth. In the open reaches the surface is stroked by a wind that never freshens, only moves the steam aside for a moment so you can see how far the same wetness continues: pump-house, spoil ridge, another pump-house, a pale scar where a roof-plate fell last winter and has not yet been swallowed, then more water, more ridge, out to where the far lip dissolves. It is a regional floor. You could lose a road in it. You cannot lose the sense of being among things.

The works are scattered, never a town, never even a yard that deserves the name. A corrugated shed on timber piles, black with damp, its door held by a brick. A diesel pump in a cage of pipe, running without anyone near it, the exhaust a short dirty ribbon that the wet air takes apart. A second pump farther on, electric, quieter in the engine and louder in the pipe, because the pipe knocks. Conveyor frames half-bolted, their belts idle or turning with nothing on them but a skin of slurry. A narrow-gauge spur that leaves a cutting in the rim and dies, three rails deep, in mud that has begun to take the sleepers. Someone is building a drain; the trench is there, geometric, already full, its sides weeping faster than the digger can stand. None of it has settled into the look of a finished industry. It has the look of work interrupted by the ground’s refusal to be finished first.

Animals take every surface the machines do not. The noise tells you before your eyes do. Midges in columns so dense they throw a shadow on the water. Frogs in the warm film, packed flank to flank along every soft edge, their calls not a chorus so much as a single pressure. Waders—small, gray, innumerable—stand in the sheets until the sheets look paved, lifting and settling in slow waves when something walks the margin, never truly leaving. Fish dimple the deeper pockets where a roof-fall has punched a basin; you see the boils more than the bodies. Swallows or something like them work the insect columns under the hanging crust, and higher, where a split lets in a slot of sky, a darker traffic of birds crosses and recrosses as if the hollow were a mouth they were obliged to feed. In the sheds, and in the warm gaps under idle belts, smaller things have nested in the grease and the burlap: a density of quick lives, unhidden, because there is nowhere unoccupied enough to hide. The fauna do not decorate the place. They are part of how full it is. Feathers mat into the algal skin. Droppings crust the pipe-bridges. A dead pump-house smells of ammonia and warm wet stone, and the smell is ordinary, the smell of a rookery that happens to contain an engine.

The sound is the one element that does not thin with distance. Close in, it is the pump and the frogs and the tick of condensation falling from a roof-plate onto a steel lid, each drop a small strike. Twenty paces on, those sounds have not faded; they have been joined by another pump, another colony, the knock of a pipe running half-full, the rasp of a belt, the soft continuous collapse of water leaving a fresh face of clay. Under the hanging crust the noise gains a roof and becomes a pressure in the teeth. In the open reaches it should spread and weaken, and it does not. The basin is wide enough to hold many sources, and every source is always on. Wind does not carry it away so much as rearrange which layer sits on top: insect-roar, then metal, then the low pouring that is not a falls but ten thousand weeps acting together. Speech is possible if you put your mouth near an ear. It is not useful at any range you would call conversation. After an hour the deafness stops seeming like an event. It is the climate.

Nothing in that climate hurries the land. The pumps lower a pool by a hand’s breadth and the pool returns overnight from the walls. A roof-plate that has been cracked since before the first shed was set still hangs, still shedding a slow grit, still not ready to fall. Channels that men cut silt themselves closed in a season and must be cut again; the water prefers to sheet, and the sheet prefers to stay. Spoil weathers, but into the same mud it was dug from. Salt crusts thicken, crack, and are wetted back into solution. The industrial marks—bolts, rail, a stenciled number on a pump housing already illegible—do not tame the hollow; they sit in it the way the reed clumps sit, as scattered evidence that something tried to get ahead of a process that measures itself in years of seepage and decades of settling. You can watch the surface all afternoon and see only the small motions: a bird shifting its weight, a belt shuddering, a skin of water finding a lower finger of clay and occupying it. The large motions are the ones you infer from what has not yet happened. The far lip has not stabilized. The drains have not captured the weeps. The vegetation has not closed. The floor is still being born, and birth, here, is loud and wet and slow, and there is no bare place in it to stand and watch from outside the event.
