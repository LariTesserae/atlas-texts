The floor sits lower than the country around it, though you would not call the drop a descent. The ground simply fails by a few body-lengths and then holds, a shallow stone room with no true door, only a lip of rock worn so round it no longer suggests a break. Past that lip the light gives up. What comes in is not a beam and not a glow; it is the memory of brightness thinned to a gray that never quite becomes a color. The chamber takes that gray and keeps it at the threshold. Farther in, the air is only warm dark.

The warmth is not weather. It lives in the stone the way a kiln holds heat after the fire has been pulled, except there is no fire and has not been one. The rock is pale where a hand would find it—fine-grained, almost sugary, the sort of mineral that once knew water and has since forgotten the lesson completely. Every surface has been worried down to the same softness. Corners that might once have been edges are now only changes in the dark. Nothing flakes. Nothing ticks. The place has finished weathering and now merely persists, each grain seated against the next as if the arrangement had been agreed upon and then left alone.

You can cross it in a short walk. That is the whole of its size: a nave without walls worth the name, a single hollow whose far side arrives before the idea of distance does. The ceiling is low enough that standing feels like a decision. In the middle it rises a little, a shallow dome rubbed smooth from the inside, and there the dark is thickest, a pocket of air that does not move. There is no draft to prove an exit. If air enters, it enters the way dust settles—too slowly to be called a current.

Underfoot the floor is not sand and not pavement. It is the same stone, planed by long settling into a gentle dish, and across it lie scatterings of grit no larger than seeds. They do not drift. Step among them and they accept the weight and stay where the foot leaves them, already at rest before the foot arrived. Here and there a pale crust of salts blooms in a patch no wider than a plate, dry to the point of bitterness if you were to touch it to the tongue, which the place does not invite. There is no damp seam, no darkening that would mean moisture waiting under the surface. Even the salts look finished, not growing.

The plants are few and they do not behave like a community. Near the lip, where the gray still has a little strength, three or four shrubs hold on, each far enough from the next that their shadows—if the light were strong enough to cast any—would not meet. Their leaves are small, hard, the color of old paper, and they do not stir. Lower stems are bare wood, gray as the stone, split along old seams and healed over. One of them has died in place and remains standing, complete, as if death here were only another way of staying. Deeper in, where the dark takes over, the growth thins to crusts: a lichen the color of ash on the sunward curve of a boulder, a mat of something moss-like that is dry as felt and does not green when you lean close. Nothing flowers. Nothing has recently grown. The living ones and the dead ones share the same posture.

There are no tracks. No shells, no husks of insects, no pellets, no webs slung in the low places where a spider would have found the geometry kind. The silence is not the silence of animals holding still. It is the silence of a room that was never occupied by anything with a pulse quick enough to hear. If you stand long enough you begin to hear the blood in your own ear and mistake it, briefly, for a sound belonging to the hollow. It does not. The stone gives nothing back—no drip, no grain sliding, no tick of cooling. Heat and dark and dryness have reached the same conclusion and are no longer negotiating.

What rules the place is simple and local. Light does not travel far. Water does not return. Heat does not leave in any hurry, held in the mineral the way a closed hand holds warmth. Things that fall here stay where they fall; there is no wash to sort them and no wind with the patience to arrange them into drifts. The shrubs drink whatever film of night moisture the lip sometimes admits, and they drink so little that the admission never becomes visible. Balance, here, looks like absence of event. A leaf that drops will lie beside its stem until the stem itself lies down, and that will not be soon.

If there is a season, the chamber does not perform it. The warmth does not sharpen or slack within any span a person would wait. The gray at the lip may be noon or the edge of night; inside, the difference is too small to settle an argument. Time has not stopped so much as found a grade it can hold without effort. The hollow keeps its shape, its heat, its few hard plants, its unsalted quiet, and does not spend itself on change.

Stand in the middle and the scale closes around you without threat. You are not lost; the lip is still a short walk, a paler nothing in the dark. You are only inside a finished thing—low, warm, dry, small, and so long past its last adjustment that even erosion has sat down. The stone under the hand is faintly warm, faintly gritty, and entirely unwilling to become anything else.
