The highland does not begin. It is already under you when the cold arrives in the mouth, a dryness that is not dry, a thin film on the teeth. Daylight is ordinary and complete—no theater in it, no colored omen—only the flat, workable light of a high place at the hour when shadows are short and honest. What the light falls on is not ground.

For a long way in every direction the surface is living mass, laid down so thick and so long that stone has become a rumor held inside it. The oldest growth is not tall. It is compressed. Generations of cold-country plants—mat-herbs, wire-mosses, resinous cushions, black-green rosettes no higher than a hand—have died in place and been overgrown without being cleared, until the dead are architecture and the living are weatherproofing. Walk, if walking is the word, and the sole sinks a finger’s depth into something that gives, then holds, then slowly returns. It is damp in the way a lung is damp. Moisture is not a river here so much as a habit: condensation beading along every north-facing edge, a rain that starts and stops without announcing itself, pockets of standing water no wider than a bowl, nested in hollows the plants have kept open on purpose or by failure. The pockets do not ripple much. Their surfaces carry a skin of pollen and fine ice-dust, and beneath that a slower dark.

The scale opens once the eye learns the grain. What first seemed a single carpet is a country. Low swells run for a morning’s travel and drop into slots where the living mat has sagged into old drainage, the gullies long since plugged and then re-cut by the mat’s own weight. Beyond them, shoulders of the same green-black rise again, and beyond those a paler terrace where the growth is thinner and the mineral body of the highland shows through as rust and bone-grey, already being closed over at the edges. Nothing constructed stands on any of it. No post, no wall, no cut stone set by a hand. The forms that look built—arches, flues, stacked galleries, culverts—are the settled shapes of erosion that has reached its own balance and then been upholstered. Water found those paths centuries ago. Plants followed the water and became the paths. The paths remain because the plants keep them.

Closer, the density is almost impolite. Stems pack until they touch. Leaves tile. A second story of smaller leaves grows in the damp shade of the first, and under that a third, pale and waxy, doing its work on leaked light and on the heat the mass itself refuses to give up. You cannot see soil. You can sometimes see, where a slump has torn the mat, the cross-section: a book of seasons, fiber and resin and packed dead leaf, shot through with white roots as thick as cord and with finer roots like hair frozen mid-reach. Some of the cords are hollow. Put a hand near an open end—not inside—and there is a faint, steady draw, colder than the air, as if the highland were breathing through its own dead.

That is the first industrial fact, and it is not a metaphor you have to force. The place manufactures. Not with sheds or wheels left by anyone, but at a scale and a regularity that the body recognizes as production. Along certain contours the mat extrudes. Slow bulbs of amber resin swell at the bases of the wire-plants, harden, crack, and are overgrown; the next bulb forms in the same scar, same size, same interval, as if a schedule were being kept. In the slotted gullies, condensation gathers on hanging curtains of liver-leaf and falls, not as rain, but as a timed drip into basins the plants have walled with their own packed litter. The basins overflow into the next basin downslope. Each holds a different stage of the same liquor: clear, then tea-colored, then a still syrup that skins over with a flexible sheet. Sheets tear free in the windless cold and travel a little way, stiff as hide, before the mat takes them back under. Farther along, where the mineral terrace is not yet sealed, the roots have sorted the grit. Pale bands of quartz sand lie in ridges the width of a road, each ridge the cast-off of a sieving that has run long enough to become landform. Nothing hurries in the way an engine hurries, and yet nothing here is idle. The highland is a works that has forgotten it might have been anything else.

The unusual part is how quietly the works run, and how little quiet has to do with stillness.

Sound is nearly refused. The mat eats it. A step that should thud arrives as a soft compression and is gone. The drip into the basins is visible—a thread, a ring, a ring—and arrives at the ear as a suggestion you might be inventing. Wind, when it comes off the higher shoulders, does not whistle. It leans. You feel it in the eyes and along the wrist, and the only noise is the small private one inside the skull. Even the resin, cracking on a cooling afternoon, cracks without report. People who know other high countries keep waiting for the clack of stone, the tic of insects, a bird turning over in the air. Those sounds do not accumulate here. What fauna exists is scarce enough to feel like an error: once, a narrow track of pressed mat where something low and heavy passed in the night and did not return; once, a moth the color of wet ash, motionless on a resin bulb, so still it might have been another exudate. No flocks. No chorus at the edge of hearing. The country is not empty of life. It is overfull of one kind, and that kind does not speak.

And yet the place will not hold a pose.

Stand on a swell and watch a gully for the length of a slow breath and the picture rearranges. A curtain of liver-leaf, freighted with condensate, lets go all at once and folds into the basin, and the basin’s skin tears, and the syrup under it climbs the tear and sheets outward in a dark tongue that the nearest wire-plants lean away from—not with animal quickness, but fast enough that you see the lean happen, a coordinated slackening of turgor along one face, a stiffening along the other. The tongue sinks. The plants close over the wet. In the same minute, two ridges away, a dome of old mat fails. It has been failing for years; today is only the hour. The dome drops a hand’s breadth, then a body’s length, and the drop runs outward as a wave in the living surface, lifting and settling the near growth in a ring that travels farther than it should, as if the whole highland were one hide stretched over a softer interior. Where the dome was, a mouth opens onto packed fiber and a gust of colder air that smells of metal and crushed green. The mouth does not stay. Roots already web its lip. By the time the ring of the slump has died out against a terrace, the opening is a slit, and the slit is sweating.

This is the turbulence, and it is constant once you know how to see it. Not storm in the sky—the sky stays in that ordinary daylight, perhaps a little milky where vapor lifts off the warmer mats and thins—but storm in the substance. Growth surges in patches: a plot the size of a room frosts over with new white shoots in the time it takes to cross it, the shoots already darkening, already being climbed by a finer growth that treats them as trellis. Elsewhere the mat scorches without fire. A whole shoulder dulls from black-green to the color of wet iron as the surface plants withdraw water downward, leaving their tops to crisp and flake. The flakes do not blow away so much as crawl. They catch on resin, lift, skitter a few spans in a wind you cannot hear, and vanish into seams. Under them the next layer is already glossy.

Water participates without ever becoming a flood you could name. A pocket that was a bowl at midmorning is a lens of ice by the time the light has moved, and the ice is not sealed; needles of plant tissue have grown up through it, and between the needles the melt is being drunk so fast the ice collapses inward, a little implosion, silent, repeated across the terrace in a pattern that looks, from the next rise, like a field of small craters opening and healing. Rain, when it comes, is local. A grey column no wider than a stand of trees walks the gullies, wets one face of the mat to a shine, and leaves the other face matte. The column makes no sound on arrival and none when it dissolves into mist against a colder shoulder. What it delivers is taken up before it can gather. The highland is damp because it is always in the middle of using water, not because water has been allowed to stay.

The cold is the governor and also the product. It keeps the resins from running away, the syrups from fermenting into something that would smell, the new shoots from outrunning the mat that must house them. In the hollow cords—the breathing ones—the draft is a machine’s draft: intake at the high mouths, a long travel through packed fiber where moisture drops out as rime, a release lower down that is drier and somehow cleaner, as if the highland were conditioning its own air in order to keep the works from icing shut or from sweating itself to rot. Stand in one of those lower releases and the skin tightens. The light on the wet leaves goes hard and simple. You can see, in that clarity, the next process already starting: a line of plants along the release-path extruding not resin but a pale thread, hundreds of threads, taken by the moving air and felted against a downslope face until they make a cloth. The cloth thickens through the day. By the time the light is lower—still ordinary, only warmer in color—the cloth has been stitched to the mat by new roots and is no longer a cloth. It is bank. It is wall. It is the beginning of a gallery that, in another season, will look as old as the rest.

Nothing tends it. That is the part that keeps revising itself as you look. There is no keeper, no track of tools, no scorch of a deliberately set fire, no stump. The regularity is the regularity of a chemistry that has had time. Erosion and growth have argued here until the argument became a shape, and the shape kept producing because production is what the shape is. A terrace sorts sand because roots of that thickness, in cold, in this mineral, sort sand. A basin skins because the liquor, losing heat at that rate, skins. A dome fails because the interior cords have emptied and the weight above them is the weight the mat always carries. The strangeness is mild and total: not a marvel inserted into a landscape, but a landscape that has been allowed to become its own apparatus, and that apparatus is restless.

Toward the far side of the open country, where the swells step down and the vapor is thicker, the processes run faster, or seem to, because more of them overlap in one view. Slumps, thread-falls, ice lenses collapsing, a walking rain, a dark tongue of syrup being closed over, a new white rash of shoots—all at once, all silent, all in daylight clear enough to count the leaves and still not fast enough for the eye to finish a census before the census is wrong. The highland is not destroying itself. It is not building toward a finished city of plants. It is staying in the condition it has already reached, which is the condition of continuous revision, and the revision has been established so long that the revised and the original are the same material.

If you stay until the light thins, the turbulence does not settle with it. The mat keeps its small collapses, its timed drips, its felted cloth turning into bank. What changes is only what you can prove. Edges go first. The galleries become suggestions. The sand ridges blur into the general paleness of the mineral terrace. Sound, which was already almost nothing, becomes nothing you would swear to. Underfoot the give and the hold continue, and the faint draw from a torn cord continues, cold on the wrist, regular as a duty. Somewhere a moth the color of wet ash may still be sitting on a bulb of resin, indistinguishable from the work. The highland does not dim its industry for the hour. It only becomes harder to witness, and no less occupied, and no less quiet, while the ordinary dark comes down on a country with no buildings in it and no empty ground.
