The place is a shelf the size of a hamlet, cut into the flank of a peak that has not yet learned to be old. The rock is new in the way of things that have only just been forced upward: faces still angular, edges still willing to cut a glove, grain still showing the pressure that made them. Frost has begun its work and then stopped, as if interrupted. There are no rounded shoulders here, no talus that has had centuries to settle into its own shape. The walls stand close enough that a voice, if anyone raised one, would have somewhere to go—and yet almost nothing answers. The basin holds more air than matter. You can walk its length before the light changes character, and you can see, from nearly any point, the three ways out: a notch to the west where the rock failed cleanly, a steep gutter of scree that is not yet a path, and the way you came, which already looks like a mistake.

Cold sits in the open volume and does not move much. It is not the cold of a storm. It is the cold of thin air and a sky that has nothing between it and the stone. Breath shows, then thins, then is gone. In the shade of the north wall the temperature drops another degree that the body notices before the mind does. On the sunward benches the rock is only less cold, not warm. Mittens come off for a task and go back on before the task is finished.

And yet the light is almost indecent. At this height the sun arrives without having spent itself, and the young rock gives a good deal of it back. Quartz seams flare. A patch of melt-ice, no larger than a cloak, throws a white sheet onto the opposite wall and holds it there until a cloud, far below the summit and still above the valleys, drifts across the source. Shadows are hard-edged. Color, where it exists, is saturated past what the cold seems to permit. People who stay an hour squint even when they are looking at the ground.

Water does not dominate the shelf. It visits. A seep emerges from a joint near the eastern wall, runs a bright thread across a slant of stone, and disappears into gravel that has not yet become soil. By afternoon the thread has shifted a hand’s width, following a hairline crack that opened in the last season of uplift. Condensation beads on the shaded faces after the sun drops behind the rim and is gone again by the time the first direct light returns. Once or twice a day a cloud snags on the notch and unravels as fine rain that never quite reaches the commitment of weather; it darkens the lichen, beads on wool, and stops. There is no lake. There is a series of pockets—some the size of a bowl, one the size of a kneeling body—where the water pauses, mineral-bright, and then continues downward through stone you cannot see. The largest pocket has a skin of ice at its margin even when the center is open. Things drink there in turns.

The vegetation should not be as thick as it is, and it is not thick everywhere. It has chosen the damp and refused the rest. Along the seep, and in every crease where last night’s condensation drained, growth piles into cushions and mats and low, branching shrubs whose leaves are small, tough, and almost waxy, as if the plant had decided that light this strong was a solvent. The green is not the green of a valley. It is denser, bluer in the shade, and in full sun it carries a pale cast, a bloom like flour, that throws some of the brightness back. Flowers are numerous and brief. They open in the hour when the rock has taken enough heat to lift the air a little off the mats, and many of them close again when a cloud crosses, as if the day were a sequence of short summers. In the drier center of the shelf—most of the shelf—there is only a scatter of crust and a few plants no taller than a finger, rooted in cracks, looking like afterthoughts. The lushness is a river of life following a river of water, and outside that river the ground is nearly bare.

That bareness is what makes the abundance of animals feel like a contradiction until you watch long enough. The shelf is empty the way a room with a high ceiling is empty: the volume is unoccupied, and everything that lives here has agreed to use the floor, the walls, and the wet line. On the mats, insects move in numbers that the quiet does not advertise. They are small, many of them dark, some of them metallic where the light finds a wing case. They work the flowers in pulses, arriving when a patch opens and leaving when it shuts, so that a single cushion can seem deserted and then suddenly occupied without any sound you could swear you heard. Spiders the color of the young grit sit at the edges of the seep and do not rebuild often; the air is too still for a web to be a good wager, and most of them hunt by waiting on the stone.

Birds use the walls. They are more numerous than the silence suggests, because most of them do not call, or call in notes so short and dry they read as stone ticking. A flock of small, gray-backed things works the vertical faces where cushion-plants have taken hold in joints, hopping upward in a loose spiral and then dropping, all at once, back to the seep to drink. Their wings make a soft tearing sound and nothing else. Higher, on a ledge that catches sun most of the day, larger birds—broad-winged, pale on the underside—take turns leaving and returning. They are hunting the slopes below the shelf more than the shelf itself, but they come back to the same three perches, and the rock under those perches is already whitening. When one folds in to land, the others do not shout. They shift. One will sometimes chase another off a perch in a short, silent scramble that ends as soon as the loser drops to the next ledge down. The chase is real. It simply has no voice.

Mammals keep to the margins and the hour when the light is slightly less absolute. A colony of small, round-eared animals lives in the fresh joints of the south wall, where the rock is fractured but not yet collapsed. They are out in numbers at the seep in the early brightness and again late, and they are not timid so much as economical. One watches while six drink. A seventh harvests seed heads at the edge of the mats and stuffs them into a cheek with a concentration that looks like anger. Their alarms, when they give them, are single ticks. The thin air does not carry a squeak far, and they seem to have stopped spending squeaks. You notice them by motion: a ripple along the base of the wall, a pause, a ripple again.

Something larger comes through. Not often, and not for long. There are tracks in the damp grit beside the largest pocket—five-toed, set wide, the claws pricking deeper on the side where the animal turned. The tracks are sharp. They will not last; the next condensation, the next shift of the seep, the next small collapse of unsaturated gravel will blur them. While they last they say that a hunter uses this shelf as a crossing, not a home, and that it comes when the small animals are at the water. Once, if you are still enough, you may see it: low, thick-furred, colored so close to the broken rock that the eye finds it only when it moves from sun into the blue shadow of the wall. It does not call. It drinks upstream of the pocket, where the thread is narrowest, and leaves by the scree gutter, disturbing a few stones that tick downward and then stop. The small animals are gone from the seep before the stones stop. They reappear, one watcher first, within minutes. The whole exchange happens inside a quiet so complete that your own pulse becomes the loudest thing on the shelf.

The quiet is not emptiness. It is a property of the air and the rock. Sound leaves the mouth and thins. A falling stone cracks once, brightly, and the crack does not return from the walls; it is absorbed by fractures too new to have been filled with anything softer than air. Wind, when it comes, comes as pressure against the hood rather than as a voice in the notches. The birds’ wings, the insects’ tiny industry, the seep’s thread finding a new grain in the stone—these are the sounds, and they sit close to the ear. Beyond an arm’s length the shelf is nearly mute. People who arrive talking lower their voices without deciding to, and then stop, because the place does not hand their words back and the refusal feels like instruction.

There are marks of hands, and they are few. Near the notch, on a flat that the seep never reaches, someone has stood three stones on end and braced them with a fourth. The stones are local, sharp, unshaped except by the choosing. Lichen has not yet committed to them; a little has begun on the shaded side of the tallest, a stain rather than a cover. It is not a shelter. It might be a marker for the notch when cloud fills it, or a sign that the shelf has been claimed for a season of grazing that has not happened, or a grave so small it would have to be for an object rather than a person. A second mark sits under the south wall: a ring of cleared grit, a pace across, with the cleared stones piled to one side in a heap that has already begun to slump. In the center of the ring the rock is paler. Someone made a fire there, a small one, fed with the woody stems of the shrubs along the seep. The charcoal is still black. No ash has been rained into the grain of the stone and locked there. If you kneel, you can find a flake of struck stone at the ring’s edge, the bulb of percussion still clean, the edge still mean. Whatever was knapped was knapped here, recently, and carried away. There are no walls, no foundations, no metal. A length of twisted fiber, sun-bleached and stiff, is tucked under the brace-stone of the cairn—cord, not rope, made from the inner bark of something that does not grow on the shelf. Whoever comes here comes from lower down, with simple tools, and does not stay long enough for the place to record them as more than a pause.

The shelf is in motion even when it looks settled. In the time it takes to cross from the cairn to the seep, the ice-skin on the pocket retreats a finger’s width and then, when a cloud covers the sun, stops retreating. A cushion of flowers opens along its sunward half and keeps its other half shut, so that it appears half-awake. The hunting birds trade perches. A joint in the east wall gives a small, dry report—not a fall, a settling—and a veil of grit slides a short distance and comes to rest on a mat, which will either shed it or grow through it before the season turns. The seep, persistent and slight, is cutting a groove so new that its edges are still the color of the interior of the rock, not the color of the weathered face. In a few years the groove will be a line you can see from the notch. In a few more it may steal the pocket and leave the animals to find another pause in the water. The colony in the south wall is already using a crack that was not open, or not open enough, when the fire-ring was made; the pale dust of fresh fracture coats the fur of the ones that emerge lowest.

What is slightly unlike other high places is the timing. Life here is not simply enduring the cold and the light. It is synchronized to the brief, local summers manufactured by the angle of the sun on new stone. When a cloud shadow crosses, the shelf pauses in a way you can watch: flowers begin to close, insects lift and do not settle, the drinkers at the pocket finish and retreat, and the quiet deepens by a degree that is not quite sound. When the light returns, the same ground resumes, not gradually, but as if a condition had been met. The animals do not sing their way through the day. They spend the brightness. They spend the damp. They spend each other. The rock, which has not yet been worn into the compromise of an old mountain, spends itself too—grain by grain, crack by crack—into a shape it will not keep.

By the time the sun is low enough to strike only the upper third of the west wall, the shelf has changed occupants without seeming to change character. The mats are fully lit in their upper leaves and dark beneath. The seep smokes, very faintly, where the colder air of the wall meets water that has crossed sun-warmed stone. The broad-winged birds have gone down into the valleys and one remains, folded, on the highest perch, facing the notch. The small mammals are out in force along the wet line, less cautious in the angled light, and the five-toed tracks by the pocket have been joined by a second set, arriving, overlaying, leaving toward the scree. The cairn throws a long, hard shadow across stone that will be in shade for the rest of the day and, by morning, may hold a crust of condensation thick enough to darken it. Nothing on the shelf is permanent except the fact of the shelf, and even that is only as permanent as a mountain that has just stood up.

If you stay until the direct light leaves, you will hear, finally, one sound that carries: a single stone, somewhere in the gutter, giving up its place and finding another. Then the quiet closes again, and the shelf continues, bright at its rim, cold in its volume, busy along its thin wet thread, and almost entirely unfilled.
