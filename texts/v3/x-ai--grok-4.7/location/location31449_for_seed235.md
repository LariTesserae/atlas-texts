The shelf begins where other mountains end, and then it continues. From far below it reads as a pale rim, a lid on the weather, but the climb does not thin the air. It thickens it. Heat comes up through the soles and down out of a sky that stays a hard, ordinary blue, noon-colored and unmoving, while the body learns that height and cold have parted company. Breath goes heavy. Sweat does not dry. It joins what is already in the air.

Water is not a feature of the place. It is the place’s near surface. Rock faces weep in sheets so even they look poured and left. Every ledge carries a skin. Hollows hold tea-colored marsh, warm to the wrist, never drawing down, never overtopping the mineral lip that has settled around them. Steam does not travel. It stands in lanes between the growth, bright and still as hanging cloth, and if you walk through one you come out wetter and the lane is where it was. Springs rise in the pavements—if pavements is the word—and run off in channels that have long since found their depth. The cuts are rounded. Nothing is fresh-broken. Erosion here finished its argument ages ago and now only keeps the verdict: lips eased, corners gone, each spillway exactly as wide as the water that uses it.

The growth is lavish and unaided by anything that crawls or flies. There is no animal life. No midges in the steam, no birds on the high forks, no tracks in the warm mud except the ones your own feet fail to leave. Mats of fine leaf knit the marshes. Thicker stands rise into trunks that split, rejoin, and split again until they are walls. Between them, creepers have plated themselves into screens so dense the light on the far side is green even though the sky is clear. Flowers, where they occur, are not visited. They are simply open, and they stay open, the same petal at the same angle, a bead of water at the same notch of the same lip. Sap smell is constant: hot, sweet, slightly metallic, mixed with wet limestone and the flat odor of iron weeping out of the rock.

What organizes all this is a city, and it is not a ruin and not a camp. It occupies the whole shelf and steps down into three hanging valleys you can see at once, a regional spread of basins, causeways, and stacked courts. Streets are the channels. Plazas are the broader pools, islanded with root-mats. Arcades are trunks leaned and fused until the undersides form vaults, and along those undersides water sometimes runs the wrong direction, a clear rope of it clinging where stone should shed it, then falling at a lip it has always fallen from. Cornices are not cut. They are grown and then plated, pale travertine and darker iron rind built up until the living wood beneath is only a rumor of grain. Layers sit near enough to touch: canopy, then a balcony of braided stem, then a dripping undercroft, then the warm marsh again. You pass through several rooms of air in a single step. Nothing was assembled with a tool. There is no joint a chisel would recognize, no course of stone, no nail, no fired brick, no device. The order is older than making and finer than accident—alignments that repeat across a valley, courts that answer one another, spillways graded so each basin holds exactly and never otherwise.

The scale stays open even where the growth is thick. A turn that should bury you in green gives, instead, a long sight down a water-street to the next basin, and beyond it a pale tower of fused trunks against the unmoving blue, and beyond that the drop, where ordinary mountains resume far below like a different country. Distance is not always honest. A court you have not reached can seem to occupy the same air as the leaves beside your face, and then, without a change in the light, stand a valley off again. Walking does not correct this. It only offers another version of the same relations. The shelf does not get larger as you learn it. It gets more simultaneous.

Sound is the other weight. It does not rise in weather and does not rest. A thousand fixed falls speak at once, and under them a bodily note from the plants themselves—tissues under pressure, wet fiber, steam finding the same cracks—and under that the low speech of water inside the rock. Together they are deafening in the plain sense: you do not hear your footfall, or your breath, or a word if you were foolish enough to spend one. The roar has no phrase. It does not swell toward anything. It is the same pressure on the ear at the marsh edge and under the vaults and at the lip where the shelf gives way. Teeth ache with it. After a time it stops resembling sound and resembles climate.

Nothing in the arrangement is becoming. Leaves do not unfurl further or fail. The bead does not fall until it falls, and the falling is the condition, not an event: the drop is in the air, the splash is shaped, the surface is unmarked by any previous splash, and if you look away and back the geometry has not advanced. Shadows lie where an ordinary sun would put them and do not travel. The light is clear daylight, neither dawn nor dimming, sufficient to see grain in bark and the red thread of iron in a wet wall. Heat does not break toward evening because evening does not gather. If you climb within the city—up a root stair that was never built, only remained—the air grows hotter, which is the local law, and wetter, and the roar does not thin with height.

You cannot write yourself into it. A hand set against a weeping face comes away wet, and the face is as it was, the same rivulets in the same paths. A torn leaf, if tearing can be made to happen, is not torn when the hand opens; or the leaf was never the one you took. Footprints fill with the water that was already there, at the level that was already the level. The city accepts a body the way a current accepts a stone it will not move: you are present, and the equilibrium does not record the presence. Paths that ought to leave a plaza return you to the same three channels, the same iron-streaked vault, the same bead. This is not a maze laid to trap. It is a form that has nowhere to go and has stopped pretending it might.

At the outer lip the roar is no louder, because it cannot be, and the view is the only gradient the place allows—the long drop into colder, thinner, animal country, clouds moving down there as if time still had work. On the shelf the steam lanes stand. The courts hold their exact water. The green screens pass a green light. The sun, if it is a sun, does not shift its ordinary glare. The city of trunks and plated lips and warm marsh continues past the immediate, basin after basin, all of it lush, all of it hot, all of it loudly and perfectly finished.
