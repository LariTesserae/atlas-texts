The water is the country. It begins where the last gray light fails, a few tens of meters under a cold skin of sea, and it does not end at any shore a person could name. What light arrives is already tired: a blue that has been sieved of red, then of green, until only a rumor remains, and that rumor dies in the suspended load. Below that, the basin is dark in the ordinary way deep water is dark. Not a void. A fullness you cannot see through.

The basin itself is old past any useful comparison with hills. Whatever stood here—ridges, stacks, the teeth of a younger coast—has been planed. Currents older than the present climate worked the stone the way a slow hand works a whetstone, and then the fines of that work settled back over what they had made. The floor is not flat in the sense of a table. It is flat in the sense of a life finished: long swales a person would not notice walking, shallow channels whose walls have slumped into themselves, a fan of ancient spill whose lobes have merged until the seams are only changes in the grit underfoot. Relief is measured in the height of a body, sometimes less, across distances you could walk for a day. The rock that still shows is rounded, dark, slick with a film of clay. Edges have been owed to time for so long that time has collected.

What occupies the place is not forest and not city. It is particles. Silt, clay, the ground-up bodies of things that lived higher up and died on the way down, flakes of stone no larger than dust. The water is cold enough that it feels thick, a little above the temperature where seawater begins to think about ice, and it is packed. At the surface of this depth—if a cloud can be said to have a surface—the load is a haze. Deeper, in the nepheloid layer that never quite clears, it is a weather. You can move your hand and feel the drag of grains that are not quite a solid and not quite a liquid. Visibility, where any light is carried down by a lamp or a biolume, collapses to an arm’s length, then to nothing. The density is the medium. There is no room between presences because the presences are the water’s contents, and they are everywhere, resting, lifting, settling, lifting again.

Sound behaves as if the place were empty. It is not. A grain striking a grain is an event, and there are uncountable events, but each is too small, and the cold water takes the energy and keeps it. What a listener hears, if anything hears, is a low ongoing hush, the way a heavy cloth sounds when it is dragged once across a floor and then left. Now and then a softer click: a stone no bigger than a fist, finally persuaded off a slope it has occupied since before any calendar, ticking once against another and going still. The basin does not roar. It is turbulent the way a room full of slowly stirred milk is turbulent—sheets of motion, billows, internal waves along density boundaries a few meters thick, water of one cold sliding under water of another. The motion is continuous and large. It does not advertise.

The rules are physical and unsurprising, which is what makes them severe. Light does not farm here. Anything green that arrives has already lost. Fragments of weed, a torn blade, a clot of plankton from the lit country above, drift down already failing, and the silt takes them. Vegetation in any honest sense is scarce: a smear of bacterial film on a stone where a little reduced chemistry leaks from the old rock; a pale scurf in a hollow that holds organic mush a day longer than the ridge beside it. No kelp. No meadow. The floor’s color, when color can be forced onto it, is the color of wet ash and old iron.

Animals are rare in the same proportion. Not absent—the basin is not dead—but you can cross a long reach of it and meet nothing with a nerve. A worm-thick trail in the mud, already softening. A pale crab the size of a palm, moving as if the cold had to be negotiated for each leg. Somewhere in the mid-water, higher than the floor and lower than the last light, a scatter of small lights that are not stars: a jellyfish or a chain of them, pulsing, the only quick brightness, gone when the sediment billow turns. Fish exist. They are few, slow, and built for not spending. They hang where a density boundary offers a little less work, and they eat what the fall brings, which is not much and not regular. Nothing hunts in packs. Nothing sings. A life here is mostly the discipline of waiting inside a moving cloud.

The turbulence is the plot. It does not look like a storm from above, because above is another country, often merely gray and windy, sometimes almost calm. Down here the weather is lateral and basal. Internal waves travel the thermocline and, where they steepen against the rising of the old fan, they break without air, a rolling of cold into colder, shedding vortices that stand for minutes like ghosts of smoke and then are only silt again. Along the subtle channels—the only roads, and they are not roads—gravity still knows how to gather. When a shelf of the upper slope, far off at the basin’s rim, fails, the failure does not announce itself as thunder. A density current forms: a tongue of sediment-heavy water, colder, darker, sliding down a grade so gentle a standing person would call it level. It moves faster than any animal here. It is a river with no surface, a few meters thick or a few tens, and it rearranges the floor as it goes. Mud that has lain since the last such tongue is lifted, held, and dropped elsewhere. Ripples form and are erased inside an hour. A hollow fills. A low rise is skinned. Then the current thins, drops its cargo in a graded sheet—coarser grains first, fines drifting on—and the hush returns, identical to the hush before, over a geography that is not the same.

This is the dynamism of a place that has already reached its oldest stable argument with erosion. Nothing new is being built. No peak is rising into drama. The basin receives, transports, and files. Creation and destruction are the same gesture: a grain leaves one rest and finds another. Over a human life the floor looks eternal. Over the life of the basin it is a slow boiling, a resettling, a library continually reshelved by a librarian who never reads. The ancient flatness is not peace. It is the shape turbulence takes when it has had time enough to remove every other shape.

There is no structure. No wall, no post, no cut stone, no line that was drawn on purpose. If anything like a tool exists, it is a claw, a tube of mucus a worm lined and abandoned, a depression an animal made by sitting still and letting the silt mantle it. These last a while and then do not. The water does not keep records of intention. Burrows collapse when the next tongue passes. The film on the rock is not a crop; it is a chemistry that happens to be alive, and it starves where the seepage pinches out. A walker—if walking were possible, boots sinking to the calf, each step a small turbidity of its own—would find no artifact to triangulate by. Orientation is by the grain of the current, by which way the fines lean as they settle, by the faint persistent slope that only a long crossing reveals. Regional, not vast in the abyssal sense of an ocean with no edge, but large enough that the far rim is a rumor of shallower water and a change in the pitch of the floor, a day’s travel or three, with mid-features that repeat: swale, skin of clay, channel axis where the last flow scoured a palm’s depth of mud and left a pavement of small rounded stones, then swale again.

Cold sits in the body as weight. It is not a wind. It is the temperature of the argument the sea has already had with winter, stored and brought down. Breath, if breath were relevant, would fog and vanish into the same haze that hides a hand. Metals would ache. Flesh would dull. The basin does not warm in the afternoon; there is no afternoon. There is a slight cycling of the internal waves, a pulse on the order of hours, and a longer cycling of failures at the rim, weeks or years, and the seasonal faintness of the fall of dead plankton, which changes the silt’s smell if anything here could smell: a thin sweetness, then only mineral again.

At the margin of the dark, where the last usable blue still comes and goes with the surface day, the packed water thins enough that shapes almost resolve. That band is not the basin. It is a lid. Rain on the real surface, heavy or not, is a different fact, felt down here only as a trivial freshening, a slight destabilizing, days later, of the upper boundary. The wall of weather that matters is the one inside: sheets of silt standing and falling, billows with hard edges where one water refuses another, a slow rotational debris that looks, in a lamp beam, like smoke in a closed room after a door has been slammed and the sound has already left. The slam is missing. The smoke remains for as long as you are willing to watch, and then a new sheet arrives crosswise and the pattern is lost without ceremony.

What lives rare keeps to habits that waste nothing. The crab pauses half-buried when the boundary wave passes over it, because swimming is expensive and the wave will end. A fish turns into the flux and holds, mouth barely open, harvesting what the billow concentrates along its face. In the bacterial smear, nothing moves at a scale that matters; chemistry proceeds, warm only by comparison with the stone. Between these points lie reaches of floor that are only floor: graded mud, a scatter of pebbles planed to ovals, a single larger cobble that has not moved since the current that delivered it lost its nerve. Around that cobble the silt weaves and drops a small shadow-bar, a comet-tail of fines, pointing the way the last tongue went. It is the nearest thing to a sign. It will be rewritten.

If the place has a season, it is the season of a particular current. Between events the basin seems finished, the end of topographic life, a country settled into its final form. During an event it is all revision, anthill-busy at the scale of grains and perfectly silent at the scale of ears, hyperactive and ancient in the same minute. Both descriptions are true, and neither is a mood. They are the two speeds of one material.

Night is not a condition that arrives. It is the default, and the surface day is a faint modulation felt only at the lid. Stars do not participate. The only lights are carried: a rare animal’s brief signal, answered or not; the lamp of anything foolish enough to come down from the lit world, which immediately makes a halo in the fines and then a moving pocket of gold that the silt begins, at once, to erase. Outside that pocket the basin continues its work without reference. Packed, cold, quiet, unbuilt, almost lifeless, and never still—an old floor under a moving mountain of dirty water, open enough to be a region, closed enough that you cannot see the next swale until the cloud in front of your face decides to thin.
