The plateau has already finished. That is the first thing about it, and it takes a long time to believe. The stone goes out level in every direction, the color of old bone and dried salt, and the wind crosses it with nothing to do. Edges that must once have been cliffs have been rubbed down to slopes a person can walk without noticing the drop. Pebbles have become meal. There are no ravines left to carry anything, and nothing left that would need carrying. Heat sits on the flat even when the light is only a high white film, and by afternoon the salt crust ticks as it expands, a small dry applause that dies as soon as you stop to listen.

You can cross the place for an hour and think it empty. The hollow does not announce itself. A last rim of caprock, rounded as a thumb, leans out over a shadow the width of a street. The shadow smells, faintly, of animals and green. Only when the wind drops do you hear it: a packed, continuous sound, not water, not weather, something with many throats in it. The entrance is a slot at the rim's inner fold, head-high, warm air pushing out against your face. Inside, the day thins to brown.

The hollow is one room that has learned to be a town. Twenty paces would take you across the floor if the floor were clear, which it is not. The stone shell is the old one — a solution pocket or a wind-scoop, it no longer matters, scoured to its stable shape and then left alone by the plateau's long retirement. Into that shell, and against it, and over it, people have built with what the shell would hold. Walls of fist-sized stone set in a mortar of dung and clay. Roofs of fossil wood, black and light as charcoal, the buried forest of some wetter age levered out of the salt and lashed with fiber. Ladders of the same wood, polished at the hands. No metal anywhere that catches; a few dull pins, maybe, no longer bright enough to matter. The lanes are the gaps between one family's stack of rooms and the next, narrow enough that two baskets must be turned sideways to pass. Sleeping platforms, drying racks, tether-pegs, querns, coil of rope, a shrine the size of a cupboard with its offering already eaten by something — all of it occupies the same air. There is no margin. A child can cross the whole settlement under the table-line of the upper rooms and never come into the light.

The light is the overhang's. Outside is harsh; in here the sun arrives only as a slant at the slot, late and early, and otherwise as a meal of dust hung in the warm dark. You see by reflection off pale leaves and off the guano-lime that paints the rear wall. It is enough to work by, if you know the work. It is not enough to read a stranger's face across the court.

What grows here does not drink, or not in any way you can watch. There is no cistern, no seep, no dark stain low on the stone where water would have loved. The air on the tongue is dry enough to catch. And still the hollow is green to the point of blockage. Fat-leaved shrubs, woody and waist-deep, have taken every ledge the builders left them and several they did not. Their leaves are thick as ears, waxy, crowded into mats that look, in the dimness, like a single animal breathing. From the caprock hang gray tongues of air-plants, packed shoulder to shoulder, each one a small cistern that is never liquid — they take what the night lays on them and what the breath of the colony lays on them, and by morning the film is gone. Underfoot, where you can find underfoot, rosettes of the same family tile the dust. A milk-vine runs the lashings of every ladder, cut and recut, bleeding a slow white sap into gourds. That sap, and the milk of the goats, and the juice pressed from the fat leaves, are what people drink. Water as a thing that pools and reflects does not occur. If a leaf sweats, another leaf has taken it before it can fall. The moisture of the hollow lives inside bodies, and moves when they move, and is not a feature of the ground.

The bodies are mostly not human. This takes longer to understand than the dryness, because the town is so clearly a town — thresholds, property marked by cords of a certain knot, a lane kept open to the slot by silent agreement — and yet the population that uses it is feathers, scales, and small hooves. Swifts pack the under-eaves in a living thatch, a hundred to a beam, and when they turn over in their sleep the beam creaks. Parrots the color of the fossil wood occupy the fossil wood, and will not be argued off a rung; people climb around them. Hyraxes, or something kin to them, warm and round and irritable, hold the mid-ledges and defecate in appointed corners that are also the mortar-pits and the fuel store. Geckos plank the clay walls at dusk so thickly the wall seems to ripple. Beetles work the litter with a sound like dry rain, the only rain. At the slot a hawk hangs in the afternoons and the whole front of the town knows it without looking. The human families are six, perhaps seven, and they are the quietest large animals present, which is not the same as being quiet.

The noise is the shape of the place. Low and constant: claws on wood, the chew of fat leaves, the mutter of birds not yet alarmed, a quern, a goat bell made of a pierced heel-bone. Then, twice a day, the exodus and the return. At first light the swifts leave as one body and the hollow inhales; the wing-sound is a single rough cloth torn the length of the court, loud enough that speech stops, and for a minute afterward the rooms feel wrongly large. At dusk they come back against the dimness and the cloth tears the other way, and droppings tick on the roofs, and the parrots shout the news of the plateau, which is nothing, and shout it again. People pitch their voices through this the way people elsewhere pitch them through surf. Transactions are held at the ear. Children learn the alarm notes before they learn the lanes. There is no water-sound under any of it — no drip at the back of the cave, no trickle in a groove — and the absence is so complete that a visitor keeps waiting for it, as if loudness of this kind ought to have a wet center. It does not. The loudness is feathers, feet, fiber, stone.

The day turns on the animals, because nothing else here turns. Midmorning, after the swifts are gone, is for work that needs the better light near the slot: splitting vine into cord on a bone flake, grinding the small oily seeds the shrubs set, mending a ladder where a parrot has worried the lashing loose. Nobody quarries. The stone shell is not to be opened further; it is already the shape it will keep, and the town's entire architecture is insertion and repair. A new room is made by convincing a neighbor to share a wall and by thinning a shrub, not by cutting the plateau. Tools are edges of flint and of the fossil wood itself, which splits true, and awls of bird-bone, and cords graded by touch in the bad light. Fires are small, of dung and dead vine, and are suffered more than wanted; the hollow holds heat, and smoke has nowhere to go but the slot, and the birds hate it. Cooking is often no fire at all — sap soured in a gourd, seeds eaten as they are, a strip of goat dried on the rack until it rings.

Through midday the plateau's wind rises and the slot moans, a single note, and dust rains a fine salt onto everything near the entrance. People hang mats. The plants along that edge are the pale ones, salt-burned and still growing, and they catch the dust in their wax and look frosted. Deeper in, the air goes still and hotter and more animal, and the hyraxes flatten into their own shadows, and work stops. This is when the hawk comes. Nobody nets it. It takes a swift that was foolish or a chick that rolled, and in exchange the colony stays inside a pocket no predator can enter in numbers. The bargain is older than the knots on the thresholds.

Late day is the market, if a market can be six households and a path two baskets wide. What changes hands is cord, seed, a gourd of sap, a cured skin, a promise of dung for someone's mortar, the right to tether on a particular peg through the next birthing season. Prices are arguments conducted in the bird-noise, settled fast, forgotten. Outsiders are rare enough that there is no stall facing the door. The plateau gives nothing edible. Anyone arriving brings news of other high places, most of them as finished and as empty as this one, and leaves with cord, which is good, and with salt scraped from the flat, which is free and heavy.

Toward evening the goats are brought in from the rim, where they spend the day on browse so sparse it looks like geology. They know the slot. They smell rank and wonderful, and their milk is the one sweetness. Children drink first. The goats stand where the shrubs have been browsed into tunnels, and the tunnels are also streets, and a person going to a neighbor's platform after dark goes on hands and knees through leaves that give no water and brush the face like hands.

Night does not silence it. The swifts settle into a thick shifting sleep. Moths come to the warmth of dung and are taken by the geckos with small sticky sounds. Someone is always awake, because the tether-pegs matter and because a hyrax in the seed-store is a political event. The fossil wood ticks as it cools, the same tick the salt crust makes outside, and it is possible, lying on a platform with a parrot muttering under the rung, to hear the hollow as a continuation of the plateau rather than an exception to it: the same ancient dryness, the same finished stone, only folded once, and in the fold a density the flatness could not hold.

By the time you have stayed a few days the rules are no longer interesting, which is how you know they are rules. Moisture is not to be exposed. A spilled sap-gourd is a shame of a particular kind, not because of waste only, but because a dark wet spot on stone looks like a claim the place will not support; someone scuffs dust over it before it can be seen. Space is borrowed, never vacant. An unused peg is a peg someone has failed to need, and it will be needed by morning. Nothing is thrown out the slot. Outside, the wind would take it and the salt would keep it forever, a confession on the flat; inside, it is dung or fiber or food, and the categories are allowed to overlap. Speech yields to wing-noise without resentment. The stone is not improved. Children may grow up and go, walking out onto the finished plateau toward other rims, but the hollow does not grow with them. It was full when the first lashing was tied. It is full now. It will be full, in the same shape, when the caprock finally, in some further age, thins enough to let the true sun in — and even that, the old people say, will not make water run. The plateau has already done all the changing it intends. What remains is this: a warm dark pocket at the end of erosion, loud with other lives, green without a drop, turning over inside itself like a hand closed around a seed.
