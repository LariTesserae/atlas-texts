The rim is only a suggestion. Whatever peak or crater once stood here has been planed down to a low shoulder of stone, pale and cold, that you step over without quite noticing you have entered anything. The air on the far side is the same thin air, the light the same ordinary daylight—high, colorless, a little hard—but the ground falls away by a few body-lengths into a shallow dish, and the dish has edges. You can see all of them. From the center to the far lip is a walk of a few hundred paces, less if you cut across the open stone. The sky occupies more of the view than the land does. There is almost nothing standing up in it.

Underfoot the rock has the look of something that finished its argument with weather a long time ago and then stayed to keep the terms. No scree. No fresh faces. Grain has been worried out of grain until the surface is a skin of cemented dust, faintly polished where old ice once slid, pitted where it did not. Seams that might once have been joints or faults have been widened into shallow troughs and then half-filled again with their own flour. The whole floor reads as pavement, though nothing cut it. It is simply what stone becomes when erosion has nowhere left to go but sideways, and has been going sideways since before the present climate arrived.

Cold sits in the dish the way water sits in a bowl. Breath shows. The stone takes heat from a hand and does not give it back. Yet the place is not a desert of cold. Moisture comes and goes on a schedule the rock seems to remember better than the sky does. A cloud snags on the low rim and drags itself apart; for an hour the air is full of a rain too fine to fall, beading on every roughness, darkening the pavement in irregular maps that fade when the cloud tears free. Between rains, water persists in the way damp persists in a cellar: in the grain, in the seams, in pockets no larger than a cupped palm, skimmed with ice at dawn and open again by the time the light is high. A few of the deeper pockets hold all day. Their edges are greener than they should be at this height.

The green is the first correction to the emptiness. It does not cover the dish. Most of the floor is bare, and the bare stretches are wide enough that a person walking them feels exposed, slightly foolish, as if crossing a room whose furniture has been removed but whose purpose has not. Where the green does take, it takes thickly. Cushion moss the color of wet wool. Liverwort in overlapping scales. A dwarf shrub with leaves like old coins, packed so tightly that a boot sinks and springs. In the lee of certain rises the growth is almost luxurious—knee-deep, cold to the wrist, smelling of mineral and pepper—and then it stops at a line no wider than a finger, and bare stone resumes. The lushness is local and stubborn. It follows water, and water follows the old grooves, and the grooves are not random.

That is the second correction, and it arrives slowly, because the structures are the same color as the floor and nearly as low. At first they register as hummocks, then as ruined curbs, then—once the eye accepts the repetition—as rooms. There are perhaps thirty of them, widely spaced, none taller than a person can reach, most lower. They are not built in any sense that implies a tool. Their walls are continuous with the pavement, grown rather than coursed: a chalky, layered stone, slightly warmer in tone than the country rock, with growth-seams instead of mortar and a surface that pits in tiny regular cells, like the inside of a shell held to the light. Some are single chambers with a mouth opening downwind. Some are nested, a smaller shell inside a larger, the inner one collapsed so that its curve has become a bench. A few are stacked two high where a later growth overtook an earlier one; the upper storey is always broken, its floor fallen through, its rim worn to the same final flatness as the mountain itself. Between them run the grooves, and the grooves connect. Not roads. Feeders. A plan laid down by whatever secreted these walls, still legible because nothing since has had the energy to erase it, only to sand it.

No metal anywhere. No cut mark, no socket, no hinge. If there was ever a maker with hands, the hands left no signature that weather could not have counterfeited. The organization is animal, or was. Colonial. A settlement-scale body that built by living in place, chamber by chamber, along a wetness it preferred, and then ended. What remains is infrastructure without occupants: channels, rooms, alignments, a kind of street plan you can walk and lose and find again by the darker damp in the grooves. Layered construction, yes, and dense in its logic. Empty in its volume. You spend most of your time in the dish inside nothing at all—open air, open stone—with a room somewhere off to the side, and another farther off, each one a hollow the size of a small chapel, full of ordinary shade.

Inside, the lushness concentrates. Ferns of a cold kind, more lace than leaf, crowd the mouths and thin out toward the back wall. Moss pads the floors thumb-thick. Condensation gathers on the cell-pitted ceilings and falls in slow, individual drops, each one loud because the chamber is a vessel. Some of the old feeding grooves still move water against the grade for a short distance—capillary stone, the cells aligned, a lift of a hand’s breadth before the film breaks and spills. It is the strangest ordinary fact in the place. You can watch a thread of melt climb a wall it should be descending, darken a patch of moss that has no business being wet, and fall. Nothing else disobeys. Light comes in the mouth and stops where light stops. Shadows are shadows.

The sound does not stop. Even on a still morning the dish is not quiet, and still mornings are uncommon. Wind at this height has no forest to argue with, so it argues with the mouths. Each chamber is tuned, crudely, by the width of its opening and the depth of its hollow, and when the wind crosses them they speak—not a tone you would call musical if you were listening for music, but a choir of hoots, flutes, and low bottles, some of them sub-audible as a pressure in the teeth. They do not quite agree. The dish is small enough that every voice reaches every other voice, and the slight differences in pitch beat against each other until the air itself seems grained. On a strong day you cannot talk across the center. You can shout and be answered only by the rooms. Rain adds its own layer: drops on the pavement, drops in the pockets, the heavier tick of drip from the ceilings, all of it amplified because there is so little soft mass to swallow it. Freeze and thaw keep their own clock. At dusk the skim-ice on the pools ticks as it knits. Near dawn, when the film on the climbing grooves stiffens, the stone gives off a fine continuous crackle, like a fire heard through a wall. The place is sparsely occupied and loudly employed. Its processes are few and all of them audible.

What lives here does not add much to the noise. Fauna is a rarity you notice by its exception. A beetle the color of the shell-stone, motionless on a warm patch of moss until your shadow passes, and then only a few steps. Once, a small bird at the rim, not entering, picking at nothing visible and leaving up the wind. No nests in the chambers. No droppings to speak of, no tracks but the ones weather makes and unmakes. Whatever chemistry the old colonies left in their walls—bitter, mineral, faint enough that you smell it only when you put your face to a damp seam—seems to be instruction enough. Animals cross the shoulder, look in, and do not stay. The ecology that remains is almost entirely botanical, and it is not still. The mosses swell by midday and tighten by evening. The dwarf shrubs lean, over years rather than hours, along the grooves, extending the old plan by a few finger-widths a season, as if the settlement had delegated its maintenance to plants that cannot know they are maintaining it. A brown patch appears where a cushion has died of a dry week; within a month the neighbors have edged inward. The pavement between stays pavement. Nothing colonizes the open stone for long. Wind and ice edit it nightly back toward the same flat sentence.

A day here is a sequence of states, not a scene. Before light, rime on every cell of every wall, the choir reduced to a single low note because the mouths are half glazed and the wind is figuring them as narrower instruments. First sun strikes the rim and does nothing for a while; the dish stays blue. Then a pocket opens, then another, and a thread of water begins its short impossible climb. By the time the light is full and ordinary—shadows short, colors honest, no veil except what a cloud briefly donates—the mosses have drunk and stood up, and the rooms smell green and chalky at once. Weather crosses in pieces. A squall that lasts less than the time it takes to walk from one chamber to the next. A clear interval in which the loudness drops just enough to hear your own pulse and mistake it for a new room. Afternoon brings the warmest cold of the day, which is still cold, and the only hour when the upper surfaces of the broken storeys hold a little heat. Insects, if they will appear at all, appear then, and not many. Toward evening the climbing water fails first, then the pools skin over, then the choir shifts register as ice changes the mouths, and the dish settles into the loud, hollow, finished quiet of a thing that has already reached the shape it means to keep.

The rules are simple and local. Water stays if the stone offers it a cell or a pocket; otherwise it leaves by evening. Anything that grows, grows on the plan the shells already drew, or it dies back to the groove. Nothing with a spine takes up residence; passage is tolerated, occupancy is not. Sound is produced by emptiness, not by inhabitants—the more vacant the room, the more it contributes. Erosion has largely stopped meaning change and started meaning maintenance: each freeze lifts a grain, each wind lays it somewhere lower and flatter, and the sum of this over ages is a surface that looks the same at the end of a century as at the start, only more completely itself. The settlement is not returning and not quite ruined. It has entered the condition the mountain entered before it—the oldest stable state, a town relaxed into geology, still urban in its spacing and its channels, already natural in every material that remains.

If you stay until the light fails you will see nothing uncanny. No glow, no movement in the rooms that is not wind or drip. The strangeness is all in the arrangement: a city without makers, loud without citizens, lush in a cold that should have kept it bare, wet in a height that should have drained it, and small enough to hold in one look while still containing streets. When you step back over the shoulder the choir drops away in a few paces, not with distance so much as with the loss of the dish’s focus, and the mountain around you is only mountain—older stone, thinner life, a wind with nothing to play. Behind you the low rim goes on resonating, faithfully, to an audience of moss.
