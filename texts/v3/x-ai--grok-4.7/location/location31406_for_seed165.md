The height does not announce itself as a summit. It announces itself as an absence of anything lower. From the first usable ground the country falls away on every side into dim tiers of plateau, and those tiers fall away again, until distance itself looks used up. The air is thin enough to make the chest work, cold enough that the cold has no drama left in it. It is simply the temperature the stone keeps.

Nothing here is young. The ridges have been ground down to their last honest shapes: long arêtes rounded at the crest, cirques whose back walls no longer shed, cols worn to the exact width the old ice needed and then left. Erosion finished its argument and stayed to inhabit the result. Scree lies where scree belongs, each stone settled against its neighbors, none of them mid-slide. A pebble placed on a flat will be there in the same attitude if you return, and you will return to it sooner than the distance suggests.

The ground is open in the way a parish of mountains is open. You can hold three valleys, a pass, a dry tarn-bed, and a line of dark nunataks in one look. Between those things there is almost nothing. Hours of pale gravel. A rib of bedrock. Then more gravel. The emptiness is not a mood. It is a measurement: very few objects per volume of air, and the air itself thin, so that even the cold feels unoccupied.

Water is present the way a held breath is present. Every surface carries the same film of damp, neither spreading nor drying. In the cracks a black ice sits at a constant thickness, not quite frozen forward, not quite letting go. The old river channels are dark with it. If you kneel and put a hand to the gravel in a channel, the wet comes off on the skin and is already back on the stone, the same beads, the same places. Condensation does not fall. It is a property of the rock, like color. Far down one valley a thread of true water shows, no wider than a wrist, and it does not move. It occupies its course. Light crosses it and returns unchanged.

Vegetation is a rumor with a few proofs. Pale lichens, hand-sized, clamped to north faces that are not reliably north. A cushion of something like moss in the lee of a boulder, dry at the heart, damp only on the windward crust, though there is no wind. No trees. No shrubs tall enough to throw a shadow separate from their own body. The green, where it occurs, is the green of things that have agreed to grow at the speed of stone, which here is no speed at all.

Light is poor and stable. A pale disc hangs over the eastern ridges and does not climb, does not warm, does not set. It illuminates less than it occupies. Most of what you see comes from the ground: a tired sheen in the mica, a gray that is not quite reflection. Shadows are short and point in more than one direction. Some of them point toward the disc. Some lie under surfaces that face the sky. In the cols the dimness pools until the far wall is only a darker sentence in the same gray.

The loudness is the first fact and the last. It does not rise. It does not thin with distance. It is the same pressure in the teeth on this ridge as on the ridge you can barely resolve. At first it seems to be wind, then a river, then rockfall, then all of those heard at once and none of them happening. There is no gust to correspond to it. The channels do not run. The scree does not travel. The sound is the record of every process that shaped this country, playing together, at full level, without sequence. It has no quieter part. Shouting changes nothing in it. After a time the roar stops seeming to come from outside the ears and becomes a condition of the place, like the damp, like the cold. You do not get used to it. You only learn that it will not develop.

That is the rule, if there is one: nothing develops.

A bird crosses the idea of the air and is not there when you look. Later, on a slope of fine gravel, there are tracks of something small and hoofed, crisp as if just made, and they end in the middle of an open patch with no turn, no scatter, no animal. The tracks are part of the equilibrium. They have been ending there for as long as the cirques have held their shape. Fauna, such as it is, does not inhabit the plateau so much as occur in it at very long intervals, and occurrence here does not imply motion. Once, between two nunataks, a dark body stands at the edge of vision, the size of a goat, facing a direction that is not toward you and not away. It does not shift its weight. If it is alive, life in this country means remaining exactly where the world has finished putting you.

The structures are fewer than the animals and harder to grant. On a shoulder above the tarn-bed the bedrock carries a rectilinear stain: four lines, a corner, the ghost of a sill. No blocks. No tool marks. No metal, no ash, no path leading to it. It is not a ruin. It is a memory the stone is having of a room, pressed into the grain from a direction that is not up. Elsewhere, a line of cairns too regular to be travelers’ work and too fused to the bedrock to have been stacked. They were not built. They eroded into the shape of having been built, and then erosion stopped, and the shape remained, meaningless and exact. In one col a doorway stands without a wall. You can pass through it. On the far side the view is the same view, delayed by a step, as if the air kept a spare copy of the valley and lent it to anyone who used the opening. There is no hinge, no timber, no device. The tech of the place is zero. Whatever intelligence is suggested by the marks is the intelligence of a mistake in the rock, repeated until it looked intentional.

The breakdown is not an event. It is the settled state.

Distances do not close properly. A nunatak that is an hour off by the look of the gravel is, after a short descent, beside you, and the ridge you left is both behind and included in the foreground, a slab of the same stone occupying two addresses without strain. The horizon sometimes sits closer than the ground underfoot, a clean edge of gray laid across the middle distance like a lid. Look down and you are still on the plateau. Look up and the underside of a cloud is visible inside the bedrock, its folds continuing the folds of the schist, dim on dim, not moving. The cloud is not weather. Weather would be a change.

Walk far enough and the country repeats a feature you have already passed, not approximately but with the same cracked boulder, the same four-line stain, the same motionless thread of water. It is not a loop you can map. The repetition does not care whether you turned around. The plateau is large enough to be a region and small enough, in its laws, to be a single held instant expanded until it has valleys in it.

Sound behaves worst, or most honestly. The roar is omnidirectional and sourceless, but sometimes a single strike separates from it: a stone falling, close, on your left. Nothing has fallen. The gravel at your left is the gravel that has always been there, each piece in its final place. The strike happens anyway, at the same pitch, and does not echo, because an echo would be a second thing and the country does not permit seconds. It only permits the continuation of what has already been completed.

At the lip of the highest cirque the damp thickens by a degree you can feel on the lips and nowhere else. The back wall rises, smooth, old, done. In its face, faintly, are the rectangles again, a row of them, as if a street had been carried up into the mountain and forgotten by everything except the grain. No doors open. No lamps. The dim disc sits on the rim and sheds its poor light equally on stone and on the shapes inside stone. The roar is full. It has been full. It will be full in the same degree, at the same pitch, for as long as this equilibrium is willing to be a place.

If you stay, you do not watch anything happen. You stand inside the finished hour of a high country that has mislaid the distinction between having happened and happening, and that keeps, in its cold, its damp, its scattered unbuilt marks, and its enormous empty intervals, the loud unchanged fact of itself.
