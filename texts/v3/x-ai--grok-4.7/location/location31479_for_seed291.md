The basin has no proper name on the maps that last more than a season, because the maps themselves are printed on sheets that the ground reclaims. From a rise that is only a rise by courtesy — a low ridge of fresh conglomerate, still wet enough to take a bootprint and forget it by afternoon — the place opens as a valley-floor the size of a modest city and its hinterland, all of it occupied, none of it finished.

Cold sits in it the way water sits in a sponge. Not a cutting cold, not wind. A temperature that has settled and stayed, a few degrees above freezing, so that breath shows and metal sweats and every horizontal surface carries a film. Rain comes as a decision the sky makes and unmakes: a quarter-hour of fine fall, then nothing, then a mist that condenses on cable and leaf until both drip in the same slow measure. There are no rivers worthy of the word. There are seams. Water collects in the joints between what was laid down yesterday and what is being laid down now — black threads of it along foundation courses, a pond the length of a loading bay that was not a pond at dawn, a glaze over the new stone so thin it reads as polish until a bird breaks it.

The stone is the first strange thing, and it never stops being strange. The basin is not eroding in the old sense. It is arriving. Geology here behaves like a trade. Beds of gray silt and pale gravel shoulder up through the streets on no schedule anyone posts, raw and unsorted, smelling of iron and crushed reed. A wall that was brick at morning may be half brick and half a fresh conglomerate by dusk, the pebbles still loose in their matrix, the mortar line continuing across both materials as if the masons had simply kept going and the earth had kept pace. Pavements swell. A tram cutting silts itself shut and is recut by crews who do not argue with the silt; they treat it as another delivery. Embryonic ridges nose through factory yards and are immediately fitted with rails, or with planters, or with both. Nothing has had time to weather. Every surface is new-cut or new-grown or new-poured, and already occupied.

The city is packed to the point of rudeness and still readable as a region. From the ridge you can see across it: stacks, sheds, the long roofs of spinning-halls, a grid of streets that refuses to stay rectangular because the ground will not hold a rectangle. Density is not vertical so much as total. There is no margin. Where a street ends, a yard begins; where a yard ends, a thicket; where the thicket thins, it thins into pipework and then thickens again into leaves. Lushness here is cold lushness — broad soft leaves the color of bottle glass, ferns that uncurl against boiler housings, mats of moss so deep on north walls that the brick pattern is a rumor. The plants drink the damp and ignore the temperature. They grow through grating as if grating were soil. Vines have learned the rhythm of the overhead lines and follow them, not climbing so much as accompanying, and the lines carry current anyway. Nobody cuts them back entirely. A cleared span fills by the next shift.

Industry runs without raising its voice. That is the second strange thing, and it takes longer to believe. The sheds are full of engines. You can see the belts moving, the presses descending, the little industrial locomotives nosing along sidings with wagons of culm and coil and cut reed. Steam lifts from vents and lies down again, unwilling to rise in this air. What you do not get is the roar that such a district owes you. Sound here is porous. The moss, the wet leaves, the new silt, the hanging damp — they take noise and keep it. A press that should ring across the valley arrives as a soft concussion in the chest, like a door closed in another room. Voices carry a few paces and then join the general absorbent quiet. The dominant sound, if you stand still long enough to name it, is drip, and under that a low circulatory hum, more felt than heard, as if the basin had a pulse and the factories had agreed to match it.

The turbulence is not weather in the ordinary sense, though squalls do cross, brief and oddly local, soaking one ward while the next remains in ordinary daylight. The turbulence is rearrangement. The place does not hold still at the scale of hours. A lane that was clear at noon is a nursery of pale shoots and ankle-deep gravel by the time the shift changes; the shoots are already being trained onto trellises that were not there either, iron frames bolted into stone that is still warm from its own making. Sheds are extended overnight in prefabricated bays, and by the following afternoon a bay may have been swallowed from below by a rising bed of sand and repurposed as a cistern, the machinery lifted clear on screw-jacks and walked, slowly, creaking, a hundred meters north to a footing that did not exist yesterday. Cranes swing. Crews move in tight groups, not shouting. Everyone seems to know that the ground is a colleague with bad timing and no malice. Destruction and construction are the same gesture seen from different ends of a street. A wall comes down as silt; a wall comes up as brick; both happen while you watch, and neither makes much sound.

Light is plain daylight, gray-gold when the mist thins, honest enough to work by, never theatrical. It shows you the seams. It shows you how much life is in the seams.

Fauna does not visit. It resides, at a density that would be alarming if the animals behaved like refugees. They behave like staff. Flocks of small dark birds work the conveyor mouths, taking the insects that breed in the warm wet gaps under the belts. Larger birds — heron-shaped, but heavier, with a gray bloom on the feathers like the bloom on new slate — stand in the temporary ponds and strike at things that move under the glaze. Between the pipe runs, colonies of short-furred mammals the size of cats maintain runs that the night shift has learned not to block; block one and they open another through a crate wall before morning, and the crate wall is already damp enough to permit it. Insects are a weather of their own: clouds of them lift off the reed-mats when a roller starts, then settle on the nearest warm housing. In the newest cuttings, where the stone is still undifferentiated and the water stands in footprints, pale many-legged things turn over the silt. They look unfinished, as the geology looks unfinished. Some of them may be. A child on a parapet — there are children; the wards house the crews — pointed once at a creature half in and half out of a fresh gravel bank, still soft along the back, and an older worker said, without surprise, that those ones firm up by evening if the cold holds, and if it does not they go back into the bank. No one wrote that down. It is the sort of fact that would embarrass a map.

Rules, such as they are, are practical and slightly sideways.

Water is not owned. A pond that forms in your yard is your pond until the ground takes it back, and you are expected to use it or drain it into the nearest seam before it lifts your footings. Leaving standing water against a new wall is a civic offense, not because of bylaws posted anywhere visible, but because the wall may decide to include the water and become something that is no longer a wall.

Nothing structural is guaranteed past a week. Deeds describe uses, not plots. You hold the right to operate a shed of a certain volume in a certain ward; the ward will move under you, and the company that owns the engines will pay the jacksmen to walk the shed. People speak of addresses the way sailors speak of weather: useful, provisional.

Cutting living wood without replacing the run is frowned on harder than theft. The plants are doing load-bearing work — binding silt, eating damp, eating sound — and a street that loses its vines gets loud, and loud is treated as a failure of maintenance. The quiet is infrastructure.

You do not dig speculative holes. The basin is doing the digging and the filling on a schedule it does not share. A pit opened for curiosity has been known to answer with a bed of gravel to the height of a person before the curious person has climbed out. Rescue is efficient and unsentimental. Afterward the pit is a foundation, and someone is already bolting a plate to it.

Animals are not fed and not cleared. They are routed around, the way you route around a pipe that carries pressure. Killing one is not taboo so much as stupid: the gap fills with three, and the three are less careful of belts.

At the western edge, where the basin's low rim should be, the city does not stop so much as loosen into a lace of causeways over ground that is still deciding whether to be land. Out there the light is the same ordinary light, the vegetation lusher because no one has trained it, the engines fewer — pump houses, mostly, and a long low mill that walks itself east a few meters a month, following a seam of water that will not stay. You can stand on the outermost causeway and hear, very nearly, nothing: drip, the soft hydraulic sigh of a ram, a bird striking. Behind you the wards seethe without noise, cranes and vines and new stone and the small bright movements of a population that has accepted motion as the condition of having a floor.

If you stay until the light goes ordinary-dark, you will see the basin's real pace. Lamps come on in the sheds, ordinary industrial lamps, yellow and sufficient. Between them, on the rising beds, a fainter luminosity — not magical in any way that announces itself, only the wet new mineral giving back a little of the day, the way some clays do. Crews keep working. A wall lengthens. A pond closes. Somewhere a press comes down, and you feel it in your sternum, and the leaves take the rest. By morning the view from the ridge will be the same city and not the same city, packed, open, cold, damp, unfinished, and already full.
