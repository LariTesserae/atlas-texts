The cold arrives first as a pressure against the teeth, then as a film on the tongue. There is no sky to bring it. The roof is only a few body-lengths up, a skin of stone still sweating itself into being, and every drop that leaves it is already half ice by the time it finds something to land on.

What it lands on is plant.

Not a stand of plants, not a carpet. The chamber is a volume of living mass, packed until the idea of ground loses its edge. Roots braid with stems that have never seen a sun, and those stems thicken into ribs, and the ribs fork into plates the color of old wax and pale copper, and between the plates the air is not empty. Filaments cross it, beaded with condensate, each bead a lens the size of a seed. Walk and you do not step; you part. The place parts back, slowly, with a sound like wet rope being drawn through a ring.

It is loud, and the loudness is not wind. There is no wind worth the name. The sound is the work. Water finds channels that did not exist an hour ago and will not exist by evening, ticking, hissing, sometimes striking a hollow in a stem so that a single note holds and decays for longer than a breath should. Farther in, where the ribs close to a passage no wider than a shoulder, something larger keeps time: a pulse in the wood, or in whatever the wood has become, low enough to feel in the knees. It does not hurry. Nothing here hurries. The stone overhead is still deciding whether to be a ceiling or a wound. Fresh faces of mineral show where a flake has only just let go, sharp, unsalted by age, gleaming where the drip has not yet dulled them. The chamber is embryonic. Its walls have the look of a thing mid-sentence.

Light is whatever the mass consents to. A dim green-gold seeps from within certain plates, not a glow you could read by, more the memory of one. It pools in the water that stands in every low fold. That water is never still for long and never gone. It beads on every surface, skins the air, rises in a cold mist that does not drift so much as thicken and thin on the same slow cycle as the pulse. Taste it and it is mineral and green, faintly sweet, cold enough to ache. The marsh-edge feeling is everywhere and nowhere: no open fen, no horizon of reeds, only the continuous fact of water at the skin of things, water inside the skin of things, water being made into structure and unmade again.

The fauna do not perch on this. They are of it. In the near-dark the eye learns them by interruption. A shoulder of the mass shifts, and the shift has legs, many, jointed wrong, sheathed in the same wax-copper as the plates, so that what looked like architecture was a back. They move when the pulse moves, a fraction after, as if listening. Smaller things travel the filaments the way messages travel a wire: bright specks, not light exactly, condensation organizing itself into a body for a moment and then forgetting. They tick against the stems. They are part of the loudness. Somewhere a clutch of them strikes the same hollow note in succession, a scale with no name, and the larger bodies answer by tightening, the passage narrowing by a hand’s width, then relaxing. No panic in it. Habit. The room breathing its tenants.

There is no made thing. No cut, no joint, no tool-mark, no wall raised by a hand. And yet the place is engineered past the point where the word plant still explains it. The filaments do not merely conduct water. Follow one with a careful finger — it will not break; it will lean into the touch, cold, faintly electric — and the bead at its end holds an image that is not a reflection. A second chamber, or this one a little later, stone further closed, a new rib risen where your shoulder is now. The image stutters when the pulse comes. It is not stored the way a picture is stored. It is computed in the tension of the living mass, in alignments of cells too small to see and too orderly to be accident, a lattice doing work that ordinary growth does not do. The sweet taste in the water is a byproduct of that work. So is the light. So, perhaps, is the pulse: not a heart, a clock the system keeps because some process inside it requires the whole volume to agree, once in a long while, on when to be denser and when to let a passage open.

Scale keeps the strangeness from becoming weather. This is one room. Cathedral-small, grove-small, a single feature you could walk the edge of in a short time if the edge would hold still. It will not. The boundary is a thickening, not a line: ribs packing until even the filaments have no gap, the sound muffling, the cold deepening as the mass excludes you. Beyond that compaction there may be more chambers, or only stone still dreaming of chambers. From inside, you cannot tell. The system does not seem interested in outside. It spends itself on becoming more precisely what it already is.

Stand long enough and the slow processes show their hands. A plate that was pale copper at the shoulder warms, over the course of an hour, toward a dull rose, and the light in the standing water shifts with it. A new filament stitches itself across a gap you had been using as a sightline; by the time you notice, three of the bright specks are already running it, and the hollow note nearby has changed pitch by a small, irritable interval. The fresh stone in the ceiling grows a skin of root-hairs, silver, drinking the weep before the drops can fall. Erosion here runs backward, or sideways: the geology is not wearing down, it is being recruited. Mineral faces are claimed, sheathed, made into substrate before weather, if weather could reach this far under, ever gets a vote.

The abundant life does not flee you and does not attend you. A many-legged back may remain a wall for as long as you need a wall. If you lean, it leans away at the speed of cooling metal, courteous, unbothered, already thinking about the next tightening of the passage. The specks will cross your sleeve if your sleeve is wet enough to count as filament. They leave no track except a brief colder patch and a faint sweet smell. Under them, if you look at the weave of a plate in the dim, the cells are visible as a grain, and the grain is not random. Rows. Addresses. A pattern that repeats at a scale smaller than the eye trusts, then repeats again inside that, as if the plant had learned to write in a hand that does not smudge when it grows.

Sound never drops out. Even in the pauses between drips there is a hiss, the condensate taking and leaving the air, and under that the pulse, and under that a soft continuous creak as ribs adjust their load. Loud, close, intimate. The kind of loud that would exhaust a forest and here is simply the cost of so much life in so little volume, all of it working, none of it finished.

When you leave — if leaving is the right word for backing out through a compaction that seals finger-width by finger-width behind your heels — the last thing to go is the cold on the teeth, and the last image is not the rose plate or the imaged second chamber in the bead. It is a flake of new stone, no larger than a palm, still sharp, still deciding, already wearing its first silver hairs. The room will not be the same room when the hairs drink their fill. It was not the same room when you entered. It is only, patiently, more itself.
