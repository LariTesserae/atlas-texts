The heat arrives before the land does. It is not a flare and not a wind; it is the temperature the air has settled at and will not leave, a heavy warmth held against the skin by wetness, so that breathing and standing are nearly the same work. Under it the light never improves. There is no disk, no hour when the vapor thins toward blue. The sky is a low lid of condensate, gray-green, close enough that the basin feels roofed, and what comes through is a steady dusk with no source you can point to and no edge that brightens. Shadows, where the leaves make them, are soft and do not travel.

The ground is low and unfinished. Across a valley’s width — far enough that the far rim is only a darker band in the vapor, near enough that one day’s slow walking would cross the main features — the stone has the look of something interrupted while it was still becoming stone. Surfaces are rind and curd and nodule, pale where mineral has set, rust-stained where iron has come out of the water, jointed in clean breaks that have never been rounded. Nothing here has been planed by weather. There are no old channels, no gravel trains, no high lines on the rock where water once stood and withdrew. The unfinishedness is not a phase. It is the shape the place keeps.

Water is everywhere and does not go anywhere. It films the new stone, beads along the open joints, stands in every shallow the crust has not yet closed, and weeps from the low bluffs in threads that strike the same leaves and the same warm lips of rock and do not cut them. A sheet occupies the middle of the basin, knee-deep and less, tea-colored, its surface barely moving except where bodies disturb it. The air is saturated past the point of rain. Condensation gathers on every face, runs, and is replaced at the same rate, so that the weeping has no aftermath — no darker stain spreading, no drying crust, no hour when the rocks are merely damp. The water is warm. It matches the air closely enough that a hand passed from one to the other feels a change of texture more than of heat.

Life takes the raw mineral directly. There is no deep soil, no buried generations, no mattress of rot under the green; roots clasp the curd and the rind, and the black that gathers in crevices is a skin, not a history. Even so the growth is thick. Broad thin leaves stack in layers from the waterline to a canopy that never quite opens, each leaf large for the dimness, each turned to take what little falls. Reeds and blade-plants pack the margins of the sheet until the water between them is a network of slots. Climbers lace the hummocks. Mats ride the warm film and meet the standing stems, so that in places there is no clear step from liquid to leaf to air. The green is not one green. It runs from a yellowed, heat-glazed pallor on the upper surfaces to a blue-black in the wet understory, and all of it is crowded, stem against stem, near enough to touch across.

What overwhelms is not the plants. The basin is occupied to the point of contact. On every rind, in every slot of water, under every leaf that can hold a body, something is living, and the living do not thin out as you go. Amphibians paved along the weeping lips, so close their flanks touch, their throats working in a chorus that does not take turns. Insects in a continuous fret across the mats and the reed, a glittering that is more motion than individuals. Birds in the mid-layer, packed on the forks, launching and returning on paths so short they barely leave the plant they left. In the sheet, backs and eyes and the slow push of bodies under the tea-colored skin, layered down through water that is never deep enough to be empty. Nothing flees in a wave. Density here is not a startle; it is the resting state. You part it, and it is already closed behind you at the same saturation.

The sound is that density made audible, and it has no rise. There is no dawn chorus because there is no dawn, no evening let-down, no weather-front that swells the calls and then passes. The bellows, the mill of wings and legs, the clicks, the thin high strands of smaller throats, the tick of condensate leaving leaves for warm stone — these sit at one loudness from the near rim to the sheet and, as far as the vapor lets you hear, beyond it. Walking in does not bring you to the noise. You were in it when the heat reached you. Individual voices can be separated if you stop and mean to: a low pulse from the bluff, a metallic stutter in the reed, a single bird repeating a phrase with the patience of something that has never needed to finish. Together they are a wall. Speech close to the ear survives. Anything farther is taken.

Across the open of the basin the features repeat with variation rather than progress. Hummocks of unset stone, some bare enough to show the mineral curd, most skinned in climbers. Belts of reed that widen and pinch. Pools off the main sheet, round as the joints that hold them, each with its own paved rim of bodies. A weeping face along one side, not a cliff, a low scarp still sharp, water leaving it in the same threads onto the same stones. Farther out, the vegetation climbs a gentle grade toward the rim and does not change its rules: still lush, still rooted in raw crust, still dimmed by the lid. Nothing marks a season on that grade. No band of spent growth, no color that belongs to a month. The leaves that are falling are matched by leaves that are not, and the fall does not accumulate; what drops is taken up into the living layer at the rate it arrives, so the floor stays a floor of stone and film and grip-roots, never a record.

The animals keep the same bargain. Young are present among the adults, and the proportions do not shift while you watch, or in any longer staying the place seems willing to allow. Feeding is continuous and local. A throat takes an insect; the fret does not diminish. A back breaks the sheet and takes something under it; the layer below restores its numbers without a visible gap. There are no trails worn into the margins, no browned roosts, no mounds of shell or bone that would mean a long attendance. Attention is constant and leaves no mark. Whatever hunts does it at the rate the hunted replace themselves, and the loudness, which might elsewhere have been alarm, is only the sound of that balance running.

The unusual thing is how completely the balance refuses time. Stand at the scarp and the threads of water occupy the same leaves they occupied when you looked away. The brighter smear in the vapor — the only hint of a light beyond the lid — does not cross. Your shadow, soft as it is, lies where you put it and waits. Heat does not peak. The sheet does not breathe in and out with any tide you can detect. Even the overwhelming life, which in any ordinary marsh would be a calendar of surges, holds one pressure: many bodies, near-touching, loud, and the same many. The land looks embryonic because nothing has ever sanded it, and it looks ancient because the embryo has not advanced. Both impressions are true, and neither one is a moment in a sequence.

Close to the body the rules are simple and tiring. Sweat does not lift off the skin; the air is already full. Vision is short, and distances in the vapor are agreements rather than measurements — the next hummock seems near until the reed takes twenty slow minutes, the rim seems reachable until the light stays dim and the grade stays low and the same. The stone underfoot is warm and slick, newly set, willing to take a print and then, where water films it, willing to lose the print without having been eroded into anything else. Leaves slap and shed on you and are as wet after as before. The chorus presses at the ears without climax. If you stop, the place does not arrive at you and does not recede. It was this packed, this hot, this unfinished, this loud, on every side of your stopping, and it continues, identically, into the vapor where the far band of the rim dissolves.

There is no path, because nothing has been made, and no artifact to tell you the basin has ever been left and returned to. The only constructions are the ones the mineral made while becoming itself, and it is done becoming only in the sense that it has stopped. What lives here lives on that pause: lush against raw stone, overwhelming in number, steady in its noise, under a dim lid, in water that wets the world and declines to use it.
