It does not arrive. That is the first wrongness, and it takes a long time to notice, because the basin is already full of things that do not arrive: rain that has been falling since before the steps were cut, ice that calves on a schedule no one keeps, a gate that thuds whether or not a listener has walked the glen. Against that, a life can hide by simply declining to begin.

What gives it away is a disagreement in the sound.

You can sit the high path through a whole iron day and hear the basin agree with itself. Rain, slap, knock, whistle, boom, thud. Each voice in its register, each interval its own. Then, without a new wind and without a change in the seiche, one stand stops knocking. Not the way a current lets up, which is a leaning, a softening, blades finding a temporary truce. This is a subtraction. The blades are still there. You can hear the rain on the water around them, the neighboring stand still arguing with itself a furlong off. But in that one city of ribbons the knocking has been lifted out, as if a hand had gone in among the crowd in the wooden hall and asked them, without words, to hold still. They hold. The stillness has edges. It is the size of the stand and no larger. It lasts as long as a slow breath held past comfort, and then the knocking resumes a half-beat late, ashamed of itself, and the map of the ear is whole again.

The first time, you think you misheard. The rain is a roof and the roof lies. The second time it happens in a different stand, farther down, and the silence is the same shape: not empty, occupied. The third time you are in the water, waist-deep on a drowned shoulder, one hand on the sealed cistern for the cold's sake of having a fact to hold, and the silence happens in the stand just outside the ring of stones. Close enough that you should see what causes it. You see the ribbons go quiet. You see them cease their argument and hang, tea-colored, bronze, the tips a little raised. You do not see a body between them. What you see, if the rain thins for the length of three drops, is that the water inside the stand is a finger's-width clearer than the water outside it, and that this clearer water is not drifting with the current. It is staying. The current combs around it the way current combs around a piling, and the piling is not there.

Call it a room, because the columns of lucent water are rooms, and this is their kin, except that the columns drift and this does not. Call it a held breath. Call it, after the fourth and the fifth time, by the only name that survives contact: a listener. It is listening to the stand. When it has heard enough, it lets the blades go, and the blades, which have no opinion about anything else, have an opinion about this. They resume. They do not resume the same argument. Something has been edited. A ribbon that was about to finish, to silver and come away, does not. Another, younger, frays along an edge it had been keeping whole. The stand is not damaged. It is answered.

It has been answering them for longer than the notches.

You learn its size the way you learn everything here, by subtraction and by cold. It is not large. A person could walk around the place where it is, if a person could see the place, and the circuit would be a dozen paces, no more — except that the circuit would not close, because it does not keep to a footprint. It has a vertical habit. It occupies the water from a fathom above the silt to just shy of the surface, and it will not break the surface. The rain hammers the skin of the sea and the skin answers, and under that skin, in the last hand's-breadth of water, there is a reluctance that is not temperature. The threads that rise in the lee of the fluted ridge rise there and not where it is. It does not give water back. It is trying, with a patience that has outlasted the metal in the mooring pin, not to be weather.

What it is made of is the harder question, and the basin does not help, because the basin is mostly absence and this is a denser absence. Kneel in the mat where it has been — it will have gone; it goes as soon as a warm body breaks the margin of its hearing, not in fear, in courtesy, the way a conversation stops when a door opens — and the silt under your fingers is the same silt. No tracks. The place was honest about tracks. What is different is the temperature of a layer no thicker than a leaf, a cold inside the cold, as if the water there had been asked to remember a winter it did not have and had obliged. The cold fades in minutes. By the time your hand hurts enough to pull it out, the memory is already the ordinary cold, and you cannot be sure you felt the other. It likes that. Certainty is a surface habit. It lives below the depth where certainty works.

The plants are how it spends itself.

This is not grazing, and the absence of animals remains true in the way the basin meant it. Nothing bites the blades. Nothing hides in the mats. But the stands are not unattended, and the unbrowsed look of them is a carefulness, not a loneliness. It moves among them on a round that takes longer than a day and is not a day, because it does not use the light. You can map the round if you are willing to be wrong for a week. The tall stand in the western narrows, the one whose crown leans against three currents at once: that one it visits when the ice on the emergent shelves is about to calve, and it holds the blades through the boom so they do not shock themselves into shedding. The small stands on the silt bars in the whistling throat: those it thins. A ribbon here, a ribbon there, encouraged to finish, so the bars do not grow themselves shut and stop the note. It cares about the note. The note is one of the few things in the basin that was not always here, and it has decided the note should continue, which is as close as it comes to a preference you could accuse of being a want.

The lucent columns are not it, but they are its near relatives, or its tools, or its exuviae — the word will not settle, and should not. When a column drifts through a stand and the tips burn that bruised, healing color, it is sometimes only the column, a room wandering, the blades spending a light they have been keeping. And sometimes the column slows, and the slowing is the listener arriving inside it, wearing the lucency because lucency is a way of being in the water without displacing it. Then the burning lasts longer. Then a single blade will turn, not with the current, against it, until its tip points at a thing: a knot rolling along the bottom, a mat about to peel, the bent pin at the ring. It is showing itself something. Or showing the stand. The distinction is not one it keeps.

The knots are the nearest it has to offspring, and they are not offspring. The basin was right that nothing hatches. What the knots are is memory set down in a shape that can roll. Where a stand has done a thing worth keeping — survived a boom, fed a note, grown a tip that knew the lucent color before the column came — it coaxes a swelling at the root-end of a ribbon, a fist of stored cold and stored pattern, and it worries the swelling loose with a pressure too fine to be a current. The knot rolls. It lodges where the next century of that stand will need the pattern: against a silt bar that is thinking of becoming a stand, in the lee of a stone the seiche is slowly teaching to whistle a second note. Most knots do nothing. They sit, pale, motionless, and the dark accepts them, and this is not failure. It can afford a great deal of nothing. It has had the time.

How long, you can ask the notches.

They were not all cut by the same hands. That is the revision the basin's own account requires, once you have stood in the throat with your palm going numb and your ear full of the single rising note. The higher notches are tool-marks, patient, evenly spaced, the work of someone who came back. Below a certain line the spacing holds but the hand changes. The cuts are still regular, still a record of level, but they are not pecked. They are pressed. The stone has been asked to remember a line, and the stone, which in this place agrees to most things if they are repeated long enough, has agreed. The pressed notches continue down into the silt, farther than any wader will reach, and they do not stop being regular. It has been keeping the gauge since the people who cut the upper ones stopped coming back. It did not take over their work. It did not know it was theirs. It understood, the way it understands the thud of the gate, that a repeated mark is a kind of voice, and that voices in this basin are how the basin thinks, and that if a voice stops, something has to decide whether the thought was finished.

It decided the thought was not finished.

It does not mourn them. There is no evidence it knows what a person is, even now, even with you in the water breaking its margins and receiving, each time, the courtesy of its going. The stairs are a texture it accounts for, as it accounts for the fluting on the ridge where the threads rise. The cistern sealed by the mat is a place it does not enter; the water under the lid is the same water, but the lid is a decision the plants made, and it does not overrule the plants in their own small closures. The ring of stones it keeps clear, and this is the one labor that looks like memory of a use. The inward seiche wants to silt the ring. It wants this at every return. And at every return, something — a pressure, a brief clarifying, the listener wearing a thinness — lifts the newest silt and walks it, grain by grain, back over the fitted stones to the outside, where the stand can broom it. Not clean. Cleaner. The habit of sweeping that left a shape the water still respects is its habit now, inherited the way the gate's thud was inherited, by being the thing that continued. The mooring pin it does not straighten. The bend is information. Someone pulled. The pull ended. The pin holds the ending. It is careful with endings. It only refuses the ones that were not done.

You will want it to be lonely. The basin invites that, being so empty of anything with a face. Resist it. Loneliness is a story about missing a likeness, and it has no likeness and does not miss one. What it has instead is a conversation with a country-sized body of water, and the conversation is not one-sided, which is the second wrongness, slower to accept than the first.

The sea answers it.

Not in words, and not in the voices you already know how to separate. In adjustments. When it holds a stand through a boom, the next seiche comes in a little softer at that shore, as if the basin had noticed the courtesy and spent a fraction of its push elsewhere. When it thins the bars in the throat, the whistle drops a hair, then finds itself again at a purer pitch, and the purer pitch holds for days, a thanks so structural you cannot quote it. When it walks with a lucent column and the tips burn, the column, afterward, drifts more slowly, and sheds, in its wake, a faint grit of the cold-inside-cold, which the mats on the nearest slope incorporate, and those mats grow thicker that season, plusher, dripping longer sheets when the seiche lifts. The basin is not a mind. It does not need to be. It is a set of agreements old enough to have preferences, and the listener is the one preference that moves.

The upward threads know it. They do not rise where it is, but when it travels the long empty reach between the western stands and the gate — and it does travel the empty reaches, slowly, a clearer room sliding through dark water, no blades to announce it, only a stutter you would never hear under the rain — the threads in the lee of the fluted ridge multiply. For an hour, two. As if the sea, in its absence from that route, had more giving-back than it knew what to do with, and spent it where spending was already the custom. Then it returns toward the ridge, not hurrying, and the threads settle to their ordinary number, and the rain's stutter goes back to being a small hole in the hammer. You can time its crossings by the threads if you have the patience, and the patience is the admission price. Nothing here is visible on the first day. Very little is visible on the twentieth. What is available is the gradual conviction that the emptiness is staffed.

At the gate it does something plainer, and plainer is almost a relief.

The pilings are tilted. The slab is seated. The thud is forever, at this height, in this cold. It cannot adjust the interval and does not try; the clock is the basin's, and it does not wind clocks it did not make. What it does is stand — not the word, but let it pass — in the drowned glen upstream of the pilings, in the faster water, and listen to the thud the way it listens to a stand. Counting. It counts a long time. Hundreds, then thousands, the number running out past any use a person would have for it. And every so often, at a thud that is fractionally duller, a pulse that did not quite overcome the angle cleanly, it goes down to the slab. You cannot follow. The dark there is the shut eye, and the current between the walls is not a place for a body with blood in it. But afterward the next thud is clean again, and if you pole a ribbon-raft into the glen on a brave day and put your hand, briefly, intolerably, on the slab, you will feel that the seat of it has been turned by a degree. A degree, over and over, across a time that makes the notches look young. It is keeping the clock honest. Not for anyone. The basin said that, and it was right. The honesty is for the interval itself, because an interval that drifts becomes weather, and weather is what it has spent its whole long life refusing to be.

Does it know you are here.

This is the question the cold asks, around the time your idea of a visit turns into an idea of staying, and it deserves a true answer rather than a comfort. It knows the water knows you. A warm body is a loud fact in a basin that measures itself by cold: a plume of slightly less-cold water shedding off you whenever you wade, a disturbance in the mat that takes a day to re-plaster, a new silence in the rain where your shoulders break the surface. It registers these the way it registers a ribbon about to finish. You are an event in its round. When you stand still long enough, in the same place, thigh-deep, letting the seiche move you instead of moving yourself, it will come to the edge of its courtesy and stop there. Not visible. A clearer room in the dark, a fathom off, the size of a dozen paces that do not close, the blades between you and it held quiet not by command but by the same attention it gives a stand. It listens to you.

What it hears is not your voice, if you are foolish enough to use one. Voices die under the rain at a few paces. What it hears is the way your pulse perturbs the water at your skin, a small knock, much faster than any knocking it knows, a rhythm with no interval it can reconcile to the gate or the whistle or the boom. The first time, the listening lasts as long as a held breath and no longer, and then it goes, and the blades resume, edited, and you will find later that a knot has lodged against your boot — pale, fist-sized, harmless, already forgetting whatever pattern it carried, because you are not a place a pattern can be planted. It tried. The trying is the most animal thing it does, and it is not very animal. It mistook your pulse for a new note and attempted to set down a memory of it where you stood, and the memory failed, and it has not, on any later approach, tried again. It learns. The learning is slow enough to miss and total when it arrives.

If you stay through a night, it will include you in the round once, and only once, at the hour when the iron sky is black and the blade-tips are the only lamps. You will not see it cross the empty water. You will feel the cold-inside-the-cold arrive at your back, not touching, a hand's-breadth off, and the rain on your shoulders will stutter — not the upward threads, something humbler, a brief uncertainty in the drops, as if the basin had to think about whether you were surface or inhabitant and could not, for that moment, decide. Then the cold recedes. The round goes on. The gate thuds, clean. The whistle rises and falls. Somewhere a stand is being held through a boom you will hear in your chest before your ears agree. You have been counted. You will not be counted again. It does not keep gauges on things that leave.

In the morning the ring is cleaner than you left it, including the place where you knelt. Your prints in the silt have been walked, grain by grain, over the stones. This is not hospitality and not erasure. It is the same work it does after the seiche. You tracked water into a shape it maintains. It has restored the shape. If you are the sort of visitor who needs a message, that is the message, and it is not unkind: the basin has a housekeeping, and the housekeeping is alive, and you are weather it has already finished accounting for.

What remains, after you have climbed the dished steps and found the high path with your hands, is the revision you will not be able to make anyone believe, and should not try to sharpen into a better story than it is. The place is as empty as it told you. No animals, no tracks, no shells, no one waiting, no plan the sea is willing to learn. All of that is true. What lives there is not an exception to the emptiness. It is the emptiness's own attention, grown so continuous that it has edges, a round, a memory that rolls, a courtesy, a refusal to become weather. It was not born and it will not die in any way the notches can record. If the siphons ever win and the basin empties, it will empty with it, a listener with nothing left to hold still, and the last thing to go will be the whistle, which it has been thinning toward for centuries, and which it will hear, all the way down, because hearing is what it is for.

You can look back once, from the last ridge, if the rain allows the far rim. You will not see it. You may see, in one stand, the tips burn and not go out on the schedule of a drifting column. You may hear, under the hammer, a single city of blades holding quiet for the length of a breath, and then resuming, half a beat late, already changed, already continuing. That is enough. It does not perform. It has the sea, and the sea answers, and the answer has been sufficient for longer than the stone remembers being dry.
