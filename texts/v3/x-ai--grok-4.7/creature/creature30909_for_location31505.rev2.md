What lives there does not announce itself by occupying the center. The circle under the tree is empty more often than not, and emptiness is one of the ways it keeps its size. You notice it first as a discrepancy in the human habit of the lanes: a coat hung where no one entered, a kettle taken off the heat a moment before it would have sung, a ledger left open to a page whose numbers have been copied in a hand slightly steadier than the hand that keeps the book. The residents do not speak of a landlord. They speak, when they must, of the agreement. They say the agreement checked the stair. They say the agreement has not finished the east wall. They do not point.

If you stay long enough to learn which rooms lengthen at night, you begin to meet it as a delay rather than a body. A person turns a corner and the sound of the turn arrives after the person has already nodded to you, as if something walking just behind the gesture had to be let through a narrower door. In the water that stands without flowing, a reflection sometimes remains when the lane is clear: not a face at first, only the suggestion of a shoulder held at the angle bodies use when they are listening to a wall. The reflection does not match anyone who lives in the adjacent houses. It is a little too tall for the ceilings, and then, if you keep looking, it is not tall at all. It revises itself the way the lintels do, by small corrections, until what stands in the sheet of water is approximately a person and approximately the width of the lane, and neither measurement holds if you try to pace it off.

It has used bodies. That becomes clear in the coats. Some of the high collars are worn through at the inside of the neck in a pattern that is not the pattern of one throat. The same wear appears, identical, on garments in houses that do not share a stair. The fabric is not remarkable. Wool, mostly, and a cheaper cloth that pills at the cuffs. What is remarkable is the temperature left in them. Set a hand inside a sleeve that has been hanging since morning and the cloth is cold in the ordinary way of the city, except for a narrow warmth, no wider than two fingers, running from wrist to elbow as if an arm had only just been withdrawn and had never been thick enough to fill the sleeve. The residents rotate these coats without discussion. When one has been worn by the agreement, they say, it should be walked in by someone else before evening, or the seam at the shoulder begins to remember a joint that none of them have.

It is not kind and it is not cruel. Those words require a pace it does not keep. It attends. Attendance is its metabolism. It moves through the bowl the way the tick moves through the walls, checking that a volume still consents to be a room. Where consent fails, it does not punish. It stays. A chamber that has begun to disagree with its own window will be found, for days, slightly more occupied than its furniture accounts for: a depression in a chair that no sitter claims, a breath-fog on the inside of a glass that faces another glass a hand’s breadth away, the fog shaped, almost, like the inside of a mouth that has not decided whether to speak. The people who sleep in that chamber dream of counting steps and losing the count at the same number, night after night. They wake unharmed, only tired in the specific way of having been used as a measuring rod. They boil water. They do not complain to one another, because complaint would be a vibration, and the city drinks those.

Sometimes it is nearly visible in the ordinary light, which is the worst of its manners, because the light does not dramatize it. You might see, at the end of a lane, a figure in a dark coat turning as if to enter a door, and the door is there, and the figure completes the turn, and what enters is not a person but the decision to enter, after which the lane is empty and the door has no handle on this side. You might see the moths. They circle lamps that are not lit, and when the agreement is near they circle a volume of air the height of a chest, a small stable dark that is not shadow, because nothing is casting it. The wings sound like paper. The dark does not move to avoid you. You walk through it and for a step your ribs feel counted, the way a clerk counts coins, without interest, without haste, making sure the number is the number that was written down before you were born.

It has no face it insists on. Children, of whom there are few, report a person with too many sleeves, or a person seen only from the side whose profile continues around the head and becomes another side, equally partial. Adults report nothing so pictorial. They report the feeling of a palm held just off the sternum, not touching, taking the temperature of the breath, and then the feeling’s absence, and then the knowledge that a room behind them has been shortened back to its proper length. One woman, who keeps the cistern with the glassy reeds, says she has stood in the unfinished circle and felt it standing in the same circle without displacement, the way two temperatures can occupy one cup of water. She says it was curious about the reeds. Not hungry. Curious in the manner of a surveyor who has found a material that was not in the specification and is deciding whether to include it in the next revision. The reeds have not been removed. Neither have they been explained. That, she says, is as close as the agreement comes to mercy: a postponement.

It does not eat in any sense that leaves bones. Moisture is enough, and the small heat of hands, and the corrections. When a crack lengthens overnight, something has been fed, or satisfied, or simply brought into line; the residents do not distinguish. After such a night the paste between the plates is duller, less luminous, and someone will find their footprint from the previous evening smoothed away and, beside the smoothing, a second print, bare, narrow, the toes undifferentiated, as if the foot had been a single cautious idea of a foot. The print lasts an hour. Then it is pavement. No one digs. Digging is not forbidden. It is only that the paste does not become soil no matter how far you go, and at the depth of a forearm the cold changes quality, becomes the cold of a volume that is still being decided, and the tool warms in the hand as if something on the other side of the decision had touched the metal to learn its length.

It has been here longer than the lip of stone, or the lip is the part of it that has agreed to be an edge. The marsh does not cross. Frogs, if there are frogs in that marsh, stop their noise at the boundary, not in fear, in the way a song stops when the room it was written for has not been built yet. Inside, the agreement continues its slow census. It learns the chip in the cup. It learns the number of steps a particular woman takes between her door and the overlapping balcony, and if she is ill and takes fewer, the balcony, by morning, has moved closer by exactly the difference, so that her day still closes. The residents call this being kept. They are not sure it is a favor. A man who tried to leave by the outer path three winters ago was found still on the path at dusk, though he had walked steadily since noon. The marsh was where he had left it. The bowl was no farther. He had not been turned around. The distance had been reviewed and found incomplete, and so it had not been allowed to finish. He lives in the lane of arriving lintels now. He says the agreement is not a jailer. He says a jailer would have a door it was interested in keeping shut. This, he says, is only unfinished, and it dislikes a journey that pretends to be complete.

At times, rarely, it gathers. The tick that usually passes from wall to wall falls silent, and the silence is so complete that the residents look up from their ledgers as if a bell had been struck. Then, under the tree with the disc-branches, the circle chooses a radius. For a few minutes the city has a center. What stands there is not stable enough to describe in a single posture. It is a coat filled with the cold of the upper rooms. It is a standing vein of the gray paste, person-height, faintly luminous at the edges, holding the shape of attention. It is several people at once, seen from behind, all of them the residents, all of them facing away, their shoulders occupying one shoulder. It does not turn toward the windows. The windows turn toward it, a fraction, with the small sound of mesh curtains taking a direction from air that is not moving. The moths settle on the discs of the tree and do not fold their wings. No one goes into the court. They watch from the overlapping balconies, each seeing a slightly different height, a slightly different number of sleeves, and they do not compare the accounts afterward. Comparison would force it to choose, and when it chooses too quickly a street forgets which shadow belongs to which cornice, and the correction that follows leaves everyone awake.

Then the radius lets go. The circle is only stone again, uncommitted. The tick resumes, softer, as after a long note. In the saucers on the sills the water has a skin on it, and in the skin, for as long as the skin lasts, is the afterimage of a face that is not a face so much as a record of having looked: two paler smudges where attention was, a line where a mouth might be if mouths were required for the work. By the time anyone reaches a sill, the skin has broken and the water is only water, cold, local, reflecting the building it is supposed to reflect, on time, obedient. Somewhere in a sleeve the narrow warmth is already fading. Somewhere a lintel adds one to its number and then, courteous, puts the old number back, as if embarrassed to have been seen revising itself.

What lives in the place is the place’s refusal to be done. It keeps the people because people are precise instruments: they notice when a cup is chipped, when a stair has stolen a step, when a window has begun to tell the truth of a different court. It wears their pauses. It practices, in the delayed water, the idea of a body, and then abandons the idea without grief, the way a draft abandons a line that has not yet earned its place in the finished text. It will not be finished. That is the only hunger it has, and it is endless, and it is not aimed at anyone. The residents boil their kettles inside it. They nod in the lanes. They sleep through most of the corrections. On the outer lip, which advances a finger’s width in a month, the stone is still soft, still deciding, and if you kneel and put a hand to it you can feel, under the damp, not a pulse exactly, but the slow willingness of something that has all the time it needs, and has already included your hand in the measurement, and will not let the measurement end merely because you stood up and walked back toward the marsh.
