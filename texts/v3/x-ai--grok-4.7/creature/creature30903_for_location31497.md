What lives there does not announce itself by standing in the middle. The middle is the one place it does not keep. It prefers the joints, the lee of the slipped slab, the film where a pan tips into moss, the notch that rattles because a stone has never quite seated. If you arrived and waited for a shape against the sky, you would conclude the bowl was only birds and water and the old marks of people. That conclusion would be almost right for a long time, which is how it prefers to be approached: as a series of corrections you make too late to be useful.

At first it reads as weather. A drip that continues after the joint has gone quiet. A flock that lifts a half-breath before your boot finds the suck of the ground, as if the sedge had been listening with more ears than a bird keeps. The underground pulse underfoot, which you took for drainage, answering once when you speak and then not again, so that you decide you imagined the answer. Thin air makes the pulse in your own ears loud enough to cover small discrepancies. It uses that. Many visitors have stood in the bowl, named the birds, drunk from a pan, and left with the feeling that the place was crowded and the belief that the crowd was ordinary.

Stay through a change of light and the discrepancies stop dispersing. The willow ring is not only willow. Among the stems that were bent and tied long ago into an arch that serves no purpose, one stem is smoother than bark should be, and it is not always in the same gap. It does not walk in any way you can narrate. It occupies the next reasonable place the way water occupies the next lower thread: you see the consequence, not the transit. A shrew-path ends where it should continue. A fern in a weeping crack is pressed flat and then, when you look back from the shelter, is only wet. The low mammal the color of stone hunts its circuit and, at one pan, refuses the obvious bank and goes the long way, ears wrong, as if the bank were already full.

It is not large. The bowl would not hold a large thing without the walls reporting it, and the walls report everything that strikes. This one has learned not to strike. Its size is closer to the shelter than to the mountain: a bunk's worth of presence, sometimes less, never the volume of the hollow. When the cloud sits on the rim and the light is honest but short, you can mistake a darker wetness on the north wall for stone that has sweated more than the stone beside it. The darker wetness has a patience stone does not. It holds still while a bird lands in the joint above it and while the bird decides the joint is wrong and leaves. It holds still while you count the pecked cups and lose count because a drop clapped in one of them. Only when your attention is fully on the clap does the darker patch revise its edge. Not a step. A revision. The wall is as it was, and the place where your eye was resting is moss again, or lichen, or the ordinary shine of a film.

Sound is how it spends most of what it is. The notches were not tuned for it; it arrived, or remained, after the wind had already learned those throats, and it fitted itself to them the way the ferns fitted the cracks. When the flutes start, something in the noise is not wind. A second partial, lower than the stone should give, that makes the loose slab's rattle fall into a rhythm instead of a worry. Birds answer that partial more readily than they answer each other. The flock's cry that seems to originate in the wall sometimes does. Not mimicry exactly. The bowl is already an instrument. It plays the instrument from inside the resonance, spending its attention as a listener spends attention, and the animals, which have lived with that spending longer than any path has led here, treat the extra note as a feature of the hall. They nest. They hunt. They do not crowd it. A hawk does not hang long above a bowl that is already ringing with a more intimate attention; the hawk's work is done by the noise, and the hawk knows.

What it wants is not a secret in the way a plot is a secret. It wants the water not to leave too quickly, and the water mostly does not. It wants the green skin to keep drinking a height that should be grit and crust, because the green is cover and food and a softener of footsteps not its own. It wants the pecked cups filled when the air is wrung, and it uses them. You will not see a mouth. You will see a cup that was brimming and is less, with no overflow trail down the boss, on a morning when nothing has spilled and the sun has not yet worked. The row faces the entry notch because that is the direction from which interruption comes. The cups are not a map of the mountain. They are a count of approaches, or a thirst broken into thumbpads so that thirst does not have to show itself as a body. Whoever cut them understood this, or learned it, and the cutting took time enough that understanding is the simpler explanation. The ties of bark on the willow arch are older than the shelter. The arch is not a door. It is a place where the presence can be, briefly, shaped like a passage, so that anything small crossing under it crosses under a condition rather than under a shrub.

The shelter is a disagreement it has been editing rather than evicting. People made it, and the fires were small, and the soot is still the width of a face. It did not need them gone so much as it needed them not to become a habit of many. The roof slipped because drip finds every seam, and because a slab that is almost seated can be encouraged, over winters, to be a little less seated, until rain and weight do the rest. The bone or wood peg was worried by a cord that held something — a bundle, a bag, a bird brought in from the noise — and the cord is gone because cord rots and because this place returns organic ties to the film. It lets the shelter stand as a bunk-sized proof that a body can be here overnight. It does not let the proof become a camp. Moss in the chinks, a fern in the roof-joint, the laid floor beginning to be filed: these are not decay as neglect. They are the bowl's handwriting returning to a sentence someone else started.

If it has a body, the body is a compromise with being seen. In the fullest occupancy you are likely to be granted, late, when the light has gone ordinary-dark and the flutes are keeping and your own pulse is loud enough to be mistaken for the mountain's, it is a low gathering at the edge of the highest pan. Not a crouch with limbs you could count. A density in the wet, the way a deeper place in a river is only a darker green until the current wrinkles over it. The density has a warmer smell than stone: willow, iron, the faint sweetness of lichen chewed or slept on. It does not breathe on your rhythm. When it draws, the draw is slow enough that the pan beside it sinks a thread and then is still. Eyes, if they are eyes, are the parts that fail first when you try to keep them: two interruptions in the shine, set wider than a fox and narrower than anything that should need a shelter the size of a bunk. They do not reflect your light so much as omit a little of the water's light. Looking at them is like looking at the gaps in the cloud and finding the gaps looking back only in the sense that distance has an attention.

It is old in the way the bedding of the rock is old, not in the way the shelter is old. The notches finished their shaping before it needed them, or it needed them so long that the difference is unavailable. It does not rule the birds. It is simply the reason a summit bowl can sound like a hamlet's big room and still be mostly unoccupied space. Unoccupied is the wrong word once you have stayed. The space is occupied by a listener that has thinned itself to fit scarce air, that spends its size on resonance instead of height, that drinks by cups and by films, that edits human traces back into drip and moss without hurry and without mercy of the kind that would be a story. The animals are many because it is here and because it is quiet in its body. The lushness is a permission it maintains. The unusual agreement — height, wet, noise — is not a miracle laid on the mountain. It is the habitation, considered from outside the inhabitant.

When you leave by the entry notch, the small stones set on edge do their ordinary work: they remind you the drop inside is more sudden than the approach. One of them may be warmer than the air. You will not be followed onto the open fell. Out there the wind is only wind, and the thinness is only thinness, and a cry does not return from three walls. Behind you, if you turn, the bowl is a green stain in a gray cup, bright or clouded according to the sky, and the flutes are a rumor the ridge steals. Something small crosses a pan you can no longer see. The water reports it to every wall. The report does not reach you. That is the edge of it: a life the size of a bunk, the age of a closed circuit of stone, audible everywhere inside and almost nowhere past the rim, becoming, as you go, only the ordinary unease of a high place that was wetter than it should have been, and louder, and for a while seemed to know you had entered.
