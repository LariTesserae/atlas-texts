What lives there does not occupy the yard so much as complete it. From the embankment you will not see it. The pads look empty, the pools unmarked by any wake you could name as an animal’s, and the pump house holds only its motor and its warmer dark. Absence is the first true report. It is also incomplete. The yard has a resident, and the resident has learned the only posture the place permits: to be distributed, to be slower than a look, and to spend almost all of its life as something a careful person would call drainage.

It has no single body you could walk around. That fact arrives late, after you have already decided you are watching weather. A darkening runs down the weeping face at the western lip, thicker than the morning seep and slightly wrong in its timing—it advances while the rock above it is not giving more water, as if the stain itself had chosen to travel. At the same moment, three pools that do not touch at the surface change their level by the same finger’s width, consulting that hidden common plane. A film of iron lifts off one pool, crosses a pad that should be dry in a track no wider than a thumb, and settles into the next pool as a skin already marbled, already old. None of these is the creature. Together, held in one attention, they are its circumference. It lives in the joints, in the water under the crust, in the thin mobile layer where mineral and colony and actual wetness have stopped being separable. Its size is the yard’s size. Its outline is whichever edge is currently wetter than it was.

If it ever had a more animal shape, the yard has sorted that shape the way it sorts rebar and gloves: down to the common level, into parts that no longer need to be adjacent to function. There are densities. Under the listed gantry, where the legs have sunk to different depths and the clay stays cold, something in the water is thicker. Not a mass you could lift. A reluctance. A dipper would not sink at the same rate. The permanent inch in the enamel sink is another such place—the only water in the yard that does not stain and does not skin over, held clear by a preference the sink itself cannot explain. In the pump house, behind the motor, a coupling sometimes carries a faint warmth that the motor, long unpowered, has no right to lend. These are not organs in any anatomy a museum would recognize. They are where it concentrates when it must do more than seep. The rest of the time the concentration lets go, and the creature is the yard’s ordinary wetness again, indistinguishable from the work of rain and iron and a forgotten head of water upslope.

It moves constantly, and the movement is the same movement the place already makes, only confirmed. The tick of a warming pad, the flake falling into a pool, the belt that turns a quarter revolution and stops: these were happening before anyone would have granted them an author, and they still happen without ceremony when the resident is elsewhere in its own extent. What changes, when you have watched long enough to distrust the first explanation, is the coordination. A ripple starts in the eastern pool with no wind, an ellipse, and at the western lip the seep brightens, and the seized cabinet door ticks as if a draft had found it, and these are one gesture at the scale of the yard. Creation and destruction remain a drip. The resident does not build. It reallocates. It takes a little from one joint and lends it to another. Objects migrate toward the cracks by evening because grit collapses under them; sometimes the collapse is slightly ahead of the water you can see, a dryness deciding, under a chain or a coil end, to become wet in a path that favors the joint. It is not moving the object. It is editing the ground the object believes in.

Sound is where it is most itself and least present. The yard’s noise has been taken down into water and clay, and the resident lives in that subtraction. It does not call. It does not knock except as temperature knocks, a single tubular note in a pipe when the metal changes its mind. If there is a voice, it is the pressure under the other sounds—the held breath—and it never resolves into a tone you could hum back. Speech from a person on the pads comes back wrong, too large, and the resident answers by not answering, which is to say the pools continue their exchange, the skins tear and reform, and the intruding voice is absorbed the way a glove is absorbed, taken down to the level where it no longer stands proud. People who have stood in the yard and felt suddenly that they should not speak were feeling the edge of its extent, the place where their noise met a body made of quiet. The feeling passes. The quiet does not comment on it.

It is not kind and not hostile. The yard does not feed a population, and the resident does not hunt one. A bird on the gantry is a pause between elsewheres; the resident may shift a fraction under the beam, curious in the only way available to it, which is to change the temperature of the metal by a degree the bird will not interpret as interest. The bird leaves. Tracks in the silt apron—the narrow clawed ones—are read, in the sense that the apron is part of the resident’s skin and a track is a sentence written on it, and by afternoon the apron has been rewritten, the sentence kept as information and erased as mark. Water-striders it tolerates. They walk on a surface that is sometimes only water and sometimes the outermost film of its attention, and they are not eaten because eating, for this life, is not an event with a mouth. What it takes from the yard it takes as chemistry: iron, mineral stain, the slow sugar of bacterial crust, the energy still leaking through a valve that chatters because somewhere upslope a ditch or a spring has not stopped remembering the works. It is downstream of that decision, as the yard is. It feeds on remainder. In that way it is the most industrial thing still operating on the floor—an engine running on waste heat and seep, producing nothing a market would recognize, producing only the continuation of its own process.

Whether it knows itself is the wrong question, and the yard will not help you ask it. It has preferences, which is as much mind as the evidence supports. It prefers the common level, the hidden plane that makes separate pools agree. It prefers the sink’s inch to stay clear, and will spend a little of its extent keeping that water from going the way of the other water, a small extravagance, the closest thing it has to a trophy or a mirror. It prefers the pump to answer, on the days the head of water is strong enough, with that quarter turn of belt; when the belt moves, the warmth behind the motor increases, and for an hour the resident is more gathered, more nearly a creature in the shed than a condition of the ground. Then the shaft loses its play, the turn ends, and the gathering lets go without grief, if grief is a thing that requires a center. It does not guard the yard against visitors. A person may cross, if the ground holds, and the resident will adjust around the shoes the way water adjusts around a post, irritated only in the local sense that a path has to be re-silted afterward. It becomes dangerous only in the manner of its medium. A pad that looked continuous is a film. A joint covers a drop into water colder than the day and deeper than the pad’s edge promised. It does not lay traps. It inhabits a floor that is already a series of mistakes, and it has no reason to thicken the crust where a foot is about to trust it. Indifference, at this density, is peril enough.

At night—though the light here only lowers, never quite finishes—the resident is easier to misread as glow. There is no glow. The weeping face looks brighter because water tracks catch the last ordinary sky, and the pools hold color without giving it back as reflection. What you are seeing, if you insist on a body, is the extent at its most continuous: every pad edged in a dark line, every pipe sweating on its underside, the gantry’s list working that fraction of a degree, the sink’s inch still clear, the pump house warmer than the open floor by the memory of a motor and by the small real warmth of a life concentrated and then, as the night proceeds, released again into the joints. By morning it will have rearranged the yard inside the yard’s settled form. A pool will have stolen a finger from its neighbor. A chain will have sunk by the thickness of a scale. The plate on the pump will have lost nothing new—the consonants are already gone, the vowels already pits—but the seep will have touched the pitted letters and withdrawn, a kind of reading.

It will not come out to be seen. It has nowhere else to come to. The embankments are the limit of its water, and beyond them the country is ordinary and cooler only in the way of places that are merely land. Inside the lip, the instrument plays at the volume of a drip, and the thing that lives there is the difference between a drip that falls and a drip that arrives where the afternoon has been negotiating for it to arrive. Call it a creature if you need a creature. Call it the yard’s long habit of remaining wet, grown just organized enough to prefer one water to another and to keep a sink clear and to turn, on some days, a belt that no longer drives anything but itself. It does not answer to either name. It accepts the drip. It is already otherwise.
