They are not many, and they are not hidden. Distance does the hiding. A body on the far terrace is a fact the eye can hold and a presence the ear cannot finish; the roar takes the last of the approach and leaves only the figure, small against amber leaf and white wall, paused in a passing-niche as if the niche had grown a person rather than received one. Walk the long way and the figure is still there when you arrive, or is not. Either way the town does not feel emptied. It feels correctly occupied: a few lives set into a volume that was never meant to be filled, only kept in tune.

Up close they are dry in the way the brick is dry. Skin the color of the finer scale, not pale from sunlessness — there is no sunlessness — but from having no moisture to spend on a sheen. The light of the lens has tanned nothing and burned nothing. It has only clarified them, the way it clarifies the copper pins: edges visible, pores silted with the same dust the curtains feed on, the inner wrist darker where resin has dried into a glaze and been worn half away by the day’s circuit. Hair, where it is kept, is fiber-stiff and the color of old bone; many crop it to the skull or braid it into the same flat plaits the mats use, so that a sleeper in a niche and the niche itself are hard to separate until the sleeper shifts. Eyes are the wettest thing they carry, and even those are narrow from the perpetual glare, the whites slightly ambered, the gaze direct because there is no point in looking away from brightness. They blink less than people who live under weather. The note has trained the lids.

They do not shout, and they do not, after childhood, try. Speech happens in the baffles, mouth to ear, or it happens as a pluck: two fingers on a curtain-fiber at a taught place, a shiver sent across the void that another reader catches on the opposite wall and answers with a shorter shiver, or with none. The vocabulary is small and exact — pin loose, slack coming, pith ready, moth-hour, lid unchanged — and most of what would be talk in another town is simply not said, because the body already has it. A person standing still in the midday grind is already reporting the weather. A person who pauses when the note drops a third is already in council. Children learn the hand-signs first, in the niches, before their teeth have settled into the grit; the signs are spare, built from the same motions as work: a tap, a smoothing, a refusal of the floor by keeping the palm turned up.

Their work is the circuit, and the circuit is light. No one hauls water. No one cuts stone anymore; the scallops under the brick are left as the chamber’s memory of a wetter age, and new work is only reseating, relashing, reading. A keeper of a ring walks it when the chord is high, stone in one hand, the other hand reading copper by warmth and by the small click a true pin makes against the nail. A loose pin is tapped home. A curtain thickening over a street is lifted and tied back with its own fiber until the passing-niche faces the wall again. At the sun-shelves under the lens, where the plants run thickest and the pith is sweetest, someone presses the inner stalks between salt-clay plates and catches the oil in the chest-cisterns. It is not farming so much as agreeing with a growth that would happen anyway. The plants do not need the people. The people need the plants not to close the last open ledge, and not to fail. So the tending is a kind of editing: enough fiber left to feed and to telegraph, enough cut back that a body can still pass, enough resin running in the gutters that the brick remembers it is mortared.

They sleep in the baffles, short sleeps, because the note does not night. A body learns to drop under the pressure the way a swimmer learns to drop under a wave: not fighting the roar, letting it become the outside of the dream. Waking is often to a change in pitch rather than to a touch. In the slack hour, when the moths unhitch, some of them climb anyway — not to work, to be in the measurement. They stand with a hand on a curtain and feel the dust decide whether to fall. This is as close as the town comes to ceremony. There are no gods named. There is the lens, the throat, the lid under the dune. Respect is procedural. You do not step the ripples. You do not store water. You do not strike a flame. A child who tests any of these is not punished with spectacle; the gardens answer, a sour ribbon in the nearest resin, and the adults let the sourness be the lesson. The floor, especially, is taught early. The scallops in the uncovered wall are pointed out once, as proof that water opened this place and then left, and that what remains under the dune is not a well but a sealed argument. No one has lifted the lid in living memory. The listening is done by watching whether the grand form of the ripples holds. If it holds, the downward thing is only being itself, and the people may go on being the small things at the edge.

They are few enough that kinship is the whole census. Names pass across the void as plucks rather than as calls, and a death is a ring that does not get walked until someone from the next ring crosses over and takes the stone. Bodies are not buried in the floor. They are given to the upper curtains on the lee side, where the fiber closes fast, and the scales that result are indistinguishable, after a season, from the shed of the plants. No one finds this grim. The town is already a process of shedding: dust, scale, resin, the parts of a life that water would have asked them to keep. What remains is the chord and the hands that can still hear inside it.

A newcomer would see them as austere, almost erased — narrow eyes, dry mouths, a reluctance to fill silence that is not silence. Living among them a little longer, you would see the opposite: a continuous, low-grade attention, sociable in a register that does not need gathering. They know who slept, who pressed pith, whose pin sang wrong, without assembling. They touch more than they speak, briefly, at the wrist or the shoulder, checking glaze and grit the way they check copper. They laugh, rarely, in the baffles, a sound like scale ticking, private and unsurprised. They are not waiting for the note to end. They are the note’s tenants, and their abundance is the same kind as the gardens’: not numbers, but a fit so complete that the empty center still looks empty, and the town still looks like a mechanism left running, correctly, by people who have become part of what it runs on.
