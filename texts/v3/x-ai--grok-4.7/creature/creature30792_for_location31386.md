Nothing here has a face you could greet, and nothing arrives. The lives were put in, or grew from what was put in, and then the door of the world became the valve under the dish, which does not open inward. What you meet, if you stay through more than one slam, is a set of bodies that have stopped resembling their original stock so completely that resemblance is only a rumor in the way a mouth still fits a bolt.

At first the open water seems held by sentries. Pale columns, evenly spaced, each one corrected toward the motors by a degree when the lamps flicker. It is easy to give them an attention they do not have. Come closer, on the slack, and the column is not a posture. It is the whole animal: a sleeve of slow muscle around a gut that faces downstream, anchored to nothing, kept upright by a small, tireless adjustment against the room’s residual turn. There is no head. The pale tissue at the upstream end is a filter of cilia so fine they look like mist, and the mist tastes the jet before the jet arrives — a sweeter metal, a slight thickening, the broth from the warm-side throat already diluted across the upper third. That taste is their dawn. They lean into it and open. For the minute of the crossing weather they are mouths with bodies attached, and the brief milk of larvae that follows the slam is not a season they chase but a rain that falls into them if they have leaned correctly. Miss the lean and the outlet takes the milk. There is no second chance until the valves speak again.

They do not school. A second column inside another’s reach fouls both filters, and the fouled one starves on a schedule so exact it might as well be law. So they tile the middle and leave the middle empty, which is the only wilderness the room consented to keep. Between cycles they are almost furniture. You can forget they are alive until one of them, old enough that its muscle has gone the color of the crust, fails to complete a correction and is turned slowly by the residual current until its gut faces the cool wall. Then it looks wrong, the way a valve installed backward looks wrong, and the others do not help it. By the next pulse it has either found the taste again or it has begun, without drama, to come apart, and the small bright things on the seams get a share they did not earn from the lamps.

Those bright things are not young of the columns. That was the first mistake, and it lasts until you watch a seam through a full darkening of the summit light beyond the lens. They are beads, hard, slightly warm to the idea of touch, each one a mouth on the underside and a clear capsule above that is not an eye. The capsule takes the two lights as two temperatures and only clamps when both arrive together — daylight through the crazed glass, and the work-lamp’s nearer heat. One light alone and they stay soft enough to crop. Both lights, and they lock to the mineral, throwing the two shadows the room is always making. Their abundance is real and local: a constellation along a failed seal, then a blank of crust as long as a body, then another seam. They eat the pale threads the cool wall writes, and they eat the crumbs of anything that fails in the open water, and they do not travel. A bead that leaves its ring does not find another. The grid is their map because the grid is where the sealant failed, and failure is where the food is. Generations have been born in the film that slicks the ring after the feed pulse, and the film is the only childhood the room allows: a few hours of drift, a taste for alloy, a landing, and then the clamp that will be the rest of the life.

In the dish the larger bodies move, and for a while they seem like the room’s livestock, which is not entirely false. Each one wears a pavement of the same rind the walls grow, so a still grazer is a patch of floor with a wrong edge. They are old in a way the columns are not. A column replaces itself in a handful of summit-years; a grazer keeps the same tongue and adds crust the way the building adds crust, layer on layer, until the animal is partly the room’s weather record. The tongue is cartilage the color of wet bone, ridged in a thread that matches the vanished bolts — not because anyone cut it to fit, but because every tongue that did not fit starved on the nubs, and the nubs are what the floor has left to offer. They rasp, pause, rasp. They never clear a patch. The cool wall’s script of pale threads survives in the margins of their paths, and that incompleteness is why the wall can be read at all: growth, erasure, growth, on the cycle’s long beat rather than the pump’s short one. When the jet fires they stop and clamp, the same instinct as the beads, and the pale veil of lifted sediment settles over them as if the floor were thinking of burying its own animals and had thought better of it.

They have no interest in the columns and little in each other. Once, in the long stillness, two of them met at a nub and stayed, rasps working opposite sides of the same remnant tooth, close enough that their crusts began to sinter where they touched. They did not separate. The joined place is still there, a low bridge in the dish, two tongues, one pavement. It is not love. It is time, and the fact that letting go would have meant tearing living rind. Other grazers detour around the bridge as they detour around the valve housing. The housing is warm. They do not know it is a mouth the world might someday fall through. They know only that the metal there sings at a pitch the rest of the floor does not, and that singing metal does not grow the threads they eat, so it is not a place to stop.

The pipe mouths are the correction to the idea that everything here is mild. One lurker to an embayment, sometimes none, never two for long. They are not eels. An eel would be a visitor’s shape, brought in from water that has a bank. What lives in the throat is a fold of the same even green as the ribbons, but the green has given up the holdfast and kept the hunger. It looks, until it moves, like a plant that rooted in the wrong direction — into the dark of the pipe instead of out of the seam. The body is a sheath. Inside the sheath is a quicker animal, muscle laid in rings, a mouth that is a ring too, toothless, sized over generations to the lip of the throat it inherits. They do not hunt in the open. The open is too empty, and emptiness is the columns’ country. They hunt the inhale.

When the lesser valves cycle, the room draws, and anything soft enough to be drawn comes a little nearer the mouths. The slam covers the sound of the sheath turning inside out — a wet reversal, fast, gone back into the throat before the ringing in the hull has finished. What they take is usually a bead that drifted too far on the pulse, or a larva that missed every column, or, rarely, the downstream end of a column that leaned too far and could not recover its station. A column bitten is not killed cleanly. The filter keeps working. The gut, shortened, faces the wrong way for a cycle or two, and then the animal thins and joins the crumbs. The lurkers do not pursue. Pursuit would put them in the middle, where the jet can pin a soft body to the far wall and hold it there until the outlet opens. That has happened. The smears on the alloy opposite the strongest mouth are old and green-black and do not grow threads. The others read those smears the way the grazers read singing metal: not as warning, exactly, but as a place that does not feed you and might unmake you, which is the only warning this water has ever needed.

Nothing in the pipes is a parent to the ribbons, though the color suggests it. The ribbons are still the crop. Their holdfasts remember the mounting diagram more faithfully than any animal remembers a parent. After the warm-side pulse they thicken; in the slack they write cursive in a current that has not changed its angle since the form of the room stopped changing. They are lush and stupid and exact, and they are the reason the other lives can be sparse. A sea with a thin pasture starves its grazers into roaming. Here the pasture is a few fat plants on a remembered grid, plus the threads on the cool wall, plus the broth, and that is enough, because enough was engineered once and the equilibrium never found a reason to become more.

There is one more kind, and it is the one that takes longest to see, because it looks like the machine.

Near the jet housing, in the wash where the crossing current is born, small shapes click. They have the silhouette of leftover hardware — cowlings, little vanes, a screw-form that catches the lamp and throws a hard shadow onto the crust. For several cycles it is reasonable to think they are debris the pump has not yet ground down. Then one of them reverses. Debris does not reverse. The vane-like plates are shell, grown, not milled; the screw is a body that drills a station into the boundary layer and holds it, feeding on what the jet concentrates: the denser fraction of the broth, the larvae that strike the housing and stun, the silt that would otherwise sand the impeller to nothing. They are animals that have spent an age being mistaken for parts, and the mistake has been rewarded. Anything that looked more like flesh was cropped by lurkers or torn by the slam’s shock through the housing. Anything that looked like a fitting survived, and bred, and the breeding kept the look. Their click is not speech and not quite a warning. It is the sound of a shell seating against alloy at the moment the valves begin their order, a seating that means: hold, the weather is about to become a blade.

They do not fill the housing. Three, sometimes four, spaced like the columns are spaced, like the beads on a seam. The room’s rule of emptiness repeats at every scale that can support a mouth. Even the larvae obey it, though they have no time to learn: they appear as a milk in one corner only, never a fog across the volume, and they are gone before they can become a crowd. Where they come from is the film on the rings and the sheaths in the throats, a spawning folded into the same timer as the feed, so that birth and weather are one event. No one guards them. Guarding would require a behavior that lasts longer than a slam, and nothing here has a behavior that lasts longer than a slam except clinging, rasping, and waiting to taste metal.

If there is a person in the room, it is not any of these. It is the oldest grazer, if person means a body that has been present for enough cycles to carry a record others do not carry — the joined bridge, the scar where a lamp’s shadow tanned one side of its rind paler, the habit of stopping one rasp-length short of the singing valve. It does not know the height. It knows a vibration that has never stopped, a note under the dish, and it has arranged its life so that the note stays under it rather than in it. That is as close as the room comes to memory that is not chemical. The columns inherit a lean. The beads inherit a clamp. The lurkers inherit a reversal timed to noise. The vane-animals inherit a shape that lies. None of them inherit a story. The story is only the cycle, repeated until repetition has worn the edges off every other possibility, and the lives fit the remaining shape the way the rasp fits the nub: not by design anymore, by the long refusal of every body that did not fit.

Between events they are quiet in a loud room. The pump does not sleep, the cool wall ticks, the sealed drop sings under the valve, the high wind worries the hull. Inside that, a column holds its degree. A bead waits for both lights. A green sheath rests inverted in a throat, which is its relaxed state, a plant-like lie it tells the pipe. In the dish the joined grazers breathe through the seams of their own crust, a slow exchange you would miss if you wanted a more obvious animal. They are not waiting for rescue, or for the lens to open, or for the great valve to decide. The summit day passes over them as a change in one of the two temperatures, useful only as a signal to lock or to feed. When the broth comes they thicken the ribbons without knowing they have been fed by a line whose other end is not a world they can enter. When the slam comes they become, for one mechanical report, purely still.

That stillness is the closest thing they have to a shared hour. Everything living in the room stops at once, and for the length of the ringing they are one arrangement: columns leaned, beads clamped, grazers paved into the floor, lurkers already back in the dark, vane-shells seated, ribbons flat against the current that is about to die. Then the slack returns, and they are separate again, sparse, exact, abundant only in the way a few lives can be abundant when the room is kind and closed and will not change its form. They will be here, in these shapes, as long as the seal holds. Nothing in them is trying to become anything else. The becoming already finished. What remains is the fit, and the warm ordinary light, and the next taste of metal moving through the upper third, right on time.
