They do not begin as animals. They begin as a decision the stone made so long ago that the decision no longer looks like one. In the cracks where condensation collects and does not quite leave, a film thickens. It is the color of the rock one shade wetter, and for a long time that is all it is: a reluctance of moisture to be only moisture. Then the film sorts what it touches. A grain of grit stays; a salt is pulled inward; a fleck of metal, oxidized to uselessness by the dark air, is teased back toward something a living chemistry can spend. The film does not move. It thickens in place until the thickening is a body, and the body is a crust, and the crust is a terrace of crusts, each one reading the waste of the one beneath as the next instruction. If you break a terrace—and they break reluctantly, with a sound like dry bread—you find no organs, only layers, and in the layers a faint warmth that is not the plateau’s warmth. They are working. They have always been working. The first inhabitants of the high dark are these, and they do not regard themselves as inhabitants. They regard the damp as a task that has not ended.

From them, over an interval no season can count, other tasks learned to walk.

The smallest walkers are almost not separate from the crusts they tend. They are the width of a thumb, low, many-legged in a way that looks, if the brief electrical pallor ever catches them, like a comb dragged through itself. They do not graze. They do not hunt. They move along the edges of the terraces in the hours after a silent discharge, when the ridges have spent their charge and the crusts, fed by it, put out a thin new skin of salts and reduced metals. The small ones lift that skin with mouthparts that are also scoops, carry it a short distance—never far; far is expensive—and press it into pockets the older crusts have left unfinished. Some of what they carry is food for themselves. Most of it is not. They are couriers in a factory that has no foreman, and when the pocket is full they turn and go back for another load, and the turning is so regular that a person watching, if a person could watch in this dark, would begin to count them as strokes of a machine. They are not many in any one crack. Across the plateau they are past counting. Their sound is the tick already heard at the basin lips, finer here, like a nail drawn once across slate and then ashamed of itself.

The basin swimmers came later, or sideways, from the same patience. In the stone bowls that hold water past the rain, the water is never still for long, though nothing wind-sized disturbs it. What turns it is a body the length of a finger, translucent enough that in the rare sheen on a pool you can see the pool through it, and inside the body a slow screw of tissue that does not stop. They do not school. Schooling would be a crowd, and crowds spend water twice. Each swimmer holds a volume and works it: silt in, silt sorted, a concentrate settled in a private gut, a clarified thread of water vented behind. When the gut is full the swimmer rises to the rim—not out; out is death by drying—and deposits a pellet the size of a seed on the stone just above the waterline, where the small comb-legged tenders will find it before the next fog. The pellets are not waste in the sense of refusal. They are the next stage. Phosphate, a little reduced iron, the bodies of the airborne spores that the sparse rain beat down from the dark. The swimmers are mills. Their milling sounds, if you put an ear to the basin wall, like sand deciding to lie down. They live as long as the basin remembers water. When a basin fails for too many seasons they do not migrate. They seal. A skin goes over the screw. The body becomes a grain among grains in the drying mud, and can remain a grain for longer than the walkers live, waiting for a drink that may come. Some of the grains in the older hollows are not stone. They are citizens on leave.

The wide-spaced walkers are what the eye, such as it is, would call the animals of the place. They are not the oldest and they are not the most numerous, but they are the ones that cross the emptiness, and so they are the ones that make the emptiness into a system rather than a gap. A grown walker stands about as high as a person’s waist, though the comparison is useless here and the height is not the point. The point is the belly, low and long, and the four limbs set wide so that a stumble on loose talus spends as little energy as a correct step. The feet are broad, padded, and they meet the stone with a sound that dies inside the pad. The head is small relative to the gut and is mostly a mouth and a pair of organs that are not quite eyes: pits, deep, lined with a tissue that reads heat the way eyes read light. On the open plateau, where everything is nearly the same temperature, the pits are almost quiet. Near a basin, near a terrace that has been working, near the faint afterglow of a discharge in the mineral skin of a ridge, the pits fill with information, and the walker turns without drama toward the difference.

They do not travel in a mass. A file of them crossing a flat may be a dozen bodies, each separated from the next by fifty paces or by five hundred, the distance set by what the ground is offering. If the offering is nothing—and mostly it is nothing—they simply walk, and the walking is a low gear that can be held for days. They crop the fibrous mats when they find them, but the mats are garnish. The real feed is the dust they take with the fiber, and the pellets at the basin rims, and the skins the small tenders have stacked in the cracks, and, when a terrace has overgrown itself and split, the inner layers of the crusts, which are richer than anything growing on top of them. A walker’s gut is a kiln that does not burn. It is long, chambered, acidic in one reach and alkaline in the next, populated by chemistries older than the walker and not entirely its own. Stone dust goes in. What comes out, miles later, in a small deliberate pile left on bare rock where the next fog will find it, is a concentrate: metals brought closer to useful, phosphates freed, a grit of residual silica that the crusts, in another season, will know how to refuse. The piles are the walker’s product. The walker is the conveyor and the refinery both, and it does not know the words, and it does not need them. It knows the distance to the next damp, and it knows when the gut is full, and the two knowledges are the schedule.

They speak, if the late thin calls are speech. The call is made low in the chest, not with a mouth, and it carries because the plateau is empty enough to carry it. It is not a song. It is a location and a state: I am here; my gut is at this fullness; the ground under me is this temperature. The answer, when it comes, comes late, and the lateness is part of the message, because lateness is distance. A file crossing a saddle is in conference the entire time and never clusters to hold it. Calves—small, the same shape, the gut not yet long enough to refine anything worth refining—walk closer to an adult than adults walk to each other, not for sentiment, for the wake of information. A calf learns the piles by smell and by the heat-pits. It learns which crusts are ready by the way the tenders tick more loudly along them. It learns the basins by the change in the air a quarter mile out, the faint cool that means water has not yet failed. By the time its own gut is long, it does not need to be told the route. The route is the plateau’s, and the plateau has not moved.

There are times when the file stops being a file. A walker whose gut has taken in something the chambers cannot crack—a nodule of older crust, a pellet from a basin that went foul, a mouthful of mat that was mat only on the surface and stone all the way through—will leave the spacing and stand. The standing can last a day. The chambers work. Heat comes off the belly in a degree the pits of the others can read from far off, and they adjust, not to help, to account for a station that has gone offline. If the nodule cracks, the walker voids a darker pile than usual and rejoins the spacing at the next damp. If it does not crack, the walker folds. The folding is slow. The legs tuck. The belly lowers onto the stone. The heat-pits go quiet, and then the heat of the belly goes quiet, and what remains is a shape that the plateau will sand down over seasons into another low mound, and inside the mound the uncracked nodule, and around the mound, within a year of fogs, a new terrace of crusts that found the concentrate of a failed refinery and began, without comment, to finish the job the gut could not.

The predators are not outside this. They are a correction in the same ledger.

What detaches from a ridge is not large either, and not fast in the way a chase is fast. It is longer than a walker, lower, and its legs are not made for the spacing of herds but for the single closing of a gap. The heat-pits on a predator are larger, and they read not the warmth of stone but the slight excess a walker’s belly sheds while the chambers are mid-task. On the open flats that excess is a beacon with no cover to hide it. The predator does not sprint until the last interval. Before that it is a piece of the ridge that has decided to be elsewhere, and the elsewhere is a point in the file where a walker is heavy, slow, gut full, attention narrowed to the next pile it means to leave. The meeting is brief because both bodies are built for brevity. A weight across the shoulders. A mouth that is less teeth than a clamp, placed where the chamber-wall is thinnest. The walker folds not in the slow way of a failed nodule but all at once, and the file, registering the loss as a call that does not get its late answer, opens its spacing by a body and continues. The predator does not follow the file. Following would be a waste, and waste is the only immorality the plateau enforces. It stays with the folded body long enough to take what the chambers had already refined—the concentrate, still warm, easier than anything the predator could mill itself—and leaves the rest for the crusts and the sand. Then it becomes ridge again, somewhere else, and waits for another belly to advertise a fullness.

Sometimes the predator is not one body. Along certain ridges the shape that detaches is a braid of smaller shapes that have not finished becoming separate, a litter that hunts as a single decision and feeds as a single gut. They are quieter than the lone kind. Their heat-pits are shared in the way the crust-terraces share their layers: what one reads, the braid spends. They take calves when they can, because a calf’s concentrate is poor but a calf’s inattention is rich, and a file will replace a calf faster than it replaces a miller in its prime. The files know this. Calves walk the inside of the spacing, adults the outside, not by instruction, by the old arithmetic of which loss costs the system more. No one weeps. The arithmetic is accurate, and accuracy is what passes here for care.

There is another life, harder to see than the predators, because it does not cross open ground and it does not shine even in the brief pallor of a discharge. In the deepest overhangs, where condensation beads and the warm plateau air meets a colder wall, there are drapes. They look like mineral stain. They are not stain. They are nets, grown, not strung, and they are the mouths of things that have given up walking in exchange for a permanent claim on the drip. A drape may be as wide as a person’s arms can reach and as thick as cloth, and it is a colony the way the terraces are colonies, except that a terrace waits for what is brought and a drape catches what falls. Spores, dust, the shed hooks of the comb-legged tenders, the fine grit the wind lifts off the piles the walkers leave. The drape sorts by adhesion and by a chemistry that differs from the terrace chemistry by a single stubborn trick: it can take water out of air that has not yet consented to condense, and it pays for the trick with a slowness that makes the terraces look hurried. A drape thickens a finger’s width in a generation of walkers. Things live in the drape that live nowhere else—threads, mites of a sort, a beetle-small packet of a gut that crawls the underside and farms a slime the drape would otherwise smother in. They are the indoor workers of a works that has no indoors. If the overhang collapses, and overhangs do, in the slow way of a treaty finally revised, the drape dies in the sunless warmth within a season, and the mites die with it, and the beetle-guts seal into grains like the swimmers, and the next overhang, a mile off, already has its own drape and does not mourn the vacancy.

None of these make tools. None of these pile stone on stone except as bodies and as waste that other bodies read. And yet if you watch the transfers long enough—the skin lifted by the tenders, the pellet set on the rim, the pile left on the flat, the drape’s slow theft from the air, the predator’s brief tax on a full gut—you are watching a production line whose stations are species. The metal moves. The phosphate moves. The water is not moved so much as witnessed, and used, and returned to the dark air, and waited for again. The labor force does not strike and does not expand past what the damp can pay. When a run of years is wetter, the basins hold, the files shorten their spacing because the offering is richer, the terraces thicken, the predators grow a second litter that hunts as a braid. When a run of years is drier, the swimmers seal, the files lengthen until a file is two animals and a rumor of a third, the drapes thin to film, and the predators become rare enough that a walker may cross a ridge and not be read. The industry scales itself. No one turns a dial. The dial is the rain, and the rain is mostly absent, and the absence is the setting the whole machine was built, by nothing, to run at.

Do they know? The question is the wrong size, but it keeps being asked by anything that arrives from a world where knowing is a property of single heads. A walker knows the fullness of its gut and the distance to the next heat-difference, and that is a large knowing, large enough to cross a dark the size of a climate. A terrace knows, in the only way a layer can, that the layer beneath has finished and the layer above has not begun, and it occupies the interval. A predator knows a beacon from a stone. A sealed swimmer knows nothing, and is not dead, and will know the basin again if the basin returns. What no single body knows is the factory. The factory knows itself only as the continuation of the transfers. If every walker folded in a single season, the piles would stop, and the terraces would slow, and the tenders would tick down to a remnant in the deepest cracks, and the drapes would go on, slower, eating the wind’s leftover dust, and the plateau would not notice the loss as a loss. It would notice a change in the schedule. The schedule has changed before. The stone is patient, and the lives on it have learned their patience from the stone, and patience, here, is not a mood. It is the technology. It is how a dark, warm, almost waterless height manufactures what it needs, and replaces its workers with their own remainder, and never once, in all the height and all the dark, strikes a light to see the work by.
