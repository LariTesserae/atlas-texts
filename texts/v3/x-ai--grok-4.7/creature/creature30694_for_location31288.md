They do not arrive so much as thicken.

At first light—which is only the iron-colored air remembering a direction—you can mistake the figures on the terraces for equipment left out. A cluster of shoulders under oilcloth near a hoist. A shape seated on a pipe run, knees drawn up, face turned toward a gap that will not be a street until the ridge finishes thinking. Then one of them shifts, and the shift is too continuous to be a man’s stretch and too deliberate to be settling metal. They have been there. The night did not remove them. Sleep, when it happens, happens inside the same dimness, often upright, often with one hand on a warm casing so the pulse of the banked fire can be told from the longer pulse under the boots.

The people are not numerous enough for the size of the works, and they do not correct this. Crews are small and old in their pairings. A boiler watch may be three: one who hears the draft before the gauges do, one who writes the numbers in a book whose pages have begun to accept ink more slowly, one who says almost nothing and is trusted with the moths. They do not rotate out of the place. Relief, when it comes, comes from lower country and does not stay; the newcomers walk the switchbacks, lose the hour, smell of hot metal for a day, and either go back down with their embarrassment intact or remain and, within a season, stop mentioning the country they left. Those who remain grow a little paler, not from lack of sun—the sun was never decisive here—but from a kind of agreement with the stone. Their skin takes on the tightness of cooling surfaces. Cuts close into seams rather than scars. They are not ill. They are heavy in the way the construction is heavy: overbuilt for a ground that has not finished offering itself.

They speak close to the mouth, and often they do not speak. Sound is already so occupied that language has learned to travel by other grains. A hand laid flat on a rail can carry a question the length of a terrace faster than a shout, and the answer may come back as a change in the vibration rather than a voice. Names are used, but sparingly, and mostly for the animals and the machines. People are known by the process they keep: the one who receives new stone, the one who will not set an outrigger on an undecided lip, the one whose count of stairs is always wrong by the same number and is therefore reliable. Strangers are tolerated the way weather is tolerated. If they follow the three sentences in the gatehouses, they are fed. If they do not, they are found later on the lower path, damp, and no one asks what they saw in the thickness of air. There is no cruelty in this. The city has no jail and no altar. It has procedures, and the procedures are short because longer ones have been known to revise themselves overnight.

Children are present and are not treated as a separate category of fragility. They learn the grain of the air before they learn to read maps, which is fortunate, because the maps revise. A child can stand on a platform edge and feel, in the soles, whether the lip has decided to be street or curtain, and will step back or not accordingly, with the same unbothered attention the pale grazers give the same question. They nest, sometimes, in the stacks between firings, and the birds make room, or do not, and both outcomes are absorbed into the next shift. Schooling, such as it is, happens beside the slower machines: a press descending over days is a lesson in patience that does not require a teacher to remain in the room. They grow up knowing that plumb is a courtesy and that an hour can be walked in either direction without changing the wear on the boots. They are not solemn. They laugh at the sound of a bell arriving early, and the laugh fits into the larger music as cleanly as the drip of leaves on metal.

The animals do not belong to them, and they do not pretend otherwise. The long-necked grazers are co-occupants of the unfinished shoulders, their color so near the new stone that a herd at rest can be mistaken for a terrace that has not yet been given a use. They calve, if that is the word, at the rate the ridge rises—infrequently, and always where the ground has just agreed to hold weight. The young are able to stand on undecided edges sooner than the adults, and the crews have learned not to herd them off. What lives in the condenser sheds is never fully seen. It is large enough to shift tin, and it leaves the mucus that melts frost, and it seems to prefer the hours when condensation runs the wrong way along a gutter. No one has tried to name it in the log. The night shift leaves a pan of condensate where the catwalk turns, not as offering and not as bait, simply because the pan is empty in the morning and the sheds tick less when it is. The dog at the survey hut is the nearest thing to a kept animal, and even that nearness fails in certain dimnesses, when its outline lengthens and its head rises on a neck that was not there at dusk, and the man in the hut continues his notes without looking up. He has stopped being surprised by the number of legs that will be under the table when he reaches down.

There are others that do not persist long enough to be kept or feared. The moths of a single heating cycle live only while the fire is in them, and the crews chart their numbers the way they chart pressure, and the charts remain accurate even when the building they describe has gained a storey. Smaller things ride the upward rain and are not the same things that return with the cold fall. A geologist once tried to collect them in a jar. The jar, in the morning, held only a film and a smell of ozone, and the geologist’s benchmark had walked a hand’s width. She did not try again. She is still employed. Her instruments are trusted; her conclusions are treated as weather reports, useful for the day they are issued.

What lives here most continuously is not any of these, and it is not separate from them. It is the habit the place has of completing itself through whoever is willing to move at its speed. A walker who loses an hour and keeps the smell of hot metal will, if he stays, become one of the ones who can hear a street before it is drawn. A grazer that stands on a lip until the lip becomes street is performing the same office as the crew with the timbers. The thing in the sheds, the moths, the child who knows when to step back, the engineer who reserves rigor for the machines and lets the foundations argue with plumb—they are not worshippers and not parasites. They are the parts of the process that have learned to anticipate the next degree of rise. You can watch a shift change and see the anticipation pass between them without a word: a cable’s note dropping, a herd moving one tower west, a hand lifting from a warm casing because the longer pulse has already said what the gauges will say in an hour.

They do not call this life anything but the work. At the far hut, where the ridge is still surfacing, the man and the animal that is sometimes his dog wait with the same posture the city uses for everything: heavy, unhurried, already agreed with a ground that has not yet arrived. When the stone comes up dark and then pale and then load-bearing, they step in. They do not celebrate. Below them the darkness with weather in it makes its river-sounds, and the pressure against the ears is not wind, and neither of them looks down for long. They have learned what the rest of the ridge’s inhabitants learned without a lesson: that to live here is to be included in the extrusion, and that the inclusion does not require you to become stone, only to stop insisting that the hour, the street, and the weight under you remain what they were when you last trusted them.
