What lives here has learned the plateau’s unfinishedness as a condition of the body, not as a story about the future.

The smallest of them never quite become visible. In the film the dusk lays back onto the stone there is a life that is mostly chemistry with a little will in it: films and flecks and pale threads that drink the mineral paste where a fresh face weeps, and die back when the noon burns the skin of water into air. They have no season. They have the cycle. A seep that ponded last month is already a city of them, and a flexure that dried is a stain the wind will sand off. The larger animals know them as smell — sweet, iron, cold mud — and as the reason a new split in the rock draws a brief, dense gathering before the plain goes empty again.

The grazers are the first bodies you can hold in the eye. They are not many in any one place, and that is how there can be so many of them. Gray-white, long in the face, with a coat that is less wool than a felt of hair and rime, they carry the cold on the outside so they do not have to spend much on the inside. Their legs are made for film-water: the hoof spreads, finds the stone under the slick, and lifts without drama. They do not call. A band talks by the angle of shoulders and by the small decisions of who drops its head and who keeps walking. When a fog-bank detaches and begins its afternoon travel, the nearer bands fall in with it, not out of obedience but because the bank is shade and the shade is where the lichen does not glaze too hard to crop. Between bands there is an hour of nothing, and the nothing is part of the arrangement. A plateau this young cannot feed a crowd. It can feed a budget.

Their young are on their feet before the film has dried off them, and they learn the map as a sequence of smells: the seep that holds, the seep that lies, the copper crust that is only stain, the bone-colored crust that is food. They do not defend territory in any way a valley animal would recognize. There is nothing to wall. A band that meets another band on a lichen pavement will stand, breathe, and part, the way two sheets of water part around a stone. Only at a tarn margin, where the sedge is actual green, is there any stiffness in it — a slowing, a showing of the long face, and then the concession, because fighting spends what the land does not replace.

The waders treat the tarns as one water that has not yet admitted it is many. The same bodies, over and over: pale, quick, general, not yet argued by isolation into kinds. They pave a shallows until the light fails or a shadow crosses the thermal line, and then they are a single rotor in the air, a weather with bones in it. Up close the sound is torn cloth. Far off it is nothing, which is how most things sound here. They feed on what the cold film grows, breed on mud that was stone a handful of cycles ago, and do not keep faith with a particular shore. A tarn that is a tarn this season may be a flexure with a bright skin next, and they will have already left it without mourning. Attachment, in this water, would be a specialization, and specialization is a luxury of landscapes that stay still.

Above them, where fog meets clear air and the boundary holds for an afternoon, the long-winged hunter waits. It does not stoop out of hunger so much as out of geometry. The boundary is a road only it can see, a line of lift, and it rides that line until a wader breaks the rotor or a grazer’s afterbirth salts a seep and the small things rise. It is not a ruler of the plain. The plain is too large to be ruled, and the hunter knows it in the way its wings know distance: by not wasting a beat. When the bank walks on, the hunter walks with it, a dark comma at the edge of brightness, and the tarn it leaves behind goes back to being a dropped piece of sky.

The runner is harder to meet. It uses the herd-paths because the herd-paths are the only softened lines in a country of wet iron, and it is gone between one rise and the next in the way a thought is gone when you try to look at it directly. Low, dark, not large. It takes the straggler, the bird that misjudged the film, the afterbirth, the thing the scavengers would have had by morning. It does not need to be frequent to be part of the order. On a floor this open, a predator that showed itself would be a fact the herds could walk around. So it is a rumor with a stomach, and the herds are shaped by the rumor as much as by the meals: they keep to bands small enough to see out of, and they do not bed in the lee of the same boulder twice.

At the active margins, where the crust has just split and the paste is new, the scavengers and the quiet small life arrive together and do not compete so much as sort. The sweet smell is a bell. What can fly gets there first and leaves the paste to what can crawl. By the time a herd angles over to investigate, the pulse is already thinning, and the rock has gone back to ticking as it cools. Nothing here hoards. Hoarding assumes a tomorrow with the same address, and the plateau keeps changing its address by a finger’s width of sky in a working life.

The people came because the water would not leave and the stone would not stop, and they have stayed by agreeing with both.

They are not tall people, as a habit of the body rather than a law. The wind on open stone takes height as a tax, and the cities, though they rise in decks, rise indoors, where height is a matter of stairs and not of being seen across a march. Out on the Sheet their silhouettes are compact, layered in the fabrics that shed rime the way the tower-frames do, faces narrowed against the level light, hands gloved before the stiffness sets in. Breath shows, and they treat visible breath as ordinary speech’s companion, not as a sign of hardship. Hardship would be a river that cut, a forest that closed, a land that hid its water. This land hides nothing. It only withholds soil, and they have made soil where they needed it — those startling rectangles of turf — and left the rest alone.

Their days are tuned to the cycle more than to the clock, though they keep clocks, because the cities must. Morning is the skin of condensate, bright and almost still, and the low decks are loud with it: pumps, the soft collapse of collected fog into reservoir, the mineral tick of pipes sweating in the same grammar as the rocks. People who work the harvest smell of cold metal and lime. They speak little while the banks are being drunk; the towers are doing the delicate thing, and the human contribution is not to be clever but to be present when a frame ices in a way the frame was not built to ice. Afternoon, when the towers have walked off as weather, is for the upper decks and for the plain. The light arrives pale and useful. Repairs are done in it. Children — and there are children, raised in the vertical of the city so that down means older and dimmer and the smell of service levels — are walked on the outer platforms to learn the difference between a fog that is traveling and a fog that is only sitting in a flexure. It is the first distinction they are taught that is not a word.

Travel is a profession and also a manners. A journey of a day or three across wet stone is not undertaken to conquer the distance; the distance is the country’s way of being alive, and the people who cross it go in small numbers, on the guide-ways when the guide-ways serve, and off them when a new tarn has to be walked to and admitted into the maps. They do not treat the plain as empty, and they do not fill it. A herd at the edge of vision is given the edge. A runner’s path is not a trail to follow out of curiosity. The rules are short because the land has not elaborated: you do not dam what has not found a channel; you harvest what the air returns; you leave the space between bands alone. Someone who broke the last of these would not be punished with a speech about ecology. The abundance would simply fail to be where they had stood, and the next season’s surveyors would write the absence down in the same hand they use for a pier that has lifted out of true.

In the civic halls, where the floors are warm enough that standing still is possible, the talk is about corrections. Whose district gained its finger of sky. Which lattice was retensioned in the night, a few millimeters, so the deck above could go on being a street. The old, who have felt the small adjustment under their feet for decades, are listened to not as priests of the uplift but as instruments that have stayed in calibration. They know which tick in the service levels is the crust and which is only a pipe. They know the sweet smell of a fresh face and do not romanticize it; a fresh face near a pier is a reading, and a reading wants a crew, not a song.

They are quiet in the way the country is quiet — not repressed, unornamented. Sound carries oddly on bare wet stone, or it dies at once, and either way a raised voice is a waste. Their humor is dry and local: the joke about the turf rectangle that looks like a wound; the joke about the bird that tried to specialize and will have to wait a few thousand years for the waters to agree to be separate. They do not tell stories in which the plateau is finished. Finished is what the country at the far edge is practicing, with its small real river and its wind in something taller than sedge, and they go there sometimes to hear the sound change, and come back glad of the hush.

At night the film returns, the stone goes from pale to black, and the cities correct themselves by increments no one feels unless they are trying to. The herds bed where the last light left a dryness. The waders become a muttering in the tarns. The hunter is elsewhere, on a boundary that will not exist again in quite that place. The runner is a rumor with its stomach full. And in the low decks the reservoirs take what the air has agreed, once more, to be. Nothing that lives here is waiting for the land to become a different land. They are the life this one can hold: sparse, general, numerous in the aggregate, exact in the particular, and content — if that is the word for so unemphatic a fitting — to be unfinished at the same rate as the stone.
