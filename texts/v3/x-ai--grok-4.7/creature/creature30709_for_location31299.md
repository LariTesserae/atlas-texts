They are a people still arriving in their own faces.

The oldest of them can remember a shallower mere and a ridge that was only a rumor under the boats, a place you poled across and felt, once, with the end of the pole, a hardness that had not been there the season before. That is as far back as memory goes that anyone trusts. Before that there are stories of walking, of a lower country and a colder rain, but the stories disagree about the direction and about whether anyone meant to stay. What they agree on is the first night the cloud-cap closed and did not open, and the water, which had been a flood, becoming a level. After that the walking stopped. After that there were names for lanes.

They are not tall. The poles are cut to a reach that suits a person whose hands work mostly at the height of the chest or just above the water, and the ladders have rungs set close. A long torso, a short step. Their feet are wide and the skin of the soles has gone the color of the wet stone they trust, thick enough that a fresh edge, the kind that still opens a hand, only writes a white line and does not bleed until later. The hands are the part of them that looks oldest. Mineral water and fiber and the grit of rind leave the knuckles mapped, the nails thick and a little green at the quick, as if the crust that takes a post has begun, very politely, on them too. They trim it. It comes back. Children are born with ordinary nails and ordinary feet, and the place revises them.

Skin, in the tea-colored day, reads as one color until you are close enough to share a platform. Then you see that it is not. Some households carry a dusk in the face, a warm darkness that does not fade in the dim the way a tan would; others are the color of the inner rind, pale green-gold, and these burn if they work the rim where the steam thins and a harder light leaks under the cap. Freckles, where they occur, are not brown. They are the flint-green of a broken crust. People notice and do not remark. A face is a face. What they remark is whether you saw the stone first.

Their hair holds damp and gives it up slowly, so it is almost never loose. It is clubbed, or bound in a wet braid that leaves a dark map down the back of a shirt, or cut to the skull in the stilt quarter where a loose strand is a handhold for the pads. Gray comes early and is not read as age so much as proof of the rim, where the cooler air finds you and the steam beads in the roots. The very old do not look withered. They look condensed. The flesh thins, but the mineral stays, so that a grandmother’s wrist has a weight to it, a grain, and when she dies the people who wash her say the water does not cloud around her the way it clouds around the young. She is already partly the same stuff as the post she tied her boat to.

They speak as if the air were a drum with a skin still on it. Low, and at the front of the mouth, and with the ends of words let go before they can ring. A conversation across two platforms is possible and is considered slightly rude unless you have been invited into it; the preferred distance is an arm and a pole-length, close enough that you can see the other person’s eyes decide. They have more words for the conditions of stone than for the conditions of weather, because the weather does not change and the stone does. A ridge can be weeping, sleeping, shouldering, sour, sweet, or claimed. Sour means the seep has gone sharp and the rind will not take. Sweet means you can taste the water off the face of it and know a garden will hold. Claimed means a stake, or a ladder-scar, or simply that someone has been seen standing on it at the hour when the light is strongest and a shape can be named across the lane.

They do not have a word that means property in the way a lower country might. They have a word that means the morning. To hold the morning of a ridge is to have touched it while it still wept, and to have come back the next day, and the next, until the crust skinned your stake and the stake became part of the ridge’s argument with the water. If you fail to come back, the morning thins. Someone else may take it up. This is the closest thing they have to law, and it is administered by nobody and by everyone, which means it is mostly administered by the women who keep the rind-gardens, because they are on the new stone earliest and they are the ones who will have to eat what comes of a bad claim. The long house hears the quarrels that cannot be dissolved by a season of parallel work. It does not issue judgments so much as it refuses to let a shout become the first shout. A shout, in a town where sound travels farther than sight, is already a kind of weather. The long house exists to keep the weather small.

What they want is not large. A sleeping-loft that does not dip. A lane that stays a lane for the length of a childhood. Enough sweet stone that the garden can be cut and trusted to return. The right to be recognized at the landing without having to say which house. They are ambitious in a narrow register: they want the next ridge, and they want it before the pads close over the approach. They are patient in a way that can look like the absence of want, and is not. A person can spend three years poling past a bulge in the mere, watching it, saying nothing, and then be at the water before full light on the morning it breaches, ladder already lashed, face already set. The neighbors will have known. In a place this quiet, intention has a sound. It sounds like someone not sleeping.

They eat what the rind gives and what the pads give and, rarely, what the mid-water gives up. The fish are not pursued. To chase one is to churn the meal into a blind tunnel and come back with nothing and a fouled lane. Sometimes a fish is simply there, beside the piling, for long enough that a child with a basket and a still hand can close the basket and have a weight in it. This is treated as a visit rather than a catch. The fish is eaten the same day, steamed over the hearth with the sharp leaves that cut the mineral taste, and the bones are put back in the water at the place where the basket closed. People say the bones remember the turn. Whether this is true is not a question they spend breath on. It is a thing that is done, like trimming the green from a nail.

Their days are shaped by the failure of light more than by any clock. Morning is when you can see your own ladder. Full day is when you can see your neighbor’s. The deep quarter never reaches full day; the houses on the longest legs work by touch and by the pale flank of the next platform, and their children navigate by the grain of pilings, which they know the way other children know a path. Evening is the hearth becoming visible through reed, first as a suggestion, then as a small furious clarity. Night is for the mats, which are never quite dry, and for the particular intimacy of hearing the person on the next platform turn over and knowing, from the sound, whether it is the shoulder or the hip, and whether they are awake.

They love, and the love is practical and sudden and long. Courtship is mostly work done in sight of one another without announcement: a stake steadied, a pad cleared from a lane you do not yourself use, a pole left where a shorter reach can find it. People fall into households the way water finds a joint, and the households persist or they do not, and when they do not, the platforms are near enough that separation is a matter of which mat, not which shore. Children belong to the lane as much as to the loft. A child who can pole can eat at any hearth that has cut rind that day, and is expected to, and is expected to carry something back—fiber, a snail for the shell-heap, the news that the far bulge has shoulders. There is very little privacy of information and a great deal of privacy of regard. You may know that your neighbor did not sleep. You do not look at their face in the morning as if you know it.

They are afraid of three things, and they do not rank them.

They are afraid of the deep middle, not as a monster-place but as a room that does not end, where the meal makes every direction the same direction and the warmth does not tell you up from down. No one who has gone under past the pilings and come back has come back with a story that satisfied the people waiting. They have come back with a look. The town does not forbid the dive. It simply receives you afterward as a person who has been somewhere the town is not, and it takes a season for your name to sit properly in a mouth again.

They are afraid of a sour year. A ridge that stops giving. A garden cut to flint and meal and no stem under it. They have stores, but the stores are the living pads and the uncut rind, which is to say they have no stores, only a faith in succession that has not yet been badly wrong. The oldest people remember one season when the seep ran sharp from the rim to the landing and the crust on the posts went white and brittle and fell away in plates. No one starved. Everyone ate snails and the inner stems and something from the mid-water that was not a fish and was not spoken of later by name. The white plates are still in a heap behind the kiln-shelf. Children dare each other to taste one. It tastes like a nail smells.

And they are afraid, quietly, of the cap lifting. They do not say this. The cap is the ceiling of the world, and the world is warm, and the water stays. But there is a story, told badly, about the first night it closed, which means there is a knowledge that it was once open. An open cap would be wind. Wind would be a kind of cold they have no practice in. It would push the mere against one shore and pull it off another, and the mornings would no longer be a matter of eyes and stakes but of which way the water had decided to lean. They have built a town that assumes the lean will not come. Every long leg, every unlashed ladder, every garden hung over the brown, is a vote for the lid. They do not look up often. When they do, it is to check that the leaf-color is still there, uniform, unmoving, the correct dark.

Under all of this, and older than the quarrels about mornings, something else is living, and they have begun, without a festival or a decision, to treat it as a neighbor.

The rind is not a plant in the way the pads are plants. The pads have roots and a season and a failure. The rind thickens. It takes a ridge the way a thought takes a face, from the edges inward, and where it has fed the stone is softer, and where the stone is softer the next rind comes quicker, as if encouraged. On the oldest gardens—the ones held by the same morning for three generations, which is as old as a garden can be here—the turf has a grain you can learn with your palm, and the grain is not the grain of the block beneath. People who cut it say it gives, slightly, before the blade, a hesitation, a gathering. They have started to cut around certain patches. Not from a rule. From the hesitation having been the same hesitation twice.

In the stilt quarter, where the houses stand over water too deep for a pole to find bottom, the pilings crust faster than the pilings at the landing, and the crust climbs. A child drew a face in it once, with a thumbnail, and the face is still there, blunted, a little higher than it was, as if the piling had decided the face belonged and had carried it up as it grew. The child is older now and does not like to be asked about it. The face has no eyes. It has the place where eyes would go, and the place is smoother than the rest, worn by thumbs that were not the child’s. People touch it when they pass, the way they touch a knot they trust.

No one has said that the bowl is waking. That would be a shout of a sentence, and they are not a shouting people. What they have said, in the low voice, at hearths, while the fish-bones go back to the water, is smaller and harder to walk away from. They have said that the sweet stone is sweeter where it has been cut and left and cut again. They have said that a ridge claimed in anger goes sour, and that this has happened often enough to stop being a story. They have said that the knock of stone rising in the night comes, sometimes, after a death, and sometimes after a birth, and that it does not come for everyone, only for those who held a morning long enough that the stake became ridge. They do not agree about what this means. Agreement is not what they are after. They are after the right interval before the next cut, and the right way to stand on new stone so that the weeping stops cleanly and the skin that forms is the green one, not the white.

If you came up through the cloud-lid and found them, you would see a crowded quiet town of short, dense, damp people, poling and not calling, eating green flinty food, sleeping badly and loving practically, afraid of depth and of sourness and of a sky. You would think you had understood who lives there. Stay through a season. Watch who reaches the breach first, and how they put their hand on the stone—not claiming, not yet, only greeting, palm flat, the way you greet a person whose name you almost have. Watch the crust decide whether to take the stake. Watch the face on the piling, eyeless, a little higher than last year, being touched by thumbs that know exactly where the eyes are not.

The town is theirs. The mornings are theirs. But the place is also in the process of becoming someone, and they are the part of it that has learned to wait, and to cut carefully, and to put the bones back, and to speak low enough that whatever is thickening in the dark of the unfinished middle can hear the difference between a call and a noise.
