They do not arrive. They accumulate, the way a film does, until the recess is no longer stone with animals in it but a single thickness of living that uses stone as a skeleton.

Closest to the rock, the sheets. Each one is thinner than a leaf and wider than a hand, and none of them has an underside that is not also someone else’s topside. What looks like an ear is only the habit of the margin: a rim that lifts to drink, then lies flat again so the next bead has somewhere to form. Under the rim there is no mouth in any lasting sense. The slit opens where the film is thick enough to want opening, and closes when the moisture has been taken into the body, which is mostly water already and does not distinguish much between drinking and becoming. Their color is the rock’s color because they are always a little of the rock — grit in the gel, a grain of phonolite caught and never expelled, a darkness borrowed from the wall they cover. If one is peeled away it does not tear so much as forget to be a sheet. The gel sags, strings, and in the string a new edge begins, already drinking, already flat against whatever it touches. They do not breed in any season you could name. They occur. Where the condensation holds longest, the boundary fails, and failure is how the wall gains another tile.

They have no eyes. Light would be a rumor if any of them could receive it, and almost none can. What they have instead is a slow attention to damp: a tightening when the film thins, a slackening when cloud-rain taps the hood and runs down in threads too brief to follow. Neighbor-sense is contact. A sheet knows the sheet beside it because their margins overlap and the overlap has a temperature. That is the whole of society at this layer — warmth shared, moisture contested without contest, a trembling that passes from tile to tile when one of the larger animals draws its flank along the arris and the bell-note stands up in the stone. The trembling is not alarm. It is how the wall hears.

Below them, where the floor’s convection never quite stops, the many-legged ones are denser than the word herd can carry and slower than any herd you have watched. A body is a low oval, dark as wet grain, with legs so numerous and so short that walking looks like a change in the surface of the mass. Each leg ends in a hook fine enough to catch the glassy phonolite and not slip, which is why the floor can tilt in its own small weather — center toward the bowl, edges toward the walls — without anyone falling through anyone else. They do not see the bowl. They lean. Over hours the lean becomes a drift, and the drift is feeding: not on prey, but on what the sheets slough when a margin dies back into gel, and on the pale filaments the larger animals shed when they stroke the arris, and on each other in the mild way of a press that is always a little too close. A body that is finished does not lie there. It softens at the seams, legs drawing in, and the neighbors take the softness as they take everything else — by contact, without hurry, without a hole left in the mass for longer than the next drift can close it.

Their eyes, when they have them, are side-set and nearly blind, good for the flank an inch away and for nothing past the lip. A few have no eyes at all, only a thin line along the carapace that catches the infrequent gleam of a neighbor and answers with a duller one, a syllable passed and spent. You could follow one of those gleams across the floor and lose it in a minute, not because it went far, but because far is not a distance this room contains.

In the air between floor and wall, the rotor. These are the only ones that leave the stone, and they do not leave it by much. Wings are short, membranous, almost the same substance as the sheets — a gel that has learned a beat. They hold position the way a swimmer holds a depth, each stroke a correction against the stroke beside it. Their number is whatever the nave will bear. Add one and the pitch of the hum drops; subtract one and it rises, a tuning the room performs without anyone counting. They feed on what the air itself carries: spores of the wire-moss, flakes of sloughed gel, the finest mist when a cloud snags the spire and the rain comes in under the hood as something too light to fall. A startle, rare, does not scatter them. It lowers them. The rotor sinks a hand’s width, wings nearer to the many-legged mass, and the sound of the room changes key for as long as it takes the pressure to pass. Then they rise again into the same slow swimming. Nothing among them looks toward the lip except by accident of turn, and the accident corrects itself.

The larger ones are few enough to count if you were willing to stand in the press long enough, and you would lose the count when one braced against an arris and another unbraced and the two, for a moment, occupied the same decision. They are heavier than the floor-creatures and older than the sheets, though age here is only thickness and the dullness of a flank that has been drawn along fresh stone often enough to polish itself. A hardened edge runs the length of one side — not a weapon, a tool that the body grew because the mountain offered an edge and the edge answered. They brace, they draw, they shed a filament pale as the starved tuft by the bowl, and the filament drifts down into the convection and is taken. Sometimes a shard, already broken by the ridge’s unfinished split, is crooked in a limb and worked once along a sheet, grooming or being groomed, it is hard to say which, and then dropped. The shard clicks. The click returns from the opposite cheek. That is as close as the place comes to an event.

They are not leaders. The sheets do not orient to them; the rotor does not clear a path; the floor-mass shoves them as it shoves everything, and they accept the shove and pass it on, heavier, so the drift bends a little around their weight and then forgets the bend. What they do is keep the bell voiced. When they tire, the note thins. Another, who has been in the press without urgency, takes the arris. The tone stacks again. Outside, wind crosses an edge that has not been rubbed and sounds like a different instrument entirely — sharper, wasted, gone.

None of them have names for each other, or need them. A sheet is the overlap it shares. A many-legged body is the gap it leaves when it drifts. A flier is a correction in a pitch. A stroker is the next interval of sound. Individuation happens and is unremarkable: a leg drawn back into gel, a wing that beats half a count off and is absorbed by the neighboring beats, a flank worn smoother than its neighbors and therefore louder when it meets the stone, until the smoothness itself is ordinary and the loudness returns to being the condition rather than a signature.

At the lip, the climate ends. Past it the air is too thin to lift a wing and too empty to return a note, and the rare cloud-glow that finds the brink dies an inch inside, spent on the first sheet it touches. Nothing here is curious about that. Curiosity would require a distance, and distance has been crowded out. What they have instead is circulation: water into body into air into film into water; sound into stone into body into sound; a floor that turns and a wall that drinks and a nave that holds its rotor at the depth the room allows. The number of them changes by the occurrence of a new sheet, by the softening of a finished body, by a filament shed and taken, and the change is never felt as loss or gain. The recess stays full to the touch.

If you stayed through a whole turn of the floor and the longer turn of the bowl, you would not see a creature so much as a way of being crowded — patient, wet, ringing — that has not yet needed to become anything more particular than the dark it occupies. The young rock under them is already being taught a duller edge. They will still be here when the teaching is finished, indistinguishable from the lesson, making no mark you could carry away except the sense, afterward, that empty air is a kind of injury, and that a room can be alive without ever having decided to house anyone at all.
