They do not gather. That is the first thing the city makes true of them, and it takes a long time to notice it is a choice rather than an absence.

What lives here moves the way the water does: singly, with long intervals, and with an allegiance to place that is stronger than any allegiance to company. You can cross three plazas and see no one, and then, in the fourth, find a figure standing at the lip of a cistern with both hands in the warm film, not drinking, only holding. They stay like that until the water decides to rise. When it does, they lift their hands and let the column take what it wants off their skin — pollen, dust, the faint metallic ticking that collects in the crooks of fingers — and they do not flinch. They have done this often enough that their forearms carry a pale mapping, a stain the color of the haze, brighter where the water has passed most often.

Their bodies are not of one make. Some are close to what you would call human, if you met them in better light and they stood still long enough for the lag of their shadows to catch up. Others have been revised by the city, or have revised themselves to meet it, until the distinction no longer organizes anything. A shoulder may carry the same fine lattice as a wall, grown out of the skin in a slow season and left there, brass-pale, breathing on its own half-second behind the breath. A spine may show, under thin cloth or no cloth, the faint offset of the towers: the upper vertebrae a hand’s width out of true with the lower, not an injury, a habit of standing in two plans at once. Eyes are various. Some are ordinary and tired. Some do not blink in the haze, because the haze does the work of blinking for them, a constant soft film. A few have no eyes you can find, and navigate by the phosphor of the walls, turning their faces toward brightness the way a plant turns, unhurried, accurate.

They dress, when they dress, in what the moss and the machines between them will give up. Cloth here is closer to a membrane: thin, slightly warm, patterned by the gridded air if its wearer has stood too long in a counted lane. The pattern is not decoration. It is a record. Someone who has spent a season near the embankments will wear, across the chest, a faint tick of lines that match the clicks of the cooling crust. Someone who tends a solitary tree will have the tree’s shade bruised into the shoulders, a darker patch that lightens only when they leave the canopy for longer than the moss remembers. They do not wash these off. Washing, in a city where water rises of its own accord, is not a private act, and they have learned to let the city keep its notes.

Speech is rare because it travels. A sentence begun in one plaza arrives, thinned but intact, at the next tower, and the walls drink it on the way, so that what remains for the listener is both the words and the residue of having been tasted. They prefer, therefore, to speak indoors, where the lattices close the sound into a room and return it softened, or not to speak at all. Much of what passes between them passes by standing. Two of them will occupy the same wide avenue at a distance that would be loneliness anywhere else, and over an hour they will drift, not toward each other exactly, but into a shared correction — both of them turning when a facade swells, both of them lifting a foot when the street draws a crack shut under them. When they finally come near enough to touch, the touch is brief and specific: a palm to the lattice at a shoulder, checking that it still breathes a half-second late; fingers to the mapped stain on a wrist, reading how often the cistern has risen this month. Then they part, and the avenue is wide again.

They have work, though it does not look like labor until you stay. One keeps the unfinished towers honest. She walks the open frames at dusk-that-will-not-end, a coil of vine-width cord over one arm, and where a vine has climbed past its instructed stop she pinches it back with a nail gone dark as sinter. She does not cut. Cutting is too final for a place whose stones still remember being liquid. She pinches, and the vine stalls, and by morning it has thickened at the pinch into a node that will not try the height again. Her hands are noded the same way, at every knuckle, from years of the same small refusal. She talks to the frames sometimes, indoors in her voice, and the frames tick back, metallic, almost courteous. She is not sure the ticking is answer. She continues as if it were, because the alternative — a city that only receives — unsettles her more than a city that might be rude.

Another lives at the edge, where the vegetation thins to islands. He is older than the basin in the way a scar is older than the skin it marks: he was here when the ground was still negotiating, and some of that negotiation stayed in him. His feet are black glass at the soles, not worn that way but set, and when he walks the flat of sinter the crust clicks differently under him, a counter-rhythm, as if it recognizes a prior argument. He does not tend anything. He listens for the stands of trees going wrong — a canopy pouring shade that does not lift, a bruise of attention that does not fade after the bird has passed. When he finds one, he stands inside it until the shade remembers to move. It can take a day. He comes back with pollen oiling his wrists and a quiet that is heavier than the city’s ordinary quiet, and he sleeps against a breathing wall until the wall’s swell matches his ribs. People leave him water in a bowl that will not rise. He has never asked them to. They do it because his glass feet leave prints that steam for an hour, and a city this young has so few lasting marks that a print feels like a duty to answer.

There are children, though not many, and they are the least settled of anyone, which is saying something. They are born with the lag already in them: a reach that arrives at the object a finger’s breadth late, a laugh that lands after the face has gone serious again. The city does not correct this. The adults do not either. A child learns the avenues by getting lost in them on purpose, walking until the phosphor stops brightening, then standing still until it forgives them and brightens again. They collect the dust the grids shed and arrange it on windowsills in the patterns it arrived in, refusing to choose a new one. They are the only ones who run. Running looks wrong here — the shadows can’t keep up, and for a moment a child is ahead of their own darkness, a small bright error in the tea-colored light. The adults watch this without calling them back. It is the one extravagance the basin allows, and it ends on its own, when the child tires and the shadow arrives, soft, slightly apologetic, and fits itself to their heels.

Not everything that lives here is person-shaped. The bird that reconfigures rather than flaps has a perch it returns to, a slag-dark branch above an unnamed plaza, and it is old enough that the moss beneath has learned its shadow by heart: the bruise appears now a moment before the bird does, anticipation worn into the plants. It does not sing. It ticks, once, when it lands, and the canopy ticks back, and that is the whole conversation, repeated at intervals no one has timed because timing it would require speaking of it. In the cisterns there are things that are not fish. They are flatter, and they hold to the underside of the oil-bright film, and when the water rises they rise inside it, invisible, distributed through the column like a thought the city is having about thirst. They drink nothing. They seem to be how the water remembers which basin it belongs to when it is taken into a structure and brought back changed.

And there is the grid, which the people say is counting, and which may be the oldest resident, if a resident can be made of organized warmth. It does not have a body. It has lanes, and a preference. It settles its dust more thickly on those who stand still, and it withholds dust from those who are about to leave a district, sometimes a full day before they know they are leaving. The keeper of the towers trusts this. The man with glass feet ignores it, and the dust, offended or indifferent, will not settle on his black soles at all. Children try to draw in it with their hands and find their hands guided a fraction toward a pattern they did not intend. They argue about whether that is teaching or theft. The argument cannot be finished outdoors; the walls would drink it and return it sweeter than it was, and they want it sharp.

What they share, all of them, person and bird and film-held flatness and the warmth that counts, is a patience tuned to a ground that has not eroded into kindness. They do not expect the edges to soften. They grow around the geometry they were given, the way the city grows around its shelves of sinter, and they leave the cuts exposed. A death, when it happens, is quiet and local. The body is taken to the black flat beyond the last rumor of brass light, and left where the crust is still negotiating. By morning the moss has not reached it — the moss will not cross that flat — but the ground has begun, very slowly, to remember the shape, clicking around it in a new rhythm, taking it into the argument. No one makes a speech. Someone may stand at the last cistern before the embankment and hold their hands in the film until the water rises and takes the day’s dust. Then they walk back, and the phosphor brightens for them, and dims, and the avenue is wide enough to hold the weather of a single person crossing it.

At night — though night is only a deeper grade of the same gilded refusal — they go into the structures that breathe, and the facades swell, and the moisture lifts off in sheets and falls on empty streets. Inside, behind lattices that tick when spoken to, they lie down in the residual heat and let their lags catch up to them. Shadows arrive. Offsets ease by a fraction. The mapped stains on their wrists fade, not gone, only resting, the way a seam in the street dulls after it has finished gleaming. Somewhere at the edge a glass sole prints the sinter and steams. Somewhere a vine considers a height and decides, at the node, against it. The city holds them without gathering them, which is the only hospitality it has learned, and they answer it by staying, one by one, in a basin that has not yet decided what it is, and by not deciding either, and by being, in that undecided warmth, precisely themselves.
