They do not arrive. That is the first correction the eye has to make, and it takes longer than it should, because everything about a colony suggests a landing, a return, a day that brought them and will take them off again. Nothing here is returning. The birds on the lower faces have the posture of animals that finished arriving so long ago the word no longer applies, and the higher dark has the same finished look, only heavier, and the insects between them are not a passage across the basin but a standing thickness, the way fog is a standing thickness and not a crowd of drops that happen to be here this hour.

Down on the ledges, close enough that a thrown stone would reach them and is never thrown, they are smaller than the sound they make. A body the length of a hand, gray across the back with a darker bar at the wing, the underside the color of the unweathered fracture-faces, so that when one turns on a ledge it flashes the same pale the rock flashes and for a moment is not there. The bill is short and slightly parted at rest. The eye is the only definite point, dark and quick, aimed outward along the wall rather than down into the basin, because what they watch is not the ground and not the sky. They watch the layer. A head snaps, a wing flicks half-open and shut, and the bird has left the ledge by the width of itself and come back with something held crosswise, already being turned, already gone down the throat, and the feet have not, strictly speaking, released the stone. Many of them feed that way for a long time: not hunting, not even flying, only intercepting what the air brings within a body's reach, the way a man in a current might drink without lifting his feet.

Where the ledge is wide enough for two ranks, the inner rank faces the rock and the outer faces the air, and they pass nothing between them. There is no feeding of another adult, no ceremony of offering. The only transfer is downward, into the cracks, and it is brief. In the pockets behind the outer rank — the places the collapse left as a lip over a darkness the width of two fingers — a second, smaller sound lives under the roar, a wheeze and a tick, and if you wait at one pocket the parent returns not from a journey but from the ledge immediately above, leans in, and the wheeze stops for the length of a swallow. Then the parent is back in the rank, bill clean, and the pocket resumes. The young do not show themselves. They are a sound and a smell of warm fish-and-insect sourness and a slight, continual adjustment of small bodies against stone. When one of them is ready, it is not announced. A bird the same color as the rest appears one day on the lip of a pocket, smaller only in the wing, and by the time you have looked away and back it has taken a place in the rank and is already flicking and taking and swallowing, and the pocket behind it has gone quiet or has not. The count on the face does not record the difference.

They do not all do this at once, and that is the part that will not settle into a season. On any face, in any hour of the unchanging dim, some pockets are loud and some are empty and some hold an egg that is not visible, only inferred from a bird that sits where the others stand, lower on its legs, bill tucked, enduring the shoulders of the rank on either side. Incubation and feeding and the first standing-up of a new bird are all happening on the same wall in the same minute, and have been, and will be. There is no week in which the faces are only eggs, no week in which they are only grown birds. The colony breeds the way the seep wets the stone: not in pulses you can count, but as a rate. A bird dies on the ledge — you find it by the slight wrongness, the head dropped too far, the wings half-open in a way no living one holds — and the neighbors do not move aside for it. They stand against it until it loosens and goes down the face in a dry slide to the scree, and the gap it leaves is occupied before the body has finished its fall. From the rim, the sheet does not change density. It is not a population rising and falling. It is a surface, like the damp, continually renewed at the same thickness.

Higher, where the gray fails and the wall becomes a guess, the other hanging has a different grammar of the body. These are smaller still, and they do not stand. They suspend. A foot, or the set of feet, takes the roughness of a joint and the body hangs below it, wings closed not folded-to-the-side but wrapped, a taper, so that a settled reach of wall looks furred, or scaled, or grown over with some tight dark fruit. The eyes, when a portion wakes, take what light there is and give it back too well; a ripple of the nap is also a ripple of small dull reds that go out as the bodies settle again. They are not asleep in any ordinary rotation. Touch one idea of them and the wrong picture comes — dusk, a cave mouth, a single exodus — and the place will not support it, because there is no hour that is theirs and no hour that belongs to the birds below. At all times some fraction is down and some fraction is up. The fraction that is up moves inside the insect layer and above it, in the volume the birds do not bother to use, and it moves like a single cloth being shaken out: not individuals choosing, but a looseness traveling through the mass, a billow, a reseating. You can watch one patch of higher wall for as long as you like and the number hanging there breathes — thins by a few hundred, thickens by the same — and never empties. They are relieving one another the way a current relieves a pool, continuously, without a shift-change anyone could mark.

What they take from the layer is not what the birds take. The birds take the larger, the wing-bearing, the ones that tick against stone and veer. The hanging ones take the fine fraction, the drift that is almost not an animal, the motes that make the air between the ledges look smoked when a shaft from a rim-notch crosses it. A billow passes through that smoke and the smoke is less, locally, for a moment, and then the drift closes. Neither kind of feeding dents the layer. The layer is fed from below, from the wet faces and the pool margins and the algal film, and it is fed at the rate it is eaten, and it hangs at its height because that is where the rising damp from the basins cools and where the day's leaked warmth, such as it is, gives out. It is not a swarm with an intention toward anything outside itself. It is a ceiling the basins have grown, living, a few wings thick, and both of the wall-peoples crop it the way weather crops a field that does not know it is a field.

Under it, the pools look simpler and are not. Wade to the edge of one — the water is ankle-cold, and the mineral rim takes weight without crumbling — and the grain of the water resolves into bodies, each no longer than a finger, blunt-headed, the color of the tea they swim in, so that a shoal at rest is only a darker thought in the pool and a shoal startled is a pale flash of bellies turning all at once. They do not face one way. They hold, in a loose ball or a loose sheet near the bottom, and the holding is the behavior. There is nothing in a pool to journey toward. No inlet, no outlet, no shadow of a channel to the next low place; the basins are bowls, and each pool is a bowl inside the bowl, finished, its shoreline a crust. The fish in it have not come from the next pool over, and the fish in that one will not come here. Bird-feet may have carried eggs in the first days, or the water they live in may be older than the roof that fell, a seep-system that was one water when it was dark and is many waters now that the dark has a dim above it. It does not matter to them. Each pool is the entire country of its fish, and they treat it that way: the ball contracts when a wing-shadow crosses, spreads when the shadow has gone, rises in a fine stipple to the surface when the insect-rain is heavy and the drowned are drifting down, and sinks again to the holding-depth, which is not the bottom and not the top but a layer they prefer, as the insects prefer theirs and the birds theirs and the hanging ones theirs. The country is stratified, and almost nobody crosses.

Breeding, among the fish, is another rate rather than an event. In any pool, at any time, some of the bodies are slightly thicker, and some carry a faint roughness along the belly where they have pressed eggs or milt against the algal film on a sunken flake of roof-stone, and some of the flakes, lifted, show a scatter of eggs like grit that has decided to be transparent. The eggs are not guarded. The adults do not pair. A thickness of them simply uses the same flake for an hour and then does not, and the flake hatches on whatever schedule the cool water allows, which is slow, and the hatchlings are eaten by the same shoal that made them, except for the number the shoal does not quite manage, which is the number required to keep the pool granular and no more. Carry a count, if you could count, from one month into another and the pool will hand it back to you. Drain it in the mind — imagine the seep failing, the crust widening, the tea going shallow — and you can see that the number would fall and the fish would be done, because there is nowhere for them to go and nothing in their behavior that has ever needed elsewhere. They are a solution the water is holding at saturation. While the seep holds, they hold.

The one life that moves as a single intention, rather than as a layer or a rate, is the one you almost do not get. It is on the floor, and the floor is the part of the country everyone agrees is empty. It isn't. Among the angular scree at the wall-foot, and in the joints of the big roof-blocks, something about the size of a clenched hand lives in the interstices and is the color of the dust in them, a gray that is slightly warm, slightly pink in the thin light, so that it vanishes not by contrast but by agreement with the stone. It has a low run, belly close, and it stops more than it moves. When it stops it is a stone. When it moves it is a stone that has remembered a direction. It does not hunt the walls and it does not swim. It works the margin the others create and then abandon: the insect that has fallen and not been taken, the bird that has slid to the base and been opened by smaller beaks and then left, the fish a wing knocked out of a pool onto the crust, still flexing. It is not a cleaner so much as the last unevenness in an otherwise exact budget. The walls and the layer and the pools balance to the number. This one lives on the remainder, the spills, and because the remainder is small and scattered it cannot be numerous, and because it cannot be numerous you will see one, and then a long time of scree, and then another, and you will never see two together.

It is the only inhabitant that seems to notice you as a fact rather than as a shadow. The birds watch the layer. The hanging ones billow and reseat. The fish contract at the idea of a wing. The small runner, when you have been still long enough that the roar has become the neutral condition and your own breathing has joined it, will come out to the edge of a block and orient toward you and stay, head slightly lifted, for a count of breaths you can feel in your own chest. It is not threatening and it is not tame. It is accounting for a new remainder. Then it is a stone again, and then it is in a joint, and the joint is one of thousands, and the floor is empty in the way it was empty before, which is to say it is not.

No one of these is the inhabitant, and there is no further animal behind them, no large shape the roar has been hiding. The country does not have a secret of that kind. What lives here is the fit among them: the layer cropped from two heights and replenished from the wet, the sheets on the faces held at a thickness by a breeding that never starts or stops, the pools saturated and sealed, the runner taking the spill that keeps the rest from having to be wasteful. They do not have a name for the arrangement and they do not maintain it. It maintains because the inputs are steady — dim, cool, seep, drip — and because nothing has yet had time, or need, to become anything else. A bird on a ledge, flicking out and back, is not waiting for the season to turn. There is no turn coming. It will flick and take and swallow, and be replaced by a bird that does the same, and the sheet will hold, and the nap above will breathe, and the tea-colored pools will keep their grain, for as long as the rim continues to leak the same small day onto a floor that is still, after all this life, only barely beginning to be worn.
