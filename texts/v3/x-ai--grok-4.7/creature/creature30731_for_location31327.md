The place does not keep a watch. It keeps a cycle, and the cycle has a body that fits inside it the way a tooth fits a gear already turning.

What lives here is not large. Largeness would be a kind of boast, and the country has no audience for boasts. It stands perhaps as high as your knee when it is up on the seep-margins, and lower when it is moving, which is most of the time — a compressed animal, barrel of rib under a coat that is not fur in any soft sense but a dense, oiled nap, dark as wet slate, beaded always with the same film that beads the stone. The water does not soak it. The water sits on it and runs off along channels the nap itself combs, so the animal is dry at the skin and soaked at the surface, a condition it has never known to be otherwise. Its heat stays in. You would not feel it if you put a hand flat to the flank. The cold would answer first.

The feet are the part of it that the mountain has worked on longest. They are not hooves and not quite paws: a splayed, low-clearance set of four, each toe edged with a horn that has grown, been abraded to the new mineral, and grown again, so the edge is always fresh and always already dulled. It does not grip rock. It reads rock. The leading edge of each foot is so densely nerved that the animal knows the temperature of the pavement a step before it commits weight — freeze ahead, seep to the left, a plate of yesterday's precipitate gone soft where a vent threaded warmer water through in the night. It corrects without appearing to decide. Watching a band of them cross a basin sheet, you see no stumbling, no testing step. The line flows. Individuals diverge by a hand's breadth and rejoin, each one tracking a slightly different reading of the same ground, the way separate instruments in one room report the same weather and none of them agrees exactly.

They do not cluster. This is the fact that takes longest to believe, because your eye wants herd, wants the comfort of massed bodies and a shared direction, and for a moment, when the cloud lifts and shows you the stippling across a slope, you think you have it. Then you hold still and count the distances. Each animal keeps a gap. Not a wide gap — a body's length, sometimes two — but a gap maintained with the same unthinking constancy as the footing. They graze the wet line as a thread, never as a knot. Where the seep-margin narrows to a single passable ledge, the thread thins to single file and the gaps compress to inches, and still no flank touches another flank. Touch, here, is an event. It has a cause. It does not happen by drift.

The cause is usually the young.

A juvenile does not keep the gap, because a juvenile has not yet learned the ground well enough to afford it. It travels inside the space of an adult, offset half a step behind and to the downslope side, where the adult's body breaks the wind and the adult's reading of the pavement is close enough to borrow. The borrowing is literal. The juvenile's horn-edges are still growing in their first season; they skate where the adult holds. So the juvenile watches the adult's feet — not the adult, the feet — and places its own a fraction of a beat later, into the slot of melt the adult has already tested and left. This is the whole of the rearing that happens. There is no den, no cache, no place returned to. The range is too new to have places. What the adult provides is a moving correctness, a stretch of ground pre-read, and the juvenile lives inside that stretch until its own nerves finish wiring and it drifts, over days, out to the edge of the gap and then across it, and is simply, without ceremony, one more interval in the thread.

You will not see this handoff. It does not announce itself. One morning the offset shape is there; some morning later the count of gaps has increased by one and no one could say which body is the new one. The band does not mark it. Marking would require a memory of number, and number is not a thing they carry. They carry spacing. An absence in the thread closes from both sides within minutes, the way water closes where a stone is lifted out — not from grief, from the rule that the gap is a gap and a missing body is only a gap that has briefly become two. If you took one, the thread would be whole again before you had crossed the basin. This is not cruelty. Cruelty needs an alternative. There is no surplus of warmth here to spend on the already-gone.

The one departure from this economy is narrow and total, and it is the reason touch exists at all. When a juvenile skates — when the borrowed reading fails, when a plate that held at dawn has gone to paste by the hour the thread reaches it, and the small body goes down and does not correct — the nearest adult stops. Only the nearest. The thread on either side continues, the gaps sealing around the interruption as if it were ordinary terrain. But the one adult drops out of line, turns, and comes back the two or three lengths, and then it does something no other minute of its life requires: it puts its weight against the juvenile. Shoulder to rib, the oiled nap meeting the oiled nap, and it braces. Not a nudge, not a gesture toward standing — a brace, a transfer of mass, holding the lighter body upright and still while the feet re-find the horn-edge purchase and the nerves re-sample the ground. It holds until the juvenile is reading again. Then it withdraws, and the gap reopens, and both bodies are in the thread, and if you had blinked through it you would have seen nothing but a knot in the line that resolved.

I have come to think the holding is not instruction. Nothing is taught in it; the juvenile already knows what a foot is for. What passes in the contact is heat, the real and otherwise unreachable heat under the coat, let out for the length of time it takes a small body to stop spending its own on panic. A minute, maybe less. In an air this cold and this wet, a minute of borrowed heat is a resource as concrete as the seep itself. The adult pays it. The adult is large enough, just, to have a minute to spare. It does not pay a second one. If the juvenile is past the point where a minute restores it — if the cold has already gone in, if a leg is wrong — the adult braces once, feels the answer, and steps off, and the gap closes, and the thread is in the next drainage by the time the basin sheet has plated over whatever was left. I do not think the adult counts this as a different act from the bracing that works. Both are the same expenditure, judged at the skin, completed when the expenditure is spent.

They feed with their heads down and to the side, never straight along the line of travel, so that one eye stays on the footing and the mouth works the margin. The food is the mat of dark green that plugs a seep and nowhere else, and the small life that hatches from it: a grazing that is half plant and half the smoke of larvae rising through it, taken in the same pull, chewed on the move. They do not crop a patch down. The thread never stops long enough. A mouthful, a correction of course, a mouthful — the green is left rooted, and by the time the band has circled through whatever basins the day's vents have made generous and returned, the mat has closed the gap the mouthful made. The country and the animal are matched in tempo. Neither hurries the other, because neither can. The deposition that plates a footprint by evening plates a grazed stem over too, and the animal that stayed to finish a patch would be standing on stone by dusk and on ice by dark, eating nothing, having misunderstood the rate.

The birds they share the hour with and nothing else. When the hatch lifts off a terrace in its brief smoke, the thread does not look up. The birds fold down into it, the soft percussive rain-on-tin sound passes over, and the animals' ears — small, set low, buried enough in the nap to be only a thicker place in the outline — track the sound without the head tracking it. They are accounting for a movement in the air above their food, not for company. Occasionally a bird comes low enough that its wingbeat fogs the film on an animal's back, and the animal's skin shivers once, the way it shivers at a change in the wind, and that is the whole of the encounter. No predation runs between them. The birds take the hatch in the air; the animals take it at the mat; the remainder settles back to the seep and is the next hour's hatch. Three mouths, one resource, and the resource is not scarce enough, and the mouths not concentrated enough, for any of them to have needed a strategy about the others. They occupy different heights of the same thin fact.

What the animals do have a strategy for, if a thing done without deliberation can be called that, is the sound of the mountain's machinery. The ticking under the hand — the coupling of pressure against a mineral flap, the idling-engine hush of a vent about to exhale — is information they weigh constantly, the way they weigh pavement temperature, and they weigh it for the same reason. A vent that is about to shift its mouth is a vent that is about to rewrite the seep the thread is grazing. The exhale that follows can be scalding at the stone and merely warm a length away, but the rewrite is the danger: the margin they are eating moves, sometimes by a few feet, sometimes out from under them, and a foot placed on the old line finds polished new precipitate with no nap to hold the horn-edge. So the thread listens ahead of itself. You can see it in the spacing. Where the ticking thickens — several vents out of phase, their knocks starting to crowd — the gaps open slightly, the line of travel drifts upslope off the wettest line, and the animals ride the flank of the event instead of its center. They do not flee. Fleeing would cost heat and would abandon the food. They adjust by the width of the uncertainty, and when the exhale comes and the white plume climbs and joins the general mist, they are already on the pavement the plume will not reach, heads down, taking the mouthful the new margin offers at its edge. The ones who read it late are the juveniles, and this is the other moment the brace is for — a small body caught a step inside the rewrite, flinching from a warmth that is not yet pain, held still by a heavier flank until the plume thins and the footing is legible again.

In the dimming, which is the only night the cloud permits, they do not bed. There is no bedding ground that would be the same ground by morning. They slow. The thread's pace drops until it is barely a walk, and the animals stand in it, heads lowered not to graze but to set the horn-edges and lock the knees, and they sleep in the line the way they walk in the line — spaced, upright, one eye's worth of attention still assigned to the ticking. A sleeper that hears the rhythm shift wakes into motion already, no transitional staggering, the gap closing as the body ahead of it moves. From across a basin, in the last gray, the thread looks like a ridge of the mountain itself, one more low terrace, and only the thin steam of their collected breath, rising and not dispersing in the saturated air, marks it as occupied. By the time the light is gone entirely the breath is invisible too, and the range is a range: raw stone, wet film, a ticking under the hand, and somewhere in it a warmth distributed so evenly and so thinly that it does not register as life at all until morning, when the cloud lifts half a hand and the stippling is on a different slope, having traveled while there was nothing to see it travel.

I cannot tell you how long one of them lives. The country offers no record and the animals keep none — no scar that persists in a coat this dense, no territory held across seasons to age a holder in. They are the size they are, or they are juvenile and smaller, and then they are not there, and the thread has closed. What I can tell you is that the same body that will not spend a second minute on the one past saving will spend the one minute, every time, without fail, on the one a minute can bring back — and that this is not tenderness and not its absence. It is a precise animal living in a precise place, matched to a process running at scale, spending exactly what the process allows and not a breath more. The mountain manufactures itself. The thread manufactures its next step. Between them there is no remainder, and no shortage either, only the gap, maintained, and the occasional brief warmth passed across it, and the sound of water becoming stone.
