They are people, first, and only afterward anything else you might want to call them. You see that in the way they stand aside on the roads: a small adjustment of the shoulders, the cart given the center, no ceremony in it. Faces are narrow from the dryness, not gaunt. The skin takes the mineral the way the stems do. At the temples and along the wrists of the older ones there is a faint crust, pale as flour, which they brush off in the mornings and which returns by the amber hour. It does not hurt them. Children are born without it and acquire it the way other children acquire freckles, after a few seasons of the surge. Their mouths are usually closed. Speech is a near thing here, done at arm's length, and they save it.

They call themselves by work before they call themselves by house. A plater, a parer, a shaft-hand, a paint-walker. Houses exist — platforms grown into the trunks, rooms cut and held — but a house is where you sleep and where your tools are racked, and the name that travels is the name of what you keep true. When two of them meet on an empty avenue they do not ask after health. They ask what moved. Which door failed in the surge. Which tendon in the mills drew short. Which beast refused the road. News is maintenance.

Their hands are the first thing that stops looking like hands. The parers' fingers are nicked white at the pads from the heated irons, the nails kept shorter than the quick. Shaft-hands have wrists thickened on one side from bracing against the curd while the threads come. Children who will be platers already stand with their elbows slightly out, the habit of not touching a skinned part until the chamber has finished with it. None of this is displayed. They do not think of themselves as marked. A visitor's soft, unmineraled skin looks to them unfinished, the way an unpainted wall looks unfinished: not wrong, only not yet in the plan.

They dress in pressed fiber, layered, the outer coat sacrificial the way the road's surface is. Salt eats cloth that is not replaced, so the coats are grown to a pattern and peeled, and a good coat is one you can still see the leaf-vein in, pale, almost the color of the floor. Under it, against the skin, something softer, rasped thin, changed when it dusts. They do not wear ornaments that water would have made meaningful — no beads that need stringing against sweat, no dyes that need a wet bath. What shines on them is plated: a clasp, a knife-spine, the caps on the irons, a filament-pin at the collar that tells the workshops you are allowed to draw from the vine. Light, on a person, means permission.

They eat little, and what they eat is not what you would dry. The grazers' tracks are a crop. The pale flour from a cut stem, caught before the threads drink it, is food if it is taken at the right hour of the surge and not later, when it goes bitter. Birds are snared at the shift-change and stewed in sealed leaf-pots with mineral until the flesh loosens; there is no broth worth speaking of, only a paste. The cart-beasts are not eaten. A city that lets its roads be rasped does not spend the raspers. There are fungi, if that is the word, that fruit only on the boundary paint when it has been laid too thick — a mistake the paint-walkers police, and also a delicacy, so the policing is imperfect, and everyone knows which walker can be asked. Thirst is managed, not quenched. They sip condensed vapor from cups grown as blisters on a stem set aside for it, a few mouthfuls at a time, and they do not speak of water as a lost thing. Most of them have never seen it. The word, when it is used, means a failure: a stem that weeps instead of dusting, a sickness, something to be painted out.

They keep time by the shafts and by the surge, which is a coarser clock. The day is the color of the light coming down: white, then amber, then the filament-orange taking over in the halls while the shafts go to a gray that is not quite night. Sleep is short and taken in the gray, often in the workshops if a plating is mid-cycle, because a chamber left sealed through the surge will split its own door. Children learn to wake at the first creak of a wall lengthening. That creak is the nursery sound. Infants are carried against the chest in a sling of the soft inner fiber, and they grow up knowing the tick of the mills before they know a lullaby. There are songs. They are counting songs: numbers of teeth on a gear, numbers of pores to open in a roof, the sequence of clicks on the vine-wire. A person humming on a road is usually working a sum.

Families are porous. Because the plan has to be restored after every surge, labor moves, and children move with it, attached less to a hearth than to a crew. A shaft-hand's child may sleep three nights in a painting crew's platform if the curd is rising fast in the lower level and the shafts need every wrist. Kin is whoever last showed you a trick that kept a door in true. They are not cold about it. You see a woman stop a cart with two fingers, lift a sleeping child down from the frame where someone else left it, and carry it the rest of the avenue without asking whose it is, because on that road, at that hour, it is hers. They touch easily and briefly: a palm to a forearm, a knuckle to a knuckle, checking heat, checking crust, the way they check a stem. Affection looks like inspection. It is not lesser for that.

They have few dead, and the dead do not stay. Nothing rots, so a body is a problem of the same order as an off-plan wall. The thread-fine roots that will not cross living tissue will take a body if it is painted at the edges to tell them the border of the task, and in three days there is a seam, and in six the floor is honest again. They do this quickly and without an audience. What they keep is a plated clasp, or a knife, passed to the person who needs the tool. Names are reused within a generation. You can be called by someone who is also the floor you walk on, and they do not find this morbid. They find it accurate. The city is the handwriting, they say, and handwriting includes the hand.

Their knowledge is practical and oddly deep. They understand the vapor's cycle better than they understand their own grandparents' faces, because the grandparents are seams, and the vapor is still here, still coming up richer every few days from the rock that is not yet rock. They know which trunks will throw a tendon straight enough for a mill and which will kink and have to be cut back. They know the birds' shift to the minute and schedule the opening of roof-pores around it, so the flyers sieve the dust outside and not in the halls. They know the cart-beasts will not step onto unfinished weave, and they use that refusal as a survey: if a beast baulks, the road is lying about being finished, and someone is sent with paint before a cart breaks an axle in a soft spot. Superstition, where it exists, is about the curd. No one likes to kneel at a fresh shaft with a bare wrist in it longer than the work requires. They will not say what they think is down there. They say the roots know, and the roots are still deciding, and it is rude to interrupt a decision with a hand.

There are disagreements, and they are slow. A low-density city with bad sound has no mobs. Arguments happen between two people standing close, then between those two and a third summoned by vine-click, then, if it will not settle, at a platform where the light is even and the wall at their backs can be trusted not to grow across the talk. The usual quarrel is about the plan: whether a new avenue should be painted before the next surge or after, whether a mill's tendon should be cut and regrown or braced for another cycle. They are capable of anger. It shows as precision. A furious parer trims a door until the door is perfect and the person who wanted it wider has to live with perfect. Grudges last as long as a bad splice lasts, which is until the surge, unless someone has painted them in. A few old wrongs are painted — a line of boundary on a trunk that everyone walks around, a room no one opens. They do not explain these to children. Children learn the long way, by putting a hand on the paint and feeling the wall decline to become more of itself there.

They are not many. From the high spine at amber hour you could count the moving ones if you cared to: crews like loose stitches on the roads, a thicker knot at each mill, a scatter of small figures on the platforms, and then long stretches where the only life is the life that does not pay tax to the plan — the grazers, the birds, the moth-mice higher in the light. The people know those others by their hours and do not romanticize them. A silverfish the size of a hand is a colleague in the salt and a nuisance in a workshop. The birds are tolerated because they follow the dust and because their nests, wedged in window-ribs, tell you if a rib is loosening: a bird will not sit a rib that will grow shut. The cart-beasts have names, short ones, and preferences. A beast that has learned a particular mill's knock will not pull for another, and the handlers accept this as a fact about gears, not about souls.

What they want, as far as a stranger can tell, is for the presentable stage to hold through the next surge. Not forever. They do not talk about forever. Forever is a wet idea, something that would require a finished world. They talk about the gray hours coming unspoiled, the doors still opening, the lower level — visible through the lace of the ceiling, younger, clumsier — not dropping a shaft into their roads. They send people down those shafts. The ones who go come back dusted finer, quieter, with measurements. Sometimes they stay down, and then they are of the lower city, and the clicks on the vine change address, and that is not death. That is another crew.

Watch one of them at the edge of the plan, where the street is only a suggestion. She is a paint-walker, coat pale, crust at the wrist, a bladder of the root-chemical at her hip. She kneels. She listens to the creak. She lays a line the width of a finger, and the weave, which was about to become a lump, considers itself and becomes a road instead, or the beginning of one. She does not smile. She checks the line with a knuckle, the way she would check a child for heat, and stands, and walks back toward the tick of the mills, already composing the click she will send: eastern edge, held, send a beast in the morning to see if it agrees.

That is what lives here. A people matched to a city that is still arriving — spare, dry, exact, fond in the manner of maintenance, uninterested in legacy, very interested in whether the door will open when the light comes white again. Around them, under them, above them through the lace, the other lives keep their shifts. None of them raise their voices. The place is new, and it is already fully occupied.
