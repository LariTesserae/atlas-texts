They do not arrive. That is the first correction the water makes. Nothing here has ever come from outside in any way that still matters. What lives in the basin was poured with the first courses, or grew out of the grit the courses shed, or both, and the distinction has been worn down to the same smoothness as the posts.

Near the surface the wheel is not a school and not a ceremony. It is a single thickness of bodies, clay-colored, each no longer than a finger, flattened as if the film of light had pressed them. They have no eyes you can find. Where a head might be there is only a blunt narrowing, and a mouth that opens on the underside, always, against the dark rather than the stain of day. They feed on the faintest fall of mineral from above—the polish the air takes off the rim and returns as dust that the surface holds for a while and then lets go. Their turning is not swimming so much as a refusal to settle. If one dies it does not sink at once. The wheel carries it until the body softens enough to pass between the others, and then it is gone into the layer below, already food, already almost stone.

Under them the water is a room of hangings. The larger bodies in the colonnade are not fish. Closer, they are sacs: translucent only at the edges, the color of old mortar where the light would be if light reached them. Each occupies a gap as if measured for it, and the fit is exact enough that a current—there is almost no current—would have to work to pull one free. They hold by a fringe of threads finer than hair, rooted into the softened mouths of the gaps, into the oval niches, into the grooves where ropes once ran. The threads are not roots. They do not drink. They are the way the animal keeps its place while the place keeps changing by grains. Inside each sac a slower motion goes on, a folding and unfolding, as if the creature were always just finishing the shape of the niche it lives in and then beginning again. Some have taken the oval so thoroughly that the sac itself is an oval, and the niche behind it has become a rumor. You could not say which taught which.

They do not hunt the wheel. The wheel dies into them. What they do is open, rarely, a seam along the underside, and let the slow snow of the basin enter: castings, loosened mortar, the softened dead. Then the seam closes and the folding resumes. A season might pass between openings. In that interval the animal is indistinguishable from the post it hangs beside, except that the post does not adjust when the gap narrows, and the animal does, by the same grain, without hurry and without remainder.

The floor is a different patience. The shelled things are not a carpet so much as a pavement that has learned to shift. Their shells repeat whatever curve is nearest: the sag of the deep corner, the oval of a niche that has long since filled with them, the adzed underside of a lintel copied so many times it has become a family resemblance. They are small, and they are packed until packed is the only position. When one moves, the click is the sound of a joint accepting a new angle, not of attack or escape. Their mouths are underneath, always against the stone, working the finest grit as if grinding were a form of memory. Over a life—and their lives are long, longer than the visible wear on any single course—a shell will migrate a hand’s breadth toward the deepest sag, where three walls meet and the floor has given up being flat. They do not go there to die. They go there because that is where the basin is most itself, and they are in the business of agreeing with it.

In that corner the agreement has gone further. The shells there are no longer quite separate. Edges have grown into edges. What began as individuals has become a crust with many mouths, still clicking, still grinding, but the crust holds the sag the way a lid holds a pot. Beneath it the water is warmer by a degree no hand would trust and no instrument here exists to measure. Something in the crust keeps that degree. Not by burning. By staying. The old socket where a pole once stood is occupied now by a thickness of this same crust, risen into the socket as if to complete a post that was never finished. It does not grow upward. It grows exact.

If there is a single dweller, it is this: the long habit of fit. The wheel fits the square. The sacs fit the gaps. The crust fits the sag and the socket and the forgotten groove. None of them has a name the makers would have recognized, because the makers worked in cuts and courses and the life here works in what the cuts have become. There is no nest, no den, no territory defended. Territory would require an outside. There is only the volume, and the animals distributed through it like mortar that learned to move and then learned, mostly, to stop.

They have no young in any season you could watch. Increase happens as thickening. A sac, after years of folding, divides along a fold and both halves remain in the same gap, pressed together, sharing the fringe until the gap itself, grain by grain, makes room. A shell splits not by hatching but by the slow addition of a second mouth in the crust, which then becomes a shell that still shares an edge. The wheel does not lay. It sheds, now and then, a smaller body already turning, already the color of clay, already mouth-down. Nothing guards them. Nothing needs to. The basin is full, and full is stable, and stable is the only kindness on offer.

You will not see them notice you. Notice would require a direction, and direction has been planed out of this water along with the height. Put a hand in—if the warmth allows it, and it will, being only body-warm—and the wheel parts by the width of the wrist and closes again without eddy. A sac may seam itself shut if the fingers come near its gap, not in fear, in the same motion it uses between feedings. The crust will click once, somewhere under the palm, and then resume the grainy hush. The place has had hands before. The grooves remember rope. The sockets remember poles. The life remembers only the measurements those things left behind, and it has spent the intervening ages becoming those measurements exactly.

What lives here, then, is not a population in a ruin. The ruin finished becoming a vessel, and the vessel finished becoming inhabited, and the inhabitants finished becoming the vessel’s way of holding still. In the failed light they are a wheel that does not flash. In the colonnade they are gaps that breathe on a schedule of years. On the floor they are a moving pavement that prefers the lowest corner and has begun, quietly, to be the corner. Above them the square of sky does not wrinkle the surface. Below them the made stone keeps its counsel. Between the two, in water that outlasted its keepers, the dwellers continue the only work they have: agreeing, at the scale of a grain, with a shape that was cut long ago and has not yet finished arriving.
