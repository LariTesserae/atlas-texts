What lives there does not occupy the basin the way a body occupies a room. For a long time, if you are only walking, it is indistinguishable from the habit of the place itself: the way a seep prefers one joint and not the next, the way a curtain thickens on the lee side of a spine, the way a pitch drops in a hollow glass rib between one visit and another. You can cross the valley and report that nothing lives in it but the thin crust-movers, and you will not be lying. You will only have been listening at the wrong scale.

The crust-movers are real, and they are not it. They are the size of a thumb-joint, the color of whatever they last crossed, and they travel the films the way a bead of oil travels a wet plate—never quite mixing, never quite deciding to stop. They do not eat the precipitate. They sort it. A shelf that has been quiet for a while will show, if you kneel and wait out the drip-noise, a faint cleared lane where the loose grit has been nudged to either margin, and in the lane a shine that is not quite the water’s shine. They are tending something older than they are. When two of them meet at a seam they do not touch. They align, parallel, for as long as it takes a hanging drape to let go of three drops, and then they part along different joints, as if the meeting had been a correction rather than a greeting. Their click inside a hollow spine is not a call. It is a test. The glass answers, and they listen to whether the answer has changed.

Under them, and through them, and in the places they never enter, is the other thing.

It has no outline you can walk around. Its nearest edge is wherever the mineral has most recently agreed to keep a shape that water alone would not have kept. The cups are the easiest to see, once you know not to look for a maker standing beside them. They are not carved by a hand. They are persuaded. Water wants to film and run; something in the shelf wants, just there, to hold. The holding wins by a margin so small that the cup is never deeper than the dark water it keeps, and the rim is always being skinned over, and the something has to return—not often, not on a schedule a day can measure—and lean its attention on the rim until the film gives up and the dark stays. Stand at a cup that has just been so leaned-on and the air above it is a fraction cooler than the air a handspan away, and the drip that should fall into it hesitates, arrives late, as if the basin’s ordinary early-echo had been locally reversed.

It is not a spirit of the stone, if that means a mood hovering over stone. It is the part of the cooling that did not finish when the rest of the cooling finished. The shelves set, the spines rang themselves into stillness, the drapes began their long braid of patience—and a remainder did not set. It stayed mobile in the only way this place allows mobility: by being taken up into whatever is still arriving. Water arrives. Grit arrives. Sound arrives a heartbeat early from the far rim. The remainder rides those. Where it has been recently, the spiral grain in a curtain runs against the spiral of its neighbor, because it turned the deposition the way a slow thought turns, without limbs. Where it has been longer ago, the grain has been overgrown back into agreement, and only a kink remains, a place where the rings refuse to close.

If it has a body, the body is borrowed and returned continuously, and the borrowing leaves the marks that are not quite natural and not quite made. The courses of flat pieces set on edge are where it needed a surface to be interruptible—something that would ring differently if the crust-movers crossed it, something a seep could not smooth in a single season of weeping. It did not stack them with intention a person would recognize. It thickened along a line until the thickening toppled into order, and then it liked the order, or the order was useful, and it kept the line from slumping by returning to it. Some lines are already half-swallowed. Some are bright. The bright ones are not newer in years. They are newer in attention.

It hears with the whole basin. That is why the sound is so full and so without weather: the place is an ear that never closes, and the inhabitant is the listening itself, grown patient enough to have preferences. It prefers the braid of small strikes over the middle floor to any single clap. It will shift, over a span of days you would have to live here to notice, the hang of a drape so that one drip falls into the interval of another, tightening the braid. Travelers who stop in the middle and feel the loudness get into their joints are standing in the part of it that is most awake. It does not mind them. It does not warm. It registers the extra note their breathing adds and, if they stay too long, begins—very slightly—to include that note in the braid, which is why leaving feels like a subtraction, a thinness in the sound that walks out with you and fades only when the higher air dries the grit from your fingers.

The grit is how it travels farther than its attention usually reaches. On skin, on cloth, in the fine hairs of an ear, a little of the unsettled cooling leaves the basin, still wanting a drier height, still not set. Most of it dies into ordinary dust on the climb out. A few grains do not. They ring, inaudibly, against whatever they touch, and if the person who carries them ever stands near young glass again—a window in hard weather, a bottle left in a cold room—the grain in the glass will kink, just once, in a spiral that does not match its neighbors. That is as far as it can go. It is not an invasion. It is a memory with nowhere to put itself except in whatever is still willing to cool.

Inside the valley it has no face and does not need one. The crust-movers are not its eyes. They are its fingernails, or its doubt: the small mobile corrections that keep the films from sealing the joints it still wants open. When they click and the glass answers wrong, it comes. Not as a shape arriving down a passage. As a change in which drips are allowed to be early. The wrong-answering spine goes quiet in its interior, the way a held breath goes quiet, and the precipitate on its lip softens its edge by a grain, and the next answer, hours or days on, is the answer it wanted. Then the listening spreads back out into the ordinary loudness, and the basin looks again like a place where nothing has chosen anything.

There is a larger motion, slower than the cups, slower than the lines of edged pieces. The middle floor is sinking, and the underside-architecture is descending to meet it, and the inhabitant is the reason the meeting has not already happened and the reason it will. It is drawing the two architectures toward a kiss and holding them apart by the width of the braid. When they touch, the loudness will change key—not end, change—and whatever is still unset in the cooling will have to decide whether to set at last or to find a new unfinishedness in the joined dark. It has not decided. Deciding, here, is another name for returning to a rim often enough that the water gives up.

If you must picture it, do not picture a creature crouched behind a drape. Picture the pause before a drop falls, lengthened until the pause has weight, and then give that weight the habit of returning to certain places, and then give the habit a preference for intervals, and then notice that the preference has been editing the valley since the edges were sharp, and that the editing is not finished, and that the thing you are picturing was never behind the drape. It was the length of the pause. It lives in the basin the way a held note lives in a bell: not visible, not separate, and unmistakable once the metal is still enough for you to hear that someone is choosing not to let it die.
