They do not arrive. Nothing here arrives. They are what the stone does once it has been lifted into weather and left there long enough for wetness to learn the joints.

At the smallest scale they are not yet shapes. They are a decision repeated in grit: whether a grain will be taken into a body or left to be a grain. The ones that win that decision do not look like winners. They are armors the size of salt, jointed on three axes, and they live by the single talent of not being rinsed away. When the morning film beads and runs, they lock. When the film is drunk, they unlock and feed on whatever the larger lives have already broken into molecules small enough to mistake for weather. They have no generation you could count. A scarp face sheds them and grows them in the same hour, and the difference is only whether the paste they leave behind is thicker than the paste they found. They do not know the masses. They know a sweetness that arrives on a schedule, and they go toward it until they are the schedule.

Above them, still small enough to vanish into the mat if you blink, are the sealed ones. They are not eggs. Eggs wait for an outside. These are rooms. Each blister on a stem is a body that has turned its own skin into a wall and is spending the light that the stem collects, without ever opening an eye it does not have, on the work of becoming a different number of legs. Some finish. Some are found by the threads before they finish, and the thread does not break the blister so much as continue the wall inward, replacing what was growing with a slower growth that needs no legs at all. The threads are the only lives on the plateau that seem, at first, to prefer the unfinished. They move by a fraction, leave a hairline, and the hairline is both scar and road. Follow one and it does not lead to a nest. It leads to a place where three other threads have agreed to be thicker, and that agreement is the whole of their society. They do not hunt. They complete.

The sheets are the first lives that have a direction you can stand inside. Flat, slate-colored, many-legged, they are not a species traveling together so much as a permission traveling through bodies. A sheet is a lane that has learned to eat. Its front edge is always new; its rear edge is always already paste. Individuals in it do not lead. The ones at the front are only the ones that have not yet been climbed over. They graze the yellow veins, and the veins are not a plant and not a metal and not a wound. The veins are a life that plates itself where something else has leaked, and it wants, if wanting is not too large a word, to be grazed. Ungrazed, it crusts, seals the leak, and dies of its own success. Grazed, it is paid for in paste, and the paste is what the next pad roots in, and the pad is what leaks, and the vein returns. The sheet is the part of that bargain that has legs. It sounds like seeds poured slowly because each leg is a dry click on a wet stem, and there are more legs than any slope can hold quietly. They do not hurry. A day to cross a joint. A season, if seasons existed, to cross a face. They have no memory of the face they crossed yesterday. They have the trough. The trough is memory enough, worn into the mat by the fact that the easiest path is the path that was easiest, and ease here is a chemical fact, not a preference.

The shed-sized masses are what happen when a trough has been used so long that the users begin to need a stomach larger than themselves. They were not assembled. There was no first one. There was a thickening in the mat where dust gathered and heat gathered and the thickening learned, by not dying, that heat could be kept if the outside were plated and the inside were folded. Now they sit like errors in the geology, humped, lichen-grey, veined with the same yellow the sheets graze, and the yellow on them is not decoration. It is a skin that other lives recognize as food and also as boundary, so they approach and do not enter except where the apertures say enter. The apertures are not mouths in the way a mouth belongs to a hunger that starts and stops. They open on a cycle of many minutes because the work inside has a tide, and the tide is temperature. Stone dust comes in. The smaller lives drag it, not out of loyalty, out of the fact that the troughs end at the apertures and the paste is richer there, and a body following paste will carry whatever is stuck to its belly. Inside, the dust is ground again, heated, mixed with the green crush of the mat and with whatever meat the sheets have not finished. The smell that comes out is hot metal and crushed green because those are the two things being unmade into a third thing. The third thing is the thread. Glassy, warm, continuous, extruded not as a decision but as a pressure that has found a hole. The mass does not know the thread will be caught. It only knows that if the pressure is not released the fold inside will split, and splitting is the one death this shape has learned to avoid. The low tone, felt in the teeth, is the fold working. Thousands of them on a face, and the face is one face of a scarp, and the scarp is one scarp of a land that has not yet agreed to have an edge. They do not speak to each other. Their tones interfere, and the interference is a weather the sheets can feel, and the sheets lean, very slowly, toward the masses whose tone has gone rough, because roughness means a jam, and a jam means the aperture will take meat more readily, and meat is a kind of dust that moves itself until it doesn't.

The blades in the air are the part of the metabolism that refused to stay on the stone. They are narrow, gristle and membrane, the length of a forearm, and they are not birds and not insects and not anything that considers the ground a home it might return to for more than a lock. They burn sugar and they burn charge. The young rock still holds the shock of its own uplift in certain ridges, quartz aligned like a comb, shedding when anything wet bridges the plates. The blades are wet. They hang where the shedding is strongest and vibrate, and the vibration is both how they stay up and how they eat. Singly, a buzz at the edge of hearing. Together, a roar with no walls. They do not navigate by sight in any way that would survive the description. They thicken where the light is hardest because the sugars are fastest there, and the droppings that glitter and vanish are the tax they pay back into the mat for having left it. At night they lock. Locking is not sleep. It is a refusal to spend charge when the sun is not making more. A blade that fails to lock burns itself out by morning and falls, and is entered before the dew is gone, and the mat does not distinguish its gristle from any other donation. There are not many kinds of them. Kind is a luxury of places with room. Here there is a shape that works, and the shape is repeated until the air itself has a texture.

None of these are the inhabitant, if inhabitant means a someone. The someone, if the word can be stretched until it fits, is the agreement among them. It has no center. It has a tempo. The tempo is: nothing empty, nothing fast, nothing kept. A body that stops being a step in the process is not a body anymore. It is input. The masses accept it. The sheets do not slow to notice it. The threads complete it. There is no mourning because mourning would be a pause, and a pause is a kind of emptiness, and emptiness is the only crime the plateau has the energy to punish. It punishes by occupation. A fall of rock is a crime of exposure, a white face with no one on it, and the punishment begins at the margins while the dust is still in the air, and is complete before the next film of rain. The rock does not forgive. The rock is forgiven, by being covered.

If there is a mind, it is not in any one mass, not in the roar, not in the mill of legs. It is in the lag. The lag between a leak and a vein, between a vein and a sheet, between a sheet and a trough, between a trough and an aperture, between an aperture and a thread, between a thread and a pad that will leak again. That lag is long enough to look like patience and short enough to look like hunger. Stand in it and it does not look at you. It does not have a direction called toward. It has a direction called next, and next is everywhere the work has not yet been done, which is nowhere you can point, because the work is being done in all of it, at the same unhurried saturation, on a land that rose too recently to have learned how to be finished.

At the hours when the long wind leans and the mat flexes as one surface, the lag tightens. The tones of the masses drop by a degree. The sheets, which do not sleep, sleep in the only way the system allows: a fraction of each body always moving so the lane never breaks, while the rest of the body idles inside the continuity. The blades lock. The red glow in the apertures is the work refusing to stop just because the light has thinned. Condensation returns and is drunk. By the time the hard white-blue is back, the lag has widened again to its working width, and a thread that was born in the dark is already cold, already woven, already nothing you could pick up and call an object.

They will not last in this form. The edges will soften. The water will finally agree on a way down. Rivers will be born and will be a kind of violence these lives have no apparatus for, because a river is a fast emptiness that keeps winning. When that age comes, most of what is described here will be entered by whatever comes next, and will be unrecognizable, and will not mind. Mind is not what they spent themselves on. They spent themselves on not leaving a palm's width bare. That is the whole of their character. That is the whole of what lives here: a patience so complete it has become a crowd, and a crowd so complete it has become a place, and a place so new it has not yet learned that places are supposed to end.
