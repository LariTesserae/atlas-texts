They do not occupy the vale the way a town occupies a street. They occur in it, the way a tone occurs in a pipe.

At first it is only the schedule. Something answers the pumps without being the pumps. When a sump draws down and the false marsh goes slack, a pale thread of movement leaves the grated bed and travels the channel margin, not swimming so much as being sorted—lighter than the water, heavier than the scum, a seam that holds its shape for thirty paces and then frays into nothing when the next pump kicks and the pitch in the teeth changes. Watch it twice and it looks like debris. Watch it through a full cycle and it has preferences: it will not cross a bright line of standing water if a darker seam is available; it bunches where iron has bled into a joint; it will not be hurried. If the draw-down comes early, it simply stops being visible, as if visibility were a posture it could put down.

Higher, in the duct forest, the scrabble resolves if you do not turn your head. There are bodies, but they are not much larger than the relay boxes they nest against, and they are the color of condensate on galvanized steel—hard to see until they move, and they move only when the fans cycle down and the air in the runs goes briefly still. Then a dry shift, a repositioning of weight. They cling upside down along the warmest ducts, the ones that still carry a little of the machines’ abandoned degree, and the fine rain beads on their backs and runs off in the same lines it runs off the metal, so that a cluster of them reads as corrosion until one of them adjusts a limb and the pattern breaks. They do not call. The vale is already too full of sentences. What they do is wait out the inhalation of the cooling stacks, then cross, quickly, along the ribs, toward whatever seal has begun to weep nutrient. A pale moth, gummed and beating, is an accident they will take if it falls into reach. Mostly they take the film.

The film is not theirs alone. It lives in the slack, a cold chemistry that is also a crowd: not one thing, a weather of things, thickening where the flow forgets itself and thinning to a shine where the channel remembers. In the manufactured beds—those empty rectangles of grit—it rises a finger’s width and stops, as if height were a permission it has not been given. Where two pours failed to meet, it enters the joint and stays, gray-green, iron-fed, and from those joints it sends out, over days, a slower staining that maps the leak the way the rebar maps the western weep. Stand on the gantry through a shift and you can see which margins it has claimed since the last time you looked. It does not climb the walls. It does not need to. The walls come to it, one drop at a time.

None of this is tended, and yet there is tending.

Far down the eastern cut, where the conveyors knock their metal call and answer, a light moves that is not one of the lamps. It is small, hooded, and it keeps to the catwalks, and it stops where a valve is hunting. The person—if the word still fits—does not hurry the valve. They set the light on the grating, wipe a palm along the pipe until the shiver declares itself, and listen with the side of the jaw the way someone else might listen with an ear. Their coat is already glossed; their breath lasts a moment and is taken. They carry no visible load except a cloth that smells of release-oil and iron, and a thin hook for lifting grates. When the valve seats, they do not mark it. They only wait through one more hunt that does not come, then lift the light and go on, toward the next held note. You will not see their face clearly. The hood is for the rain that is not quite rain, and the lamps throw their features down into the collar. What shows is the habit: they walk the sharp edges without rounding them, step over the bright lines, never cut across a channel even when it is only a fingernail deep. They know the floor was poured and has not yet learned to be old. They are teaching it, by not damaging the lesson.

There are few of them. Sometimes two share a gantry and do not speak, because the turbines are exhaling and speech would be a waste of the air they have already warmed. They pass each other the cloth. One points with a chin at a sump where the bubble-chain has started again—not the pump, the other thing—and the second nods and does not go look. Looking stops it. They have learned that the way you learn not to open a door into weather. Their concern is not the bubble-chain. Their concern is the seal upstream of it, the one that feeds the film, the one that if it fails wide will turn the false marsh into a true one and drown the rectangles and make the channels forget their ruler-straight jobs for longer than a minute. They are not guardians of a mystery. They are the reason the mystery stays small.

What lives in the bubble-chain does not have a coat or a cloth. It keeps to the deep of the civic squares, under the sheet, and it surfaces only as that brief argument with the water—bubbles in a line, too regular for decay, too irregular for a bleed-off valve. When the light from the gantry reaches it, it subsides, and the sump goes back to being volume. In the slack between draw-downs it will come higher, not breaking the surface, only lending the surface a different tension, a place where the reflection of the duct-sky bends as if something broad and slow had thought about rising and then thought better of it. The pale thread in the channels is not its young, though they share a reluctance to be seen in full. The thread is a margin animal. This is a sump animal. It likes the muscular patience of water that has been given a job and is doing it. When a pump lags and the aprons sheet over and the floor becomes a river with no banks, it does not follow the flood out. It sinks, and waits for the remembering.

The moths are the only true travelers, and they do not last. They come in on the ventilation rivers, already damaged by the temperature they were not built for, and they spend themselves on the sodium lamps, wings taking on condensate until flight is a argument they lose. A few reach the colder white of the pump halls and die more slowly, walking the housings. The duct-clingers harvest what falls. The film takes what the clingers miss, in the way low water takes anything that stops moving. Nothing here builds a midden. The place is too new for leftovers; the pumps are too faithful. A wing is a gloss on the concrete by morning, and by the next cycle even the gloss has been sorted into a channel and carried toward a square.

If you stay past the point where the processes separate themselves—draw-down, slack, the tower’s fine rain, the call and the response—you begin to tell the living from the running by what cannot be reset. A failed lamp leaves a darkness the size of its reach, and into that exact darkness the thread will not go, and the duct-clingers will not cross, and the hooded light, when it comes, pauses at the edge and then goes around, not through. The machine darkness is empty in a way the wet dark is not. The wet dark has thickness. It has the broad thought under the sump, the repositioning of small weight when the fans die, the stain advancing a finger along a joint that was never meant to be a bed. The machine darkness has only the absence of a bulb, and everything that lives here is too newly fitted to the lamps to trust a hole that has no condensation in it.

They are all first-season things. None of them remember a valley that was stone. The clingers were born on metal that still threw weld-heat; their resting posture is the posture of a duct. The thread has never met a bank that was not a pour. The sump-thought knows only squares. Even the tenders, who must have come from somewhere with weather that wets a coat instead of glossing it, walk as if the formwork teeth were a kind of north. They do not bring soil in on their boots. They wipe them, carefully, on the grating, before they step down onto the apron—not from cleanliness, from a sense that the floor is still learning what it is, and that a footprint with real dirt in it would be a rumor of the mountain, and the mountain is meant to stay behind the polymer skin.

Toward the end of a long slack, when the channels have gone quiet enough that the drip from the western rebar can be counted, one of the tenders will sometimes stop on the high gantry and do what you did: let the distances open. Three sumps, the yard of pipes, the towers squatting like mills, the vale continuing until haze closes it. They do not look like someone admiring a work. They look like someone counting livestock they cannot see—margins, seals, the one bubble-line that must not be encouraged, the rectangles that are still mostly empty and should stay that way. A pale moth beats itself out against their lamp and falls. They do not catch it. Below, on a duct, something the color of wet steel shifts, and is still again. In the nearest sump the surface tightens, thinks, and lets the thought go.

Then the pump catches. The pitch returns to the teeth. The river withdraws into its channels and leaves the concrete darker, the edges shining, the formwork marks holding their first sharpness a little longer. The thread frays and is gone. The clingers settle into the lines of the rain. The tender lifts the hooded light, wipes the film of the rail from their palm onto the cloth, and goes on down the catwalk, toward a valve that has not yet begun to hunt, but will.

What lives here lives at the speed of maintenance and the speed of leak, and almost nowhere in between. It will not round the corners. It will not come forward when a lamp fails. It is already, in the slow way of metal and the quicker way of scum, forgetting that the under-valley was ever not running—and being, in that forgetting, the first natives of a place that was poured.
