They live as if a floor were a courtesy rather than a right, and they have been doing it long enough that the courtesy has entered their posture.

From the ridge they are easy to miss. The wards look unmanned because no one stands still long enough to read as a population, and because the damp takes color out of cloth until a crew is only a darker movement against darker shed-wall. Come down off the conglomerate and the basin is crowded. People pass within arm’s length on lanes that will not be lanes by evening, and they do not apologize for it. Space is not something they own in the moment; it is something they are using on the way to the next use. Shoulders turn without breaking stride. A barrow of wet reed and a woman carrying a child on the same hip and a man walking backward with his hands on a screw-jack all occupy the same narrowing at once, and the narrowing gives, or they do.

They are not tall. The cold that sits in the basin seems to have sat in their bones as well, not as sickness, as proportion. Compact bodies, dense through the trunk, short in the neck, with a forward set of the head that comes from watching the ground more than the horizon. Hands are large for the frames they belong to, the pads of the fingers thickened, the nails kept short and often gray under the rim from silt that does not wash out in water that is never quite warm. Skin holds a film. Even indoors — and indoors is a relative term when a wall may be half brick and half yesterday’s gravel — the face keeps a slight sheen, as if the body had agreed with the weather not to bother drying. Veins show blue at the wrists and temples. The young look older than they are until you see them move; the old look merely more settled in the same damp, not dried out by it. Hair is worn short or bound tight under caps of oiled cloth. Loose hair catches on vines, and the vines are not asked to yield.

Their clothes are a second weather. Wool next to the skin, then a canvas or rubbered layer that beads and never quite stops dripping, then sometimes a leather apron gone black with use. Nothing is new in the way the stone is new. Everything they wear has been walked from one footing to another, patched where a belt caught it, stained in horizontal lines that mark old water-levels the way a pier is marked. Boots are the only thing treated as permanent property. A lost boot is a story told more than once. The rest can be traded at a shift-change without ceremony: a dry cuff for a wet one, a pair of gloves for a length of cord, a child’s coat that no longer closes for one that does.

They do not have a name for themselves that survives a season any better than the maps do. Outsiders who come down the ridge — surveyors, mostly, and the occasional company agent who still believes in plots — try words on them and watch the words fail. In the wards they call each other by the machine they are walking, or by the seam they are following, or by a task that will be finished or obsolete by dark. A person is the Jack on the Number Four bay, or Reed-sewer, or the one who drains the west pond before it lifts the footings. When the bay has walked a hundred meters north and the pond has gone back into the ground, the name walks too, or dies, and no one holds a funeral for it. Children are the exception for a little while. They keep a given name until they are old enough to take a task, and even then an older sibling may use it, the way you might use a word from a language no one else on the shift still speaks. The child on the parapet who pointed at the soft-backed thing in the gravel bank is called Silt by everyone who works that yard, and Mara by her sister, and she answers to both without seeming to notice the difference.

Kin here is a practice, not a line you could draw. People share sleeping-platforms in the upper lofts of sheds, where the air is fractionally less wet and the new stone has not yet reached. A platform holds whoever last bolted the ladder and whoever they will not turn away: a pair who have walked the same engines for years, a night-shift feeder and the day-shift feeder who never meet except at the rail of the loft, three adolescents who have decided they are a household because they route the same mammal-run around the same press. Children are raised by whoever is on the platform when the shift that is not theirs comes up from the yard. Parenthood is real and also distributed. A woman may be the one who carried a child and also, by the following spring, only one of four people the child looks for when the lamps come on. No one finds this tragic. The basin rearranges structures. They have learned not to store love in structures that the gravel can close.

What they store instead is procedure, and they store it in the body. A child learns the civic rules before anything that could be called a lesson. Do not leave standing water against a new wall. Do not open a hole to see what the basin is doing; the basin will show you by rising into the hole, and then you are a story about rescue and a bolted plate. Do not clear a mammal-run; open your crate wall to it if you must, or better, leave the gap the night shift already left. Do not cut a vine-run without stringing another the same day. Do not expect an address to be there when you return from the causeways. These are not written. They are corrected, briefly, by whoever is nearest, in the absorbent voice the basin permits — a few words, a hand on a shoulder turning a body away from a fresh seam, and then the work resumes. Loud correction is itself a fault. The quiet is infrastructure, and a person who shouts in a yard is treated the way a blocked run is treated: routed around, then fixed.

Their days have no clean edge. Shifts exist because engines exist, not because the light is a reliable clock. Gray-gold comes and goes; a squall soaks one ward and leaves the next in ordinary daylight; work continues under both. What organizes them is the pulse they have agreed to match. Presses come down. You feel it in the chest before you hear it, and by the time the sound arrives it has already been eaten by moss and hanging damp and the soft bodies of leaves. People time their lifts to that concussion. A jack-crew walks a shed between strokes, a few centimeters, then waits, then walks again, the whole bay creaking like a ship in a dry dock that has forgotten it is supposed to be still. They do not look heroic doing it. They look employed. Someone calls the distance. Someone watches the footing for the moment the new stone stops being trustworthy and starts being hungry. Someone else keeps the vines from binding the screw before the screw has finished its turn, not by cutting — that would be stupid, and loud by afternoon — but by lifting the runners and laying them along the new line, training them the way you train a hose.

Speech fits the same economy. They talk, but not far. A sentence is built to survive three paces of wet air and then be done. Beyond that it joins the drip and the hum, and you were a fool to spend it. So their talk is concrete: the west seam is running high; the herons have taken the pond by the spinning-hall and will not be moved, so drain around them; the pale many-legged ones in the fresh cutting are still soft along the back, which means the cold may not hold, which means do not schedule the plate-bolting until evening. Technical, local, exact. What they do not say is how they feel about the fact that their house may be a cistern by morning. Ask, and you get a look that is not hostile so much as busy. Feeling is not absent. It has been folded into maintenance. Affection looks like walking someone’s shed without being asked. Grief looks like continuing to drain a pond that has already taken the wall they slept against, because leaving the water is how the basin spreads a loss into the next yard. Anger is rare and expensive. The ground is not malicious. Being angry at it is like being angry at drip.

They make small things that can survive a move. Not monuments. A spoon carved from a dense root that grew through a grating and was taken only when a new shoot had already replaced the run. A whistle of reed that a child uses to call the platform-household, pitched low enough that it does not quarrel with the quiet. Marks cut into brick — a tally of sheds walked, a private sign for a seam that has been reliable for more than a week — and then, when the brick becomes half conglomerate and the mark is crossed by a mortar line that the masons simply continued, they recut the mark on whatever surface is still a surface. Writing, where it exists, is on slate boards hung inside lofts, and the boards are the first thing lifted clear when a bay is jacked. Deeds, when a company agent insists, describe uses and volumes, never plots, and the people who can read them treat them as weather reports written by someone standing too far away.

There is a religion, if the word can be made to fit something with no temple and no promise. They do not worship the basin. Worship would be a kind of standing still. What they do, at the change of certain shifts, is put a hand to the newest stone and leave it there while one press-cycle completes. Not prayer exactly. Acknowledgment. The stone is warm sometimes, fresh from whatever trade the geology is conducting, and the warmth in the palm is as close as they come to a sacrament. A few of the very old talk about the unfinished creatures as if they were kin on a delay — not ancestors, not omens, just earlier drafts of the same agreement. Most people do not talk about them at all. They step over them. If one is half in a bank and half out, still soft, they give it the room they would give a hose under pressure. By evening it may have firmed into something with a preference for the dark under belts, or it may have gone back into the gravel. Either way it was never theirs to finish.

The animals are not a separate estate. They are the other shift.

The small dark birds work the conveyor mouths with a professionalism that puts some of the feeders to shame. They know the gap between belt and housing where the insects breed in the warm wet, and they time their dips to the pause in the rollers. No one nets them. A bird in the mechanism is a stopped mechanism, and a stopped mechanism is a loud one, so the crews have learned the birds’ entries and leave them. In return the birds do not foul the lofts. This is not a bargain anyone negotiated. It is a truce that holds because both sides are employed by the same damp.

The heron-shaped birds — heavier than herons, slate-bloomed, patient to the point of insult — own the temporary ponds. A pond is yours, by the civic rule, until the ground takes it back, but only if the herons have not already stood in it. If they have, you drain around them. They strike at what moves under the glaze and they do not share. People give them names that last exactly as long as the pond: the Miller, the Gray at West Four, the one with the notch. When the pond closes overnight the name closes with it. The birds walk to the next glaze as if they had been informed in the same briefing as the drain-crew. Children are taught not to throw gravel at them. Not from tenderness. From the knowledge that a startled heron-bird lifts and lands three ponds over, and then three yards have a labor problem.

Between the pipe runs the short-furred mammals keep their own city, low and serious, with customs the night shift has learned by damage. Block a run and by morning there is a hole in a crate wall, edges darkened with the damp that permitted it, and the goods in the crate have been sorted according to a logic no quartermaster would accept — coil left, reed dragged, anything edible gone into the run. So the night shift leaves gaps. They even, without ever putting it in a report, improve them: a turned board, a brick left proud to make a lintel. The mammals accept the improvement without thanks and without scattering when a lamp passes. They are the size of cats and shaped more like a compromise between a cat and a water-vole, coats short enough that wet does not load them down, eyes small, feet broad for silt. They watch people the way people watch rising footings — for information, not for fellowship. A litter raised under a boiler housing will use that housing as a landmark for as long as the housing stays, and when the jacksmen walk the housing north the litter walks with it, under the shadow, as if the landmark were the point and the meters were irrelevant.

Insects are weather, and the people dress for them as for squalls. When a roller starts, a cloud lifts off the reed-mats and resettles on the nearest warm housing, and anyone leaning there learns to lean elsewhere. They bite, some of them. The bites welt and then vanish into the general sheen of living here. No one fumes the yards. Fumes would rise poorly in this air and would kill the vines, and the vines are doing the work of walls in more than one street. Instead people oil their cuffs and keep moving. Movement is the reliable repellent. The basin dislikes a still target almost as much as it dislikes a speculative hole.

And then there are the ones in the newest cuttings, which the people do not call animals in the same breath.

They are pale, many-legged, soft along the back when the cold is uncertain, and they turn the silt with a motion that looks like reading. In the footprints that fill with water at the edge of a fresh bank you can watch one work a pebble the size of a thumb, turning it, setting it, turning it again, as if the conglomerate were a text that had not yet agreed on its sentences. Some firm by evening. The back darkens, the legs take a direction, and by full dark the thing has a preference — under the belts, or along the seam, or back into the bank it came from. Some never firm. They remain a possibility the gravel is still entertaining, and if you are foolish enough to lift one it comes apart into wet mineral and a smell of iron and crushed reed, the same smell the rising beds have, and you have learned nothing except that your gloves are now a problem.

Silt — Mara, on the platform — has been watching them longer than the yard prefers. She is young enough that her given name still answers, old enough to be trusted with a drain-line if an adult is at the other end. She sits the parapet when the cutting is new, boots hooked on the inner face so a swell in the pavement cannot tip her, and she does not throw anything and does not dig. She points, sometimes. An older jack-hand told her the firming-up rule once, and she has been testing it without instruments: which ones are still soft at the hour the lamps go yellow, which have gone slate along the spine, which have decided against the air. She does not think of them as hers. She thinks of them the way the drain-crews think of seams — as information that expires. Still, she has begun to recognize one. A particular arrangement of legs, a hesitation before it turns a pebble, a return to the same footprint even after the footprint has been walked through by a boot. If it firms, she will not feed it. Feeding is not done. If it goes back into the bank, she will not mourn it where anyone can mistake mourning for a speech. She may, in the loft, tell her sister that the hesitant one chose the gravel. Her sister, who sleeps days, will say that choosing is a strong word for what the basin does. They will both be right, and the board with the slate marks will be lifted clear before either of them has to decide what to call the loss.

Out on the western causeways, where the city loosens into lace and the mill walks itself a few meters a month after a seam that will not stay, fewer people live, and the ones who do are slightly different in the way a dialect is different — still intelligible, pitched to longer silences. Pump-house tenders. A family that has followed the walking mill for three seasons and now thinks of the mill as the household and themselves as the ones who keep its feet from binding in reed. They see the wards as a weather behind them: the yellow lamps, the cranes, the soft concussions. They do not miss streets. A causeway that remains a causeway until morning is luxury enough.

At night the basin’s inhabitants become easier to count and harder to separate. Lamps in the sheds. The faint give-back of light from wet new mineral, which people use as a path-finding the way others use stars — unreliable, local, good enough. A jack-crew still walking a bay, their breath showing, their voices limited to numbers. Birds settled into the vine-runs they have agreed not to overburden. Mammals in the gaps. The soft ones either gone hard or gone back. Children on platforms, listening to the drip and the hum they will spend their lives matching. Somewhere a wall lengthening under hands that do not need to see the brick to know when it has stopped being brick.

No one among them will tell you they are at home. Home is a plot, and plots are a kind of lie the ridge still believes. What they will tell you, if you are close enough to hear a sentence before the moss takes it, is that the footing held through the shift, that the vines were restrung, that the pond was drained before it could choose the wall, that the hesitant one in the cutting either firmed or did not. They live inside that news. It is a crowded living, cold, unfinished, and full — full the way the basin is full, at every scale, with no margin left over for the idea that life ought to have been happening somewhere stiller. By morning some of their names will have walked north with the sheds. The people will still be there, already at work, already slightly elsewhere, already themselves.
