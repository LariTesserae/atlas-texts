They do not arrive as kinds. Kinds are a later mercy. First there is only motion against the crust, and the motion is already wrong in scale: too many small things for the emptiness, too few large ones for the noise.

Closest to the glaze, where the night film still darkens the joints at midmorning, the fiber is not bare. It wears a pelt of bodies no thicker than a finger-bone and no longer than a hand, pressed flat, the color of the plate they lie on until the heat makes them lift. They are not grazers in any proud sense. They lap the sweet edge where a seam has split and the interior shows, and when the seam seals they move to the next, a slow punctuation along lines that look, from a chimney’s height, like writing. Their mouths are underneath. You learn this by stepping near one and finding it will not flee so much as adhere, then release, leaving a pale oval on the stone-plant and a brighter oval on your boot. They have no call. Their contribution to the highland’s sound is the minute stick and unstick of a million leavings, audible only if you lie down, which you should not do for long. The film they drink breeds a second life inside them, and that second life is what the chimney-nations come down for.

Those are the ones you hear and do not see. In the shafts they are a weather of their own: a granular dark that travels the interior in sheets, up with the warming air, down when a throat of the column cools. Individually they are slight, winged or nearly, with a body that seems assembled for resonance more than for travel—thin chest, hard little mouth, legs that grip fiber the way a climber grips a rope already known. A single one, cupped (they hate this; the highland answers), makes a click so dry it might be a seed. A thousand make the hiss. A chimney that has been singing for years holds more than thousands. They nest in irregularities the plant did not intend and has not bothered to heal: old bore-holes of something larger, pockets where two growths fused and left a room. The rooms are lined with a paper of their own saliva and the dust of the crust, and the paper hums when the column hums, so the house and the instrument are the same object. They do not migrate across the open plates if they can help it. A fallen chimney is a city ending. You can watch the evacuation as a smoke that is not smoke, pouring from the break and dividing, some of it accepted into neighboring shafts, some of it taken in the air by the broad riders before it finds a door. The ones that make the long, nearly musical notes are not a separate nation. They are the same bodies at a different hour, chests full of the damp the plant keeps, using the whole hollow as a throat. Wind at the mouth does half the work. They know the wind. They start and stop with it, and the beats you feel in your teeth are arguments between shafts about which gust belongs to whom.

The herds on the open crust are the first things a walker trusts, because they look like a decision a landscape is allowed to have. Low, heavy at the shoulder, lighter in the leg than their mass suggests, they drift the joint-lines and graze the living lip without gouging it. Their mouths are wide and dry. They do not bite so much as plane, taking the soft annual growth the way a careful knife takes a curl of wood, and the plate seals behind them in a paler band you can follow for a day. They are not many, spread across a country that could hold nations of them, and they do not bunch except at the basins, where they drink in a ritual that looks like patience and is only thirst managed. Calves—or whatever the small ones are—stay inside the shadow of the adults until their hides have learned the color of the crust. A herd disturbed does not run toward cover. Cover is too far. It runs along the grain of the plates, and the sound of that running is a long dry applause, and then the chimney-nations, hearing it, change pitch, and the whole highland seems briefly to have noticed you.

Among them, not of them, are the still ones. You will pass three before you admit they are animals and not shade-errors. They stand in the lee of buttresses or in the shallow pits where a flange has slumped, and they hold until the holding becomes the fact and movement the accident. Their bodies are narrower than the herds’, higher in the hip, and their heads—if the forward mass is a head—turn with a slowness that makes the turn look geological. They take the young of the flat-lappers, and sometimes a calf that has stepped out of the adult shadow, and sometimes, it is said, a sleeper who chose the wrong flange. Proof is poor. What is sure is the stain they leave when they finally move: a darkening of the fiber, sweet when the sun is on it, gone by the next film of night. They do not call. In the troughs of the chorus you can hear them breathe, a wet catch, close, when you had thought the nearest life was the herd on the horizon. They are the reason the routes between basins bend, not the storms and not the falls. The people who notch the seed-plates know which buttresses are occupied this season. The marks are not pictures of the still ones. They are absences: a plate left blank where a warning would be too slow to read.

Above the height of the chimneys, where the local clouds tear themselves apart and reform, the riders work a different economy. Broad is the right word and not quite enough. Their span is a weather-event; their bodies, by contrast, are modest, slung beneath the wings like a cargo they have agreed to carry. They do not flap often. The crust feeds them thermals, and they spend the thermals, and when a thermal dies they side-slip to the next pylon’s rising air with an indifference that looks like wisdom. They eat what the chimneys spill: the evacuation-smoke, the condensation-flies, the occasional flat-lapper lifted too high by a gust and not let down. They also eat, rarely and visibly, one another’s young, which is why the young ride the adult’s back until the wings can find a column of heat unaided, and why a pair of riders meeting over a gap will circle for an hour in silence, sorting some claim the ear cannot audit. Their shadows on the plates are the only clocks the herds trust. When a shadow passes, grazing stops. Not from fear of being taken—the riders are not interested in that much meat—but because a shadow that size means the air is organizing, and organized air means a storm deciding, and a storm on this highland is a vertical event. Rain here falls as if poured through the riders’ corridors. They know the corridors. They are often the reason you can see the rain coming as a darkening that has edges.

There is another life, and it does not show itself to distance. In the deep flanges, where a rib has split and the split has not sealed, where the sweetness under the green smell lives, something takes what the highland kills or sheds. You do not get a body for it. You get evidence arranged like a courtesy: a herd-beast’s leg drawn half into a seam by morning, the bone cleaned in a way teeth do not clean, the fiber around the seam warmer than the day’s heat explains. The people call the process by a word that also means the sealing of a cut, and they do not use the word at basins. Whatever works there is not a nation and not a herd. It may not be animal in the sense the singers are animal. It may be the plant’s own appetite, delegated, given motion. Or it may be older tenants, from before the crust learned to be pavement, still performing a job the organism has found useful and therefore not expelled. When a column falls and the quiet after is too complete, before the neighboring shafts redistribute the pitch, there is sometimes a second sound under the quiet: a soft, industrious pulling. By the time you walk to the fall, the sound has stopped, and the dead wood is already being taught to lie flat.

The people are the least of the population and the only ones who arrange the others into routes. They are not a tribe in the dense sense. You can cross the highland for a month and meet two, or meet a line of them at a basin and not meet another until the season turns. They are dark from the sun and pale in the creases, short-haired or shaved because hair holds the sweet damp and the sweet damp draws the small mouths. Their clothes are fiber taken from dead flanges, beaten, oiled with resin, and they smell, up close in a trough of the chorus, like the basins. They carry little. A blade of shaped bone. A coil of cord. A hollow for water that is itself a cut of chimney-wall, stoppered, already trying to grow a lip across the stopper. They sleep in the galleries and the dead wood, and they post no guards, because a guard could not hear an approach under the singing and the still ones do not approach in any case if the marks are honest. Their talk, when the trough comes, is technical. Which shaft changed note. Which herd has drifted off the known joints. Which basin has gone warm, meaning the deep appetite has moved closer to the water and the water should be left for a year. They do not name the riders or the singers as spirits. They name them as conditions, the way a sailor names a tide. Children—there are few, and they are carried until they can read a blank plate—are taught the troughs first, before walking, before blades. A child who cannot wait for the quiet will not be heard, and a child who is not heard, on this crust, is already half lost.

They have a story they tell only when two troughs come close together, a surplus of silence they can spend. It is not about a beginning. It is about a chimney that sang a note no neighboring shaft could answer, and sang it for a year, and then split, and in the split there was not the usual dark traffic but a single body, large, winged in the rider’s way and voiced in the singer’s way, which rose once, cast a shadow the herds did not understand, and went up into the ordinary sun until it was neither shadow nor note. They do not say whether this was an animal or a failure of the plant or a permission. They say the shaft it left has been pavement for three generations and still, on certain winds, throws a pitch that does not belong to the living columns. You can go there. The route is marked with blank plates. Most people do not. The highland is wide, the basins are enough, and a sound that continues after its body has gone is not a destination. It is only another proof that the country keeps what it uses, and uses more than it shows, and is not lonelier for the keeping.
