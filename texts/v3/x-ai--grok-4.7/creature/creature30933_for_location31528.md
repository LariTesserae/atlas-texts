They do not begin as figures. They begin as interruptions in the meal.

A pale rise that is not a wall will shift its weight, and the shift will have a joint in it. A shadow under the vault, moving faster than the body that throws it, will resolve into something with a spine held too flat for a person and too long for a herd animal, and then the distinction will fail, because the light is complete and the color of everything is the color of the floor. You learn them by what they refuse. People refuse to lie down in the glass lanes. The long-legged herds refuse the fresh-wept flagstones until the shine has dulled to a film. The rim-keepers—the quick small ones—refuse nothing wet, and so they are the first to tell you where the basin has changed its mind.

The people are few in the way a grammar is few: enough parts to keep generating sentences, not enough to fill the page the sentences were copied from. They are built for walking that never quite arrives. Shoulders sit high and a little forward, as if the vault’s brightness were a weight they have agreed to carry on the collarbone instead of the crown. Skin is the color of the cistern crust when the crust is almost dry—chalk with a yellow under it, not a pigment so much as a long exposure. It does not tan. There is no darker noon to tan toward. It thickens on the shins and the heels and the outer forearms, where the meal works at them, into a hide that can be scraped without drawing more than a bead. Eyes are wide-set and pale, not because the dark taught them to drink light, but because the light never leaves and they have stopped squinting against it. They look at you the way they look at a wall: to see if it has moved since they last touched it.

They do not have a word you would translate as citizen. They have a word for a person who slept inside the same cluster of foundations as you the last time the mote-front passed, and a different word for a person who will be inside it the next time, and these words are not interchangeable even when they name the same body. Belonging is a tense. You can be coming-back or already-gone while you are standing still, because the damp decides the tense, and the damp does not consult anyone.

Children are carried on the hip until their feet learn the glass, which is early, and then they are not carried. They do not play in the sense of inventing a city. The city is already invented and already ruined, and invention would be a kind of lying about the grooves. What they do is practice revision. A child will build a knee-high wall at the edge of a pan, walk away while a thread of water occupies the channel, and come back to find the wall canted, and take it down without anger, and stack it two flagstones to the left, where the weeping has stopped. An adult watching does not correct this. Correction would imply there was a right place. There is only the last true place, and it expires.

Speech is spare and mostly shape. In the open, a sentence that matters is given with the hands first—the flat of the palm for wait, two fingers dragged along the forearm for the crust is thick here, a turned wrist for the herd has revised—and the mouth supplies only the part that hands cannot hold: a name, a count, the small dry click that means this vessel is empty and I am not asking you to fill it. Close in, under a hide, where the meal cannot lean into the mouth, they talk more, and the talk is practical and a little funny in a way that does not survive the open. They tease a person for trusting a wall. They invent short cruel names for cisterns that have failed them twice. They do not sing. Melody needs a wall to return it, and these walls do not return anything. What they have instead is a rhythm of work that other people can fall into: scrape, lift, turn, set. If you cannot hear a camp, you can still see whether it is in time with itself.

They eat the film and what eats the film, and they do not pretend this is a cuisine. The film, scraped fresh, is faintly sweet and then mineral, like a stone that has been in a mouth. Left too long it powders and becomes nothing you would swallow on purpose. The herds crop it with a lateral grind of teeth that are not teeth so much as ridged plates, pale as the rest of them, worn to the same polish as the lanes. People take the herds when a herd is old or injured or has simply stopped revising its path and begun to circle, which is how they die: not of hunger, usually, but of a map that has closed. The killing is quiet and close and done with glass, and the glass is abandoned afterward in the lane it came from, as if returning a word to a language. Blood darkens the meal and is gone under the next mote-front. Nothing is wasted that can be worn or burned or stitched. Nothing is kept that cannot be carried across an empty that may be wet at the far side.

There are people who do not cluster. They are not outcasts. Outcast would require a center with the authority to push. These are the ones whose hands have gone too sure. They can feel a weeping start in a wall before the wall shines, and so they walk the grooves alone, a day or three ahead of the porters, and leave signs that are not signs to anyone who does not already know them: a chip of glass set on edge, a cord of sinew knotted once around a foundation stone, a scrape in the crust in the shape of a bent arm. The clusters read these and bend. The solitary walkers sleep in the lee of whatever shoulder of stone is dry, and they are the ones most likely to be overtaken by a herd in the middle of a rest, and to wake inside a moving pale thicket and stand up slowly and be allowed, because the herds do not spook. They have forgotten spooking. Startlement needs a sound, and the basin will not keep one.

The herds are not livestock and not quite wild. They are the city’s other grammar, older in their occupation of the plan, less interested in foundations. A herd is a decision distributed across many bodies. When the damp-line shifts, you do not see a leader turn. You see a reluctance pass through them like a change in the light, and then they are facing differently, and the reluctance is gone. Calves—narrow, almost glass-colored, legs too long for the rest of the sentence—are born on the move and are standing before the cord has been mouthed off. They learn the lanes by where their mothers do not place their feet. People name individual animals only when an animal has stopped being fully of its herd: a limp that persists, a preference for one cistern, a habit of lying in the warm-cool seam beside a hide wall. Named animals are already halfway to meat. Everyone knows this and names them anyway, because a name is a way of noticing, and noticing is the only wealth that does not have to be carried.

Smaller lives occupy the seams the herds and the people both miss. Along the glass, where the black is warm from the light’s even pressure, things like flattened hands move by rippling, never lifting, feeding on the iridescent end of the film. They are the color of the glass until they cross onto chalk, and then they are an embarrassment of soft blue, and they hurry back. In the rims, after a weeping, a denser life packs the crust: many-legged, armored in the same mineral they eat, so that a rim can look like a rim until it stands up and walks a hand’s breadth and settles, and the stone is a finger lower. People brush them off their shins without dislike. They are not parasites. They are the basin being slightly more organized than dust, for a while.

Above, the flyers turn in wheels that do not center on anything you can stand under. Their bodies are light enough that the motes buffet them; a front will break a wheel into streaks and the streaks will find each other again on the far side, as if the circle had been a suggestion they were polite enough to keep. They feed on what the fronts lift—the finest meal, the spores that are not spores of the film—and they drop, rarely, when a wing takes the powder wrong. A dropped flyer is eaten by whoever is nearest, person or herd or rim, and the bones, hollow, last longer than hides last, and get used as needles until they split. No one watches the wheels for omens. Omens require a future different in kind from the present. Here the future is the present moved slightly to the left.

What they fear is not darkness. There is no darkness to practice on. They fear a stillness that lasts. A district that does not powder, does not weep, does not receive a herd for longer than a body can remember, becomes wrong. People will walk into it to check, and walk out again, and if it is still unrevised when they return, they will not roof there. Stillness is how the city died the first time, and they can read that death in the dressed stone: beautiful joints, meant to stay, and staying is what killed the idea of staying. So they keep the turbulence going even when the basin would allow a pause. They move a wall that does not need moving. They scrape a crust that could have waited. It looks like restlessness. It is maintenance of the only condition under which they know how to be alive.

They do not bury. The meal will take a body if you let it, slowly, the way it takes a wall, by leaning. They prefer to be faster than that. A death is carried to the nearest glass and opened along the long muscle, and the herd is allowed, or the rim, and what remains that is hard—teeth, the thickened shin-hide, a copper piece if the person had one—is set on the glass and left. Someone will use the copper. No one will move the teeth. The glass keeps a duller patch where a body has been rendered, and the patch weathers back, and that is as close as they come to a marker. Names do not outlast the next few mote-fronts unless a solitary walker needs them for a knot-code. Most names go out the way sound goes out: shape first, then a thin remainder, then the floor.

They are not sad in the manner of a people who have lost a city. The loss is too complete to be a mood. Sadness needs a picture of the intact thing, held beside the broken thing, and they do not have the intact thing. What they have is competence, and a humor that lives indoors, and a continuous small attention that would look, from above, like the herds’ attention: always revising, never arriving, not unfinished because it failed to finish, but unfinished because finishing is the one change the basin has already spent.

If one of them stands on the glass long enough for the light to seem to deepen, they do not feel the edges drawing in. They feel their own heels beginning to mineralize into the polish, a pleasant heaviness, the first temptation the place offers. They shift their weight. They look for the nearest sign of revision—a chip set on edge, a calf too new to be steady, a wall someone is taking down without anger—and they go toward it, not hurrying, not called, simply declining, one more time, to be the kind of stone that stays.
