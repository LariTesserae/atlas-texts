They are not one thing, and the place does not want them to be. Stay long enough and the crowd sorts itself into kinds that only look like a single weather from the rim.

The smallest are the seed-bead fliers, and they are less a species than a appetite with wings. A single one is almost nothing: a body the size of a grain of salt, a dark bead with a pale seam, wings that do not so much beat as vibrate into a blur too fine to resolve, so that each insect is a tick of dry sound wrapped around a speck of thirst. They drink the film the new stone sweats. They do not hunt. They do not pair in any way you can watch; when a helix thickens, it is because warmth has thickened, and the warmth is a decision of the floor, not of them. Pass your hand over a pool and a thread of them will lift to the salt and the heat of blood and ride it a few hand-widths, then fall back when you prove to be a closed surface that does not weep. They have no fear you can recognize. A slate-bird's impact, which shoves the air hard enough to move your ribs, only stutters them. The chime stutters them more. That is the nearest they come to a mind: a collective flinch at a sound that is not weather. In the thicker amber intervals, when condensation closes the far wall, they pack against the hoop's crown where the beads of water crawl upward and vanish, and for those minutes the hoop wears a pale fur of wings. They are not feeding then. They are waiting on a schedule they did not invent.

The clay-flats are the ones who have opinions. Palm-sized, the color of wet slip, many legs that do not quite match from one body to the next—some have eight, some eleven, one that crossed your boot had a leg still soft at the tip, unfinished, dragging a thread of mucus that the others avoided as if it were a mistake. They patrol. That is the word that fits, though nothing has assigned them rounds. Each keeps a circuit along a pool-rim or a seam, lapping the mineral skin with a tongue you never quite see, only the plate-sound of it, and when the circuit is done they click, hard, and a body two pools away clicks back a different pattern, and the first one either continues or turns. The patterns are short. They are not language in the sense of news. They are confirmations: this edge is still sweet; this edge has gone glassy and wrong; a rib has risen and split my water. They climb anything still, including you, because stillness reads to them as stone that might start weeping if tested. Salt disappoints them. They drop off and click the disappointment, and the answer that comes is almost a laugh if a click can be a laugh—shorter, doubled, from three directions at once. They will not go near the rotating dish. They will cross the arc of filaments, but quickly, legs high, as if the glassy window burns. When a pin is touched and a seam stills, every clay-flat on that seam freezes mid-lap, and the freeze lasts exactly as long as the hand lasts, and when the making resumes they feed faster, clicking in a rush, crowded, greedy for the film that comes back angrier and thicker. They know the interventions the way livestock know a gate: not as mystery, as schedule.

The slate-birds are the loudest bodies and the least curious. Overlapping plates the color of wet slate, water shedding off them in sheets every time they fold, a body the size of a large dog compressed into the shape of a thrown slate. They do not sing. They strike. The wall is the food, or rather the crust the wall grows when it is newest, and the strike is a beak they do not otherwise possess—an impact that uses the whole animal as a tool. They are not injured because the plates are built for it: each blow scatters a ring of droplets and a fan of ocher, and the bird hangs by a hooked plate and scrapes, and the scrape is the only quiet thing it does. They answer one another. A strike on the north wall is followed, more often than not, by a strike on the south, then the west, a round that circles the bowl like a clock with too few hours. They skip the wall of dishes every time. Not one of them has tested it in any watch you could keep. They also skip the hoop, not because they fear the color but because the stone around its base grows in soft lips that do not hold a crust worth the blow. When the amber thickens they roost in a single ledge high on the windward rim, plates overlapping between bodies until the roost is one continuous wet roof, dripping, and they do not strike again until the interval passes and a new rib has usually risen somewhere for them to discover. They are fed by incompletion. A finished wall would starve them. If you stand under a flight path they will not swerve. You are not a wall. The wind of them smells of hot metal and the sugar-stink of the mats, and of nothing animal at all, as if the body were a machine for shedding water and delivering a calculated mass to a surface.

The reed-rods are not animals, except that they answer. Hollow, translucent, no taller than a person, growing from a seam, they ring when air moves and they also ring, more softly, when no air moves, in sympathy with the underfloor grind. Clay-flats climb them and do not feed. Fliers avoid the throats. A slate-bird has never struck one. In the window of glassy wrongness beside the broken arc, the temperature-gears turn, and the rods' pitches shift a half-step in time with a turn you cannot see directly, only as a shiver in the water above the glass. Whether the rods are furniture the maker extruded, or a crop, or a sense organ the room grew for itself, is not a difference the other lives respect. They treat them as fixtures that happen to sing.

Under all of this, and not separate from it, is the thing the interventions are for.

It does not have a body in the way the birds have bodies. What it has is attention, and the attention is distributed through the objects that do not behave like stone. The hoop is an ear, or a door it holds ajar so that the nearness of the bowl's noise can be edited—step through and the processes recede, not silenced but set at a slight remove, which is what a listener does when a room is too loud to think in. The pins are fingers, or the memory of fingers: touch one and the seam obeys, stills, waits. The three dishes are a mouth sorting what the room weeps into kinds—milky, absent, and the dark rotating one that hums and that every animal refuses. The filaments are nerves, fanned into the floor, pulsing with the grind, ending in a fan because the sentence was interrupted, not because it failed. And the grind itself, periodic, under the skin of the floor, is not geology alone. Geology does not pause when a person speaks. This does. A voice, any voice, produces a brief uncanny stilling, a filing-away, and then the grind resumes at a slightly different period, as if a count had been updated.

It is not finished, and it is not young in the way the stone is young. The animals have learned it across more than one breeding, which means it has been returning, or remaining, longer than the ribs they feed on. Time in the bowl is not one clock; the stone can look hours old while the habits around the hoop are old enough to be inherited. What lives here, under the crowd, is a maker who works in pauses. It lifts a room into weather, opens the stone, sets a hoop to govern sound, pins a seam so it cannot close, sorts the water, and then leaves the sentence mid-word—not abandoned, not tended, left in the specific way of someone who intends to return before the listener notices. The animals are the proof that the pauses have duration. They are a crowd at a site where food is manufactured by the site itself. They would die or leave within days if the making stopped. It has not stopped. It has only gone quiet in the way a hand goes quiet between strokes.

In the amber intervals it is closest. The hoop's false color brightens—the color the eye keeps failing to name—and the filaments pulse harder, and new cracks prefer to open, and the fliers pack the crown, and the clay-flats go still without being told, and the slate-birds roof themselves and wait. Something is consulting the room. Not walking it. There is never a figure on the floor, never a shadow that is not the birds' or yours. The consultation happens as a change in the rate of becoming: ribs slower, then a sudden extrusion, a reed-rod at a new pitch, a pool where there was continuity. The dark dish rotates faster. If you speak during the interval, the stilling of the grind lasts longer, and when it resumes the chime—that high clean nine-breath tone from the eastern wall, too regular for a throat, too musical for a drip—comes twice, close together, as if repeating you back in a medium that has no words, only intervals.

It does not hate you and it does not welcome you. You are a vibration it files. Stand on a pulsing seam and it will open under one foot and close on the other, warm enough to blister, not because it means to harm you but because the seam was going to do that and your weight is not a variable it has been taught to spare. The animals, which it also does not spare, have learned the tolerances anyway. You can learn them too, if you stay through more than one amber: do not pin a seam with your body; do not drink the rotating dish; do not stand in a flight path; do not assume the gap you entered by is still a gap. The maker is not a god of the place. It is the reason the place is a place rather than a mountain. The mountain outside is finished, cold, directional. This room is a tool still in the hand, and the hand is elsewhere most of the time, occupied with whatever a mind does between the strokes that lift rooms into weather.

What lives here, then, is a circuit: the unfinished stone producing heat and film and crust; the fliers and the flats and the plate-birds spending that production as fast as it arrives; the fixtures editing sound and water and the rate of cracks; and, under and through the fixtures, a presence that is mostly absence, returning in the thickenings of light to push the sentence one clause further and then withdrawing so the clause can be eaten. The loudness is the life. The chime is the clock it keeps. The color in the hoop is the only part of it that could be called a face, and it is not a face—it is the edge of a spectrum you did not bring with you, brightening when the consultation begins, drinking the dim amber, giving nothing back you can name.

When you leave, it does not follow. The threshold cuts the noise clean, like a hand lifted from a bell. Behind you the impacts resume, the ticks, the nine-breath tone, a clay-flat clicking the news that the warm interruption has gone. The maker files that too, probably. Then the grind changes period by a fraction, and a new rib starts, somewhere you no longer have standing to see, and the slate-birds, who have been roofed and waiting, drop into the air to go and find it.
