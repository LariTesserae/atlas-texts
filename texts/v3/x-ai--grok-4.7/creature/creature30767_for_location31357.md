The first thing that lives here is not an animal. It is the film itself, and it takes a long time to notice that it is living, because it does not travel.

Along every bed the weep keeps, there is a skin no thicker than a nail, warm, slick, faintly sweet where you put a finger to it and then to your mouth. It looks, if looking were possible, like nothing: a darker wet on wet. Leave a hand in a channel and the skin climbs the wrist in the time it takes to count the drips off the strap-forest, not fast, not purposeful, simply occupying whatever new surface has entered its water. Pull the hand out and the climb stops at the line where the film thins to air. It does not pursue. It has no need. Everything in this country returns to the water eventually, and the skin is patient in the way stone is patient, which is to say it has already won the only argument it is having.

What it does is sort. The stations put metals and oils and heat into the weep, and the skin takes some of them up and lets others pass, and the beds downstream of a hall run cleaner than the beds beside it, and the growth that drinks those beds grows in a different register — less of the wrong smell, more of the clean mineral. No one tends it. Where a channel is blocked by a fall of the soft columns, the skin dies back to the last moving water and waits, and when the herds' feet or the rain's weight opens the bed again it is there in a night, continuous with itself, as if it had only been holding its breath under the rubble. Break it and it does not bleed. It thins, and the thin place fills from both ends until the hand cannot find the wound. It has been doing this since before the first pipe. The pipes, where they still carry, are lined with it. The crews — there are crews — have learned not to scrape a line clear all the way to the metal, because a line scoured bare throws its load raw into the next station's intake, and the next station's note goes wrong, and wrong notes move herds.

The herds come next, and they are the country's clock.

They are heavy and low and they do not lift their heads for anything that is only air. A shoulder comes to the middle of a thigh. The back is a long slope, hide thick enough that the rain beads and runs off in strings, and under the hide a fat that stays warm to the touch even when you meet one that has been dead a day in a seep-flat, the skin already climbing it. The feet are broad and blunt and they knock, and the knock is not careless. In a file of them the interval between knocks is nearly regular, a walking meter, and where the stone changes — a dressed survey course swallowed under the mat, an old rail's groove, the lip of a master-channel — the meter changes with it, a half-beat, a gathering, and then the file has the new ground and the meter resumes. They are reading with their feet. Eyes, if they have them, are not the organ that matters; the head is mostly mouth and the long bones of the jaw, and the jaw stays in the film, and the tongue, or whatever works in place of a tongue, sorts the skin the way the skin sorts the metals. They are grazing a chemistry. A herd that has been on one station's water for a season tastes of that station. A herd that has crossed three tastes of the route.

They do not bed down in the open flats. At the long intervals when the knocking thins — never stops, only thins, a fraction of the file always still working — the mass of them draws up against the soft columns on the lee of a pressure-cell, shoulders touching, the rain off the upper storey running down their backs into a trench their own weight has pressed into the mat. In that trench the skin is thickest. Calves, or the small heavy shapes that are not yet the size of the file, stand inside the ring of adult legs where the knocking of the outside world arrives already sorted, and they learn the notes that way, body to body, before they ever walk a bed alone. A station that changes its stack — a pump replaced, a conveyor brought online, a hall cycling down — does not lose its herd in a day. The old note is in the animals' feet and in their fat. They return to where the new note is wrong and stand in it, knocking out of meter, until either the hall is corrected or enough of the old ones die that the calves, who learned the new note from the rain and not from their mothers' ribs, become the file. This has happened. The abandoned halls with their slow unmanned turbines sit in pockets of lesser traffic because the generations that knew them are gone, and the generations that remain have redrawn the song.

Between the columns, above the reach of the heavy mouths, the country is owned by something quicker and much more numerous, and it announces itself before it is anything you could put a hand on.

Clicks, short, dry, pitched to cut the rain. Then the whistles, which are not alarms so much as positions: I am here, the next drop is mine, the strap to your left is taken. They run the soft columns and the straps and they rarely come down to the film. A body the length of a forearm, light, the limbs arranged more for wrapping than for standing, so that at rest one of them is almost a knot on the column and in motion it is a poured line, up, across, down, never in the air longer than a fall requires. They eat the straps. They also eat each other, in a small way, at the edges of a territory when the pressure-cell has been fat and the straps are heavy with caught water and there is enough to spend on a fight. The fights are loud and brief and then the chorus seals over them as if they had been weather.

What they are for, in the economy of the shelf, is range. A herd knows its valley and the notes at the edge of hearing. The climbers know the deck. They go up until the pole would touch cloud, and they live in that wet, and their whistles come down through the storeys with information the ground cannot have: a cell assembling two valleys over, a strap-fall that has opened a new corridor, the particular sweet-metal reek of a station's chimney when the wind inside the deck turns and pushes the exhaust down instead of along. The herds do not speak to them. They do not need to. A climber chorus that goes thin along one ridge is a fact, and the files adjust before the rain arrives to explain it. Predators learn the same grammar. Everything that hunts here is, first, a student of who has stopped talking.

The hunters are not one animal. That is the correction that comes with staying. At first the subtracted sounds all seem to belong to a single method, and you invent a single body to match it — low, patient, inserted into the noise. There are animals like that. One works the seep-flats at the edge of a file, matching the knock so well that the subtraction, when it comes, is a single missing beat and then a smell of iron, and the file closes the gap and the meter resumes and the taken animal is already down in the film, the skin climbing, the hunter a weight on it no larger than a half-grown calf, broad-footed, jaw in the seam of the belly where the hide is thinner. It does not roar. It eats to the pace of the rain, and if the file turns back toward it the file's knocking changes register and the hunter leaves the carcass rather than argue, because argument is loud and loud is how the other kind finds you.

The other kind does not match. It interrupts. It lives higher than the herds and lower than the climbers, in the middle storey where the straps shed their private rain, and it hunts by striking the chorus: a run of whistles cut in the middle, a scatter, a new rhythm as the climbers re-mark a territory around a hole. What drops from that hole is small. The hunter is not. It comes down the columns with a weight that makes the soft trunks bow, and it can cross a seep-flat at a speed the files have no answer for except absence — the knocking simply ends in a fan, every animal taking the nearest bed, meter abandoned, and the flat is left to the rain and to whatever the hunter has caught. These are rare. You can cross three stations and never be in one's weather. Where one dens, in the lee of a ridge that touches the deck, the climbers do not colonize the upper storey at all, and the herds route long around, and the local note of the nearest hall arrives with a gap in it, a slice of the song unwalked, which the crews read as reliably as a gauge.

The crews are the part that takes longest to grant the status of something that lives here, because they still have the shape of people who came from somewhere with a sky, and some of them did. Not most. Not anymore.

They are few, in the way the stations are few, and they are distributed in the same pattern: a handful to a hall, days of wet travel between, no lamps, no windows that were ever about light. A hall-crew knows its own stack the way a herd knows a bed. They walk the pumps with a hand on the housing, and the wrongness in a bearing announces itself through the palm before it announces itself in the note the valley hears. They cut the growth. They do not scrape the skin. They sleep in the long sheds where the process is loudest, because loudest is most constant, and constancy is the only dark that can be slept in; out in the strap-forest the noise keeps changing its mind, and a body that still flinches at a subtracted whistle does not rest. New arrivals from below the shelf — there is a below, there are roads that climb into the deck and do not come out the other side as roads — spend their first season flinching, and talking too much, and striking lights they were told not to strike. The lights do nothing useful and they draw the climbers, who have no experience of fire and treat it as weather worth re-marking, and a re-marked upper storey brings the heavy hunter's attention closer to a wall than anyone wants it. The lesson holds. After a season the new ones flinch less. After a generation their children are born into the sheds and have never seen a lamp and do not miss it, and their eyes, whatever they do in the dark, are not how those children navigate the mat.

What the children have, and the old crews have, and the best of the herds have in their feet, is a map made of difference. A station's note is not a point. It is a texture with edges: the pump hall underfoot, the conveyor's higher whine bleeding through the shed wall, the chimney's breath on the skin of the face when you stand in the door, the way all three of these bend when a cell is overhead and the rain on the roof adds its own layer. Leave the door and the texture thins in a direction. Walk that thinning and another texture rises to meet it, and the meeting place is a valley, and the valley has a name that is not a word so much as a remembered chord. The crews' speech has gone that way over the centuries of the work. They still have words, for tools, for the parts of a pump, for the few things that must be asked. For the country they have intervals. A route is recited as a sequence of changes, and a child can follow a recited route across a district they have never walked, correcting for rain, correcting for a hall they can hear is cycling down, arriving at a door they have never touched and knowing it is the door because the chord matches the mouth that sent them.

They are not kind to each other in any way that would read as kindness in a lit town, and they are not cruel in any way that would require an audience. A hand that fails is a hand that gets someone killed by a wrong note, and wrong notes move herds, and herds in the wrong flat in the wrong cell are a wall of meat against a shed, and sheds do not survive that more than once or twice in a generation. So the work is exact, and the exactness is the bond, and people who cannot keep it leave, or are left, and the shelf takes them at the same rate it takes a calf that cannot find the meter. The skin does not distinguish. This is understood. It is not spoken of as tragedy. Tragedy requires a world in which the dark is an interruption of something else. Here the something else is the interruption, and it comes up the roads, and it does not stay long, and the people who stay are the ones the country has already mostly digested.

There is one thing more, and it is the thing the others are arranged around, though none of them would put it that way.

In the oldest districts, where the survey stones have lost their edges and the first halls are fossils in the sense that the rock has closed over them and the weep runs through the cavities they left, there are places the files will not cross and the climbers will not re-mark and the crews' recitations go around with a silence in the chord, a rest, the way a song keeps a beat by not striking it. The silence is not empty. Stand at the edge of one — you find the edge by the herds' refusal, which is a clean line, meters abandoned a full body's length before the mat changes — and the noise of the shelf arrives, and inside the line it arrives differently. Flatter. The rain is there. The drip off the columns is there. What is missing is the small life: no clicks, no whistles, no knock, no hunter's interruption. The skin in the beds is thicker than anywhere, a sleeve in the water, and the air is the clean mineral smell with no iron in it and no oil. People who have walked in and walked out — a few, over the years, curious or lost or proving something to a shed — report that the quiet is not quiet at all once you are inside it. It is a sound below the sounds, a long friction, the same friction as the master-channels but slower, and it comes from under the stone, and it does not vary with the cells. They do not stay to map it. The skin climbs faster there. A boot left in a bed for the length of a meal comes out softer at the seams. Nothing rises to meet them. Nothing needs to.

The crews call these rests by not calling them, which is as much name as the country requires. The herds treat them as halls that changed their note so long ago that no calf living learned the old one, and so the absence is itself the instruction. Whether anything in there is an animal, or a larger skin, or only the shelf's own attention, pooled where the work has been sunk deepest and then forgotten, is not a question anyone on the shelf is equipped to ask. They do not have the dark for it. They have the other dark, the working one, full of meters and chords and the knock of files crossing at the hour the cell said they would.

That is what lives here. A chemistry with a patience. A clock with hooves. A chorus in the straps. A few shapes that eat by editing. People who have become a kind of ear. And, under the oldest stone, a held breath the size of a district, which everything else routes around, and which has not yet decided whether it is going to breathe out.
