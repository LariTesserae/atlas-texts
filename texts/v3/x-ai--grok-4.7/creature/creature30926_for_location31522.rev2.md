They do not look, at first, like a people who have ever been anywhere else. The dim makes that easy to believe. At the distance where color fails, a figure in a lane is only a darker movement against wet stone, shoulders slightly forward, head turned as if listening sideways. Closer, the listening is the first true thing about them. The eyes are there, and they work, but they are not the organ the city trained. Lids sit low. The iris is a narrow ring around a pupil that stays wide even under the gray lid of noon, and the gaze does not lock; it checks, confirms, and returns to the hands. What the eyes miss, the ears have already placed: the higher note of a narrowing race, the change in clatter when a hoof slips, the particular cry that means a pen-wall has shifted by a hand’s breadth. A person can cross a lane with a full basket and never quite look at the person coming toward them, and neither will collide. The negotiation happens in volume and in the small corrections of the feet, which know the packed ridge where the water is only a film over clay and shell.

The body is compact and warm and always a little wet. Skin takes the basin’s color—wet ash, warm bone—and holds a sheen that is not sweat so much as the air deciding to stay. Hands and feet are paler where the water has leached them, the pads thick, the nails short and ridged, more tool than ornament. There is no lean season to hollow a cheek and no feast to swell it, so faces keep one measure all through a life: broad across the cheek, short in the jaw, mouth closed by habit because the air is full. The cloth across the lower face is not costume. It is a second lip, changed when it slicks with the copper film, beaten clean against a wall and tied again. Under it the breath is slow. They do not pant. The work is continuous, and a body that spent itself in bursts would be a body that paused, and a pause is how water backs up.

They eat with the same continuity. The paste from the mills—film, fish, bone, a thread of salt—is warm and faintly metallic, and it is taken standing, from a clay cup hooked to a belt of gut-cord, between one task and the next. No one waits to be hungry. Hunger, like quiet, is a failure of arrangement. Children learn the cup before they learn any story: three mouthfuls, the basket shifted, the net shaken, the cup again. Teeth are strong, close-set, yellowish from the mineral, worn flat early by grit that no rinsing quite removes. There is little speech at meals because there are no meals. Speech happens inside the work, at a shout, and it is mostly numbers, directions, and the names of faults. A cracked wheel-peg. A race silted at the bend. A bird-line on a roof that has shifted toward a cistern. The language is large in its verbs of maintenance and small in its verbs of arrival. Nothing arrives. The grammar does not bother to pretend.

Names are procedural too, though they do not think of them that way. A child is called by the sound-mark of the place they were born into—the mill-note, the pen-clatter, the spring that sings higher—and by a second sound that is theirs alone, a short cry the household uses when the general roar would swallow a longer word. These calls do not describe ancestry. Ancestry is the line of hands that set the same wall back into the same course. When someone dies, the call is dropped the way a worn peg is dropped: not mourned in public, not kept as a relic, simply no longer used, because using it would summon a weight that is not at the wheel. The body goes into the circuit without ceremony that an outsider would recognize as ceremony. It is opened, shared with the pens and the film-pools in the proportions the circuit already knows, the bones dried and sacked for meal or for needles, the skin cured if it will take a cure. People speak of this as returning a tool to the rack. They are not cruel about it. Cruelty would require a difference between the dead and the fish, and the city has spent its whole memory refusing that difference. What they feel, if feeling is the word, is the brief wrongness in the count—one less set of hands at a platform—and the correction that follows by the end of the day, a younger pair of hands moved over from a less urgent station. Grief, if it happens, happens in the private interval between one shout and the next, and it does not alter the volume.

They are not identical. The basin’s sameness is a pressure, not a mold. Some are taller by a head and get sent to the flag-roofs, where reach matters and the bat-colonies must be shifted without panic. Some have a finer ear and stand at the races, eyes half-shut, listening for the pitch that means a carcass has caught. Some are restless in a way the city cannot quite use and become the ones who walk the rim, not outward—no one keeps walking outward—but along the last course of walls, checking that the empty wet stone beyond is still empty, still sharp, still offering nothing the baskets can take. They come back hoarse and a little foreign for an hour, as if the drop in noise had rinsed them, and then the city fills them again. There are arguments. They are about sequence, not about desire. Whose turn at the mill-stone. Whether a pen should be split before or after the film is scraped. Voices rise, and the rise is only a sharper edge on the same roar, and when the sequence is settled the voices drop without apology. Apology would be an event. Events are what they do not have, and they do not miss them in any way they can say.

Children are not kept apart from the noise. A cradle of lashed bone hangs where a worker can reach it without leaving the platform, and the child sleeps inside the sound the way a fish sleeps in current. They walk early, because the lanes teach balance or they take a knee against stone, and a scraped knee is packed with the dried film that serves for everything from food to binder to poultice. They learn the wrong notes before they learn the right ones: the silence that means a wheel has stopped, the splash that means something has left its pen, the thinning of the fly-haze that means a pool has been stripped by a mouth that should not have been there. Play is real, but it is play with the tools of maintenance. They race baskets. They imitate the bird-stab until an adult’s hand corrects the angle, not as discipline, as tuning. By the time they are strong enough to lean on a mill-handle, they already speak in counts. A childhood here is not a passage from one world to another. It is the gradual increase of the load a body can carry without changing the city’s pitch.

At night the light drops from poor to almost none, and they do not become different animals. Lamps—fat in clay, a wick of twisted gut—burn in the lower rooms where stores must be watched, and along a few lanes where a race is temperamental in the dark. Most work continues by touch and by ear. Sleep is taken in shifts, in upper rooms that are only slightly less wet, on mats of hide stuffed with the down of the day-fliers. A sleeper’s breath joins the city’s breath and is not missed when it pauses, because another has already woken to the same wheel. Dreams, when they tell them, are almost always about sound going wrong: a note that will not resolve, a clatter that moves away instead of toward, a quiet that spreads from the rim inward like a stain. They laugh at these dreams in the morning, short and loud, and the laugh is relief. The wheels are turning. The quiet was only sleep.

There is one habit that does not fit the circuit as neatly as the rest, and they do not have a work-name for it. Sometimes, in the hour when one shift lies down and the other has not yet taken the full weight, a person will stand with both hands flat on a wall of raw stone and hold still. Not praying—there is no figure at the center, no name spoken upward. The stone is still new. You can feel the grain it had when it first lay exposed, the glassy joint, the edge that rain has not been allowed to round. Under the palms it is cool, then warm, as if the city’s heat were only waiting for a skin to enter it. They stand until someone needs the lane. Then they take their hands away, and where the hands were the polish is a little deeper, two faint ovals among thousands of others, none of them dated, all of them the same gesture repeated until it has become part of the mineral. If you asked what they were doing they would say they were listening. It would not be a lie. Under the slap and the grind and the cries there is the spring’s own note, older than the wheels, the sound of water rising through stone that has only just become ground. They put their hands on it the way you put your ear to a chest. They are checking that the larger body is still there. It always is. That is the whole of their theology, and they would not call it that, and they do not need it to change.

What lives in the basin is not separate from the basin. The people, the fish in their granular schools, the wrong-goats, the long gray birds, the day-fliers shoulder to shoulder under the flags, the film that sheets and does not rise—these are organs of one continuous warmth. The people are the part that knows the count. They are the part that corrects. They are the hands that keep the edges from learning how to be old, not because they love sharpness, but because sharpness is what the stone still is, and their life is the interval in which that is still true. They will not be the last thing in the basin. The springs were there before the walls, and the film will sheet after the last call is dropped. But while the wheels turn they are the basin’s attention, animal and exact, never soft, never finished, eating the circuit and being eaten by it, shout by shout, in a light that is never quite enough and a noise that is always exactly enough.
