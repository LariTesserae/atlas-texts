What lives here does not arrive as a figure against a ground. It arrives as traffic, and only afterward does a body detach from the traffic long enough to be followed.

The first to accept the eye are the runners of contour. They are not birds, though the word keeps offering itself and being refused. The arms are feathered; the faces are bare, dark, and too knowing of edges. They do not fly so much as spend the air in short debts, launching from a frond to a shoulder of rock to the back of whatever is already moving. A column of them is a single decision distributed through hundreds of chests. When the column kinks, the ones in the middle do not look for the path. They step onto the ones below until the kink opens, and then they are separate again, insulted by nothing, remembering nothing of having been walked on. They eat what the larger bodies shake loose: seeds still in the cup of a crushed flower, insects jarred from bark, the pale tips of shoots that were underground an hour ago. Their calls are the high weave in the valley’s shout, so constant that a gap in them is what you hear, not the sound itself. A gap means something large has passed close, and they have lifted as one skin and not yet settled.

Under them, and often through them, move the disputers of the seep. These are low, throat-heavy, and quarrelsome at a scale that has nothing to do with threat. The warm water beading from the young rock is not scarce in any way a map would understand, and still they pack the bead-line shoulder to shoulder, coughing, shouldering, drinking, and coughing again, as if the argument were the drink. Their hides take the color of the mud they have just left, so that a body leaving the seep seems to un-draw itself from the ground and, ten paces on, to draw itself back in. You learn them by the low register. Three answers come back from the scarp for every call, and the animal does not seem confused by this. It calls into a place that answers in chorus and treats the chorus as company. Their feet are broad. They do not cut themselves on the glassy ribs of basalt, or they do and do not stop; the basin does not keep a record of small blood. What it keeps is the flattened path their arguing makes along the warm line, a path that the next rain softens and the next argument remakes.

The branch-carriers are harder to hold still in the mind because they are built to be miscounted. Hide like cracked mud, drying even while it moves. Legs: enough, and not arranged for looking. One of them pauses where a thorn-branch has been torn half off a soft trunk, worries the branch free with the side of its mouth, and carries it sideways. Not as food. As a part. It walks into a wall of leather-vine and the branch goes first, and the vines part around the thorn the way they will not part around the muzzle. Behind it, two more have found branches of their own, or have taken up branches already dropped. There is no circle of watching. There is proximity. The habit passes the way heat passes, from body to body, and fails the same way: a carrier drops the branch when the thicket opens, and the branch is a branch again before the drip from its last use has quit the leaves. By the time the light—such as it is—has shifted, growth has begun to claim the wood. Nothing in the basin consents to stay a tool. The knowledge consents to stay only by moving.

The insects are not a background. They are a surface that happens to be alive. They travel in sheets over bark, over fern, over the cracked-mud hides, over a boot if a boot is foolish enough to become still. One the color of wet bark and the length of a forearm flows across leather without a change in pace, reading the new country with the same attention it gave the log. Most of them are smaller than that and more numerous than attention can honor. They are the reason a fallen branch does not remain a fallen branch. They are in it before the animals that knocked it down have cleared the next ridge. Their sound is the grit inside the roar, the part of the noise that does not echo from the scarp because it is too small to travel and too total to escape. Stand near a tear in the canopy when a dull gap of sky shows, and you see them as motes in the failed light, rising off the wet wood in a brief glittering argument with the air, then settling as the gap seals.

Larger still, and mostly rumor, are the shoulder-herds. You meet them as aftermath more often than as bodies. A thicket that was a wall is a lane. The lane smells of crushed fern and of the particular heat of many flanks. Far ahead, or off to the side where a flow-ridge makes a corridor, there is a trot that is almost panic and is not: it is only the speed at which bodies can move when there is no room to move otherwise. They do not flee. Fleeing would require an elsewhere. They travel, and their traveling is a local apocalypse that the basin has already budgeted for. Shoots come up in the torn ground while the last of them is still in earshot. The smaller lives are in the wreckage before the wreckage has finished being made, eating what was shaken loose, and being eaten in turn by whatever hunts the eaters of shaken things. The herds’ calls, when they give them, are not the seep-cough and not the high weave. They are a blunt syllable, repeated, and the scarp sends it back late, so that the rear of the herd is always answering a front that has already changed its mind.

There is another presence, and it takes longer to become itself, because it is not a species.

It is the way a gap organizes.

Where a sink-line runs—where the brief streams go under the uncracked sill and the calling animals avoid the vanishing—the noise thins by a degree so small you would miss it if the rest of the valley were not so full. That thinning is inhabited. Not by emptiness. By attention of a different grain. The runners of contour will cross the sink-line in a rush, all at once, spending their air-debts faster, unwilling to be the last one over. The seep-disputers will not drink there even when a warm thread beads out beside the cold one; they crowd the warm and leave the quiet road alone. The branch-carriers, crossing, drop what they carry and pick up again on the far side, as if a tool were a liability precisely where the sound fails. Something uses that reluctance. You do not see it at first. You see the reluctance, and then you see that the reluctance has a schedule.

A cane-brake knocks in a gust off the scarp—the only wind, a punch, not a breeze—and in the half-second after the knock, before the roosting sheets of insects have resettled, a low place among the canes is no longer the shape it was. Fronds that were leaning are not leaning. A patch of leaf-mold has been turned, not stacked, the sharp side down, the way the basin turns stones into brief standing-places, except nothing stood there to turn it. The patch is simply no longer arranged as falling would arrange it. And a small runner, the last of a column, is not on the far side with the others.

No cry. The high weave closes over the absence the way water closes over a finger. Within three heartbeats the column has kinked onward and the missing one is not a fact the column can carry. The basin does not keep absences. It keeps density. Density repairs itself.

What took the runner is not large in the way the herds are large. It cannot be; the sink-line is a thread, and the animals avoid it without ever breaking into the full trot of a wall of bodies meeting a wall of bodies. If it were herd-big, the avoidance would be a stampede, and the stampede would be news, and news here lasts as long as an echo. This is older than news and younger than the rock: a habit of hunger sized exactly to the width of a reluctance. It waits in the relative quiet because the relative quiet is the only place a single removal does not announce itself. It does not need a den. A den would be a structure, and the basin eats structures. It needs the sink, the punch of wind, the half-second of resettling, and a body that has learned the landscape’s one rule: do not be separable.

You begin to find its other signatures once you know to read missing arrangements rather than missing animals. A flat stone that should have been a standing-place, turned sharp-side down again not by growth but by a reversal, the flatter face hidden, as if something had used the flat and then unmade the use. A vine tested and released—not eaten, not climbed—left with the crook in it that a grip leaves, at the height of nothing you have seen walking. The cracked-mud carriers will not retrieve a branch that has lain across the sink-line, even a good one, even one with the thorn still mean. They strip a fresh branch on their own side. The refusal is not taught. It moves by proximity, like the carrying itself.

At the warm seeps the presence does not hunt. The argument there is too loud, too many-throated, too willing to become a single packed body at the touch of anything that is not more seep. It wants the places where the valley’s shout dips just enough for one subtraction to be absorbed. It is not cruel. Cruelty would require the subtracted life to remain a story. Here the story is the part that does not survive the minute. Creation and destruction share the wreckage equally: the runner is gone, and already a shoot is up in a hoof-print three paces away, and already a sheet of bark-colored legs is dismantling the shoot, and already something is coming for them, loud, numerous, unsecret.

What lives in the basin, then, is a speed. The runners, the disputers, the carriers, the sheets, the herds—these are how the speed is bodied. The thing in the sink-line is how the speed is edited, one separable life at a time, precisely so the density never thins enough to see itself thin. None of them are old. The rock under them still remembers fire. Their habits have no ancestry in this soil because this soil has no ancestry; it is leaf-felt, this year’s, over basalt that has not learned roundness. And still the habits are complete. Branch, grip, chorus, kink, reluctance, subtraction. A whole ecology with the manners of an inheritance, standing on ground that has not yet earned a memory.

If you stay, you will be indexed. An arm is a contour; a boot is a log; a held breath is a rhythm that the shout will swallow until you cannot find it again. The runners will use you. The sheets will read you. The carriers will not hand you anything—they do not hand—but one may drop a thorn-branch across your feet and take up another without looking back, and the habit will have touched you and moved on. The seep-disputers will not make room. The herds will not notice you until you are in the lane, and then they will treat you as thicket.

And the quiet road, if you cross it last, or slowly, or while the canes are knocking and the insects have not yet settled—the quiet road will notice you in the only way this place notices anyone. Not as a visitor. As a shape that has become separable from the roar.
