The tenants do not enter. They accrue.

Begin with the ones that never quite land. The swifts own the column of light and nothing else. They do not nest in the honeycombs; the cells are too still for them, the walkway too low. They sleep on the wing somewhere beyond the rim and arrive only when the disk of sky has gone white enough to cut their silhouettes clean. Six is a common number, though the sound invents more. They turn inside a volume no wider than a room, wingtips ticking brick, and each tick is a correction, not a collision—they know the batter of the wall the way a hand knows a banister in the dark. What they eat is the drift that falls in with the light: midges risen off the trough, a beetle that chose the wrong moment to open its wing-cases on the rim. They do not drink here. They do not stay past the hour when the shadow-blade reaches the far curve. When they go, the court loses its highest register and seems, for a breath, larger.

Below them, the stone-knock birds are the opposite bargain. They do not fly the column. They occupy the dusk under the walkway and the cells three deep above it, and they treat the court as a house with too many rooms. Their nests are mud and fiber packed to the module of a brick, the mouths worn smooth by the same breasts season after season. A pair will hold a run of five cells and ignore the sixth even when it is empty, as if the sixth belonged to a rule older than vacancy. Their call is two stones. It is not alarm and not song. It is a count: I am here, the wall is here, you are not on my ledge. They answer one another in volleys that the brick multiplies until a quarrel between two sounds like a parish. They foul the piers into black glass and seem proud of the glaze, or at least unbothered; rain polishes it and they return to the same perches, feet dark with their own weather. They drink at the shield-basin in the last of the light, one at a time, never both, and the one waiting knocks once, softly, as if reminding the water it is being used.

The lizards are the court’s masonry made briefly animal. Mortar-colored, throat-pulse the only confession, they hold the cells at eye-height along the lower walk and the ledges where sun from the disk can still reach. A swift’s shadow is the only thing that moves them in daylight, and even then it is a pulse, not a flee. They hunt by waiting inside a flaw. Beetles misread them as brick and walk onto a tongue. At dusk they come down to the channels—not to swim, to lick the film off the tile where the drip has just passed—and their bellies leave faint pale smears that the next drop erases. They shed against the rough of a corbel. The skins stay, translucent, finger-long, snagged until a knock-bird steals one for a nest and the lizard, new and dull, does not seem to notice the theft.

Beetles work the seams the way a file works a joint: patient, dry, audible only in the gaps the swifts leave. Some are dark and oval and live entirely in the damp under the walkway, eating what the guano and the lime together make of fiber and dead midge. Others are narrower, bronze at the rim of the elytra, and they climb the batter at an angle so constant you could set a tool by it. They do not form colonies so much as a traffic. A seam that was quiet at noon is a road by midafternoon, and the road has rules: up on the left of the drip-track, down on the right, and where two traffic-lines meet at a heaved tile they stack, briefly, a little tower of shells, then unstack without apparent decision. Their rasping is the court’s smallest continuous sound. If you put a palm to the brick you feel it before you hear it.

The water has its own parish, and it is stricter.

In the shield-basin the frogs are no bigger than a thumb and the color of wet tile. They do not chorus except after rain, when the basin has risen a handspan and the channels are speaking; then the bowl fills with a noise like pebbles poured slowly into a jar, and as the water falls the noise thins to single notes and stops, each frog retiring to the crack it came from as if the song had been a duty discharged. Between rains they are almost ornamental. One sits on the lip. One sits under the lip, only the eyes above the film. They eat what the drip delivers and what the beetles lose. They do not cross the dry pavement. The court, for them, is three wet places and the brief wet roads between, and the rest is country they do not acknowledge.

The fish are the scandal, and they have outlived the scandal. Pale, fingerling, a little too large now for the word, they hold the long north trough and, in the wet season, the cup at the pier where the stalactite drills. They should not be here. There is no stream, no inlet a fish could have climbed, no keeper who stocks a cistern for sport. Yet the trough has a population with ages in it: the small ones that still read as slips of light, the thicker ones that have learned the shadow of the sedge and wait there, and one, heavier, that noses the overflow lip as if remembering a river that was never attached to this brick. They live on the drip’s cargo—midge, frog-spawn when the frogs miscalculate, the occasional beetle that trusts a wet tile—and on each other, without drama. A rise to the surface is a pop the court turns into a finger against clay. They do not school. The trough is too narrow for a school and too known. Each has a station: under the sedge, at the kink where a tile heaved, in the shallow where the channel delivers. When the basins fall after rain they concentrate, and the concentration is the nearest thing the court has to a crowd. Then the water sinks to its ordinary secretive measure and the crowd becomes three or four pale thoughts again, holding still enough that a knock-bird drinking at the far end does not register them as food, or registers them and declines, having learned the trough is deeper than a beak.

The sedge is not generous and is not eaten down. Blades bent, yes, by the birds that drink around it and by the heavier fish that brushes it from below, but the clump stays, rooted in a crack with no depth, living on lime-water and whatever dust the rim sheds. The moss at the lip is a slower animal: brown at the heart, emerald only where it advances, stitching the condensation and never making a mat. Liverworts occupy the film and look like the film. Nothing here competes in the open. The plants have accepted the terms the pavement set—surface, patience, no deep—and the animals have accepted the plants as furniture.

There is a slower tenant that does not eat and does not call. From the corbel that was meant to be dry, lime has been leaving the mortar for longer than the nests have been mud. It hangs now as a finger of stone, and the drop at its tip is the same drop, in kind, as every other drop in the court, only more patient. It falls into a cup it is itself drilling in the pier’s base. The frogs avoid the cup; the water there is harder, and the drip arrives with a different tick, higher, as if the stone were counting in a smaller unit. The stalactite will meet the floor. Everything else in the court behaves as if this meeting were weather—not an event to prepare for, a climate already included in the rules. Beetles climb around it. A knock-bird has never nested on that corbel. The refusal is the only acknowledgment.

And people.

They are not absent, and they are not residents in the way the fish are residents. The stair is dished because feet have used it, and the walkway’s undersides are stained because hands have steadied themselves, and the repairs—the smaller brick, the sistered pier, the channel re-cut a finger deeper—are not animal work. Someone still comes. Not often enough to leave a path through the moss, often enough that the moss has learned the place on the lip where a bucket used to be set and no longer grows there, a bald oval the size of a palm, shiny with the same condensation, empty.

When they come, the court hears them before the rim does. The stair reports each tread to the bowl, and by the second step the knock-birds have gone silent in a way that is not flight— they pull into the cells and become mud-colored, and the volleys stop so abruptly the drip seems to grow louder. The swifts, if it is their hour, do not care; they tick the brick and cry and the person on the stair is only another surface. The lizards vanish into flaws. The frogs sink until only the water’s skin shows. The fish do not move at all, which is its own kind of vanishing.

The person who still belongs to this place—there is usually one, and the one changes slowly, the way the stalactite changes—does not come to the middle of the pavement. The middle is for light and for channels running away. They come to the shield-basin, or to the re-cut channel, or to the bald oval on the lip. They carry something simple: a wooden scoop, a short-hafted broom of the sedge’s own cousins cut from wherever the country above still grows, a coil of cord. They clear a joint. They lift a film of guano-glaze that has begun to dam a fall the masons set and the centuries confirmed. They drink, sometimes, from the shield-basin after the frogs have gone under, and the drink is lime and bird and the faint metal of old pins, and they make a face they have made before and drink again. They do not feed the fish. They look at the fish the way you look at a crack you have decided not to widen: noted, included, left.

They speak rarely, and when they do it is to the brick, not to the tenants—a number, a curse at a pin that has swollen past use, the name of a tool they did not bring. The court takes the voice and returns it as weather. The knock-birds do not answer a human voice. They wait until the feet have climbed past the turn in the stair, and then one stone, then the volley, as if the count had to be restarted from zero.

What lives here, then, is not a bestiary so much as a set of tenancies stacked in one small volume, each keyed to a surface the builders happened to leave. Swifts to the light-column. Knock-birds to the module of the brick and the dusk under the walk. Lizards to the flaws. Beetles to the seams and the left-hand side of a drip. Frogs to the three waters and the duty of rain. Fish to a trough that should have been only a trough. Moss to the film. A finger of lime to a cup it refuses to hurry. And a person, intermittent, with a scoop and a knowledge of which joint is about to fail, who keeps the cuts true enough that the rest can go on mistaking maintenance for climate.

None of them owns the court. The court is the agreement they have all signed by staying: water where the cuts allow, sound that does not leave, warmth held in the mass, and no root deeper than a crack. Stand long enough in the plain light at the center and you stop being a visitor and become, briefly, another surface—something the swifts correct against, something the knock-birds count, something the drip will eventually include in its small, unfinishable arithmetic. Then you climb the dished stair, and behind you the volley starts again, and the place, which was never waiting for you, continues.
