They are not many. A count made from the rim, in that ordinary hour when the crowns show clear, might find thirty, or a few more if the young have not all gone under yet and the old have not all drawn into their pockets of air. The number has been near that for as long as anyone bothers to remember. The basin does not feed a crowd, and they have never asked it to.

What you see first, if one of them is crossing the open water, is the pole. It is held at an angle that looks wasteful until you understand the mat: they do not push off the bottom so much as find the braided causeways by touch and walk the pole along them, a long tap, a correction, a glide. The body above the pole is low. They do not stand tall in a craft. Most of the time there is no craft. They are in the water to the ribs, or they lie along a short raft of bundled stems no wider than their shoulders, chin nearly on the weave, and the pole does the steering while the legs trail and correct. Heat leaves them constantly. They do not shiver in the way a visitor would. The shiver has been bred out, or trained out, or simply worn out of usefulness; what remains is a slow flush along the collarbones and a habit of keeping the hands moving so the fingers do not go white and stay white.

Their hands are the part of them the place has written on most clearly. Palms broad, skin thick as cured hide across the pads and thin, almost translucent, between the fingers, where the cold has lived longest. Nails short, not from cutting but from wear against reed and cord. The webbing people expect, looking at them, is not there. What is there is a patience in the joints: they can hold a wet stem, or a bone awl, or the lip of a leaf-lining, for a length of time that makes an outsider's grip cramp and fail. Under the water their fingers read the difference between road and mat the way another person's fingers read a seam. Children learn this before they learn to keep their heads up. A child who cannot yet pole will be set on the pale tracks and told to walk them with the eyes closed, and the mat, which does not close those tracks, is the teacher that does not grow bored.

They are short in the leg and long through the trunk, as people get when the work is reaching and hauling from a seated or a floating posture rather than striding. Shoulders wide from the pole. Necks corded. The faces are narrow, and the eyes sit deep, used to light that arrives broken into nets and to long looking across a surface that lies about distance. Color is not one color. Some of them are the brown of wet reed; some are a gray-olive that matches the stone under the splash line; a few, usually the ones who spend more hours in the air-pockets mending linings than out on the lanes, are pale as the underside of a leaf, with a blue vein-map at the temples that the cold keeps visible. Hair is worn short or braided tight and pitched with the same waxy leaf-gum that seals the rooms, because loose hair in this water becomes a net for weed and a handle for the current at the notch, which is stronger than it looks. They do not ornament the braids. A single copper needle, green as the tarn, might be stuck through one, doing duty as a pin and as a small weight. That is as close as they come to jewelry, and it is not thought of as jewelry.

They speak little when they are in the open, not from sullenness but because the basin already holds a volume of sound and a shout carries whether you mean it to or not. What they do say is pitched under the fall, in a register that sits beside the notch-overflow instead of fighting it. To an ear from lower country it sounds half-swallowed, consonants softened, vowels held a fraction long, as if each word were being set down on the water and not thrown. Names are short. They use them seldom. Kin is known by which column you rise into at dusk, and by the particular knock of your pole on the root lintel — each house has learned its own rhythm, and a wrong rhythm brings a head up through the crown before a face is seen. Between houses they call across by place, not by person: the west causeway, the tower under the swifts, the sled where the fire was last lit. A person is where they are working until the work moves.

Inside the columns the quiet changes. The leaf-lining dulls the fall to a pressure more than a noise, and in that pocket they talk the way people talk who have been wet all day and are finally not. Low, practical, overlapping. Mending is the evening art. A split in a lining is cobbled before it is discussed. A basket that finished dying into stiffness is rewoven at the rim with stems that are still half alive, and the weaver's mouth moves while the hands do, recounting which school of trout held where, which newt-run on the mat has thickened, whether the northern sheet is coming harder. They do not keep a written mark. Memory is in the roads that stay pale and in the recrowning of the pipes. If you ask how old a column is, they will tell you how many times the living green at the top has been cut back and allowed to return, and they will not be guessing.

Food is what the corridors give. Trout taken on bone hooks drifted just under the shade of a tower, never in a rush, never more than the day wants. The small fish that move like weather are not scorned; they are lifted in a scoop of woven hair-weed, a mass of silver the size of two fists, and eaten with the fingers while the water drains back through the mat. Newts are not eaten. That surprises outsiders, who see the floor crawl with them and assume a resource. The newts are left. They are part of how the mat stays a mat and not a sludge, and everyone in the basin can tell you, without ceremony, that a year the newts thinned was a year the pale roads tried to close and had to be walked twice as often to teach them open again. Birds are taken only when a nest fails or a swift strikes the rock in the spray and is already dead. Eggs are not robbed. The colony on the northern lip is treated as another weather, like the fall: loud, constant, not yours.

They dress in what can be wetted and worn dry in the same hour. A short wrap of pounded stem-fiber, oiled until it sheds, belted with cord. Over the shoulders, if the wind off the rim is cutting, a cape of dipper-skins sewn with copper needles, the feathers still attached, smelling faintly of the iron that lives in the tarn. Most hours they do not bother. Skin dries. Skin wets. The cape is for the stone rim, when someone climbs to cure poles or to look down the notch at the white thread leaving, and for the very old, whose flush no longer comes.

The old do not leave the columns much. Their hands still read cord and leaf, and their talk is the talk that keeps the plan of the place from being improved. Improvement is the temptation they spend their last years refusing. A younger one will sometimes want a third causeway, or a lining of something stiffer than leaf, or a fire that lasts. The old will listen, and then they will say where the mat is thinnest, and which tower's raft-floor has begun to drink, and how the swifts changed their hour the last time someone cut reed out of season. They do not forbid. They wait. The basin usually finishes the argument. A fire that is asked to last gutters on the stone sled and leaves a smear of ash that the water will not take; someone has to pole out and scrape it before it sinks and stains the road. After that, the younger one comes back to the leaf-lining and the bone awl, and does not speak of it again for a year.

Children are born in the air-pockets, on the raft-floors that rock with the tarn's breathing, and the first thing they learn about the world is that it moves under them and does not drown them. They are in the water early, held under the arms, set on the pale tracks, taught the pole before they have the strength to use it as anything but a feeler. They learn the fish by sitting still in the shade of their own house until the trout forget them and hold again. They learn the birds by the change in the fall's note when the swifts pour off. They do not have toys that are not also tools in the making: a short length of withe, a blunt hook, a basket-start. Play is a smaller size of work, and they do not seem to mind, or they have no comparison that would teach them to mind.

There is no chief. There is no shrine in the sense of a place set apart and swept. What they regard, they regard by maintenance. The oldest copper — three pieces, maybe four, passed from column to column when a lining must be weighted true — is kept not in one house but wherever the work is, and returned when the work is done, green and cold and ordinary. If there is a ceremony, it is the walking of a road that has begun to knit. Everyone who can pole comes out, not in a line, not in time with each other, and they simply use the track until the mat gives up and the pale shows again. It may take a day. It may take three. They do not sing over it. They talk about the trout and the sheet of melt, and their poles tap the braid under them, and when the road is a road once more they go back to their columns as if they had been crossing to mend a basket on the far side.

They are not unkind to a stranger, and they are not curious in the way that opens a life. A person who comes up the notch, wet to the waist from the white thread, will be seen long before they reach the open water — the swifts tell, or the change in shout-carry, or simply the fact that thirty people in a glass bowl know the shape of their own traffic. Someone will pole out, not hurrying, and look, and ask, in that under-the-fall voice, whether the visitor can read a causeway or will need to be rafted. If rafted, they will be taken to the edge of a column and given the lintel-knock to learn, and a place on the stem-floor out of the wind, and fish if there is fish. They will not be told the plan of the roads beyond what their feet require. The mat's lesson is not a story they hand over. It is a difference the feet either learn or do not.

At night the crowns go dark. The columns are black pipes with a faint warmer black at the waterline where the leaf-linings hold a little of the body's heat. Someone may still be out, a single knock of pole, checking the notch in case the thread has thickened with a warm rain on the stone above; if it has, they will be at the southern lip before dawn, not to stop the water — they have never imagined stopping it — but to see which causeway the extra pull is teasing, and to walk that one first when the light returns. Then the pole goes quiet. The fall does not. The overflow does not. Under the surface the fish hold in the tower-shade as if night were only a thicker shade, and the newts continue their slow census of the mat, and the roads stay pale because they were walked, and because they will be walked again.

What lives here is a people who have made their peace with being slightly cold for all of their lives, and with being few, and with a town that is also a plant, also a floor, also a sound that does not peak. They do not describe themselves as keepers of it. Asked what they are doing, they will say they are mending the lining, or finding the braid, or waiting on the trout to forget them. The rest — the crowns in the glass, the roads that do not close, the air that stays sweet in the dark — they treat as the condition under which mending is possible, not as a work they have accomplished. It has been possible for a long time. They intend, by the same methods and no others, that it remain so.
