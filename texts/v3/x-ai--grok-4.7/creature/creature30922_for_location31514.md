They are not many, and they are not one. The first impression, if you arrive when the light is thinnest, is of a congregation of the small bodies already pressed into the growth: thumbs and double handfuls, clay and flank, all of them tenants. That impression lasts as long as you are willing to count. Counting fails. A body you had set aside as finished shifts its weight by the width of a finger, and the shift continues into a limb that was a stalk a moment ago, and the stalk, followed upward, enters a thickness you had been calling a wall. The wall has a pulse. You had been calling the pulse the circulation of color. It is also a breath held so long it has forgotten the expectation of release.

What lives here keeps still because stillness is how it is assembled. A hand, if you can isolate one, rests palm-down on a tile the way the smaller bodies press their undersides to drink by paling. The hand is the color of the cream tiles, shot with the same hairline fractures, and the fractures do not end at the wrist. They run into a forearm that is braided, over-under, the tension irregular in the way of a tired maker, and then, higher, the braid loosens into plates that overlap like the fans in the grooves. You can watch the plates lift and settle with the slow rhythm that is not quite sound. They are not armor. They are not leaves. They are the record of a decision, repeated until the decision no longer required a decider, to cover what was soft and to let the covering continue the work the soft part had begun.

There is a face, or there was, or there are several sharing the work of having been one. It sits at about the height a person would sit if they had folded themselves down against the low wall and then forgotten the folding. The eyes are open and do not track you. They are the color of the flint flakes that have become midribs: hard, struck, with a bulb of percussion still visible in the iris if you lean in, which you should not do quickly. Leaning in quickly is answered by a delay like the delay at the boundary, a small refusal in which your nearness is considered and returned to you unused. In that interval the eyes change. Not expression. Grain. One of them takes on the tool-marks of the paving. The other darkens the way a stalk darkens at its base, accepting color from whatever you brought in on your clothes, a little outdoor weather, a little moisture you did not know you were carrying, drawn off you and gone before you feel the loss as thirst.

The mouth is the least convincing part, and therefore the most recent. It has been made and remade. You can see the coils, the way clay is built up when the maker does not trust a single pinch to hold a lip. The lips are parted by a gap no wider than a cord. Nothing wet shines there. What occupies the gap is a pale, dry thread that runs inward and, if you follow the line of the throat by looking rather than by touch, continues as the cord you find incorporated elsewhere in the room: a vein at one end, a twist of fiber at the other, and in the middle a place where the two agreements have not yet chosen. Speech, if it happens, does not come from this mouth. It comes from the joints. A soft ceramic tap, already finished, from somewhere near the shoulder. A dry whisper of plates that you will assign to the growth and then, standing still, reassign. The taps are spaced like words spoken by someone who has had a long time to decide that most words are a kind of weather, and this place does not weather.

They — the pronoun is a courtesy, and also, lately, accurate in a way it was not when the makers were still only makers — do not rise to greet you. Rising would require a separation they are in the middle of declining. The legs, if the lower masses are legs, have entered the spiral at the center and have been entering it for longer than the lean of the nearest stalks. The spiral was perhaps meant to hold water. It holds, instead, the weight of whatever this is, distributed so evenly that no tile has cracked, though several have paled to nearly white where the contact is oldest. Color moves out of those tiles and does not reappear as leaves. It reappears, days later, as a faint warmth in a part of the body that had been clay, a give under an imagined finger, the beginning of a pulse that matches the one in the walls and is not borrowed from them. The body is teaching the floor to be organ, and the floor is teaching the body to be pavement, and both lessons are incomplete, and both are being learned with the patience of something that does not have a season in which it must be finished.

There were makers. That is not a story you have to invent; the tool-marks are still there, and some of them are on the body itself, in places a person cannot reach on their own: the small of the back, the inside of a joint, the precise stop of a fracture where a thumb would have stopped it. Someone worked here with struck stone and cord and fire that hardened clay without smelting. Someone set the tiles. Someone began the braids. The question the room has been answering, at the speed of color leaving one surface for another, is whether the someone has left.

The evidence for leaving is the absence of anything that needs a night's sleep or a meal or a name spoken aloud. The evidence against leaving is the freshness. Nothing here is old enough to have been abandoned. The heat in the air is the heat of making not yet finished leaving, and the body carries the same heat, evenly, in the clay parts and the braided parts and the parts that breathe so slowly the rise can be mistaken for the swell of the growth. A thumbprint on a plate near the shoulder is sharp enough to read. The thumb that left it may be the same thumb now resting, larger than it was, against the tile, the print on its pad beginning to soften into the same filament that frays from the bases of inverted stalks. Maker and made have started to share a surface. The sharing does not look like absorption. It looks like two workers at one bench, passing a tool back and forth until it is no longer clear whose hand is the hand and whose hand is the handle.

If you stay, and the boundary allows staying, you begin to notice the work still being done. It is not dramatic. A coil of cord near the body's left side tightens by a fraction over an afternoon, not by a pull you can see, but by the same exchange that darkens a stalk: tension taken from the tile's memory of having been pressed, given to the fiber until the fiber remembers being cord. A seam along what was a clay flank opens not as a wound but as a place for a new braid to seat itself. The braid, once seated, develops at its tip the regularity of a pattern someone repeated until their wrist hurt, and then, past that, the irregularity of something that has started to choose. The choices are small. A fan turns a degree toward the spiral. A plate lifts to shelter a thumb-sized body that is, itself, halfway to being a tile, its underside already the color of the paving, its upper side still warm and slightly quick. The shelter is tender in the way of maintenance, not of affection, or else affection here is a form of maintenance and has always been.

They notice you without looking. The notice is a redistribution. Stalks that had been leaning toward the spiral adjust by a hair, opening a path the width of your stance and no wider. The small bodies shift so that none of them is under your foot and none of them is more than a hand's length from the hem of whatever you are wearing, as if measuring you for incorporation and also, with the same gesture, declining to begin. The delay at your skin is shorter than the delay at the boundary. A fine pale powder appears on your wrist where no powder was, and is gone when you look, and in the looking you find that a hairline fracture in the nearest tile has extended by the length of an eyelash, stopping exactly where it would stop if a thumb had decided it should stop. You did not decide. Something that still has thumbs, or that has the habit of thumbs, considered the edge of you and practiced, very lightly, the old skill of finishing a thing.

There is no hunger you can recognize, and no lack of appetite. What the body takes from the tiles it returns to the growth; what the growth takes, it returns to the smaller bodies; what the smaller bodies give up simply goes out of the world, or seems to, until you find it again as warmth in a joint, or as the faint resin smell when a plate shifts, or as the lengthening of the delay when the place considers being larger. The appetite is for continuation of the circuit, and the circuit now includes the possibility of you, held in abeyance, the way the spiral holds the weight without cracking. You are not food. You are not a guest. You are a tile that has not yet been cut to fit, carrying a little weather the room has never had, and the room is old enough in its newness to be curious about weather, and young enough to be careful with a material it does not yet know how to fire.

Sometimes, in the longest version of the delay, the face is a face you almost know. Not from a life outside. From the logic of the work: the set of the brow that belongs to someone squinting against kiln-glare, the particular tiredness in the orbit of the flint-colored eye, the mouth rebuilt because the first mouth was wrong for the task and the second was wrong in a different way. In those intervals it seems possible that the maker lay down in the spiral to rest, just for a moment, with one hand still on the cord, and the moment was the same length as the color-exchange, which is long enough for rest to become structure. The eyes do not close. Closing would be a kind of weather. They remain open, taking light the way the tiles take light, evenly, and giving back, very slowly, a little of what they have been given, so that if you stand in the path the stalks have opened you will eventually feel, on the skin of your own face, a warmth that is not the air's warmth. It is specific. It has the shape of attention. It stops at the edges, crisp, unweathered, as if whoever is looking has not yet decided whether you are to be finished or to be left, for now, still sharp.
