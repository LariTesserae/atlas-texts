The chamber does not empty when you leave it. That was the mistake in the first account — treating the brightness as an absence because nothing large stands in it. Stand long enough, and the open middle shows itself as occupied by something that has no margin to keep to.

It is not an animal in the way the cup-makers are animals. It has no fixed size. On some days it is only a thickening in the air a little left of center, a place where the white-gold light slows, as if passing through warmer water. On others it fills the grove-width of the hollow from wall to wall, and you know this only because the hanging roots from the roof bend slightly toward it and then correct, as if remembering they do not feed on light. It does not cast a shadow. It is the reason the other shadows are short.

What it is, most nearly, is a residue of the river that is no longer a river. Water leaves a shape when it goes. Usually the shape is only the stone. Here the leaving was so gradual, and the lens above so exact in the way it folds the day back down, that the habit of flowing stayed after the water did not. It circulates. Not visibly. You learn it by what it does to the few things that enter its volume: a grain falling from the roof does not drop straight. It drifts a finger's width sunward, then settles. Dust on the floor lies in faint arcs, refreshed whenever condensation breathes and withdraws. The spiral of the cups on the walls is not only the animals' agreement with the stone. The animals are following a current they cannot see and do not need to see, the same current that once carried the grit their mouthparts now powder back into place.

It has no face and does not approach. Nearness is something you do, and it answers only by becoming more itself — the light a little denser, the air a little less empty, sound dying even sooner than it should. There is a temperature to it, half a degree above the rest of the chamber, held the way the stone holds the night. If you put a hand into the thick of it you feel nothing on the skin. What you feel is in the bones of the hand, a mild urge to complete a circle. People who have stood there a long time come out with their shoulders turned, as if they had been walking a bend in a path.

It does not drink the pocket of water. It does not feed on the lichens, or on the pale shrimp, or on the season-bird when the season-bird comes. Those are beside its life, the way furniture is beside a draft. What it takes is motion that would otherwise leave. A step. A falling grain. The slow tick in the groove. It folds each small expenditure back into the chamber's own turning, so that almost nothing is lost upward through the lens. That is why the scatter of grit on the floor never grows and never vanishes. That is why the fern at the water's edge is older than its fronds. The place is not sealed; it is thrifty. The thing in the middle is the thrift.

Over a century the spiral of cups shifts by the width of a grain, and so does it — its center migrating with the spiral, never quite reaching a wall, never quite returning to where a visitor last felt it. The animals do not startle when the thickening passes near their cups. They have no memory of a chamber without it. The condensation beads and withdraws on its own clock; the thing merely keeps the clock from running down. In the blue hours, when the lens goes nearly clear and the chamber is only dim, it is easiest to know it is there, because the dimness is not even. One region of the hollow stays a shade more gold, as if noon had forgotten a small piece of itself and left it on the floor.

It will not be larger in a thousand years. It will not be gone. The climate above may shift another degree, the seep may stop entirely, the fern may brown and withdraw like the roots, and the cup-makers may narrow into something that no longer powders stone. The residue does not depend on them. It depends on the lens, and on the hollow, and on the fact that water once moved here in a curve and was not permitted, by the exactness of the stone, to forget the curve when it departed. So long as noon arrives as a flood with no beam, the center will keep being occupied by what the river practiced until the practice no longer needed water.

You can stand inside it and not know. Most of what lives in the margins never knows. It does not announce the rules, because it is not a rule imposed on the chamber. It is the chamber's way of remaining finished — the last motion, going around, wearing nothing down.
