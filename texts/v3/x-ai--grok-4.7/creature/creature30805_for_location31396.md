They are not a people, or not only that. What lives on the bench is a single, crowded arrangement that learned the plateau’s manners and then kept them until the manners were its body.

Begin with the ones you notice last, because they do not move until the light fails. In the afternoon the sinter looks empty of anything larger than a fist. By the time the beads form on the cable, the stones have stood up. They are lizards only in the way a machine is a house: the outline is borrowed. Their backs are plates of the same pale sinter, flecked with the same rust, and the plates do not merely resemble the ground—they are crusted with it, a living masonry that they add to whenever they shed. A large one can lie across a trail all day and be walked past by everything that uses the trail, including the smaller ones of its own kind, who step over the ridge of its spine as if it were a curb. When it rises it does so from the middle outward, legs unfolding from under the plates, and the sound is the sound of a loose flagstone settling. They drink at the seeps by lowering the whole front of the body into the oval and letting the water climb the grooves between plates. They do not lap. They do not hurry. A kill, when one happens, is a matter of being already where a nestling or a beetle-sheet will be, and then not being stone anymore for the length of a swallow. Afterward the plate settles, and the trail uses them again.

The birds are the opposite economy: all negotiation, no disguise. They are small and kiln-brown and almost identical, and they treat identity as a local fact rather than a species fact. A bird that nests in a gear-housing will not acknowledge a bird from the fins except as weather. Their territories are measured against vibration and shade, not against land. The housing birds time their returns to the motor’s cycle. They leave when the shaft is between strokes and come back in the half-second when the metal is neither shaking nor still, so that a mite shaken loose has nowhere to land on them. The fin birds drink the evening beads and spend the day facing the same direction, bills slightly open, painted white at the base by the salts they scrape. They do not sing. In this air a song would be a private expenditure. What they have instead is a system of small displacements: a shift of weight that means the seep has risen, a turn of the head that means the walking raptor is on the third tower, a simultaneous blink along a whole ledge when a shadow that is not a cloud crosses the bench. You can watch a message move across a mile of birds without a single throat opening. They nest three and four to a cavity not from affection but because a cavity that holds one is a cavity that will be taken, and a cavity that holds four has already been paid for in mites, heat, and the particular grudge of its neighbors. The cups are salt-crust and fiber, and the fiber is whatever the machines shed—thread from a belt, hair from an insulation that has been worried for years, a twist of wire too fine to be useful to anything with hands.

The beetles are the country’s cash. They move as sheets because a single beetle on this ground is already dead, optically, and a sheet is a decision the ground makes and then unmakes. They rise off the brine pans in the same soundless way the flies do, except the beetles do not rise so much as reassign themselves from wet to dry. Their color is not camouflage in the pretty sense. It is a refusal to become an edge. Predators that hunt by edge—the walking raptor, the plate-lizards when they are hunting rather than being curbs—lose them the moment the sheet stops. The flies are simpler and more honest: they are the condensate, briefly animal. A wetting, a skin of microbes olive and rust on the seep, a hatch, a day of feeding, and then the dry interval in which their eggs wait inside the mineral fur of the cistern rims, patient as the pumps.

The raptor walks because flight here is an expense the food will not cover. Its wings are long enough to matter in a drop from a cable to a ledge, and not much more. It has learned the line the way a person learns a stair in the dark: which tower still holds, which span ticks in a crosswind, which bucket’s creak means the gear at the far end has slipped and the bird on the housing below will have left its nest a beat early. It does not cry. It pauses, drops, and is a darker seed among seeds until it is not. What it takes it carries back along the wire, not to a nest in any soft sense but to a slot under a tower footing where three seasons of pellets have mortared themselves into the sinter. Other things use that mortar. A cushion plant has rooted in one of them, on the cooler side, and is ignored.

There are people, though the plateau does not arrange itself around them, and they have learned not to insist.

They are few, and they are not a remnant in the tragic style. They stay because the machines still have one job each, and because a job, here, is a kind of weather you can live inside. A person might keep the pump whose flywheel still turns, not by repairing it toward its old purpose but by keeping the shaft clear of sinter-dust and the nesting cups from fouling the bearing. In return the housing stays warm, the seep below it stays a finger higher than the others, and the birds tolerate a body in the doorway so long as the body keeps the rhythm. Another might walk the cableway once in a long interval, not to restore it, but to free a bucket that has hung up, because a bucket that stops teaches the raptor a new pause, and a new pause empties a ledge, and an emptied ledge is a quarrel that spreads. They do this with their hands and with a patience that looks, from outside, like idleness. Their speech is short and aimed, because a shout dies and a long sentence arrives already missing its end. They talk the way the birds shift: a fact, a direction, a level in a cistern. Names for one another are place-names. The one at the third tower. The one whose pan still skins. If they have older names, they are kept for the night, when the film has gone and there is no one to spend them on but the person in the same shed.

They do not farm the cushion plants. They do not hunt the plate-lizards, which would be like hunting the trail. What they eat is what the cycle already concentrates: brine-fly in a skim, beetle when a sheet has blundered into a pan and can be lifted as a mat, eggs from a cavity only when the housing has been fouled past the bargain and the birds have already begun to leave. They drink the cistern water, warm, metallic, and they taste it the way other people taste weather—for what the day will do. Their clothes are the same grammar as everything else: worn to the color of the bench, patched with whatever holds, never new. A bright scrap would be a claim the country does not honor. Children, when there are children, grow up knowing which fins sweat at dusk and which machines are inhabited by agreement rather than by right. They learn to step over a ridge of plates without announcing that they have seen it. They learn that a message can cross a mile without a voice, and that adding their own voice to it is a way of being lost.

None of them—lizard, bird, beetle, raptor, person—thinks of the plateau as harsh. Harsh is a word for a place that has not yet been agreed with. This one has. The agreement is visible in the nests built to a motor’s stroke, in the trails that use a living stone as a curb, in the bucket that still goes out because something still depends on the creak, in the person who clears a bearing and is paid in a finger of water and a tolerance in the doorway. At night they all withdraw into the same pockets: warm metal, damp ovals, the lee of fins, the sheds whose roofs will jewel again at evening. The country does not empty. It closes, densely, around the heat it has never quite let go, and what lives there is the closing itself—the packed, quiet tenancy of a finished ground, which has room for every life that will keep its manners, and no room at all for a life that needs to be heard across the bench.
