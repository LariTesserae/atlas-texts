What lives there is easy to miss because it never arrives. It has no season of coming and no hour when it is more present than the stone. You can stand in the dish at the warmest part of the afternoon, when the upper storeys hold a little heat and the rare beetle makes its few steps, and conclude the place is only weather using old architecture. That conclusion is almost right. The correction is that the weather has a tenant, and the tenant is what keeps the weather on its route.

It does not have a body you can point to. Pointing is the wrong gesture; the right one is the one you already make when you follow a groove with your eye and find it still wet past the place where gravity should have emptied it. The life is in that refusal, distributed through the cell-pitted shells the way damp is distributed through a cellar wall. Each tiny pit is a room inside the room. Most are mineral and done. A few, always a few, hold a film that is not quite water: clearer than the pools, faintly bitter when a drop reaches the tongue from a wet finger, and slow to bead. Under a lens it would look like nothing much—grains, threads, a cloudiness that shifts if you breathe on it. Without a lens it announces itself only by work. The film climbs. It chooses which moss drinks. It keeps the open pavement clear by a chemistry faint enough that you smell it only with your face in a seam, and strong enough that a spine will not stay the night.

Call it a colony if you need a noun. It was one, once, in the sense that built the walls: many mouths, one plan, secretion as masonry. What remains is not a survivor in the heroic sense and not a ghost. It is the plan still running on a thinner budget. The shells are its skeleton and also its city, and it occupies them the way a thought occupies a sentence it has already finished saying—by keeping the grammar from collapsing. Where a later growth overtook an earlier one and the upper storey fell through, the film retreated to the intact cells and did not mourn the height. Height was never the point. Continuity of wetness was the point, and the dish still offers that, on a schedule of cloud and thaw the colony meets halfway.

If you stay long enough to watch a single cushion of moss through a day, you begin to see the tenant’s pace, which is not the moss’s pace. The moss swells when the light is high because that is what moss does. But the edge of the cushion does not advance evenly. It fattens along the groove and starves a finger’s width off it, even when the stone there is just as damp from a passing rain. By evening the starved edge has tightened back, not dead, merely unfed, as if a mouth had been withdrawn. Overnight the rime locks everything. At dawn, when the climbing film wakes and darkens one patch that has no business being wet, that patch is always on the old alignment—never a random generosity, never a new street. The colony is not exploring. It finished exploring before the climate that now crosses the rim had a name. It is keeping house.

Its senses, if the word can be stretched that far, are chemical and mechanical and shared. A dry week is felt as a thinning of films in the higher cells; the response is a small abandonment, a browning at the far end of a feeder, a retreat toward the pockets that hold all day. A squall is felt as surplus. Surplus is not stored in any bladder you could find. It is spent immediately on the nearest authorized green, and what cannot be spent is allowed to fall and freeze and be wind. There is no hoarding and no innovation. The beating of the choir—the slight disagreements in pitch that grain the air—may be part of how the separate shells stay one thing. Vibration travels the continuous stone better than any signal the film could send through dry pavement. On a strong day, when you cannot talk across the center, the colony is, in its own terms, loud with itself: each mouth a pressure, each hollow a reply, the plan reasserted as a pattern of stress in the walls. On a still dawn, glazed and reduced to one low note, it is nearly asleep, and the climbing water starts late.

It does eat, in a manner that would bore a predator and satisfy a chemist. The mosses and the coin-leaved shrubs are not decorations and not quite livestock. They are a crop the colony did not plant and cannot do without, tended by witholding and permission. Their dead parts, the brown that appears after a dry week, are taken back not by jaws but by the same film that wets them, softened, drawn into the grain, returned to the chalky layering as a stain you would call weathering. The beetle is tolerated because it does not dig and does not nest; it is a rumor on the surface, and when your shadow sends it walking it is already leaving a life the colony does not count. The bird at the rim is outside the jurisdiction. Passage over the shoulder is nothing. Occupancy would be a chemical argument, and the colony has not lost one in a very long time.

You will want it to be older than it is, or younger. Both are available and neither is comforting. The shells are old; the films in the living cells are only as old as the last thaw that filled them. There is no individual here to outlive a season, and no individual to die when a chamber finally sands down to pavement. What persists is the spacing: the decision, repeated without anyone deciding, that water belongs in these grooves and green belongs on their margins and the open stone belongs to wind. If every film were scraped out and the dish left empty in the strict sense, the weather would begin, slowly, to erase the plan—the feeders filling with their own flour, the mosses loosening into circular cushions with no streets, the choir detuning as mouths clogged. That erasure is always underway at the edges and always being refused in the center. The refusal is the life. It has no face, so it cannot look at you. It has no gait, so it cannot approach. When you put your hand on a wall you feel only the cold the stone has always had, and underneath it, if you are very still, nothing you could swear was movement: a tackiness as a film adjusts to the salt of your skin and then, politely, withdraws from an animal it has classified and declined.

At dusk the skim-ice ticks, and the tenant’s day ends in the only way it ever ends—by becoming indistinguishable from maintenance. The climbing threads stiffen and stop. The authorized greens tighten. The mouths change their notes as ice narrows them, and the colony, which has been speaking through those notes all afternoon, drops into the single pressure that means night. Nothing hunts. Nothing is hunted. The lushness in the chambers darkens to the color of wet wool and waits for a cloud. If there is a desire in any of this, it is the desire of a groove for the next film of water, and it is satisfied often enough that the city has not had to move, or grow, or remember any other shape, for as long as the rim has been low enough to step over without noticing you have entered anything. The mountain around it is only mountain. Inside the dish, the oldest habit still practices itself, cell by cell, loudly, to no one, and calls that living.
