The bench begins where the last loose slope gives up. There is no timberline to cross, because there is nothing that could have made one. The rock simply becomes level enough to hold a body, and then it keeps becoming level: a high dish of stone tipped only enough that water knows a direction. The air is thin and cool and violently clear. Light arrives without softness. Every wet face gives it back.

Underfoot the ground is not a surface so much as a crowd of surfaces. Low ribs of pale stone stand a hand apart, sometimes less, their tops worn round and their sides darkened where water has lived against them for longer than any weather the eye can date. Between the ribs are cups, throats, shallow pans, and slots no wider than a finger. None of them are empty. A film occupies the pavements. A fixed depth occupies the cups—always the same depth, lip to lip, as if the place had been filled once and then forbidden to change its mind. Where a lip is lower, water leaves in a thread the width of a coin and does not hurry. The threads find other threads. Together they make a braid that covers the whole bench and never quite becomes a stream you could point to and name.

The water is not rain. The sky is too bright and too settled for that, a high hard blue with nothing in it, and the stone is sweating from inside. Along every joint a seam glistens and feeds the film. A hand set on a dry-looking crest feels the cold coming through before the bead forms at the edge and lets go. The mountain is leaking at a rate it has already agreed upon. Nothing pools beyond its measure. Nothing dries. Mineral skin—thin, chalky, slightly gritty—rebuilds the lips as fast as the threads wear them, so the forms look ancient and also finished, the way a river stone looks finished. Erosion here is not an event. It is the shape.

There is no soil. There is nowhere for soil to rest. What might have been grit is carried off in the film or locked into the pale crust at the rims. The eye keeps waiting for a stain of green in a crack, the way high wet country usually cheats, and the waiting is the nearest thing to strangeness the place allows. The cracks are clean. The crust is mineral and only mineral. Even the black that gathers in the perpetual shade of the deeper slots is not life; it is wet stone gone dark, and under a thumb it wipes away to the same pale grain as everything else. If anything simpler than a plant has taken the benches—some film too thin to count as a stain—it does not announce itself. Refused, the eye starts counting stone instead.

And the stone does not thin out. From any rise that deserves the name—a rib a little higher than its neighbors—the bench runs on for a distance that would hold a town, then breaks into a second bench, then a third, all of them packed to their edges with the same cups and lips and crossing threads. Farther, a wall of higher rock closes the basin, itself seamed and shining, feeding the dishes below. Nothing interrupts the pattern. There is no cairn, no cut, no socket where a tool might have worried the grain. The openness is real: you can see across it. The emptiness is not. There is no gap large enough to set a body down without being among the ribs, in the film, at the lip of another cup. The region is one occupancy repeated until the eye tires of repetition and starts to hear it instead.

The sound has been present since the slope, but on the bench it becomes the climate. Each thread is nothing. Ten thousand threads, falling a finger’s height into the next pan and never stopping, add to a roar that does not rise and does not rest. Wind works the same way. It does not gust. It leans, steady as the leak in the stone, and the ribs break it into a hiss that sits inside the roar rather than beside it. Eyes closed, the place is a single pressure in the ears, without rhythm, without a louder side. Eyes open, the light is worse: wet planes throwing it upward, crusts glaring, the blue too large. The cool does not relent under the brightness. Breath clouds thinly and is gone. The noise does not cloud at all.

What moves, moves inside the agreement. The threads keep their widths. The cups keep their depths. A bead forms, travels, is replaced. Watch one lip long enough to believe you have seen a change, and you have only seen the same departure again. There is no hour when the bench is louder. There is no drying edge advancing by afternoon. The equilibrium is so complete that time feels misapplied—present as light and cold and noise, absent as event.

Life is almost a rumor, and then it is not quite. In a cup deeper than the others, where the mineral skin has failed to close and the water stands green-clear over bare grain, something small holds against the current that is barely a current. It is the color of the wet stone. It does not swim so much as adjust—a brief correction of place, then stillness that might be waiting or might be the only motion it has. A second one does not appear in the next hundred cups, nor in the hundred after that. The thing, if it is a thing and not a trick of grain and light, lives entirely inside one pocket of the agreement. It does not colonize. It does not need to. The water will be there at the same depth when whatever passes for its season ends.

Higher, once, a bird crosses the wall—dark, brief, gone into a seam that cannot be reached—and the roar does not register it. No call comes back. The seam could hold a nest. It could hold nothing but a pause in a longer flight that treats this bench as a line to cross, not a country to enter. Whatever it takes from this place, it takes rarely, and the bench does not rearrange itself around the taking. There is no scatter of feathers at the lip of a cup, no pale dropping bleaching on a crest. The visit, if it was a visit, leaves less mark than a bead.

Between the almost-animal in the deep cup and the bird that does not stay, the bench remains what it has been: occupied by its own processes more thoroughly than by tenants. The tenants are so few they function as punctuation. You can walk until the light changes angle and still be telling one story—the leak, the film, the roar—and the living things will not have interrupted it enough to count as a second story. That scarcity is not a failure of looking. The cups are open. The slots are shallow enough to read. If there were more, the eye would have it by now, the way it has the mineral skin, the braid of threads, the way the third bench mirrors the first until distance finally softens them both into a single shining.

Stand long enough and the rules settle into the body rather than the mind. Water is everywhere and never spare, never excess. Stone is crowded and stable. Light is a blunt fact. Sound is the medium you move through, not a signal arriving from elsewhere. Nothing is built. Nothing is finished by hands. The only work is the old work of leak and wear and redeposit, locked in a balance that does not need the sun to rise differently tomorrow. When a foot leaves the film, the film closes over the print before the print is a print. The cup that was troubled returns to its depth without a sound that could be separated from the sound that was already there. The roar, which never noticed the passage, continues at the same unbearable level, bright and cool and without cease, across every bench the eye can still hold, and across the ones past that, where the wall keeps feeding the dishes and the dishes keep their measure, as they will when no one is left on the slope to give the level ground a name.
