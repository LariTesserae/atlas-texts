They are easy to miss because the town was built at their speed, and the town does not move.

At first what you take for them is only maintenance. A mat on a roof sits a finger’s width differently than it sat when you looked from the far rim, though you cannot say when the difference occurred. A cup rests on a tank-lip that was empty of cups. A course of stone that seemed unfinished has one more block in it, fitted so cleanly the wall looks as if it had always ended there. No one is in the street when you look for the worker. The grit between the channels holds no track that lasts; damp takes a print and then, because nothing walks with weight enough to cut, the print eases shut the way a film of water eases over a dry spot, from the edges in.

Stand in a doorway and the room is occupied without announcing it. The occupation is a temperature. The warm air of the valley is already close, but inside the house the closeness has a center: a place the warmth has agreed to gather, slightly drier than the seep-wall, slightly more the temperature of skin than of stone. If you turn your head quickly you still see only the heavy lintel, the copper bowl, the mat, the tank of water level with every other tank. If you do not turn your head, if you let the eye rest the way the water rests, a figure becomes available in that center the way a word becomes available after you have stopped hunting it.

They are upright. That is the first clear fact, and it is almost the only one that would let you call them people from the rim. The height is a person’s height, not always the same person’s; some come up under the lintel with room to spare, some must bend a little, and the bend is not discomfort. It is how they pass through, a fold at the waist that does not wrinkle anything you would call clothing, because what covers them is not exactly worn. It is a close layer, the color of the stone when the stone is wet but not dark—gray-tan, faintly green in the folds, matte. It does not hang free. Sleeves, if they are sleeves, end where the hands begin without a cuff. The hands are hands. Five fingers, or what resolves into five when the light is allowed to do its slow work; at a glance the hand is a single shape with suggestions of division, the way a fern crozier suggests the frond it will be and, here, never quite finishes suggesting.

The face is the part that takes longest to arrive, and it arrives by subtraction. You expect eyes and then understand that the expectation is yours. There is a face-plane, smooth as the young rock, with a change of plane where a brow would shelter eyes, and beneath that a quiet modification that is a mouth only because breath—or something performing breath’s office—moves there. They do take air in. You see it as a slight settling of the torso, spaced so widely that between one settling and the next you will have looked away and back and decided they were holding still on purpose. The interval is longer than a sleeper’s. It is closer to the interval at which a leaf releases the drop it has been growing. When the air leaves them it leaves without force. No plume. The hanging moisture of the valley simply accepts a little more of itself.

They do not have hair in any manner that would move if there were wind. Some carry a fine nap along the skull and the forearms, pale, more like the first mineral crust on the upper rim than like fur: a bloom, a decision of the surface. It does not grow while you watch. Nothing of them grows while you watch. They are at their size. A smaller one in a lower courtyard is not a child becoming larger; the courtyards hold smaller ones and larger ones, and the difference is as stable as the difference between one terrace and the next. If there is youth among them it is not legible as change. It may be legible only as position—who stands nearer the newest rock, who is trusted with the upper channels, where the crust is thin and a wrong level would matter. You will not see them instruct one another. Instruction, if it happens, has already happened, or happens in the placement of a cup.

What they eat, if eat is the word, is the same circuit the plants use, entered from the other side. You will not find a market. You will not find stores beyond what a house keeps for a day that is not counted in days. In the tanks, along the still margins, the film that looks like scum is not scum. It is a soft green continuous with the moss in the joints, and they draw it up on a flat stone or with the edge of the hand and take it in at the mouth-modification without chewing you can see. It is not a meal. It is maintenance, as the roof-mat is maintenance, as the extra course of stone is maintenance. Some of them do not do even this while you are near. They stand where a seep wets a wall and the wall darkens in a shape that includes their shoulder, and when they move on—the movement a translation more than a step, weight never quite striking—the darkening on the wall is their width and then, slowly, only the seep’s. They are in correspondence with the water. Drinking is only the visible form of a longer fact: their surfaces pass moisture as the stone does, in and out, and the valley’s refusal to rain is also their refusal to thirst in any sharp way. They are wet enough. The air is wet enough. Equilibrium is a kind of feeding that does not require a mouth, and the mouth is there for the occasions when equilibrium needs a correction of a thumb’s width.

They do not hunt because there is nothing to hunt, and they do not seem to have kept the gesture in reserve. The hands are strong in the way a root is strong, suited to fitting stone and to lingering in water, not to closing fast. No animal would fit among them even if one reached the rim. The quiet would not be a habitat for it; more than that, the people—if they will bear the word—do not make the small donations an animal’s life requires: scraps, droppings, a corpse, a nest disturbed. Their leavings are water returned to a tank, a spent stem laid in a joint where fungus will take it, a film of themselves on a cup that the next moisture erases. The town is clean in a manner that is not discipline. There is almost nothing to clean.

How they build is how you will come to believe in them most firmly, because the town cannot be a fossil of a people who have gone. The chisels exist. You find one in a hand, and the hand is closed around it without strain, and the blade is against a block, and over the course of an afternoon the block accepts a new edge so narrow you would not call it progress unless you had set your own mark. They work in the open, in the even light, and they work alone even when two of them share a wall. Collaboration is spatial. One holds the level the water has already given—eye to a channel, stone to be matched to that height—and the other brings the block. They do not speak to coordinate. Sound from them is rarer than the drop that finally falls from the fern. When it comes it is not language in the shape of sentences. It is a low settling tone, felt as much as heard, the pitch of a large vessel when a rim is circled by a wet finger, except the circling has already been done by the climate. One voice, then, after a long while, an answer from another terrace. The answer is not an echo; the damp stone eats echoes. It is the same tone, agreed to. You can live a day in the town and hear it twice and carry both instances out with you as if they were a conversation, and you will not be wrong, and you will not be able to translate it.

Whether they think as you think is the wrong question, but it is the one that will press. They attend. That is observable. A figure in a doorway is not vacant. The face-plane is oriented; the orientation shifts when something in the visible world changes by enough to count, which is not often. Your arrival counts. They orient to you and do not startle and do not approach. Approach is expensive here. To cross from one terrace to the next is to enter a slightly different level of the water table, and they do it when the work is on that terrace, not to close distance for its own sake. So they look, and the look has duration, and duration is their courtesy. You are a change in a place that has arranged itself to have none. They give you the time the stone would give a new crack: not repair, not welcome, a long assessment of whether you will widen.

If you stay, and do not widen, something else becomes visible that the first hours will not grant. They are not identical, and the differences are not the differences of a crowd. One has a mineral bloom thicker along one arm, a pale sleeve the others lack. One carries a notch in the face-plane, old and smooth, not a wound in progress; the notch holds a bead of water that does not fall, and this seems neither injury nor ornament but a personal climate, a place where their body has decided to be more like the fern-tip than like the wall. One is broader and stays by the lower tanks, and the green of the film is deeper on that one’s hands, worked into the fine separations of the fingers so that the hand, at rest, looks cuffed in moss. None of this is decoration chosen in a morning. It is the record of where they have stood. They weather, but at the rock’s pace, and because the rock is young they are not weathered far. A life among them, if life divides into lives, might be a long occupation of a few postures. Birth, if it occurs, is not public in the days you will have. You may come to think they increase the way the plants do, by a slow extension that at some unnoticed hour is simply another body standing in its own warmth, already the size it means to be, already oriented toward the channel it will keep. You will not see a joining. Privacy here is not a door shut. Privacy is the slowness that makes an event invisible to anything that watches at the speed of meals and weather.

They sleep, or they enter a state for which sleep is the available word, in the houses, near the tanks, sometimes seated, sometimes aligned along a bench of the same stone as the walls so that a glance records architecture. In that state the settling of breath becomes rarer still, and the warmth that marks them spreads and thins until the room is only the valley’s warmth again. This is when a visitor, walking the organized quiet and losing heart, decides the town is empty and has been empty since the water found its level. It is a reasonable decision. It is also how they pass the part of the day that does not require them. The light does not change enough to name an evening, but there is a portion of time when more of them are architecture and fewer are the slight dry centers in doorways, and another portion when the centers gather again. No one rings it in. The town inhales on a period longer than a day and shorter than a season, and the period is their clock because no other clock could stand the stillness of the sun.

They do not worship in any building set aside for it. The whole settlement is already an agreement with a condition: high ground that is warm, water that will not run, stone that will not round, green that will not tangle, lives that will not hurry. If they have a god it is the level line repeated in every tank, and their devotion is the refusal to ask it to become a river. You can watch one of them for the better part of a day and see nothing you would call a rite, and then see them lay two fingers in a channel and leave them there until your own patience fails, and understand that the gesture was complete when it began. They are checking that the town is still the valley. The valley is still the town. The check succeeds. The fingers come out no wetter, in any way that matters, than they went in.

What they fear, if fear belongs to them, is not predation and not storm. Storm does not gather here; the air is already full and will not be talked into more. What would undo them is a change in height. A true rain. A wind with a direction. A crack that finally carries water down instead of holding it in a film. They are fitted to a pause in the land’s youth, the interval after the rock has cooled and been lifted and before the years arrive that round edges and teach channels to sing. They do not seem to know this as a story. They know it as a craft: keep the weirs level with the surface they govern; do not cut a slope; do not call out in a way that would require an answer from a distance the damp cannot cross. Their ethics, if the word is not too quick for them, is the ethics of not starting. Do not start a flow. Do not start a crowd. Do not start a fire larger than the air can thin. Do not start a sentence.

And yet they are not nothing, and not only stone rehearsed in a human outline. When one of them, at last, comes the width of a courtyard closer—because you have been still long enough to count as a feature, a new post in the loose grid—the warmth that precedes them is unmistakably a body’s, and the face-plane tilted to your height has in it the specific attention of a creature that has decided you are singular. Not kind. Not unkind. Singular: one event, not the climate. The mouth-modification opens a little. The tone that comes is lower than the terrace-calls, meant for the distance between two bodies and no farther. You will not learn the language. You may learn that you have been included in the town’s accounting, the way a tank is included, the way a seep is included, as a volume that must not be allowed to rise or fall without someone knowing. A hand lifts, not to touch. It holds at the height of your sternum, palm a single shape that could divide, and between the palm and your chest the air is the same air, thick, beaded, not quite rain. They are showing you the distance they keep from everything they mean to keep. It is not far. It is exact. Then the hand returns to the side, and the figure translates back toward the channel, and the courtyard is only a courtyard, except that the cup on the tank-lip has been turned so its mouth faces the path you took, a small correction in the level of things, made without haste, already complete.
