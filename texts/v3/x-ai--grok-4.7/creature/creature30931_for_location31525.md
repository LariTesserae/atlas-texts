What lives here has learned the plateau’s pace, which is not stillness but a refusal to hurry past the heat. Nothing on the benches is native to haste. Even the smallest life moves as if the stone were a clock it has agreed to keep.

In the crust itself there is a skin of feeders too fine to see as bodies until a seep-line dries at the edge of a pan and the film lifts in a curl. Then you can see it was never one thing: a lace of mineral-eaters, pale as ash, threaded through the yellow rinds and the black glassy blooms. They do not migrate. They thicken where the water is oldest and thin where a foot has passed, and by evening the track is only a paler writing on a page that is already being rewritten from below. People who work the flues call this the first mouth. They do not mean it as poetry. The cisterns clog with it if the gutters are neglected, and the clog is sweet in a way that makes the tongue go numb. It is not eaten. It is scraped, dried, and burned under the floors when the engines want a cleaner flame than coal the rail can bring. The plateau feeds its own works at the smallest scale, and the works, in turn, keep the scrapers employed.

Above that film the ash-colored crawlers work the open flats. They are not insects and not quite the lizards the lowlands would name them for. Too many legs, set in pairs that do not all touch the ground at once, so that a body seems to pour rather than walk. A hand’s length, most of them; a few the length of a forearm where the outflow pipes have fattened the crust for years. Their mouths are on the underside, a rasp that leaves the pale tracks. They do not flock. If two meet they turn, not sharply, and continue on bearings that look random until you watch a whole flat for an hour and see that the bearings tile the ground. No patch is grazed twice in a day. No patch is left for a second day. They carry their water in a clear sac under the forward plates, filled at the pans in the last brass light, and through the hot middle of the day that sac is the only shine on them. Children in the compounds—there are children, though you do not hear them—learn early not to lift one. The rasp is not venomous. It is simply still working, and a palm comes away with the same numb sweetness as the cistern clog, and a night of thirst that water does not fix until morning.

The shoulder-high ones are older in the settlement’s accounting, because they stand where people can see them and because they do not pour. They plant. Four limbs, thick, the hide a dull plated gray that takes the color of whatever steam they have been standing in. The head is low and long, not a grazer’s head: no teeth you would trust, a mouth built to draw. What they draw are the filaments, and the filaments are the true strangeness of the water, though the people do not call them strange. In the hotter pools the threads hang from the surface down into a dark that is only a hand deep and looks deeper because the light will not enter it. They grow toward heat the way a root grows toward damp. The shoulder-high animals lower their faces into the steam and the threads come up pale and beaded, and vanish. After a feeding the animal stands a long time with its eyes half-lidded, not asleep, working something in the throat. Salt crusts at the corners of the mouth and falls in a grit the crawlers will not touch. These animals do not come into the compounds. They drift to the outflow because the pipes make a richer thread, and they stop at the stain-line on the stone where people’s boots have polished a boundary without ever fencing it. A calf—if calf is the word—stands inside the arc of an adult’s legs for its first season, learning which pans scald and which only sting. The adults do not defend them. Nothing on the plateau hunts in the open. What dies, dies of a bad step onto crust that looked solid and was only a lid over a flue. The body sinks a little, steams, and by the third day is a darker crust the crawlers ring but do not cross.

What occupies the office of birds does not land on the works. Their wings are too narrow for perching on gutters, or they have never learned the trick. They ride the updrafts off the hotter pans, high enough that the vapor takes their edges, and they drop only to the standing fins of old glass when the light goes brass. Then you can count them, if you want a number: never many, never none. Their cry is a needle that does not arrive. What they eat is argued in the engine houses and never settled. Some say they take the crawlers at the moment of molting, when the plates are soft and the sac is empty. Some say they drink the steam itself and void a white dust on the fins that the scrapers collect and will not burn, because it flashes too hot and pits the plate. The dust is real. The drinking is not proven. They do not fear people. They do not attend them. A thrown stone falls short in the thick air and they do not even turn their heads.

The people are the ones the works were built for, and the works are older than anyone’s count of generations, so the people have become a fitting as exact as a flange. They are not a crowd. A compound holds a dozen, perhaps twenty, spaced through brick rooms that share walls and do not share much else. Kinship runs along the rail more than through blood: the shed at the eastern bench sends its young to the mid-basin flues when a keeper dies, and the mid-basin sends ore-sense and pipe-measure back along the same cars. Outsiders do come, rarely, with the coal and the replacement plate. They stay a season or they stay for good. The ones who stay lose the habit of raising their voices first, then the habit of expecting an answer in sound. Talk is hands, mostly: a flat palm for stop, two fingers walked across a palm for the crawlers’ evening drift, a fist closed and opened for the engines’ turn under the floor. Spoken words are for the ledgers and for naming a flue that has begun to breathe wrong. There is a spoken language. It is short, and it does not carry, and that suits it.

They dress in cloth that was once a color and is now the color of condensate and brick-stain. Soles are thick. Heads are covered, not for modesty but because the midday light, even through vapor, cooks a scalp. Faces are weathered younger than the hands. The hands are the record: white scars from plate, yellow nails from the sweet clog, a missing tip here and there from a joint that closed while a filter was being cleared. No one is careless. Carelessness is a bad step, and a bad step is the same for a person as for a shoulder-high calf. The fences exist where a rail wheel or a drive-shaft could take a body; they do not exist to keep the plateau out. At the outflow the stained boundary is respected because the animals respect it, and the people have had long enough to notice that forcing the matter makes the filaments sour and the engines burn dirty for a month.

Children learn the hands before they learn the ledgers. They learn which pans are only sting and which are scald by being shown, not by being told a rule that the vapor would swallow. They do not play at distance. A game that needs a shout dies on this plateau, so their games are close: bone-tiles on a cistern lid, a race between two crawlers set on a marked hexagon and gently turned until one pours off the edge, the collection of white dust from the glass fins at brass-light, weighed and compared. They are not solemn. Solemnity is a lowland idea about quiet. They laugh with the chest, almost without the mouth, and the laugh is a small shake you see if you are looking. They grow into keepers or into rail-hands or, less often, into the ones who walk the seep-lines with a rod and a book, mapping cracks that have twins half a kilometer off. That last work is not mystic to them. The basin is one skin. A new split is a message about where not to lay pipe. The ones who walk the lines sleep in a different room, nearer the outer door, because they rise before the brightness hardens and they do not like to wake the compound with the latch.

There is a life in the engine houses that is not the engines and not quite the people. Long-timers call it the turn. It is the way a house feels when the muted rotation under the floor has been matched, for years, by the habits of the bodies above it. You can move a keeper from the eastern shed to the mid-basin and the new house will feel wrong under his boots for a season, not because the machine is different—the machines are the same cast, the same breath—but because the people in it have worn a rhythm into the day: when the filters are pulled, when the scrapers come in with their pails of sweet film, when the shoulder-high shapes appear at the stain-line and the children go to the inner rooms without being signed to. A newcomer who ignores that rhythm makes the vapor in the house feel thicker. No one can explain this in the ledgers. They can demonstrate it. After a month of keeping the house’s hours, the thickness goes. The newcomer has been fitted. If they cannot be fitted, they take the next car down the rim and do not write back. The plateau does not punish them. It simply remains itself, and they remain audible to themselves in a place where audibility is a kind of nakedness.

At night, which is only a deeper brass and then a brown, the crawlers slow and the shoulder-high animals fold their legs at the rims of the pans they trust. The narrow wings settle on the fins and become part of the glass until dawn. In the compounds the engines do not stop. Someone is always awake, not as a guard against an enemy—there is no enemy in the accounting—but as a hand on the pressure and an ear that has learned to feel a knock through the boot when the ear cannot hear it. They drink the cistern water after it has been run through a bed of sintered crust and cooled in a coil that sweats into a second pan. It tastes of metal and of the far edge of sweetness. It does not make the tongue numb if the bed has been changed on schedule. People who have lived here long enough say they dream of lowland rain and wake with their mouths open, embarrassed, as if they had been about to shout.

What lives here, if it must be said in one motion, is a chain of appetites matched to a chain of leaks. Film eats seep. Crawlers eat film. Filaments eat light and mineral and heat. The shoulder-high draw the filaments and salt the rims. The narrow wings take whatever the argument has not settled, and leave a dust the works will not burn. People eat the stores the rail brings, and they eat, once in a while, a shoulder-high that has taken the bad step and been pulled clear before the crust closed—this is done without ceremony and without waste, and the hide becomes sole-leather, and the bones become the children’s tiles. The engines eat the sweet film and the coal and the careful attention of hands. The plateau eats nothing. It condenses, splits, seeps, and remembers a crack across half a kilometer. Everything that lives on it has made a bargain with that remembering: do not ask the stone to be only stone, do not ask the air to carry a voice, do not bunch, do not hurry, and in return the water will not fail and the crust will reform behind you and the light will go on pouring down, hard and white, onto a life that has become the exact size of the quiet.
