They are not a people you can pick out by a single face. The shelf has been making them too long for that, and the districts differ by a century or two of iron and by how worn the corners are, so a body from the older flats does not stand exactly like a body from a street that still shows the saw. What they share is closer to what the walls share: a way of holding water, a patience about grade, a density.

They are not tall. Height is expensive in stacked streets, and the air at the rim of the shelf already costs something on the first long stair. Shoulders are broader than the rest of the frame, from loads carried in the wet, from the sideways bracing you learn on paving that holds a finger of water along one edge and is never reliably flat. Hands are wide, nails short, the skin at the knuckles thickened and faintly stained the color of the mineral film, a brown-gold that does not scrub off in a season. It is not dirt. It is the same skin the stone wears, worked into the creases. Children show it first on the knees and the heels. By middle life it maps the places a person leans: a forearm, a hip against a rail, the side of a neck where a wet collar sits all day.

Their skin otherwise runs warm and a little glossy, as if the damp had been taken in and never fully given back. They do not sweat the way dry-country people sweat, in sudden sheets. Moisture stands on them the way it stands on the masonry, a film you notice when you take a hand. They smell of the streets because the streets smell of what they eat and what they wear and what they sleep against: warm iron, wet feather, the sour paste, the clean nothing of rain that has already fallen. A stranger’s nose sorts this as one smell. They sort it into rooms. They can tell a drying shed from a cistern stair from a lower gutter where the bands have been, and they can tell whether the paste was made from the darker film or the ordinary gold, which is a difference outsiders lose after the second bite.

They dress for damp, not for cold, and not for display that would not survive a week of warm wet. Cloth is milled here and it is heavy, close-woven, the kind that darkens when it takes water and does not fall apart when it stays dark. Colors are the colors the stone and the dye vats allow: iron-rust, cistern-tea, the gray of cornice birds, a dull yellow from mineral that fades to the color of old facing. Nothing stays bright. Brightness is a week’s vanity, and they know the schedule. They wear layers that can be peeled when a mill-room runs hot and put back on when a causeway wind, wet and tireless, finds the gaps in a walkway. Boots are the serious garment. Soles are thick, often pegged with the same iron the works use, and people resent a bad sole the way other places resent a bad roof. The lower streets flood to a hand’s breadth in any organized rain. A life is a sequence of judgments about where the water will sit by evening.

They walk close. Speech is close. The shelf is loud in layers — water, belt, flock — and a voice does not win by rising. You see two people stop in a market and put their mouths near each other’s ears, not from secrecy, from acoustics. The calling of sellers is brisk and short, pitched to cut a narrow band of air under an awning, not to cross a square. They do not shout at distance. If they need someone three walkways up, they send a body, or they wait for the flock-roar to thin, or they use the mill signals, which are older than most of the engines: a pattern of knocks on pipe, a board lifted and dropped in a race, a stack’s exhaust changed by a damper for the length of a count everyone knows. These are not codes in the sense of hiding. They are the grammar of a place where the air is already occupied.

Work is not a separate life. The mills do not stop in any way that would let a person be, for long, a person who does not belong to a line. A district’s throb is the schedule. People sleep in pieces. Full dark is not empty; the works drop to a reduced clatter, and the sleepers are the ones whose hands are not required for that reduction, plus the children, plus the old who have aged out of the belts and aged into the water rooms. A shift is a portion of the throb, not a heroic span. You come off a line with your ears still counting, and you eat, and the food is meat and eggs and fat and the paste, and mineral salt baked until it knocks, and you do not miss greens because you have never had the category. Outsiders who stay long enough stop asking. The ones who do not stay keep looking for a stall that is not there.

The paste is the food that marks them. They eat it without comment, on flat ceramic, with the fingers, folded into rendered fat when there is fat to spare. It is sour, mineral, faintly alive in the way the pool-film is alive. Children are started on it early, a smear on the lip, and there is a window in which a child may refuse it and be coaxed, and after that window the refusal is not treated as taste. It is treated as a failure to join the street. They are not cruel about it. They are practical. A person who will not eat the paste will not thrive on the shelf, and the shelf does not have another menu waiting at the edge. There is no edge.

They keep animals the way they keep water: not as property in a tidy sense, and not as wilderness. The cornice birds are not theirs and are also the reason there are eggs in impossible quantities; people take from nests on a schedule that does not empty a ledge, and the birds return because the film and the meal-spill return. The low bands in the flooded gutters have names in each district, short names, the way drains have names, and children know which band will nose a hand and which will not. They are not pets. They are neighbors with teeth, and the rule is the same rule as with a race: do not stand where they have decided the path is. The heavy grazers on the flats are turned, not herded. Turning is a skill. You read a shoulder, you open a gap in a wall of bodies with a board or a voice or the simple fact of standing where the water-smell is wrong, and the herd revises. People who are good at this are not celebrated with titles. They are fetched when a line of rail or a new spillway has to cross a bedding ground. Fetching them is the celebration.

Killing is ordinary and not casual. Meat is a district fact. A grazer taken on the flats is a long day’s work and a distribution that runs down the walkways by evening, portions argued with the same patience as a centimeter of grade. The small dark animals are eaten when a band overgrows a gutter and starts to undermine a course of stone. Birds are eaten in molt, when they are fat and slow, and the taking is loud and communal and over by the time the flock-roar would have peaked. Nobody pretends this is separate from the cycle. The flies come to the gutting. The film takes what is washed into the races. A person who grows solemn about it is tolerated, the way a person who grows solemn about rain is tolerated. The work still has to be done before the meat spoils in the warm.

They live stacked, and stacking is kinship as much as architecture. A household is not a set of rooms on one level. It is a vertical splice: a sleeping room above a belt-room that belongs to an uncle’s line, a cistern-share two streets over that came with a marriage, a drying-rack right that is older than the people who use it. You know your people by which water they drink and which throb they sleep against. Names are used, and they are local, and they repeat across centuries because there is no pressure to invent. What matters more than the name is the location: which stair, which mark on the cistern wall, which side of the street holds the standing water. Ask where someone is from and they will tell you a district and a level and, if they trust you, a seep. The seep is the intimate fact. Steal a seep and you have done a worse thing than take a purse. The water courts exist because everyone understands this and still everyone, given a new foundation, is tempted.

They argue slowly. A dispute about grade can last months, and the people in the stained-map rooms are not priests and not quite judges. They are the ones who have walked the causeway in the rain often enough to know where the water wants to sit. They listen to a centimeter. They send someone to watch a stain dry, which takes days, and the watching is the evidence. While this goes on the rest of life continues at the reduced or the full clatter. Nobody strikes over a grade. Striking would mean stopping a line, and stopping a line spends heat and labor that the district will feel in the paste and the belts for a week. They have other ways to be immovable. A crew can work exactly to the mark and no further. A spillway can be opened on the old schedule and not the new one, until the cisterns say, in the only language they have, which schedule was right. The plateau has taught them that stubbornness outlasts shouting. The stone is the model.

Children grow up in the sound. The first thing a baby learns to sleep through is the throb, and a child who wakes at every belt-slip is a child who will be tired for years, and the household arranges itself around that without much talk. They play in water because water is the street. They learn the bands, the nests, the hot iron you do not lean on at noon, the soft-edged shadow you can still read by. They learn not to look for far. Far is a stack lost in haze, and haze is normal, and a clear hour — truly clear, the next district’s facing visible as facing and not as a paler grain — is rare enough that people stop and look, and then feel slightly foolish, and go back to the work. Schooling, where it exists, is practical and brief: grade, measure, the knock-signals, the marks on the cistern walls, the difference between a street in working order and a street whose drains have failed. Stories are about weather that organized and spillways that were opened in time, or not. The heroes are procedural. A person who stayed awake on the western rim and called the bank correctly is a person children hear about. A person who wanted to leave the shelf is a person in a different kind of story, told less often, and never with a map.

They do leave, some of them. The plateau is broad, not infinite, and there are descents, cut roads and old pack-ways down the face to countries that have trees. The ones who go are not exiles by default. They go for iron the local works cannot draw, for a dye, for a tool, for a reason that is sometimes only the rumor of a dry wind. Most come back. The air down there is said to be loud in the wrong way — full of leaves, full of birds that are not their birds — and the food does not sit right, and the nights go cold enough to be an insult. A few stay down and are spoken of as if they had died in a respectable fashion: not cursed, not hated, simply no longer on the shelf, which is the definition that matters. Those who return smell, for a week, of a different dust. Children follow them to smell it. It fades. The film comes back into the creases.

They are not solemn as a people. The loudness around them is not theirs, and they spend their closeness on jokes that do not travel, on arguments about eggs, on the pleasure of a sole that does not slip, on the small brightness of fat in a good month. They laugh with their heads tipped, mouth to ear, so the laugh stays in the gap the shelf has left them. Festivals are tied to the cistern marks and to the molt and to the rare afternoon when the cloud thins enough that shadows sharpen. Nobody pretends the mills will stop for it. The reduced clatter is the concession. People eat more meat than the distribution quite allows and settle the difference later, in portions, without interest. They sing, some districts more than others, and the songs sit under the throb rather than against it, short lines, repetitive, useful for walking a causeway when the flock is a long soft roar and speech is pointless. The words are about water and about not falling and about someone who opened a spillway with their hands when the board had swollen. There are love songs. They are about warmth that does not leave at night, which on the shelf is both the weather and the only metaphor they need.

At night the lower levels are dimmer but not dark. The stacks breathe. Lamps are few, and they are watched, because fire in a wet city is not impossible and is always expensive. People sleep in the vertical splice of their households, often more than one to a mat, because space is grade and grade is argued, and because the warmth of another body is the same warmth as the stone and therefore not a luxury. The grazers bed against the outer walls. The bands mutter in the gutters. A person waking in the middle of the reduced clatter can lie and count the layers and know, without looking, whether the races are running hard. If they are, someone is already at the spillway. That knowledge lets them sleep again. It is the closest thing they have to a prayer, and they would not call it one.

They age into width and into stain and into a preference for the water rooms over the belts. An old person on the shelf is a store of grades: which seep stole from which, which stack was bricked up around a newer boiler, which herd path was revised and never fully returned. Their authority is not mystical. It is memory of centimeters. When they die, they are not carried far. Far is a waste of a day and the body will not keep in the warm. Districts have their ways — a clean shaft of stone above a race, a burning in a works furnace that can take the load, a giving of the body to the flats where the film and the flies will do what they do to every other body. The arguments about which way are as slow as the arguments about water, and a household’s way is itself a kind of seep, inherited, not to be redirected lightly. Afterward the name stays in use. The location stays in use. Someone else sleeps against that throb.

What lives there, then, is not a tribe arranged for a visitor’s eye, and not a set of trades you could list and finish. It is a human density matched to the other densities, continuous with film and flock and wheel, shaped by a warm that does not break and a daylight that does not reach far and a sound that never offers a clean silence to think inside. They are practical, close-spoken, stained at the creases, vain about soles and spillways, humorous at short range, slow to settle a grade and immovable once the stain has dried. They will feed you the paste and watch, without cruelty, to see if you are going to be able to stay. If you stay, they will stop looking at you as weather. They will ask which stair you sleep on, and whether you can hear, from there, the moment the races change their mind.
