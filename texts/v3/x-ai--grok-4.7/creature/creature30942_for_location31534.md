They are not many for the size of what they keep. The basin looks inhabited the way a machine looks inhabited: everywhere a trace, and only now and then a person, and then usually more than one, because no one here does a large thing alone and no one wastes a walk. You meet them first at the wrong scale. A figure on an upper catwalk is a mark the size of a rivet against the kiln-hall behind it, and then the mark turns, and lifts an arm in the small economical way they have, and you understand the arm is signaling not to you but to another mark three terraces off, and that the two of them have been in conversation for most of the morning without raising a voice.

Up close they are ordinary until they are not. The first thing is the chest. Generations in air that arrives already spent have given them a ribcage that takes its time: a long, unhurried draw, a pause at the top as if the breath were being weighed, and a release they do not spend on speech if they can help it. They are not tall. The lanes are built to a hand’s reach of the level above, and people grow into the measure they are given. Shoulders tend to a slight cant, not a deformity so much as a dialect—the lean of the street they were raised on, written into the hip and never fully corrected, because correction is something you do to masonry, on a schedule, and a body is allowed to keep its history. Their skin runs dry and pale under the mineral colors of the place, and on anyone who has worked the damp band for years it carries a faint iridescent film along the forearms and the hollow of the throat, the same not-quite-life that maps the pipes. They scrape it when it lifts, the way they scrape a facing-plate. They do not scrape it for vanity. Left alone it cools them, and in this country a degree is a kindness. Newcomers who wash it off every night spend their first season feverish and offended, and then stop washing quite so thoroughly, and then stop mentioning it.

The hands tell the trade before any slate does. Press-workers have a flattened pad at the base of the thumb and a patience in the fingers that comes from waiting on a bite that takes half a shift. Condenser crews have nails rimed white, and they smell, even after washing, of the metallic water. Re-levelers—you know them by the way they stand on a lane and refuse to trust their feet. They carry a small level in the pocket the way other towns carry a knife, and they touch it without looking, the way you touch a fact. Surveyors are leaner and more sunburned, and they look at a new face of rock the way a person looks at an animal that might or might not know them. Everyone, whatever the trade, has a faint sweetness in the teeth from years of leaning on live rails. They notice it in outsiders, the little flinch, and they find it faintly funny, though they will not spend the breath to laugh about it more than once.

They do not eat what the country looks like it should refuse them. Staples come up the outer rail from older ground: hard grain, dried salt meat, oil in sealed tins, the ordinary poverty of a supply line that cannot fail and so is never abundant. What the basin itself gives them is smaller and stranger and, to them, not strange. The films scraped from the damp band are not only kiln-fodder. A portion is rinsed, soured in fault-water, pressed, and eaten as a slick, mineral-green cake that smells of hot coin and tastes of almost nothing until the second bite, when it tastes like the pipe it grew on. They call it by the name of the terrace it came from, so a meal is also a location. The shell-things in the deep cisterns are not fished. There is a rule, old enough to be unposted, that anything rare enough to stop a yard is not for the pot. When a flock crosses without landing, people go to the rail and watch it out of the basin, and afterward they are quieter at the evening meal, not from reverence exactly, but from the sense of having been briefly in the same sentence as something that does not work shifts.

Children are born into the thin air and do not know it as a tax. Their first lessons are not letters. Letters come, on slates, and they learn them well enough; the city keeps records with a clerk’s devotion. What they learn first is how to stand on a floor that will not be the same floor in ten years, and how to pass a tool up to the level above without dropping it into the gap, and how far a voice will carry before the air eats it. They play, but the play has the pace of the turbines. A game that requires sprinting dies out before it can become a tradition. A game that requires waiting, or stacking, or matching a signal from out of sight, lasts. They are not solemn. They have a humor of small misalignments: a door rehung a finger too high, a chalk mark the sun has half-erased so that a whole shift arrives an hour early, a survey stake planted where someone meant to lean a ladder. Laughter here is a short sound, close to the body, and it ends because the next breath is needed for something else, not because the joke has failed.

They pair and unpair without much theater. A household is less a romance than a negotiated level: two or three or four people sharing a room above the condensation line or just under it, according to whether their work wants them dry or wants them near the vents. The rooms are small and scrupulously bare of anything the heat will warp. What they decorate with is stone. A shelf of facing-pieces taken from walks that have since been re-leveled, each piece remembered, a private street plan. Weddings, if that is the word, are a matter of moving the chalk and the cistern-right and informing the signal arm. Funerals are slower and more exact. A body is not buried in ground that is still deciding to be ground. The dead are given to the kilns that take mineral, and the ash is pressed into a brick, and the brick goes into a wall the person had a hand in, if such a wall can be found, and if it cannot, into the newest course at the rim, where the city is still arriving. People visit the brick. They do not expect it to stay at the height they left it. Part of the visiting is to see how far it has risen or leaned, and to feel, in that, that the person is still inside the work.

They argue, and the arguments have a particular shape. Nobody shouts. A dispute that cannot be settled at arm’s length goes to the re-levelers, not because re-levelers are judges, but because most fights here are about a surface that two people remember differently, and the level is a third memory that does not take sides. When the fight is not about a surface—when it is about a slight, a theft of water-right, a chalk schedule altered—they still tend to pace it out on the lane, walking the length of the disagreement until the thin air has made the angry version of the story too expensive to keep repeating. By the third pass, what remains is the part both parties can bear to carry back up the stairs. It is not that they are good. They steal, they hoard tins, they favor their own terrace in the posting of shifts, they let a film thicken on a neighbor’s pipe and call it weather. It is that the climate bills them immediately for excess, and so excess has to be worth it.

There is a change that comes over those who stay, and it does not announce itself as a change. Outsiders notice it before residents do, and residents resent the noticing. After enough years the pause at the top of the breath lengthens. The cant of the hip deepens. Sleep comes easiest against a south face that is still warm after dark, and a person who has slept that way for a decade will wake if the rock’s ticking changes pitch, the way a mother wakes to a smaller sound than her own name. Surveyors are the furthest along this, or the most willing to admit it. They talk about a new extrusion as if it had intentions—not mystically, and they will correct you hard if you use a word like spirit—but in the practical sense that a course of blocks has intentions, a direction it wants to run, a place it will fail if you argue with it. They lay a palm on a face that was not there last season and go quiet in a way that is not performance. Some of them say the rock is learning the city. Some say the city is only the rock’s latest manner of cooling itself, and that people are included in the manner, the way the films are included, useful, temporary, and exact. The argument between these two positions is the oldest one they have, and it is conducted, characteristically, at a walk, on the unfinished rim, where the ground rings underfoot like a bowl not yet fired.

They do not think of themselves as a people apart, most days. They think of themselves as the shift that is on. The signal arm moves; a bearing warms; a cistern is sounded with a rod and found honest; a child is lifted to see a flock that will not land; a brick goes into a wall and begins, immediately, to be part of the wall’s slow decision to rise. In the evening, when the shadows lay the terraces into a single instrument, they stand in their doorways a moment longer than rest requires, feeling the heat come up through the soles, matching their breath to a rhythm they would not, if asked, call a rhythm. Then the lamps that are few are lit, and the voices that are low find their distances, and the city, which is also the ground, continues its work without needing them to watch—only to be there in the morning, when the chalk is rewritten, and to put a hand on what has moved in the night, and to call the movement by its right name, which is not damage.
