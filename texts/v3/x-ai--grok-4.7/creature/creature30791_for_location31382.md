It does not arrive. It is already the temperature of the iron when the motor begins its hour, already the color the film takes after the fine fall has rinsed and resealed it. For a long time that is all you can say of it: a preference. The shells do not graze evenly. They leave a corridor, two palms wide, that runs from the polished rail to the seam and back again along a slightly different line, and the corridor stays bright for the length of a breath before the glaze knits over it. The jointed drinkers in the pump housing never face the corridor. Their blind undersides turn toward the warmest flange, and their locked stillness is a little tighter when the corridor is fresh.

At night the corridor is used. The thing that uses it is low enough that its back has taught the rail its shine, and heavy enough that the standing sheet does not splash so much as shoulder aside and close behind. You do not see the whole of it. You see the water’s opinion of it: a moving absence the width of a barrel on its side, the surface climbing a little at the leading edge and falling flat again, as if a stone were being rolled just under the film. Where it passes, the mineral skin parts and does not immediately resent the parting. The shells clamp and wait. The scratcher in the pipe goes silent in a way that is different from its caution around you; it goes silent the way a tenant goes silent when the landlord’s key is already in the lock.

It has no single mouth you could point to. Along the flank that rides nearest the stone, a row of pale, collar-like openings flex and settle, each one taking in a tongue of the coppery seep and giving back nothing visible. The gel at the seam is not its food and not its waste. When the low shape reaches the seam it does not feed. It presents one of those collars to the extrusion until the gel beads against living tissue instead of concrete, and for a moment the two temperatures negotiate—the seam’s colder, cleaner push and the animal’s slower, slightly warmer accept—and then the collar closes and the shape turns, and the excess gel strings and breaks and is left for the queue of shells, which advance the moment the water’s absence has gone. The arrangement is old. The stain under the seam has the same patience as the streak on the rock, and the animal’s flank has a corresponding patch, tea-colored, where the gel has been received so many times the tissue no longer bothers to match the rest of its hide.

Hide is the wrong word and also the only one. It is not scaled and not furred. It is closer to the glaze on the walls, but thicker, and it can be moved. In the slot-light, when the shape holds still against the pump housing to take the motor’s faint heat, you can watch a dull copper migrate under the surface, following some internal weather, pooling where the iron is warmest and draining toward the collars when the seep is rich. Scrape it—though you will not—and it would not bleed so much as weep the same tea the rock weeps, and then knit, as the walls knit, only faster, and with a low tick like the jointed drinkers relocating. It has learned the building’s repairs. A crack in its flank that might once have been a wound is lined with the same sinter that lines the little thumb-high chimneys, white and ridged, and the animal carries the chimney as if it had always meant to grow one.

It does not have eyes in any arrangement you trust. Along the leading curve there are shallow pits, each holding a bead of the basin’s water, and the beads do not spill when it moves. They darken when the slot overhead darkens. They pale when the motor’s tremor rings the sheet. Whether they see or only register the same changes the shells register, the effect is the same: the corridor shifts a hand’s width to avoid the place where you stood an hour ago, and the shift is already decided before the water at your ankles has gone back to stillness. It is not afraid of you. Fear would be a quicker alteration. This is accounting. You are a new temperature, a new scrape-risk, a possible plug in the overflow notch. The basin has kept its level for longer than the paint on the pump lasted. The animal keeps the basin’s habits.

What it was before the works were abandoned is not available, and may not matter. The rail remembers wheels, but the polish is newer than the rust’s patience, and the animal’s weight matches the rail’s remaining strength so exactly that the iron does not complain. Perhaps it came up the overflow in some wetter year, when the notch ran deeper, and found the heat and the seep and the gel and stayed until staying became the shape of its body. Perhaps the film grew ambitious and learned to travel. The larvae in the ceramic cups do not care. When the low shape passes beneath their insulators, a fine grit of them rains onto its back and is neither shaken off nor eaten; by morning they have settled into the copper migration and vanished, or been incorporated, and the cups have bred again. The bats, or the things that counterfeit fixtures, sometimes drop and ride a short distance, wings folded, sipping whatever the collars leave on the hide. They too are accounted for. Nothing in the room is outside the accounting except the motor, which answers to a switch that is not here, and the weather, which arrives through the slot whether the corridor is open or not.

If you stay through a full cycle of the motor and the darkening and the fine fall, you can watch it complete its round and then do something that is not travel. It eases half its length into the gap beneath the pump housing, where the jointed drinkers live, and the drinkers pour once, tick, and lock themselves against it instead of against the iron, as if the warmth had been transferred and they had always known the transfer was temporary. The visible half of the animal subsides until the water closes over the copper and only the row of collars remains, each one just breaking the surface, each one opening and closing on the slow pulse of the seep. From the dry patch of iron you could touch one with a stick. You do not. The bead-pits along the submerged curve are still holding their water, still darkening and paling with the slot, and the corridor in the glaze is already brightening again where the shells have begun, carefully, not to use it.

By the time the overflow notch starts to take its centimeter, the collars have sunk. The sheet is whole. The motor’s hour ends. What remains is the shine on the rail, the tea-colored patience at the seam, the shells resuming their millimeter shifts, and the sense—persistent, unhurried, exact—that the room is not storing an animal so much as an agreement, and that the agreement was here before the last of the factory’s paint surrendered, and will be here when the slot finally narrows to a crack and the weather can no longer find its way in.
