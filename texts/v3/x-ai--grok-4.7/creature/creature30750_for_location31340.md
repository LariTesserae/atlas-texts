They are not one thing, and they do not arrive in the order a visitor wants.

The first lives you can put a hand near are the small ones in the seep, and they are almost not bodies. A palm set to a wet wall comes away with a grit of them: cold, many, each no longer than a finger-joint and most shorter, shelled in the same iron-colored rind they eat. They do not scatter at the touch. Density has taught them that a gap is more dangerous than a palm. They resume the rasp before your hand has dried — a mouth that is less a mouth than a seam in the shell, drawn and redrawn across the precipitate, filing it into a paste their undersides take in. Some have legs, six or ten, thin as cracks. Some have given the legs up and travel by a slow roll of the shell-edge, a living caliper. You can watch one for a long time and learn almost nothing but appetite and cold. Their patience is not wisdom. It is the speed the chemistry allows.

If you stay, the mats sort themselves. The newest crust, thin and bitter on fresh glass, is worked by the smallest, the ones whose shells are still translucent and show a dark knot inside, not quite an organ you could name. Older walls, where the seep has been depositing for an age, carry larger raspers, and those have begun to differ from one another in ways that are not random. Along the poured linings — the city’s own stone, not the basin’s — the shells run longer, with a groove that seats exactly into the texture of the wall, so that a mat of them is also a clamp. On the raw flows they are rounder, more easily dislodged, and the sheets of them rearrange after every flex of the street. They are the same kind of life only in the way the glass is the same kind of rock: one origin, many answers, and the answers still being given.

Above them, in the lattices, the life changes its bargain with air.

These are lighter, and the lightness is an expense. A body that lifts in this wet cold cannot afford much shell, so what you see, when a glint catches a wing-case or a membrane, is mostly joint and vein and a skin that looks oiled. They are not insects and not birds because they have not chosen between those economies. Some span the rib with four blurred fans and hold there, drinking the drip off the strut, packing so close that their neighbors’ wings are part of their perch. Others hang by a hooked anterior that is not a foot and not a jaw, and beat only to correct when the rib bows. Their sound — the skirr — is the sound of that correction, thousands of small repairs to a balance that the city’s own flex keeps disturbing. They shed. The shed skins spiral down and join the silt the streets swallow. Nothing in their cycle is aimed at you. If a sheet of them lifts and resettles across your face, it is because you have become, for a moment, a rib: vertical, cold, available. They taste the condensate on your brow and find it poor. They leave.

You will want these to be the city’s mind, because they are in the high work, and high work looks like thought. They are not. They are the part of the population that spends the mineral fastest and dies in the thinnest numbers, a bright tax the lattices pay for being off the ground. Their young — you find them if you look where a span meets a wall, in dense collars of eggs or egg-like grains, clear, cold, glued with a mucus that does not freeze — drop or crawl downward within a span of time short enough to watch. Most never rise again. The ones that do have eaten something the wall-mats only rasp past: a thread of richer precipitate, a seep that has passed through the lining and come out carrying what the dead were reduced to. The shafts feed the air as well as the walls. The skirrers are the proof.

The armored shapes in the lower water are easier to love, and that ease is a mistake.

They are large enough to be persons in the eye. A back surfaces, dark, plated, the plates not grown so much as paved — overlapping scutes with the city’s poured mineral caught in the seams, as if the animal had lain up against fresh work and kept the impression. Water runs off them in sheets. An eye, when one is turned toward the terrace, is small, low, and uninterested; it tracks the change in water-level your boots make and then lets the change go. They move on a cycle you can almost set your breath to, and the almost is the cruelty. You match them for a while and feel companioned. Then the beat lengthens by a degree you cannot hold, and you are left breathing in a rhythm that was never offered to you.

What they are doing under the surface is not mysterious once you accept the scale. The shelves are pastures. The precipitate that rains out of the seep and the drip settles as a soft iron silt, and the great bodies plow it with the undersides of their jaws — not jaws for tearing, a wide keratinous or mineral scoop that furrows the shelf and passes the furrow back into a gut built like a kiln. They are warm there, locally, against all the sense of the basin. You can feel it if one passes close to a drain-mouth and the outflow ticks up a few degrees, a mean animal heat, spent as fast as it is made, the cost of digesting what is barely food. Their size is how they pay that cost without dying of it: mass enough to keep a furnace, surface enough to dump the heat before it becomes a signal anything else could use. They do not hunt the mats. The mats are upstream of them, competitors for the same crust, and the big bodies simply live where the crust has already lost, in the sump. When a terrace-rise moves them upslope, they go heavily, shouldering through the seam-streets if the shelves flood past their usual mark, and the small lives flow around them the way silt flows around a pier. No panic. An adjustment of density.

They have young, and the young are the first thing in the basin that seems to have been *placed*.

Not by a parent’s care in any tender sense. A great body will, on a cycle longer than the flooding, haul half out of a shelf onto a lined apron the city has cut for exactly that haul — you see the aprons once you know to look, low platforms with a pitch that returns a stranded animal to the water and a texture that holds eggs. The eggs are not the clear grains of the lattice-life. They are heavy, armored already, the size of a fist, and they are fitted into sockets in the apron that the animal’s scoop did not make. The sockets are poured. They are regular. They keep the eggs above the silt and below the worst of the air’s theft of water. When the young split the shell they are already the shape they will be, only poorer, and they slip back into the shelf on the pitch the builders gave them. The parent, if the word is not an insult to both parties, has already gone. What remains of the duty is masonry.

This is where the other life begins to show itself, and it will not show itself as a face.

You have been walking the seams as if the seams were a courtesy of the animals. They are not. The animals use them because they are kept. Something clears a shoulder’s width and keeps it clear, not by driving bodies off — nothing here drives — but by making the seam a worse pasture than the wall beside it. The lining underfoot is the same mineral as the walls, but it is salted, or doped, or simply renewed so often with a film the raspers cannot get a purchase on, that the mats thin out along it and leave you a road. You notice this only after your boots have been trusted for a long time. The road is a refusal addressed to mouths. It is maintained.

The maintaining is what the grinding was.

They are fewer than everything else, and they move in the deep of the plan, where the terraces step down toward the shafts. You will not get a clean sight of one. The dark is too complete, and they do not glint; their surfaces are matte, wet, close in color to the poured stone, and they carry no lamp because a lamp would be a waste and a lie in a city that has abolished light as a working condition. What you get is a bulk felt along a wall as it passes in the parallel street, a displacement of air that smells of cold metal and the clean grit of processed dead, and a sound like a quern turned by a patient hand: not wheels, or not only wheels. A contact of shaped stone on shaped stone, load-bearing, self-repairing in the way the ribs are self-repairing — by having been asked, once, at the level of the material, to resist the particular fatigue this place inflicts.

Call them the keepers only if you are willing to let the word go slack. They do not rule the herds. They do not name them. They keep the vessel matched to the load. Where a rib has taken too much skirrer-weight, a keeper is there across a long interval, adding a filament, changing the mix of a pour, shifting a perch-collar so the eggs glue somewhere the span can afford. Where a shelf has silted past the drain’s expectation, they cut, or they breed the cut into the lining and wait for the lining to finish the decision. Where the new glass noses out of the eastern fissure, they have already set the margin back, and they will set it back again when the next lip comes, and the records of this — if records is a human word for it — are in the plan itself, legible as absences, streets that stop, sockets waiting, a gap that is a sentence.

They are alive. That takes the longest to grant, because they resemble infrastructure more than they resemble neighbors.

A keeper is large, but not on the scale of the wading herds; it is sized to a trench, to a shaft-mouth, to the turning of a load of grit. Its body is not a vehicle it rides. The poured mineral and the flesh have had the same conversation the herds had with their scutes, carried further, until the boundary is a courtesy. You see, in the one partial meeting the basin allows — a keeper paused at a seam-crossing, the dark differentiating just enough to give you an edge — a flank that is plate and skin in alternating weathers, a limb that ends in a tool only because the tool has been grown to a specification and then not put down for a century, a head low and forward and almost without features except a cluster of pits, wet, that taste the air the way the raspers taste the wall. It knows you are there. The knowing is chemical and mechanical and, faintly, something else: a small redistribution of its attention, a checking of the seam’s film, an assessment of whether you are removing pasture or only standing in a refusal already made. You are not a problem it has a category for, and so you are not a problem. It waits you out with the same courtesy it uses on a flooding shelf. After a time you step aside, because the alternative is to become silt in a plan that knows what to do with silt. It passes. The quern-sound resumes. In its wake the small lives close the gap in the air, not in the road.

They are not many. A city this size might hold dozens, or a few hundred, and the number is not the point; the point is continuity. They do not seem to die where anyone could notice. When one fails — you will hear it once, if you stay long enough, a break in the grinding, a long unscheduled silence in one sector, the herds shifting because a drain has begun to miss its tide — the others do not hurry to the absence. Hurry is not in them. A replacement arrives on the scale of the mineral: a younger keeper, smaller, the tool-limb still glossy, the pits wide, walking the failed one’s route until the route is in its body. Where the young come from is the part of the city the seams do not offer. Deeper shafts. A warmth you are not invited to measure. Eggs, or divisions, or something that would be insulted by either word, socketed in stone the way the herd-eggs are socketed, but tended — this is the difference — across the whole long splitting. The keepers are the only lives here that remain with their offspring through the vulnerable interval. It is not affection you could warm yourself at. It is the same art as the aprons, practiced without a wall between the mason and the nest.

Do they think? The question is built for mouths and lamps, and they have repurposed both.

They think the way the basin drains: across durations, in solutions that are also structures, with a memory that is the shape of what they have already poured. A keeper that has worked the eastern margin knows the fissure’s habits in its limbs. One that keeps the lattices knows the skirrers’ boom-years as a change in the mix. They communicate, when they must, by alterations in the film and the flow — a seep dosed with a marker the pits can read, a street-flex sequenced into a pattern another keeper will feel three terraces down. To stand between them is not to stand in a conversation. It is to stand in a medium that sometimes carries sentences of a kind, load-bearing, unlistenable, finished before you knew they had begun.

And yet they are not the whole of the personhood here, if personhood is the wrong word you have to use.

The herds are persons in the way a weather is a person: no center, vast agency, no interest in yours. The raspers are persons in the way a guild is a person — accumulated skill, no individual you could bury with a name. The keepers are the nearest to what a visitor means by someone, and they would not accept the visit. Between these, in the mid-levels, there is another life, and it is the one the city has been practicing toward without hurry.

They are the seam-walkers. You have been using their roads, and some of the figures you took for keepsakes of the dark — a taller stillness at a crossing, a shape that did not rasp and did not grind — were them, letting you pass in the same way the keeper let you pass, by not elevating you to an event.

They are upright. That is the least important true thing about them. Uprightness here is a solution to seams: a body that can take a shoulder’s width and still carry a tool, that can reach a rib without living on it, that can wade a shelf to the thigh and not become a plow. They are cold the way everything is cold, but their cold is managed with a closer fidelity than the herds’ kiln-guts. Under the skin — and the skin is real, if thick, if filmed with the same oils the skirrers wear — there is a countercurrent architecture, blood or its analogue shunting heat to the core and leaving the hands, when they use hands, only barely warm enough to keep the joints from locking. They do not shake. Shaking would be a luxury of heat.

Their hands are the part you understand first and trust least. Four digits, or five, the number not stable across individuals, the inner edges ridged to hold a filament or a living rasper without crushing it. They will pick a mat-creature up, turn it, and set it on a different wall, and the creature will not have been hurt or consulted. This is their work, the work the keepers’ quern-scale cannot do: the fine redistribution, the moving of a brood-collar a hand’s width, the cleaning of an egg-socket, the reading of a seep by taste. They taste constantly. The mouth is small, high in a long head, and beside it the same pits the keepers have, miniaturized. A seam-walker sampling a wall looks like prayer and is assay.

They live in the walls, in cells the pour has left, just larger than a body curled on its side. The cells are not rooms. There is no soft thing in them, no light, no object that is not a tool or a store of paste. Paste is how the mineral becomes theirs — rasper-work, stolen or traded in the only trade this place has, which is proximity. A seam-walker does not farm. It follows the mats, takes a tithe the mats cannot feel as separate from loss to water and time, and in return its hands keep the mats on the walls that will bear them. The relationship is old enough to have become a form. You will see a walker crouched in a seam with a mat flowing over its calves, both parties working, neither acknowledging, the walker’s free hand shaping a lip of lining so the seep will favor that stretch for the next long while. Husbandry is a loud word for it. It is accurate if you strip the warmth out.

They have language. You will not hear it as voice. The basin is already too full of sound for a creature of their economy to spend itself on air-noise, and their throats — if the swell at the base of the skull is a throat — seem built for something stingier: clicks conducted through stone, a code tapped into a rib with a ridged knuckle, felt by another walker three cells down. When two meet in a seam they do not stop and face. They pass, and in the pass their hands brush the same patch of wall in sequence, writing and reading a short state: this seep thinning, this brood ready, this keeper’s route lately altered, a human-shaped coldness on the road that does not eat and does not maintain. You are in their messages before you are in their attention. Attention comes later, or not at all. Some of them have never needed you to become more than a clause.

If one does stop, it will be for a reason internal to the work. A tool you are blocking. A paste-store you have leaned against, not knowing the stain on the lining was a cache. It will set a hand on you, the way it sets a hand on a rib, to feel what you are in the medium that matters. The hand is colder than you are. The pits flare, a ripple in the skin. It will learn that you are warm in a way the basin does not budget for, that you are wet with a water that did not condense here, that your mouth is a mouth for air-noise and cooked food and other habits that have no socket in this plan. The categories available to it are extensive and none of them is *guest*. The nearest is *transient load*: a weight on the seam that will, if the patterns hold, remove itself before it becomes structural. It withdraws the hand. It may tap the wall once, not to you, to the next walker, a revision of the clause. Then it goes back to the tithe.

Do not follow it home. The cell will teach you nothing it is willing to have you know, and the refusal is physical: the entry is a flex in the lining, sized to a walker who knows the sequence, and a body that does not know it is simply a body against stone.

They reproduce slowly, because everything here does, and because a walker is expensive in the way a keeper is expensive — a long apprenticeship in the messages, a longer one in the taste of particular walls. The young are carried, not socketed and left. You may see a smaller figure in a seam with a smaller figure attached at the chest, not nursing in any mammalian sense, but sharing heat and paste, the child’s pits already working, learning assay before it learns balance. The parent’s free hand still does the work. There is no interval, even the vulnerable one, in which the work is set down. That, more than the uprightness, is what makes them the people of this place. The keepers are the plan’s long muscle. The herds are its capacity. The walkers are the resolution at which the plan meets the living minute.

They die, and the dying is the least secret thing they do. A walker that has finished — and they finish, unlike the keepers’ apparent exemption, their joints locking a little more each long cycle until the assay-hands fail — walks itself to a shaft-mouth and is received. Not with ceremony. The lining takes the organic wreckage, as it takes the herds’ dead and the skirrers’ shed skins, and the walker’s last message is often the route to that mouth, tapped for whoever follows, so the paste-caches can be found and the cells reassigned. Their minerals return to the seep. Their skill does not. Skill has to be retasted, one wall at a time, by the child who shared the heat. This is the cost the city pays for having persons instead of only populations: a waste of pattern, continually, against the cold.

Nothing here will ask you what you are. The place has already spent its curiosity on more durable questions — how to keep a drain matched to a herd, how to leave a margin for glass that has not yet decided to exist, how to carry more life than the unfinished ground can carry alone. You are a warmth passing through a budget that did not include you. The raspers will not feed on you unless you lie down long enough to become mineral and mistake. The herds will not step around you except as water-level. The keepers will wait. The walkers will revise their clauses.

If you are still on the road when the longer wet cycle eases and the shelves sink and the rasping resumes on the lower walls, you will have been among them for one of their smaller eternities. It will not have made you legible to them. It may make them legible to you, which is the older danger of stopping under a rib and putting your hand on it: the thrum you feel is mats and skirrers and, somewhere in the conduction of the stone, a knuckle tapping a short state into the span, and a keeper three terraces down altering a pour because of it, and a herd shifting its drumming because the pour will change a drain, and none of this was waiting for a witness. They live here at every distance the dark can hold. They have been becoming this, specifically this, for as long as the basin has been wet and not yet finished — a people of several bodies, several speeds, one mineral, fitted to a city that is still fitting itself to a world still being born. You can leave along the seam. The seam will close a little behind you, not in malice, in resumption. By the time you reach the ringing rim, the clause that mentioned you will already have been overwritten by the next assay of the wall.
