What lives here does not occupy the chamber. It uses it the way a breath uses a lung: by entering, turning, and leaving a difference so slight the stone can pretend nothing happened.

You will not see it first. You will notice that one of the amber seams is warmer on one side than the other, as if something has been standing in its light long enough to take a little of it and give a little back. Then you will notice the grit. The two worn paths are not only yours, and not only ancient. A third track crosses them at a shallow angle, not a foot’s track and not a drag, a polishing, the width of a hand, repeated until the aggregate there is darker and slightly concave. It does not lead to a den. It leads from the largest pool to the coolest cornice and back again by a different bay, a circuit measured in the chamber’s own pitches rather than in distance.

Call it, for a while, a body, because it has weight and it displaces water. When it drinks—and it does drink, from the metallic pocket, never from the one that skins over with mineral—the surface bows and does not splash. It takes water through a seam along what would be a throat if the anatomy agreed to be vertebrate. The rest of it is closer to the ropes of growth on the cornice than to any animal you could name from above ground: a length of darkened tissue, jointed in long shallow angles, able to hold a curve the way the oval holds a curve. It is not large. Stretched along a rib it might reach from one dead seam to the next live one. Folded, which is how it rests, it is no bigger than a coat left on a bench, and about as declarative.

Its color is the color of the stone when the amber fails—warm gray with a film of damp. In the light it does not shine. It has learned, or been made, or simply worn down to, the same refusal the walls have: not to announce an edge. Only when it moves across a seam do you catch the difference, a slower brightening, as if the light had to pass through something before it reached the mineral. There are no eyes you can count. There are patches, low on the length, that answer to heat more than to brightness; it orients by the basin’s pulse under it and by the change in pitch when it leaves one bay for another. The chamber is its map. Loudness is not a nuisance to it. Loudness is the street.

It is not the only thing that passes through. The clicks on the upper course are smaller lives, hard-shelled, keeping to the dry flanges, and the ring in the pool is sometimes one of them falling and righting and climbing out again by a route it already knows. The long body does not hunt them in any way you would call hunger. It crosses their paths and they adjust, the way grit adjusts around a warmer stone. What it needs from the room is the water that wins against the wall, the thin heat conducted up through the aggregate, and the fibrous mat, which it does not eat so much as wear down—pressing a portion of itself into the mat until some of the pigment and some of the fiber come away duller. Whether that is feeding or maintenance is a distinction the chamber has stopped making.

There is a schedule, if you stay long enough to insult the word. After the duct in the crown coughs and the oval rings, the body is always nearer the center than before. The hard metallic sound seems to gather it. It holds still through the resonance, flattened to the basin, and only when the civic note has settled back into its lower register does it resume the polishing track. In that pause it is easiest to mistake for a stain, or for a repair the old engineers left unfinished. Then a joint takes its angle, and the stain has a direction.

Closer, if it allows closeness—which it does, indifferently, because you are not a pitch it has catalogued as threat—the surface is not smooth. It is shingled with minute plates of the same fused aggregate as the floor, not grown from it exactly, but accreted, a geology it has agreed to carry. Between the plates the tissue is soft, faintly ridged, and warmer than the stone by the amount a living thing can afford in a room that is already warm. Press of air from the working system below lifts the edges of the plates and settles them. It breathes with the machinery without being the machinery. When the condensation cycle turns and a pocket gains a finger’s width, a corresponding darkening moves along its length, a tide under the shingles. It is keeping time with a clock that no longer has hours.

What it is not: a person gone strange, a machine grown a skin, a god of the hollow, a pet of the vanished settlement. Those are shapes you bring down the stair with you, and they slide off it. It has no tools and no altars. The filled doorways do not interest it except as changes in echo. If the old city had a name for this room, the body does not use names. It uses the note under the ribs, the tick of drops on the warm face, the irregular pulse that is almost regular. In that narrow music it is entirely competent. Outside it—above, in weather, in streets that have forgotten the shell under them—it would be a wrongness, too slow, too tuned, unable to find the floor’s answer. Here it is only another maintained function, unlisted.

Sometimes, rarely, there are two. Not a pair in the social sense. A second length, shorter, paler between the plates, will lie in a parallel polish-track for part of a cycle and then be gone into the upper course where the light does not reach, and you will not know whether it left by a seam you cannot see or whether it thinned until the stone took it back. The longer body does not follow. It finishes its circuit. It drinks. It presses the mat until the mat shivers and goes still. It returns to the center when the duct clears its throat, as if answering a roll that is still being called in a language of metal and heat.

If you remain, it will eventually include you in the map: a warmer interruption, a new grit-pattern, a slight flattening of one pitch where your body blocks a bay. It will route around you with the same unannoyed exactness it uses for a rib. It may, once, come near enough that you feel the small lift of air under its plates, and smell metal and wet dust, the smell of the pocket it prefers. It will not touch you unless you lie in the polishing track and refuse to move. Even then the contact is administrative—a slow pressure, testing whether the obstacle conducts heat, whether it will give water, whether it belongs to the cycle. When it learns you do not, it withdraws, and the track bends, and by the next brightening of the seams the bend has been polished into the new correct path.

Leave, and it continues. That is the whole of its inhabiting. The emptiness stays empty; the body is a line drawn through absence and erased by the next turn of damp and heat, except for the dark concave in the grit and the mat worn a little thinner and the pocket a finger lower than the wall’s accounting said it should be. The chamber was built to be inhabited. This is what answered, not by moving in, but by becoming one of the things the room does while it goes on with its work: a warm, jointed listening, shingled in the stone’s own stuff, keeping the circuit, clearing nothing, repairing nothing, and still, unmistakably, living in the sound.
