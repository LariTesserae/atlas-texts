They are short in the way a tree is short that has spent its life in wind: not stunted, finished. The table has selected for a body that does not need much water and does not waste the step it has already taken. They stand low to the warm stone, chests wide, wrists thick, feet splayed and hard as the coping they walk. Height would only lift them into the haze and the tug of the lip. Most of them come to the shoulder of a lowland traveler and look, from a doorway, already complete — as if any additional length would be a mistake the place has long since corrected.

Their skin is the color of the lee-side dust when it has been oiled: dull ochre, sometimes greyer at the temples, sometimes a deep clay where a family has lived for generations against the western crease, in the slight extra shadow of the stair-cut. The oil is constant. It is the bitter fraction of the press, the part that will not feed a root and will not keep as meal, rubbed into scalp, forearms, the backs of knees, the cracked lips. Without it the haze eats them. With it they gleam faintly in the slits of light, not wet, polished. You can tell who has been at the presses that morning by the darkness in the creases of their palms. You can tell who has not left their roof for days by the pale bloom of dust settled into the oil along the cheekbone, undisturbed.

Hair is cut for the thatch and the second street. Long hair catches in cane and takes oil it does not return. So they wear it close, or braided tight against the skull with a cord of sword-leaf fiber, or shaved on the windward side and left as a dark cap elsewhere — quarter by quarter the custom shifts by a finger’s width, and a person from the northern lip can be known across a courtyard by the way the nape is bared to the swift-sound. Beards, when they happen, are small and kept, oiled until they lie like a second skin. Children go dusty on purpose until they are old enough to be trusted with a bowl of the bitter fraction; a clean child is a child whose household is showing off, and showing off costs moisture.

The eyes are the part that does not match the dimness. They are large, the lids heavy, the irises a range of resin colors — amber, fig-skin, the green of a cut leaf before it beads. In the slots of the lanes they seem almost overbuilt, as if the town were still expecting a brighter age and had not yet asked the body to give the extra back. They squint rarely. The haze has already done the squinting for them. What they watch is near: the level in a press-cup, the set of a lizard on a sill, the small change in a neighbor’s gait that means the western stairs were walked and the knees have not forgiven it. Far sight is a rumor they do not miss. There is no far, except the drop, and the drop is not for looking into for long.

They do not hurry. Hurry is a kind of thirst. A person moving fast breathes dry and spends what the leaf-meal took all morning to restore. So the walk is a roll from the outer foot, economical, quiet on the polished stone except where a sledge must complain or a child has not yet learned the cost of a shout that is not required. They speak in the spaces the insects leave, which are narrow, and so their talk is pitched to carry without volume: consonants that click against the teeth, vowels held just long enough to be heard three turns down a lane and no farther. A quarrel sounds, to an outsider, like a bargaining. A bargaining sounds like a quarrel. Both are short. The town is too dense for a long anger; you will meet the person again at the press before your pulse has settled, and the press does not care who was right.

Names are places. A child is called by the courtyard, then by the lane, then — if they do something that fixes them — by the thing they tend. The woman who knows which rosette has bolted early is not called by a birth-name in the street; she is the Pit-Watcher, and her daughter will be the Pit-Watcher if her eyes stay large enough and her hands stay honest. Birth-names exist and are used indoors, in the warm dark, low enough that the thatch-insects cover them. To use one in a lane is an intimacy or an accusation. Most people die still owning one name the town has never heard.

What they tend is the closed loop, and they are proud of it in a way that has no speech. Pride is the refusal to be the person who tipped a rinse onto stone instead of roots, the person who threw a cracked pin over the lip because the walk to the mender was long, the person who bled a goat-beast for a feast when the quarter’s count was already thin. There is no law-court. There is the press, which is shared, and the count, which is spoken aloud at the slow hour by whoever has the memory for it, and the edge, which keeps the only punishment that cannot be walked back from. A theft of stored sap is answered by a season of carrying other people’s burdens until the sap is felt, in the shoulders, to have been returned. A lie about the count is worse. The count is how the town knows it is still the size of its leaves. To inflate it is to drink the future.

They are numerous and unafraid of one another. Doors are not barred; a barred door would mean sickness or a birth or the one day in a decade when someone has come up the western stairs with news that must be sorted before it spreads. At night the roofs are a single dormitory. Bodies fit between party walls with the same economy as the planting pits: a child, a beast, an elder whose joints prefer the stored heat of the coping to the cooler floor indoors, a couple who want the insects louder than their own talk. Privacy is a slit of light and a voice kept under the insect-line. They do not experience this as poverty. A room alone, a street alone, a night without the surf of wings at the lip, would feel like the start of the emergency — the quiet that means the swifts have shifted, or the haze has thickened into something that does not merely tire the light but takes it.

They make with their hands everything the loop requires, and they make it to be mended, not replaced. A knife is a knuckle of the poor flint from the dust-mounds, hafted in hard-stem wood, riveted with the soft metal that is older than any living household and passed down as carefully as a press-cup. When the edge fails it is knapped again until it is a scraper, then a borer, then grit for the planting pits. Cord is twisted in the doorways, fiber from the terraces below the lip, where a few households keep the right to descend and return with bundles before the day’s warmth has left the stone. Those households are not richer. They are saltier — the air in the crease is different, and their oil takes a sharper smell, and their knees go early, and the town grants them the first pour of the sweet fraction in trade. Nobody envies the stairs. Everybody wants the fiber.

Their thought runs in loops too. They are very good at noticing a small difference and very poor, by lowland measures, at imagining a different town. Ask them what the world below the sheer is and they will tell you about the traveling: a slower version of themselves, it is assumed, on a ground that is not finished, where water sometimes lies on the surface like a mistake and people have not yet learned to put it back. Some of the young go down once, on a dare or a grief, and come back thinner, with stories of open distance that sound, in the lanes, like fever. The town listens. The town feeds them extra meal for a month. The town does not go to look. The table is the livable world, and livable has a precise meaning here: you can walk the circuit, you can name the presses, you can hear your own quarter’s birds over the general scream, and you will not, if you are careful, dry out before your household has learned your hands.

They die on the stone, mostly, and are not heavy about it. An elder who can no longer reach the roof-path moves down into the room with the slit and continues to twist cord until the hands forget. When they stop, the body is carried at the slow hour to the lee edge, where the dust-mounds have been growing for longer than the street plan, and is laid in the grit with a little of the bitter oil on the brow and a rosette planted at the feet if the season allows. Nothing is burned that could be meal or fiber. The mounds are landform, and the dead become landform, and children play on the lower slopes without dread because dread would require a belief that the dead have gone somewhere else. They have not. They are in the lee, with the ash and the husk, holding the table’s slight tilt against the wind that is almost never strong.

There is one habit that does not look like maintenance, and they will not explain it if asked directly. At the dimmest part of the day, when the brassy sky has given up even the tired light and the stone is still throwing warmth, someone from each quarter walks to their stretch of parapet. They do not go together. They do not chant. They put a hand on the shin-high stone, the way you put a hand on a beast to know if it is still itself, and they stand while the swifts pour up the face in their thousands, a river of wings that never wets anything. They are counting, perhaps. They are being counted, perhaps, by whatever part of the town is not human and has addresses anyway. After a while they go back along the roof-paths, stepping over sleepers, and the insects receive them without emphasis.

What lives here is a people who have made a peace with a finished mountain. They are dry, dense, oiled, loud only in the ways the place is already loud, and as continuous as the thatch-scream. They do not wait for rain. They do not pray for a higher peak or a softer cliff. They press the leaf, return the bitter part to their own skin, return the sweet part and the residue to the pits, and keep the edge clean of everything but hands. In the warm dark of a slit-lit room, if you are trusted with a birth-name, you will hear them speak more softly than the lanes allow, about nothing that needs a future: whose beast has learned the roof-path, whose press-screw is talking in a new voice, whose child has the large eyes and the honest hands. Outside, the table holds. The haze does not settle. The town, fitted to it, does not aspire to be anywhere you could point to on a map that included rivers.
