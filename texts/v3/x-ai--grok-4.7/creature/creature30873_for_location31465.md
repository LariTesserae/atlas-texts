They leave the evidence before they leave a face. A tin cup set in a wall-niche at the height of a child’s reach, still wet inside. A shirt hung on a guy-wire so the heat will finish what the basin started, one sleeve longer than the other from being wrung out the same way every morning. A line of wet footprints that stop where a person remembered, halfway up a flight, that they had left the lower gate on the latch that does not hold, and turned back. By the time you meet anyone, you have already been walking through the decisions they make without looking up.

They are not many, and they are not few. The valley holds them the way it holds water: not as a flood, as a standing level. If you tried to count them from the upper parapet you would get it wrong in both directions. A court that looks empty is only between tasks. A walk that looks crowded is three households using the same stair because the other stair is wet and the goats have claimed the coping. They live stacked, and they live sideways. A family is not a set of rooms so much as a vertical right of use — the tank on the roof, the bed on the third terrace, the drying rack that belongs to them even though it is bolted to a wall everyone passes. Kinship runs along pipes. You know who is whose by who is allowed to open which valve without asking.

They are brown and sun-dark and concrete-pale by turns, depending on the hour and which face they have been working. Hands are the first true portrait. The skin at the thumb-web is thickened from gate-latches and from the coarse fiber of the amendment sacks. Nails are short and gray at the quick from lime. There is almost always a pale line at the wrist where a cuff was rolled and the sun could not follow. Faces, when you get them, are quieter than the hands. The brightness has taught them not to perform looking. They glance, place you, return to the task. Strangers are not rare enough to be an event — the cable car brings people, the far notch sends people, the town’s-width of roofs across the valley is other people — but strangers who stop are unusual. Stopping is what you do when something has failed.

Their clothes are the color of whatever was cheapest to dye and then of whatever the dust did to that. Cotton mostly, some of it very old, patched at the shoulder where a strap wears through, patched again at the knee from the terraces. A few wear the pale coats of the engine-house, canvas gone soft, cuffs tied with wire because buttons are a thing that disappears. Children go barefoot until the upper walks, where the concrete in full sun will raise a blister, and then they put on the rubber shoes that everyone owns and nobody likes, which live in a row inside every lower doorway like a second family that never comes in. Hats are less fashion than a negotiated truce with the light: brims of split reed, occasionally a metal basin-lid pressed into service and laughed at and kept.

They speak in a way that fits the carrying sound. Not loud. Pitched to land one terrace away and no farther, so that a call for a tool does not become a call for the whole valley. The words are quick on the verbs and slow on the place-names. Every court, every tank, every flight has a name, and the names are mostly practical and mostly old and occasionally both at once — the Black Joint, the Third Overflow, the Stair That Ate the Barrow. They swear by the engine when something sticks, a short flat curse that is also a kind of respect. They do not talk much about the valley as a whole. Talking about the whole is a visitor’s habit. They talk about the level in the east tank, and whether the night-scrape was worth piping, and whose vine has crossed the line again.

Work is the true portrait, and it changes them as the day changes the stone.

In the early brightness, before the roofs begin to ping, the water-keepers are already on the upper walks. They are not a caste so much as the people whose turn it is, though some turns have lasted twenty years because the person was good at it and the valley is conservative about goodness. They move with a dipping step, listening at joints, laying a palm on a pipe to feel if it is conducting or only pretending. A woman with a bad knee — she has had it since a stair failed under a sack, and she will not discuss the sack — runs the highest rank of tanks. She knows by the smell of the damp which roof-scrape was heavy with the dust of the upper road and which was clean. She will not let the dirty water into the drinking line. She has opinions about this that are not negotiable, and the valley has arranged itself around her opinions so thoroughly that they no longer look like opinions. Her name is used as a measure. If a pipe is “as she left it,” it is correct.

Lower, in the courts where the light comes down in coins, the pruners work. They are younger on average, because the reaching is endless, but the one who actually decides what is cut is old and sits on a crate and points with a stick. He does not trust anyone else’s sense of what the vine is trying to do. Left alone, the planting would seal the walks in three seasons. Cut too hard, the made soil bakes and the next rain — such as it is — runs off instead of in. So he points, and they cut, and the spent green is not waste. It goes into the hoppers. The hoppers feed the engine-house, or rather feed what the engine-house is for, which is not power first. Power is a byproduct people have come to depend on. The first product is the breakdown: leaf and rind and the pale cuttings, chopped and warmed and turned with the amendment into the dark fibrous stuff that goes back into the beds. The valley eats its own shade and sweats soil. The pruners smell of sap by midmorning. They talk more than the water-keepers. They sing, sometimes, a work-song that is mostly counting, so no one loses the rhythm of the blade near a hand.

The engine-house people are fewer and damper. They come up into the courts at the change of belt looking slightly boiled, hair stuck to the forehead, and they blink in the hard light as if they have been underground, though they have only been inside a room that has no outside wall — the house is built into a cut, so three sides are the mountain wearing concrete. Their hands are black in the creases no soap quite reaches. They are patient with machines and short with people who do not understand that a knock means wait, not strike it. One of them, a narrow man who has never been seen to hurry, keeps a notebook in which the knock-intervals are written in a private notation. He claims he can hear a bearing going a week before it goes. People believe him because he has been right often enough that belief is cheaper than doubt. He eats standing up. He has a daughter who will not go into the engine-house, not from fear of the belts but because she has decided the vines are more interesting, and this is the great unresolved argument of their household, conducted mostly through other people.

Children are everywhere and nowhere in particular. They are used. That is the honest word. Not cruelly — the valley is too dependent on their eyes to waste them — but used. Small enough to walk the pipe-galleries where an adult must crawl, they are sent to listen for the particular tick that means a joint is taking air. They learn the galleries before they learn to read, and some of them never do learn to read with any love, because the galleries already taught them a system, and systems are what they respect. They keep the goats from the new render. They carry messages that are too nuanced for a shout across a court — not urgent messages, urgent ones get shouted, but the ones that would sour if they were public. A boy with a scar across the bridge of his nose, from a fall he describes differently every time, is the best of the listeners. He can tell you which tank is stressing its wall by the way the overflow talks. Adults pretend this is a game he is good at. It is not a game, and he knows it, and the pretending is a kindness he has started to find insulting. He will be a water-keeper. The woman with the bad knee has already begun to leave certain valves in positions only he would think to check, which is her way of hiring him without the indignity of saying so.

There are old people who no longer climb, and the valley has built them a life at the level they can still reach. This is not softness. A person who cannot do the stairs becomes a person who watches a gate, or keeps the seed, or mends the reed hats, or sits where the channel pulse goes past and notes the hour it started, because the hour matters and someone must be still enough to catch it. They are sharp. Stillness has given them the whole noise of the place as a single instrument, and they hear the wrong note sooner than anyone walking. An old man at the lower gate can tell a stranger’s errand from the way the stranger takes the first stair — the paused foot of someone who was not raised on uneven risers. He does not always share what he hears. Information is a tool, and he is not a generous man with tools. He was, once, a cable-car rigger, and he lost two fingers to a cable that slipped its clamp, and he has organized his remaining opinions around the idea that most accidents are a failure to respect a schedule. People nod when he says this. They also send the children the long way around his chair when they are carrying something they would rather not explain.

Animals live among them not as pets and not as wild things but as neighbors with worse manners and better excuses. The collared goats belong to households and also do not. A collar marks who pays if a goat ruins a bed; it does not mark who the goat believes it belongs to. They know every gap in every rail, and they know which pruner will throw a cutting and which will not, and they time their appearances to the second. Birds nest in the failed render and are sworn at and also left alone, because a court with birds is a court where the insects do not take the fruit, and the fruit is money, or what passes for it — credit at the sheds, weight at the hoppers, a sack of amendment you do not have to buy. Someone always feeds the lizards, pretending not to. Someone always fails to feed the lizards and then complains about the moths. The frogs in the basins are nobody’s, and therefore everybody’s good luck, and a dry basin is reported with the seriousness of a broken pipe.

At midday the valley changes inhabitants without anyone arriving or leaving. The workers go in. Shutters come across. The loudness drops by one layer — not the engine, never the engine — and what remains in the white open is the goats, the hawks, and the people whose work cannot be paused: a man scraping a roof because the tank below him is low and the day’s condensation is the day’s drinking; a pair of riggers on the cable incline, small against the pale cut, moving with the ritual slowness of people who work above a drop. Inside, in the stacked rooms, the dark is a mercy and a smell. Cooked rind. Lime. The sweet close air of too many people breathing in a space that was sized for the mountain’s convenience rather than theirs. They eat what the terraces are giving. This week it is a sour fruit that stains the mouth and is better cooked with the pale amendment’s cleaner cousin, a ground seed that thickens. They argue about portions with the fluency of people who have never had the luxury of not counting. They sleep in the worst of the heat if they can, short and shallow, the way you sleep when you know a belt will need changing before evening.

There is a person the valley is currently in the process of making, and has not finished.

She came from the far notch — not recently, not a stranger anymore, but not born to the pipes either, so she still sees the system instead of only inhabiting it. She keeps the seed. This is more power than it sounds. The made soil will grow almost anything that can stand the brightness and the lime, but it will not grow the same thing twice without help, not well, and the seed that works here has been selected by decades of someone paying attention to which plant survived a dry week and which one only looked hardy. She writes. That is the unusual thing, and it has made her slightly apart. Not a notebook of knock-intervals: a book of what was planted where, and what came back, and what the fruit weighed, and what the leaf did in the hopper. People bring her sport-plants the way you bring a suspicion to someone who will not laugh. A boy from the third court found a vine throwing a flower no one remembered authorizing. She has it in a box on an upper ledge, away from the enthusiastic pruners, and she visits it at the hour when the wall behind it gives back the morning’s heat, because she has a theory about warmth and color that she has not yet said out loud. She is wrong about several things and right about the ones that matter, which is the local definition of an expert. The water-keeper with the bad knee does not quite trust her, because writing feels like a way of taking the valley out of the valley. They are polite. Between them, in the politeness, the next twenty years of the planting are being decided.

And there is something else that lives here, which it would be dishonest to leave out, though it is not a person and not an animal and the inhabitants would be annoyed to hear it called a presence.

It is the accumulated attention. Decades of people noticing a joint, a level, a vine crossing a line, a knock arriving early. The noticing has stayed in the place the way lime stays in a wall. You feel it as a pressure to be accurate. A lie about the tanks does not survive the afternoon; the tanks themselves will contradict you, and someone will have heard, and the old man at the gate will add it to his schedule of disrespects. So they have become, as a people, unusually bad at self-deception and unusually good at the smaller dishonesties that keep a stacked life bearable — the pretended game, the long way around the chair, the valve left in a position that is a job offer. They love the valley without saying so, because saying so is the visitor’s habit, and because love here is indistinguishable from maintenance. To love a thing is to know when its knock is wrong.

In the evening, when the western cut takes the sun and the bats come out to work the vines, they return to the walks not for labor but because the rooms are still hot and the parapets have begun to give up their heat in a way that feels like company. They stand in the same places, household by household, and look across the basin at the other roofs, where other people are standing and looking back. Sometimes a lamp appears over there, and sometimes one is lit here in answer. It means nothing codified. It means the day held. The engine turns under them. The channel, if it is going to pulse, pulses now, a quick dark movement at the spine of everything they have made and keep making, and the children run to see it as if they had not seen it yesterday, and the woman with the bad knee listens without turning her head, and the seed-keeper closes her book on a flower she does not yet have a name for, and the valley, which is only people and the consequences of people, settles one more night into the shape of their lives.
