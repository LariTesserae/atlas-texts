The first sign is not a body. It is a correction in the work of the place.

Along the iron apron, where the metallic curls should lie as the rain left them, a lane has been combed. Not cleared—the flakes are still there—but turned so their concave faces all point the same way, like leaves after a careful wind. The combing is too even for water. It resumes after every shower and is finished again by the time the light goes pearled. Nearby, on the warm terraces, the crust is not broken so much as tasted: shallow scoops the width of a palm, edges softened by new mineral already laying itself down, as if the pan had been asked to remember a mouth and had agreed. The yellow-green film is thinner in those scoops and thicker just beyond them, a tide pulled and released.

In the tea-colored pools the tracks at the margin sort themselves if you wait. The wading birds are punctuation. The narrow toes are something else, and they do not stop at the water. They enter, lose their edges, and come out again farther along, heavier, the claw-marks deeper where the mud has been loaded with silt. Between those exits the broader pad appears, oval, slightly warm to the touch if you find it before the pool reclaims the heat. A belly has passed there too, not dragging in distress but in use, a sled of itself, packing the margin into a low road that the moss begins to claim by the next day and never quite finishes claiming.

What makes the road is slow to be seen because it prefers the thicknesses. It keeps to the layer under the fern and above the true floor, where hanging roots and the undersides of leaves make a corridor the height of a crouched person and the width of a narrow door. In that corridor the light is the color of weak tea, and movement reads first as a change in drip: a leaf that should have unloaded does not, because something has taken the drop on its back and is carrying it. Then a trunk of one of the pale trees shows a dark streak that is not the usual stain. The streak shifts. It has weight. It has decided, for this hour, to be vertical.

When it comes into the more open seep it does not arrive all at once. A shoulder, if the word can be used for a thing that has no clear front, presses the moss flat and then releases it, the cushion rising in a slow breath. The skin there is not hide and not bark. It is a living deposit, fine-grained, the color of wet iron where the seep has fed it and the color of old brass where the moss-light has. Water beads on it and does not run off quickly; it sits in the grain, as it sits in the rock, and the body uses that. In the hottest hours a faint skin of vapor lifts from the places the mineral is newest, and the vapor smells of metal and crushed green, the same breath the vents give at night, only warmer and nearer.

Limbs emerge as needed and otherwise belong to the mass. There are four that meet the ground when it travels, and two more that spend their time in the work of the pans, thinner, jointed wrong for any animal you could name from a lower country, ending in narrow toes that match the tracks exactly. The toes are dark, almost glassy, and they click faintly on crust—the same ticking the gas makes, so that for a while you think the terrace is only speaking to itself. When the broader pad comes down it is the heel of the traveling limb, a thick oval of older deposit, polished by years of the same roads. The belly is a long sled between them, paler, plated in scales of travertine that overlap like the rims of the pans. Some plates are loose at the edges. Some have already been left behind in the crust, indistinguishable from geology until you notice they cup a little hollow, the size of the scoops, stained inside with a film the terrace’s own films have not yet matched.

It feeds without spectacle. At the iron apron it lowers the side of its head—or the end that leads—and draws a curl of flake into a mouth that is less an opening than a softened seam. The flake does not crunch. It softens. A dark moisture comes out along the seam and returns, and the curl is gone, and the combing of the lane advances by one increment. At the warm pans it sets its mouth to the scoops and stays. The ticking changes pitch. Gas that was going to leave the water leaves through the body instead, a small regular sigh you feel in the soles of your feet if you are standing on the same crust. The yellow-green film climbs the seam and is not eaten so much as hired. When the body lifts away, the film on its mouth-plates is brighter, and the film in the pan has been combed into fine parallel lines that catch the pearled light like thread.

It is not large in the way of stories. If it stood off the ground it might come to a shoulder, but it does not stand off the ground. Length is its size: two body-lengths of a person, sometimes three when it is full of water and the belly-plates have spread. Weight is more than that. The moss remembers it. Pools accept it with a single slow displacement and then are still again, the surface holding sky around a low dark island that might be stone. It can remain that island through a shower and the return of sun. Fish rise beside it and are not taken. Dragonflies land on the warmest plates and leave. It is not the appetite of this country. The films and the seep are the appetite; this is a larger instrument of the same patience.

You understand the tracks of the belly on the days it is translating water. It drinks at the upper vents, where the stone is hottest and the static pricks, and the prick does not trouble it. Fine hairs—or roots, or neither—lift along the seam of the mouth and along the older plates, and the static spends itself there in tiny dry ticks, a private lightning no larger than a spark off wool. Then it carries the water down. You can watch the level in a pan rise by a finger’s width after it has settled and opened the seam and let the load go, mineral-rich, already beginning to crust at the rim before the body has fully left. In this way the terraces grow a little faster along its roads than beside them. The landform is its path accumulated. Whoever comes later and sees only geology is not wrong. They are only late.

It has no call that gathers others. Now and then, under the fern, a lower version of the frog-note leaves it, a single syllable absorbed by wet, and somewhere upslope another answers, far enough that the two never become a chorus. They keep distance the way the pools keep distance, each a room of the same house. Young are not carried and not hidden in dramatic nests. They begin as a clouding in the scoops: a milk in the mineral water that the films do not clear. Over days the milk gathers grit. Over weeks it is a cluster of grains that roll when the adult’s toes stir the pan, not yet bodies, already drinking in the way the pans drink. The ones that persist fuse, slowly, into a button of brass-colored deposit with a seam. They ride out showers in the lee of the adult’s belly-plates or in the cups of bromeliads the adult has brushed past and left charged with seep-water. Most do not persist. The terrace reclaims them as crust, which is not a tragedy here so much as a return to the general work. The few that harden and take on toes leave the first road already knowing it, because the road is older than they are and made of their kind.

By day it is almost possible to miss, even when you are looking, because stillness is its competence. It will let a shaft of white-gold light travel the length of it and not move, the new mineral on its back shining as the wet rock shines, the eye—small, dark, set under a lip of plate, and not always where you expect an eye—open and unhurried. It sees motion more than shape. A hand waved at the edge of the fern draws the seam a fraction wider and then nothing else. It has learned the difference between weather and interruption, and almost everything here is weather.

Night is when it is most itself, and still not spectacular. It leaves the island of the pool and follows the seep-lines upslope, clicking softly, the static gathering and spending along its hairs as the vents exhale. The lights that stitch the mineral margins are not indifferent to it. They drift to the plates and cling, a sparse jewelry, and wink out when it slips under fern, and relight when it emerges at the next pan. It does not hunt them. They come for the warmth, or for the small charge, or for a reason that does not need you. In the full dark the combed lane on the iron apron advances by touch. The scoops deepen. Water is carried down. A plate that has grown too thick splits along an old seam and is pressed into the rim of a pan, where by morning it will look like every other lip of travertine, and the body underneath will be a little newer, the color of a fresh seep, already beginning to stain.

If you stay long enough to be weather to it, it will pass close. The heat of it is the heat of the ground, no more. The smell is the vent’s smell with a greener edge, crushed fern where the corridor brushed it. One narrow-toed limb may find your boot and test the resistance, the glassy toes ticking once, the way it tests crust, and then withdraw, the road adjusting by a hand’s width to go around what does not give the right answer. It does not threaten and does not welcome. You are a stone that has not yet decided whether to become part of the terrace.

At the western steepening it turns back. Always. The long drop into other weather is not its country, and the second iron stain to the east is as far as the answers to its note ever come. Within that span it lives as the moisture lives, distributed, repetitive, exact. Leave for a season and return, and the lanes will still be combed, the scoops still tasted, the buttons still clouding in the warmest pans. You will not be sure you are seeing the same one. You will not be wrong if you decide the question does not apply. What lives here is the part of the terrace that learned to move, and then, having moved, learned to put itself back.
