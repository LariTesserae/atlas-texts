What lives here treats the ground as a rumor it has agreed, for the moment, to repeat.

The smallest of them never surface into names. They ride the tea-colored water as films and flecks, bloom and sink with the clearing rhythm of the disks, and are eaten in sheets whenever a bank lets go. Above them the air is a second river of wings—midges, then the birds that net the midges, then the birds that take those. None of them own a reach. They own the interval between one slump and the next. When a filament sings and a cutbank holds, the interval lengthens by a minute and the feeding thickens until the water itself looks feathered.

Heavier bodies keep to the grammar of the scrub. They are low, wide through the shoulder, fur plastered to the shape of muscle so that in the mist they read as moving sections of the pelt itself. Their feet are broad and leave prints that the next sheet of water revises before you can count the toes. They do not dig so much as borrow. A drifting willow mat snags, they climb it, they nose the silt it drops, they sleep in the lee of a needle while the rain ticks on the stone, and when the mat tears free again they go with it or they don’t, according to whatever the current has just offered. You can watch one for an hour and never see it decide. It is always already in the next arrangement.

The ones that stay longest have learned the works the way a tongue learns a salt. They do not tend them. They use the corrections as weather. Around a disk the water clears on its own pulse; in the clear minutes a pale, side-swimming shape rises and takes whatever the film has concentrated, then drops when the cloud returns. On the pylons—only the pylons, never the soft ridges—certain birds pack their nests into the joints just above the ordinary flood line, because those joints have not moved in the memory of the nest. The filament tones sort the territories of the low-bodied things more cleanly than scent does: a drop of a note means a bank will fail, and the animals are already on the far gravel before the clay brightens. They are not grateful. The machines are simply another cold fact, like the seep-water and the dark, and the animals that survive here are the ones that can hear a fact before it finishes happening.

Nothing among them is singular for long. A shape you have been tracking as one animal divides at the edge of a pool into three, or was three and has closed into the appearance of one because the stems forced them together. Young are carried in the current as often as on a back—small, blunt, already slick—and are retrieved or not according to the same indifferent arithmetic that moves the willow mats. What looks like care is timing. What looks like mourning is a body turned back toward a channel that no longer leads where it led at dawn.

If any of them watch you, it is with the attention they give a new rib of stone: a possible footing, a possible obstacle, unlikely to last. They pass upwind when they can. They do not gather. The basin is already gathered, and they are the part of it that can still choose, for a few heartbeats, which way the choosing goes. By the time the rain thickens again and the flocks lift, the prints on the bar have gone shallow, and the only resident that remains obvious is the water, revising its own bed, and the lives braided through the revision so tightly that to remove them you would have to stop the basin itself.
