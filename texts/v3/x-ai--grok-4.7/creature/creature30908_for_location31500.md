They are short in the way people become short when ceilings are low and loads ride the spine, not a breed so much as a habit that has had time to enter the bones. Shoulders sit forward. Hands are wide from clay, the nails kept short by work rather than by care, the skin across the knuckles split and resealed so often it has gone pale and maplike. Faces take the light of the basin: not sunburned, not cave-pale, a cooled color, as if the blood has agreed to stay a little deeper. Eyes are ordinary and very used. They look at edges the way other people look at weather—the lip of a block, the hairline in a wall, the dark thread of damp that means a joint drank last night.

No one dresses to be seen from a distance, because no distance is available. Cloth is plant fiber beaten and twisted, clay-dyed only where a mineral stains it in the making, hides cut small and worn where the stone is coldest against the body: a wrap at the hips, a shoulder piece, sometimes a cap of the same hide against the drip from a roof. Children go bare through the warmest hours of a dry day and are wrapped like jars when the rain comes. Sandals are a luxury of the outer houses, where the fracture grit has not yet been walked into powder; farther in, feet are bare and the soles have learned the temperature of every block between a doorway and a seep.

They do not have a name for themselves that carries past the rim. On the high ground, if anyone still walks there, they are called after the drop, and they know this and do not use it. Among themselves they say house, or street-of-the-three-jars, or the people past the moving joint. Kin is whoever shares a wall and a water skin. A household is not a lineage so much as a stack: the people who sleep in one room and on the roof above it, who know which crock is brine and which is drinking water, who will be the ones to lift the lintel when the block tips again. Blood matters less than this, though blood is present—children born into the stack, elders who can no longer climb to the roof and so have become the keepers of the ground floor, the ones who watch the interior shoreline and say when the jars must move.

Speech is low and economical, shaped by walls. They do not call across streets; a call dies in the turn. What travels is a tap on clay, a particular rhythm that means water, or fire, or the kiln is drawing, or come down, the rain is starting. Names are short. People are known by what their hands do that the neighbor’s hands do not: the one who seats lintels, the one who turns brick so it dries even, the one who can tell by smell whether a hide will take the smoke. Argument happens at the width of a blanket. It does not become shouting, because shouting would enter every room on the street, and the street would have to answer, and no one has the time. Grudges are stored the way jars are stored—vertical, labeled by use, brought down only when needed. Forgiveness, when it comes, is a shim in someone else’s wall, offered without a speech.

The children are the only quick things in the town, and even they are quick within a grammar. They run the roofs because the roofs are the long way, the one continuous surface, and they know which roof will take a running foot and which has been thinned by last month’s rain and will give. They are sent for water as soon as they can carry a skin that does not drag, and they treat the seeps as a geography that adults only visit. A child can tell you which crack sounded in the night—not from fear, from attention, the same attention that later becomes the adult’s habit of looking at edges. They are not solemn. They laugh, and the laugh stays in the well of the courtyard, and someone on the next roof smiles without knowing why and goes back to scraping a hide.

Elders sit where the stone is driest and the light reaches longest, and they mend what hands with less patience would throw on the kiln-heap: a hoop reset, a basket rewoven from the sound withes, a bone awl resharpened on the house wall itself until the wall shows a pale cup from years of the same stroke. They are the memory of which block has never moved and which one lies about it. When a corner cracks, people come to them not for permission but for the history of that corner—the year it was built square, the season it first racked, whether the clay there was the gray seam or the red. They answer briefly. Their authority is not dress or seat. It is that they have been correct about the ground often enough that ignoring them is a kind of weather you can predict.

There is no ruler in any sense that would show from the lip. Density has made certain people necessary: the ones who keep the kiln schedule when rain interrupts it, the ones who say which household may take brick from a fresh face before the face is needed as a wall, the ones who walk the moving joints at dawn and mark, with a chalky scratch, the places where a sledge must not pass that day. They are obeyed the way a person is obeyed who is standing in the only dry doorway during rain. When they are wrong, the ground corrects them publicly and without drama, and someone else begins to be asked. Power here does not accumulate. It puddles, and it moves when the block tips.

What they share, more than any story, is a competence that would look like pessimism from the plateau and is only accuracy. They do not expect a wall to stay. They build it so that it can be unbuilt in an afternoon and carried, brick by brick, to the next square the block will allow. They do not expect a seep to remain a seep. They drink it while it is there and know the next one. They are not grim about this. There is a pleasure in the fit of a brick that has been turned all day and set still warm into a course; in the moment a lintel drops true and the wall stops answering the block with that small sick sound; in the roof at the dry hour, when the town is all motion and almost no voice, and the far fracture takes the ordinary gold of late light, and for a little while the work of staying is simply the work, and the hands know it, and that is enough.

At night they go down into the rooms. The roofs empty. A few keepers of kilns stay up if a firing has been caught between rains, and their silhouettes are the last things on the upper surface, patient, feeding the mouth with the scrub that was carried from the rim at some cost. Below, the town becomes a grid of low hearths and the smell of wet clay drying in places it should not, and of yeast, and of bodies in a cool that never quite leaves the stone. They sleep close, because there is no other way to sleep, and because the stone gives its cold up through any gap in the bedding. Someone is always awake enough to hear the next soft report from the floor—the block giving up another hairline—and to decide, in the dark, whether it can wait until morning.

They are not waiting to leave. The rim is wind and scrub and a long walk to nothing they need. Everything they need is in the joints: water, clay, a shelter from the moving air, a stone that will take an edge. They fill the basin because the basin is usable, and they repair it because it moves, and the repairing has become the shape of a life. If you met one on the lip at dusk, coming up for a bundle of scrub, you would see a person already turning to go back down—hide on the shoulders, hands chalked pale, eyes on the path—not as a figure of endurance, only as someone with a wall at home that will need them before the light is gone.
