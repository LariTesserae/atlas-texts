What lives here does not fill the town. It occupies the rate of the town.

You will not see them at the lip. They dislike the open daylight, not from fear of it but because the work is in the seams, and the seams are where the water decides. A person crossing the hall at midday is an accident of errands. The ordinary presence is a shape at the edge of a joint, half behind fern, hands wet to the wrist, not hurrying and not hiding. If they notice you they do not startle. They finish the stroke they are on — clearing a lip with the edge of a gritstone, or seating a block by the small shifts of a shoulder — and then they look, and the look is the same weight as the stroke. They have time. The basin has taught them that anything urgent has already flooded or already split, and what remains can be met at the speed of water finding a crack.

There are fewer of them than the platforms suggest. A dozen, perhaps, if you count the ones away up the eastern joint for days at a time, and the two children, and the old one who no longer lifts but still knows which waiting-gap will take the next face. They do not call themselves a town. The word they use is closer to a shift, a turn at the channels, as if the pocket were a watch that must be stood and they are simply the ones standing it now. Others stood it before. Others will. The blankets were taken by people who left while the mountain was still the same mountain, which is to say a season or ten ago; memory here is measured in courses of stone, and a course is a slow thing.

Their bodies have agreed with the wet. Skin on the hands and forearms is pale, thickened, mapped with the fine white splits that constant water opens and the mountain’s grit packs. Bare feet are wide, the soles ridged, able to read a flagged sheet by pressure alone: where the film is a finger deep, where a lip has silted and begun to lie. They wear little that holds damp. A twist of dark fiber around the hips, sometimes a cape of split cane that sheds the mist and can be stood on end to mark a passage — that is one of their messages, a cane set where a cane was not, visible to anyone walking the circuit before the wind takes it down. Hair is kept short or bound, because long hair drinks the falls and swings into the eyes at the worst moment of a lift. They smell of moss and cold stone and the faint iron of the tools. They do not smell of fire. Cooking is rare and small, a nest of charcoal in a niche the film cannot reach, used for roots pulled from the deeper fractures and for the rare bird that strikes a cane roof in a blow and is not wasted. Mostly they eat what the seams offer: the pale crawlers, which are numerous if you know to part the moss at the right hour; the tender crooks of fern; a tart berry on the dwarf shrubs that only colors on the side away from the spray. Hunger is a low companion, not a crisis. No one here is storing against a winter the pocket does not keep. The cold is constant and survivable. The wet is the season.

They speak little, and when they do it is under the falls, so speech has learned to be short and pitched into the gaps between strikes. A sentence is often a noun and a direction. Names are short. They have a way of pointing with the chin at a course of masonry that means both *that one has risen* and *I will take it after I eat*. The children — a girl with a shoulder already canted from carrying gritstone, a younger boy who still slips on the sheet and laughs once, swallowed at once by the hall — are not kept from the work. They are kept from the fresh joints, the ones still opening, where a face can shift while a hand is in it. Their play is the old circuits: racing the overflow from the room-pool to the western mouth, stacking and unstacking the practice blocks in the market shed, listening. Listening is the skill the pocket demands first. By the time the girl can seat a stone she can already tell the eastern fall from its echo, and she can tell when the echo is wrong, which means a lip up there has silted and the drop has changed its throat. She will walk up and clear it before anyone asks. That is not obedience. It is how a person in this place proves they are in this place.

The tools are the same grit as the rock, knocked into edges and resharpened by knocking. They do not hold a bright line. A mason’s kit is three stones and a loop of fiber and the knowledge of which face wants to come away. Metal has been tried, in some earlier standing of the watch; you can find a rust-bloom in one high niche, a flake that was once a blade, now feeding a stain down the wall that the ferns have organized themselves around. Metal softens here, or rather it gives up, and the people have stopped bringing it. What they need, the mountain supplies by failing in the right direction. A block loosened by the rise is already half-quarried. Their labor is consent: agreeing to the direction of the failure, dressing it so the next failure has a bed to lie in.

They are not solemn. Solemnity would be a waste of heat. In the evenings, when the mist comes back over the lip and the wind leans on the cane and the falls go on regardless, they gather in the hall not for ritual but because the hall is where the sheet is widest and the sitting-stones stay driest. They mend fiber. They argue, briefly, about whether the new eastern course should include a gap wide enough for a person’s shoulders or only for water; the argument ends when the old one taps the floor and the tap means the mountain will decide, and their job is not to out-decide it. Someone hums. The hum is not music so much as a marker, a tone set in the pocket between the eastern strike and its return, so that a listener two corridors away knows the hall is occupied and the watch is held. If the hum stops for too long, someone goes to look. That is as close as they come to a law of persons. The laws of water and stone are older and are not debated.

What is easy to miss, standing in the empty corridors at noon, is that the emptiness is populated by their attention. A cleared lip is a person. A fourth stone lying beside its bed is a person who will return before the rise makes the bed untrue. The fresh tool marks are a few hours old; the mason is up in the unworked joints on the south wall, where a new seep started in the night, watching it, learning its temper before touching it. They treat a new seep the way other people treat a guest: not with ceremony, with accuracy. Where does it want to go. What will it ruin if it is left, what will it starve if it is taken. Sometimes the answer is to cut it a path into the hall sheet. Sometimes the answer is to move a sleeping platform and let the seep have the room. Rooms have been given up before. The town is allowed to migrate inside the pocket as the pocket changes its mind. Nothing they have built is more precious than the film that keeps the seams from drying and splitting.

They do not know the mountain’s age and do not need to. They know their own. A life here is long enough to see a joint become a street, and not long enough to see the street become anything but a street still answering. The old one remembers when the hall roof — the overhang — sat lower, and when the room-pool was a crack you could span with a hand. The children will remember the unfinished eastern course as the place the watch was held in their time. None of them expect completion. Completion would be a dry basin, a finished mountain, a town that could be abandoned without the lips silted and the faces splitting, and nothing in the stone suggests that bargain.

If you stay, they will not drive you out and will not fold you in. They will look at your feet. If you track mud onto a clear lip they will clear it after you, without comment, and that is the comment. If you lift the fourth stone onto its bed and the bed takes it true, the girl may show you, without words, how to check the waiting-gap with a cane laid across it, so you can see whether the mountain has moved in the night. That is the whole of welcome: a share of the watch, for as long as you stand it. When you leave, the cane cape will not be set for you. The falls will not change their throats. By evening the sheet will have erased your print, and the pocket will be as populated as it was at dawn — not by numbers, but by the ongoing, wet, exact attention of the few who have agreed to answer, at the mountain’s own speed, for one more course of stone.
