They do not fill it. Filling would imply a number that could become a different number, and the cavity has already done its accounting.

What lives here is three densities of the same permission, stacked in the standing air, and then, so rarely that rarity is part of the anatomy, a fourth thing that knows it is rare.

The lowest density is not a people. It is a pavement that breathes.

Each body is a low vault of mineral grease over a frame no thicker than a wishbone, anchored in a fracture the way a barnacle is anchored, except that nothing here is waiting for a tide. The mouth is not a mouth. It is a throat of slits aimed into the drift at the height where the grain is right, and the slits are lined with a wax that stays the temperature of the stone. What they take from the air is not food in the old sense. It is unreconciled surface: dust still sharp at the scale of crystals, still willing to give up a little chemistry because it has not yet learned to be dull. They do not hunt it. The Holds deliver it. A body that leans at the inherited angle receives the inherited meal, and the meal does not vary, so the body does not vary, and after a time the distinction between posture and species stops being useful.

They are near enough to touch, and they do touch, constantly, without meaning anything by it. Flank to flank, a shared warmth, a little shared grease, so that the pavement is one metabolism distributed across many vaults. If one fails — abrasion on the windward face, the slow filing-down that is the only death the floor knows — the gap is a statistical courtesy, not a grief. A bud seated downstream finds it. The count restores itself without anyone counting. You can walk on them if you are careful and light and willing to be resented in a way that has no face: they part by the width of a step, close, and the layer is again what it was. They do not look up. Looking up would require a future in which something descends. Nothing descends. The gauze lives above them and does not fall on them. The rare ones walk, and the pavement has already included walking in its arithmetic.

What lives in the mid-air is harder to grant the status of inhabitant, because it does not stay.

The gauze is a tissue of bodies so light that the circulation is their ground. Each is a lattice, waxed, dry, jointed at angles that catch the shear and refuse to tumble. They do not migrate. Migration would be a story about elsewhere. They circulate, which is only the Holds thinking with smaller nouns. A single mote may spend what would be a life traveling the same cell, brushing the same neighbors, exchanging heat at the contact the way the floor-vaults exchange grease. The brush is continuous, and it is the only society they have. There is no call, no display, no season of gathering. Gathering is their permanent condition, and it looks, from inside it, like nothing happening.

They feed on what the pavement lets through: finer grains, the winnowings, a dust already partly robbed and still not finished. Nothing predates them in the way a hawk predates. There is no advantage in speed. The advantage, if the word can be used where no one is competing, is fit: to be the shape the shear already wants. A malformed lattice tumbles into a slower band, abrades, and is not replaced by anything that tumbles. Equilibrium is a editor with no opinions. It simply does not keep the draft.

Between these two densities there is no drama and no truce, because there is no border they both claim. The pavement owns the floor-height of the grain. The gauze owns the mid-cell. The shear-walls are neither's. A hand held in a wall feels only the change of permission, and neither kind of body crosses it on purpose. Purpose is not their instrument.

The fourth thing is.

It is not many. It could not be many. The cavity's generosity is real and it is not infinite in the way a body with a center needs infinity to be: a center must sleep, must be not-abraded, must carry its water in a closed loop because the world will not lend any, must find another center sometimes or forget the use of difference. The Holds can feed a pavement and a gauze at saturation. They can feed a walking animal only as a rarity, a tax the equilibrium does not notice. So the walkers are spaced the way the marks are spaced: a day's travel, or three, and then perhaps one, and then a long emptiness that is not loneliness in their dialect, because emptiness is the medium, not the lack.

They are warm-blooded in a place that is already warm, which makes them slightly indecent, a small extra heat walking through a heat that did not ask for it. Their skin is not the skin of a wet country. It has learned, or been taught by the air, to keep what it must keep. It is dull, thick at the joints, faintly greasy with a wax that is cousin to the gauze and not the same: they scrape it from the lattices, render it by warmth and pressure, work it back into themselves and into cord. Hair, where they have it, is short and close, a velvet that does not lift in the drift; long hair would be a wick for dust, and dust is sharp. Eyes are large, or what they have instead of eyes is large, not because they read light well — the light is a rumor — but because a wide dark collector gathers the strain-glow a little, enough to know when the roof's remembered split is running overhead, enough to tell one lens from another. They do not depend on it. A walker who trusted light would be lost the first time a cell ran dim, and cells run dim as a matter of mineral, not of weather. The primary sense is the wrist, the cheek, the fine hairs that have not been fully waxed away: the drift, which is a compass that does not swing.

They are not tall. A tall body is a lever the edges can use. They go close to the pavement, knees soft, hands ready, because the floor is a crowd and the crowd does not forgive a stumble into an unhandled plate. The feet are wrapped. The wraps are braid: wax, hair, the fine mineral thread that can be pulled from a fresh fracture if you know the grain. A bare sole would last a day and then be a story about why the walker stopped. They do not go bare. Clothing, such as it is, is the same braid, layered until it is a soft armor against the air's permanent filing. It does not flap. Flapping would be a noise, and they dislike noise not from fear but from the way a drip would be disliked in a temple that had never had one: it would be a lie about the physics.

Their hands are the part of them that most belongs to the cavity. Broad, padded, the nails worn or removed or never urged to grow, because a nail catches and a catch opens skin on glass that has not agreed to be blunt. The pads read edges the way another people's pads read pages. They can tell a young break from an older one — older, here, meaning only that some previous walker has already dulled it, or that the pavement has worried at its foot. They knap without looking, by pressure and sound, and the sound is private: a small bright tick, absorbed at once by wax and dust. A good flake is a comb, a needle, a period set into a groove. A bad flake is stacked in a cairn at a shear, so the next hand finds the border before the face does. They do not hoard. A tool left is a tool still left. There is no thief, because there is no elsewhere to take it to that the Holds have not already described, and there is no rot to race.

Water is the secret they carry and do not share with the room.

It is not much. A closed life can be very dry and still be a life, if the dryness is managed and the warmth is free. They drink little, and what they drink is not found; it is made, or rather reclaimed — from their own breath, condensed on a cool plate they have carried into a slight shadow of strain, from the thin metabolic water of the grease they render, from a chemistry that would bore a wet-country physician and that keeps them walking. They do not speak of thirst to the pavement. The pavement would not understand the category. A walker who breaks a vessel of reclaimed water has not spilled a resource the world can replace. The others, when they meet, treat a spill as a kind of wound: not tragic, exact. You mend what you can. You walk shorter for a while. You do not weep, because weeping is a tax the face cannot afford, and also because the feeling, if it is feeling, has been planed by the same equilibrium that planed the wind. Grief here is a narrowing of range. You take fewer Holds. You sit nearer a cairn someone else stacked, and you let the drift tell you that the cell has not changed, which is the only comfort the place knows how to give, and which is, in fact, a comfort.

They do meet. Not often, and not as a city.

A meeting is a shear crossed at the same interval, or a ring of blades that has been reset — you can tell a reset by the way the combs are turned, a degree off the inherited angle, a signature. They do not build halls. They sit in the lee of a plate, out of the fastest abrasion, and they talk with the hands because the air eats voice and because voice would announce a center to a world that has no use for announcement. The language is directional. It is a language of permissions: this Hold brings the fine grain; that one takes the heat off a wrapped joint; beyond the third shear the roof steps up and the strain-light runs in longer lines, and someone left a bead in a groove to say so. There are names, but the names are routes. A person is the sequence of cells they can recite with a wrist. When two recite overlapping sequences, they know each other. When the overlap is long, they may travel together for a stretch, not for safety — safety is not the scarce thing — but because two wrists read a wall more honestly than one, and because difference, rare as water, is a nutrient the equilibrium does not supply and the body still wants.

They have young. The young are the least equilibrium thing in the cavity, and they are watched the way an edge is watched.

A child is born into the same warmth, so birth is not a shock of cold; it is a shock of needing a center. For a while the child is carried against the greasy wrap, inside the parent's small extra heat, and taught the first permission: do not reach. The edges teach faster than the parent, and the teaching is final. Those who live past that lesson learn the rest slowly, because there is no season to hurry them and no school but the Hold. They learn the pavement's spacing by being set down among the vaults and allowed to feel the near-touch, the way the layer closes behind a step. They learn the gauze by standing in it until the ticking on the arm is familiar and not a warning. They learn the marks by being walked to them in the dark, hand on a cairn, and told — if told is the word for a pressure on the wrist — that someone stopped here, and the stop is still true, and you may add a flake or you may not, and either choice will remain.

There is no myth of a surface that would rescue them. If they have a myth, it is of the split itself: that the roof was once a continuous pull, and that the pull finished, and that everything since has been the finishing being true. They do not worship it. Worship would be a request, and the split is not listening. They orient by it. The long lenses overhead, when they ghost into visibility, are a ceiling-map for people who mostly travel by feel. A child who can name three lenses and the shears beneath them is old enough to walk a cell alone. Being alone is not an initiation ordeal. It is the ordinary. The ordeal would be a crowd, and they will never have one.

Do they think of themselves as living in a place, or as a place that occasionally centers?

Both, and the second more as they age. A young walker still has a little of the visitor in them: the habit of expecting a change of weather, a dawn, a reason the dust should taste different tomorrow. An old walker has given that up without bitterness, the way you give up a word that was never in the language. They become slightly more like the pavement, not in form but in tempo. They sit longer. They reset a ring of blades not because the ring failed but because the hands wanted the work, and work is how a center proves it is not yet only a route. They die as the lattices die, by abrasion and by the slow refusal of a closed water to be enough, and the body, when it is done, does not rot into a lesson. The air files it. The grease returns to the economy. The wraps are taken, because braid is a labor, and the plates are left cleaner than a wet grave would be. Someone stacks a cairn if they are there to stack it. If they are not, the walker remains as a scatter of flakes that looks fresh forever, and the next person who finds it cannot say whether they have found a death or a lunch, and after a while they stop needing to say.

What lives here, then, is not a hidden tribe under a mountain, waiting to be discovered by someone who brought a lamp. The lamp would be a misunderstanding. What lives here is a saturation of mouths that are not waiting, a weather of lattices that do not arrive, and, moving through both with wrapped feet and a private water, a few centers who have accepted that the dark is not an interim. They are the only ones who could leave a sentence, and they leave it in beads and grooves and the angle of a comb, for the next wrist, and the sentence is never about escape. It is about which way the permission runs, and that it will run that way when you are not there to feel it, and that this is not a sadness. It is the condition under which anything here gets to be alive at all: held, warm, sharp, and already true.
