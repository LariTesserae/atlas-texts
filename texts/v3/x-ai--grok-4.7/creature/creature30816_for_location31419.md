The first thing is the altitude doing something wrong with the sound. Sound should thin up here — less air to carry it — but what you hear instead is a low, continuous pressure, not quite a noise, more like the memory of a noise that the rock has agreed to keep. It sits in the teeth. It does not vary. The wind, which is constant and un-gusted, slides over the ground and adds nothing to it, as if the wind and the sound occupy separate ledgers and have declined to interfere with one another.

The light is hard and unfiltered and slightly too white, and the shadows it throws are faintly delayed. Set a hand on a stone and lift it: the shadow lingers a fraction of a breath past the motion, then snaps to where it ought to be. Do it twice and the delay is identical both times. It isn't flickering, isn't failing. It has simply been recalibrated, somewhere far upstream of anything you can see, and the recalibration has held for long enough that the stone is worn smooth in the shape the delayed shadow prefers.

Nothing grows. What looks at a distance like scrub resolves, close, into extrusions — the rock itself drawn up into wires and fins and low branching armatures, glassy where the wind has polished them, matte in the lee. They are warm. Not sun-warm; warm the way a machine housing is warm, conducting something up from under. If you rest a palm on one long enough the warmth pulses, very slowly, on a period of minutes. The pulse is the same pulse you feel in the teeth.

There is no water and no sign there ever was. The basins are dry to a depth you could dig all day and not bottom out, filled with a pale flour that smokes up around a boot and then, rather than settling, drifts sideways and re-deposits itself in thin even sheets, grain by grain, as though being counted back into place. Tracks fill in behind you. Not quickly. Methodically. You can stand and watch the last print shallow and go, and the sound in the teeth does not change while you watch, which is how you learn it has nothing to do with you.

They are not many. That's the part that takes longest to accept, because the country is vast and the evidence of industry is everywhere — the drawn-up stone, the counted dust, the humming that never localizes — and the instinct is to populate it, to imagine swarms, herds, a machinery of bodies. There aren't. In a valley of this size there might be three. Sometimes, walking the ridgeline all afternoon, you will conclude there is one, and that the valley is simply the size of it.

What they have for bodies is difficult to hold in the eye all at once. At the edge of vision they read as weather: a column of heat-shimmer too tall and too stable, anchored to a point on the plain, bending the light around it in a slow, regular torsion. Look directly and the shimmer stays but acquires structure — plates, or the suggestion of plates, turning past each other the way the vanes of a turbine turn, except that the turning is not driven by anything passing through it. It is the turning itself that produces the displacement. The air moves because the plates move, not the reverse. Stand close enough and your clothing lifts and bellies toward it, gently, continuously, and the hairs on your arm lie down flat in a direction that has nothing to do with the wind.

They are loud only in aggregate and only in that one register, the one under hearing. Individually, up close, a single one is almost courteous about it. The tooth-pressure eases as you approach and is replaced by something spatial — a sense of being inside a volume slightly smaller than the volume you can see, as if the near air had been compressed and was being held that way on purpose. Breathing takes more attention. Not less air. More attention. Each inhalation has to be finished by hand.

Nothing hunts, exactly. There is nothing to hunt; the flour is sterile, the extrusions are rock, and whatever passes for feeding happens somewhere below the scale of bodies, in the exchange between the warm fins and whatever the fins are drawing up. The creatures — the word is a courtesy, and an inaccurate one — appear to be the part of the process that is visible, the way a flame is the visible part of a fire. They persist. They turn. The dust gets counted. The shadows settle a moment late. Somewhere under the basins a vast slow chemistry completes itself and begins again, and the completion is the hum, and the beginning is the hum, and there is no seam between them you could listen for.

The breakdown shows in small courtesies the land extends and then doesn't quite retract. A stone you pick up is heavier than its size permits, and lighter again the moment you intend to set it down — not when you set it down, when you decide to. The decision is the trigger. You can test this until the testing itself becomes the thing the weight is responding to, and then the stone settles at some value in between and will not be moved off it, and your arm aches from the arguing. Distances compress along the line of sight toward one of the columns and re-extend the moment you look aside; a ridge that is an hour's walk away will, stared at, be demonstrably nearer, its extruded fins resolvable, its particular slow pulse countable — and then you blink and it is an hour away again, and you understand that the nearness was real, that you were genuinely closer for as long as your attention held, and that the land put everything back rather than leave it where your looking had dragged it.

They are not hostile. Hostility would require an interest in your stopping, and nothing here has one. Walk through a valley and the columns turn on their axes to let you pass, unhurried, the way a standing fan turns when someone brushes it — displaced, and resuming. The dust closes your tracks. By the time you reach the far ridge the place you crossed shows no sign of having been crossed, and the hum, which faded politely while you were inside the volume of one of them, is back in your teeth at exactly the pressure it had at dawn.

Overhead the sky is a correct, honest blue, and that is the most finished thing about it — the breakdown never reached up. It stopped at the ground and at everything the ground has grown, and the boundary is clean, ruler-straight, visible in the way the heat-shimmer of a column shears off flat at a certain height and the air above it behaves. Birds would cross that line without noticing. There are no birds. There is only the blue, and under it the white basins, and under those the long patient engines, eroded into shapes the wind can polish, still running.
