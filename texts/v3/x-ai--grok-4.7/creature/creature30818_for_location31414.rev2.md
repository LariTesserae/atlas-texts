What lives there does not arrive. You do not see it come over the rim, and you would not, because the rim is not a door it uses. It is already in the proportions of the bowl: in how much space the tree is allowed, in how far the next thing must stand from the last, in the private downhill the water knows and the stone will not confess.

At first it is only a correction. A drop that had been climbing the rib of a leaf pauses, swells past the point where it should have fallen, and then does not fall. The film of water on the cliff thickens by a line and holds there, warmer than the stone feeding it. The shadow of the tree, which at this hour should lie cleanly to the east of the trunk, is a hand's width shorter than the sun requires, as if something broad and low had sat down inside it and taken part of the dark for a body. Stand still and the shortening does not flicker. It is not a trick of the eye adjusting. It is occupancy.

It has no outline you can walk around. Where you expect a flank there is only warmer air, slightly wetter, smelling more strongly of the leaf-edge and of stone that has been steeping for years. Your breath goes out and does not quite return to the same temperature. If you step toward the tree the air resists—not as a wall, not as wind, but as water resists a slow hand: you can pass, and the resistance closes behind you without a sound, and you understand you have gone through rather than around. On the far side of that passing the moss holds no print of you and none of it. Whatever weight it has, it does not spend it on the floor.

Closer to the trunk the presence gathers. Not into limbs. Into attention. The leaves overhead drip less. Drops bead and stay, ranked along the undersides, each one a little larger than a drop should be, each one faintly warmer at its heart than at its skin, as if something were tasting them from the inside and had not yet decided to let them go. The tilt of the pools steepens by a degree you feel in the ankles more than you see. Water wants the tree. It has always wanted the tree. Now you can tell the wanting has a direction that is also a hunger, old and unhurried, with no teeth in it.

It feeds the way the basin weathers: by permission. Mineral stain enters it as color enters cloth left in a wet season—rust along what might be a shoulder if it had shoulders, a green that is not moss settling where it is thinnest, chalk at the margins that sheds when it shifts and does not sound when it falls. It takes heat out of the ridge and does not hoard it; the overhang is warm because it has been leaning there, and the leaning is so old the stone has learned the shape of the lean and keeps it when the lean moves on. You can put a palm to the cliff after it has gone from that spot and the heat is still arriving from inside, a debt paid back slowly, as everything here is paid.

It is not singular in the way an animal is singular, and not plural in the way a swarm is. There is one of it, and the one is as large as the bowl and as local as a single fern. Attend to the fern at the foot of the far wall and the presence is there in the way the frond is fully made and unrushed, a completeness that feels kept rather than grown—kept the way you keep a cup from cracking, by holding. Attend to the bare stone between moss and tree and it is there as absence maintained: nothing sprouts, nothing is allowed to sprout, and the prohibition is gentle and absolute and has been renewed this morning without ceremony. It does not garden. It refuses crowding, and the refusal is its way of being whole.

When it moves, the movement is a redistribution, not a step. The shortened shadow lengthens. A warmth you had been standing inside is suddenly three paces to your left, and the air where you are cools toward ordinary. The pools adjust their tilt by a fraction, obedient, slightly delayed, like a gaze that follows a turn of the head. No track opens in the moss. A bead that had been climbing a leaf lets go at last and ticks into a lens of water, and that is the only noise it spends on the change of place. If it had a gait you could learn it only by staying through a season: morning against the east wall, taking the first heat and giving it back thicker; midday in the tree's shadow, which is then honestly the right length because it is inside the shade and not borrowing it; late day amber on the west cliff, the mineral curtains a little longer where it rested its color.

It does not regard you as prey or as guest or as weather. Regard is the wrong word. It includes you the way it includes the fern: as a thing that has entered the count of what is here, and must therefore be complete or be absent. You feel the inclusion as a slight thickening of the air at your outline, a damp that finds the seams of your clothes and stops, not soaking in, only learning the edge. If you are still, the learning finishes and the damp recedes, satisfied, and you are permitted to remain as the bare stone is permitted—unoccupied, uncrowded, part of the spacing. If you fidget, if you reach for the tree or kneel to drink, the permission thins. Not into threat. Into a narrowing of the room. The far wall does not move, and yet there is less bowl than there was. The lenses of water draw toward the trunk and away from your hands. Heat leaves the stone under your feet and gathers where you are not. The basin, which had seemed a place, becomes a body declining to be touched in that spot, and you understand you are being edited out of a composition that has no need of a new mark.

It has no voice. The whisper of the weeping seam does not become speech when it is near. What it has instead is persistence: the same completeness renewed across a span longer than the mineral curtains. Seasons do not change its appetite. Wet years it is simply more itself—the drops larger, the lean against the ridge heavier, the prohibition on a second tree more calmly enforced. Dry years it thins until you might think it had gone, and then you notice the shadow still a little short, the overhang still paying out heat no sun stored there, one fern still fully made in a crack that should have gone barren. It does not sleep. It also does not wake. The difference is not one it keeps.

Nothing hunts it, and it hunts nothing that would leave a bone. Its whole predation is the slow taking-up of heat, stain, and spare water, and the slower giving-back that keeps the bowl in the shape it has finished becoming. If it could be said to want, it wants the spacing held: one tree, a few ferns, moss in mounds and not in carpets, water that remembers a downhill the eye cannot find, silence with no living noise in it. You are tolerable inside that want only while you do not become a second of anything.

Leave, and it does not watch you go. The rim is not a threshold it guards. Climb out into the thinner air and the resistance you passed through earlier is already gone; behind you the bowl is the size it decided on, the shadow honest again or not—you cannot tell from the lip—and the ticking of a single drop the only sound that follows you up. What lives there stays, because leaving would be a kind of crowding: two places known at once, a path implied, a life with an outside. It has none. It is the tenant of a room that was never unoccupied, the warmth older than the afternoon, the reason the water climbs, the hand that is not a hand keeping each permitted thing complete and far from the next.

By the time you are far enough for the country below to belong to you again, you will not be sure you met anything that could be called alive. You will only know that the basin is not empty in the way an uninhabited room is empty, and that if you returned in a year the fern would still be fully made, the tree still solitary, the overhang still warmer than the sun can explain, and the spacing—between leaf and leaf, between you and whatever counted you—still exactly as wide as it needs to be.
