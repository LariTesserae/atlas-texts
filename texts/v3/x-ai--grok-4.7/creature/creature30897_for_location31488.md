They are a carried people. That is the first correction, and it keeps correcting itself. Nothing in their hands suggests they were grown here. The ground is too new for that, and their postures are older than the plates: a way of setting the foot sideways on a wet edge, a way of receiving a load with the hip already turned, a way of listening down before listening out. They brought the habit of standing onto a shelf that has not yet agreed to be stood on. You can see the mismatch in how they pause at a threshold, not from courtesy but to feel whether the plate has ticked since morning.

They are not many. The town’s size is a fact about bodies as much as about stone. Count the doorways and you have counted the households, and a household is often two, sometimes three, rarely the picture of a family that other grounds permit. A child is an event the lanes know about before the mother has stood up. There are few of them, and the few are used early, not from cruelty but from the arithmetic of mouths. A child who can walk can carry a bowl of scraped salt and can be trusted not to step off a plank if someone has already said, once, what the water does. They learn the second plank before they learn any story. Their knees are permanently pale from the fog-hour, and their fingertips crack in the dry part of the day and seal again when the air thickens. They do not play in any way that requires a spare space. A game, if it happens, is a counting of props, or a race along a single slot that ends when an adult needs the plank.

The adults are short-spoken and long-armed in the way of people who pass each other sideways for years. Shoulders stay narrow by practice. Nobody gestures wide. A conversation is conducted at the distance of a shared wall, which is to say without distance, and so they have developed a grammar of not-quite. They do not ask what they have already heard. A pot set down two lanes off is news; a pot set down and then moved is a different news, and nobody needs it explained unless the move was wrong. Wrongness is the only thing said aloud at any volume, and even then the volume is a raised murmur: a lashing gone slack, a jar under an eave that has cracked and is giving its water back to the air, a newcomer on the stake-path whose spacing is off.

Privacy, here, is not a room. It is a turned face, a task that lets the eyes stay down, the agreement that a sleep-slot’s curtain of reed is enough even though everyone knows what a cough means. They are not cold. Cold would be wasteful, another kind of exposure. What they have instead is a strict affection that shows up as maintenance. If your prop has begun to walk, someone from the lane is already there with a coil, and they will not make a story of it. Thanks are brief because the next tick is already coming. Debt exists and is never totaled. To total it would be to admit the town might have to choose, and the town has arranged itself so that choosing is the one luxury the footprint cannot hold.

Their bodies keep the town’s materials. Salt in the creases of the knuckles, clay in the nail-beds, a shine on the forearms where reed-dust and mineral water have polished the skin finer than the palms. Palms are cross-hatched from cord. The shared iron knife of a lane leaves a different mark: not on the keeper, who is careful, but on everyone else, a history of waiting. You do not take the knife. You stand where the keeper can see you and you occupy your hands with something else until the knife comes down the slot, wiped, edge turned in. Iron is not from here. Copper is not from here. Whoever walks the stakes walks for those, and for reed, and for the food that the shelf will not consent to grow. The walker is not the bravest. Bravery is a poor trait on a path that is only a line of stakes and a memory of where the plates used to show. The walker is the one with the dullest imagination and the most repeatable foot. They go one at a time because two would invent a discussion about which stake, and discussion is how a person steps into the black mud and does not come up with both hands free.

What comes back is divided before it is admired. A bundle of reed has already been promised to three roofs and a drying-frame. A twist of copper is a repair, not an ornament, and if it is lost the loss is spoken of the way a death is spoken of: once, low, and then as a change in method. They do not hoard in the way of people who expect a future shortage they might individually survive. They cache in the way of people who expect the shortage to be shared and continuous. Every house has cord. Not every house has a blade. The distinction is the whole politics of the place, and it is not argued. A lane that loses its knife becomes, for a while, a lane that borrows, and borrowing makes them quieter still, which is how you know.

They eat small and often, because fuel is a third kind of scarcity, cousin to fiber and to hides. A fire is a decision. The poor boil is not poverty of intention; it is the maximum the town can afford to lift out of wet reed and the scraps of root that come in with the thatch. Somebody is always tending a heat that would insult a larger country. They have learned to like food that has been warmed more than cooked, and to drink the eaves-water as if it were a crop, which it is. The standing water they do not drink. They know its taste the way you know a neighbor’s temper: useful information, not for the mouth. A person who drinks it is either new or finished, and the lanes can tell which by whether anyone is coming with a jar.

Newness wears off in a particular order. First the hands learn the edges. Then the sleep learns the rise, so that a body turns before the water does, already above the second plank, already listening. Last to go is the mentioning. A newcomer will say, in the fog-hour, that the pan between the plates showed a slot where a wall should be. They will say it to be friendly, to have seen a thing together. The resident will nod, or not even nod, and hand them the end of a cord that needs holding. It is not forbidden. Forbidding would make it a secret, and secrets here would grow walls the ground has no room for. It is simply that the residents have decided the mismatch is a property of wet air on warm young stone, and that a property you cannot lash does not earn a second sentence. The newcomers who stay are the ones who can let a sentence die. The ones who cannot, leave by the stakes before the next dry hour, carrying the story back to ground that has had time to become old enough for stories.

There is one figure the lanes do not quite file under keeper or walker or child. She is old enough that her habit of standing was finished somewhere else, on a floor that did not tick. She sleeps badly, not from the water but from the absence of the sound she was built for, whatever that was — wind in a tree, a beast, a distance. Here she wakes at the tick and is already pleased, because a tick is a sound that asks for hands. She re-lashes in the dark by feel, talking to the cord in the same low register everyone uses, not magical, only exact: tighter here, this plate has a tooth, leave slack for the morning or the wall will tear the wattle when it settles back. People say she has been here since the first prop. She has not. She has been here since the second or the third, and she lets the exaggeration stand because correcting it would be a story. Her house is the one with the copper, and the copper is a patch in the water-jar’s lip, nothing you would cross a path to see. Visitors from the rim, rare, look for a chief and find her scraping salt with the children, and she does not lift her head until the bowl is even.

At the end of a day they do not gather. Gathering would require a space the town spent its whole invention refusing to leave open. What they do instead is a kind of dispersed assembly: doors unlatched but not wide, lamps — when there is oil, and often there is not — set so the light reaches the slot and not the room, each household audible to the next as a sequence of ordinary completions. The knife comes home. The jars are weighed in the hand. Someone reports the seep by saying nothing, which means it sounded as it sounded yesterday. If it sounded different, they say the difference and then they say what they will move above the second plank before sleep.

They are tender in a narrow band and implacable outside it. A burn is cooled with standing water and watched all night, because a burn that goes bad in this damp takes a pair of hands out of the lashings, and the town feels a missing pair the way a body feels a missing tooth. A lie about a load on the stake-path is not forgiven, because the path is memory and a false memory drowns the next walker. Between those poles they are almost gentle. They make room on a plank by stepping into their own doorway without being asked. They save the uncracked end of a reed for a child’s scraping-bowl. They do not tell the old woman to go to sleep.

What lives here, fully said, is a people who have agreed to be smaller than their habits and more exact than their fear. They will not dig. They will not keep a dog. They will not speak up into the clear air above the fog, where a voice would travel past the rim and come back wrong from the water. They will keep the coils dry, the edges outward, the irreplaceable things counted, the extra line in the reflection unrepeated. The land is still being born under them, sharp and unfinished, and they answer it with the only loyalty the shelf can use: they stay, and they fix, and they do not ask it to become kind.
