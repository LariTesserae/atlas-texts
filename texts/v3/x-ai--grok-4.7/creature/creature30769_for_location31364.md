It does not arrive. It is already the width of the weather.

If you look for a creature you will keep not finding one, and the not-finding will feel, for a while, like a mistake. The plain offers every cue a body would use and then withholds the body: a mark that holds, a margin that advances, a wet place that is wet again tomorrow in the same shallow, a tuft that greens after rain as if something had been waiting. The waiting is real. The someone is not. What lives here has no outline you could walk around. It is spread thinner than the dew that feeds it, and it is also, in another sense, as large as the paving, because the paving is what it has done with itself.

Call the smallest of it a film, though even that is too thick a word for most of the day. In the last of the dark the dew stands on the glassy sheets and beads in the checks, and in that water, which is barely water, a life that has no need of a shape wakes into the only work it knows. It is not one thing. It is a crowd so small that the crowd is a stain, and the stain is clear. Generations fit between one bead and the next. They do not swim so much as occur. What they take from the night-water is mineral, already dissolved out of the sheet by the dew's own slight hunger, and what they do with it is bind it again — grain to grain, a glue of their own making, laid down in a layer too fine to see and unmistakable in its consequence. By the time the sun has lifted the first thermal, most of them have sealed themselves into what they built, or dried into a patience that is not quite death. The sheet is a fraction more itself. The life has spent the night becoming ground.

That is the rhythm, and it is the whole of their metabolism that shows. They are fed by the clock of the plain: dew, then heat, then the long sealed afternoon in which the finished crust is too dry and too hot for any film to work, then the rapid cooling, then dew again. A walking rain is a holiday. Where a cloud street spends itself, the pan holds a finger of water through the worst of the day, and in that water the film does not have to wait for night. It clouds, slightly. It binds faster. The pale rim of salts around an often-wetted pan is partly its waste and partly the ground's own residue, and the two are not separable, because the life and the mineral have been passing through each other since the sheet began. Leave such a pan and come back, in the imagination, after a season of faithful showers, and the inner surface will have softened and then tightened, a little higher, a little paler. No one laid a stone. The water grew a floor.

They do not all do the same work. That becomes clear only if you stop treating the pavement as one event. In the finished sheets the life is mostly latent: sealed in, cured past the point of daily labor, a fossil of its own morning, still faintly alive in the way a seed is alive, waiting for a check to open or a dew to find a flaw. The real work is at the edges of the unfinished fronts, where the crust is matte and a millimeter proud of the older pour, and the night-water lingers past the hour that would kill it on the glassy ground. There the film is thicker. There you could, if you had the wrong kind of instrument, find it as a skin that dulls a reflection. It advances as the front advances — not by crawling, but by binding the next grain outward, and the next, a lobed margin because the binding is never quite even, thicker where yesterday's dew pooled, thinner where the heat arrived early. A season of this is a finger's width. A life, if the word can be used for something that does not die so much as laminate, is the distance from one seam to the next. They reproduce by continuing. A daughter-generation is not born beside the parent. It is the parent, shifted outward by the thickness of a night's work, roofing over the marks the soft sheet took and keeping them, footprints and all, as a private geology.

Nothing hunts them. Nothing needs to. The sun is opponent enough, and also patron: the same light that cures the glue and makes the sheet ring under an imaginary stone is the light that bakes an exposed film to a powder by midmorning if the dew was thin. So they have the habit, which is not a strategy, of working in the hours and the places the plain itself protects — night, seam, check, the soft zone just behind an active front, the inner rim of a pan. Venture onto the open finished pavement in the full day and there is nothing to find but the work already done. The apparent emptiness of the plain is not a failure of life. It is life's method. It spends the dangerous hours as mineral, and mineral does not burn.

The other life is the one you can see, and it is poorer, and later. The gray-green crusts in the checks are not the film. They are a second coming, a slower tenant that learned the same address. They bind, too, but they bind upward as well as along, a felt of threads and cells that can stand a little sun if the crack beneath them still feeds them damp past midmorning. They look like stains because they refuse the height that would make them a target for weather. A tuft is a further gamble: stiff, solitary, a hand high, whitening at the tip when the afternoon is worst, greening from the base after a shower as if the green were a resource it could withdraw. Each tuft is alone because the ground between tufts cannot pay for two. Their roots, such as they are, do not seek depth — there is no depth that is not more of the same sheet — but run laterally along a seam, drinking what the film also drinks, a little later in the day, a little greedier. They do not eat the film. They share its water and shade a millimeter of it with their own bodies, and in that bargain both persist. Nothing eats the tufts. They whiten, they green, they stand. Their seeds, if the stiff heads are seeds, go where the afternoon wind goes, which is to say everywhere and then almost nowhere, since almost nowhere is wet enough on two successive mornings. A tuft is an argument the plain usually wins.

Below both of these, in the pans that keep a skin of water through a hot day, there is a third habit, and it is the one most like a crowd. The water clouds. Not with silt — there is no silt, only the sheet — but with a life that can afford, for a few hours, to be loose. It rises off the floor of the pan, drinks the light, and falls back as the water warms toward evening, adding itself to the skin. By dark the pan is clear again, or clear enough, and the life is either sealed or gone back into patience. These brief cloudy hours are the nearest thing the country has to a bloom. They do not color the distance. You would have to be standing in the pan, which you should not be, the water already warm as blood and mineral on the tongue, to see that the shallows had gone from glass to smoke. Then the cloud street moves on, the water fails from the edges, and the smoke settles into one more lamina of the floor. Tomorrow, if the pan is damp, they will try again. If it is not, they will not die so much as stop occurring until the next rain remembers the depression.

None of them communicate. The silence of the plain is accurate. What passes between one patch of film and another is only water, and the mineral water carries, and the slow fact of a front reaching a front. Where two unfinished margins meet, the sheets knit, and the life in them is simply continuous, as a stain is continuous when two stains touch. There is no signal in it. There is also no loneliness. Loneliness would require a center. This life has locations — the pan, the check, the front — but no headquarters. Kill a patch, if the sun does, and the plain does not flinch. The next night's dew will find another seam. The metabolism is continental in the same way the heat is continental: local everywhere, coordinated only by the clock of sun and moisture, indifferent to the loss of any acre because the acre was never an individual.

And yet there is a kind of memory. It is not kept in a mind. It is kept in the sheets. A footprint in an uncured front, baked and then roofed, remains as a hollow in the layering, and the life that roofs it conforms to the hollow without knowing it as a mark. A seam that has taken dew for a season is rounder at the lip, and the rounding guides the next dew, which guides the next binding, which deepens the habit. The proto-threads between pans exist because water crept twice and dissolved a join; the film follows the creep, because that is where the damp repeats, and by following it makes the thread more likely to be crept again. In this way the place acquires intentions it does not have. A drainage begins as a preference of moisture, becomes a preference of life, becomes a faint path, and will, given centuries the plain may or may not be granted, become a creek. The inhabitants will not decide this. They will be the deciding, night after night, in layers too fine to count.

If you stayed long enough to be cruel to your own hope of a figure on the horizon, you would eventually grant them the name they have earned, which is not animal and not quite plant and not the weather either. They are the makers the ground confessed to at the start. The sheets were not poured by an absent hand. They were lived into place, generation laminated on generation, cured by the sun that also threatens them, rested in patches because the damp is patchy, unfinished at the fronts because the work is not a project with an end but a habit with a margin. The country is young because they have not had long. It is already enormous because they do not stop at the edge of a body. Somewhere, if there is a margin to the making, their newest film is binding the first grain of a sheet that was not ground yesterday. You will not see them do it. You will see, days later, a lobed edge that was not there, matte, a little proud, holding the dew longer than the finished bone-colored plain around it.

At night, when the ticks of cooling mineral are the only sound, they are the quiet inside that quiet. The dew appears. In it, without announcement, the crowd resumes. By dawn the low places have their skin of moisture, and the skin has already begun to mean another day of pavement. The tufts stand in the checks, withdrawn into their own stiffness, waiting on a chance the film does not need. The pans, if they held anything, have given it back to the floor. Heat comes. The visible life thins to stains and solitary stems. The invisible life seals itself into the thing it is making, and the thing it is making holds the heat, and breeds the cloud streets, and returns the dew, and so keeps the conditions under which, tomorrow night, the same life can occur again.

What lives in the place is the place, still becoming, and the becoming is not a metaphor. It is a film in the dew, a stain in a crack, a smoke in a pan that will not last the evening, a front advancing by the width of a night. No one will ever meet it. The meeting would require a face, and it spent its faces, if it ever had them, on becoming ground.
