The place holds to its shape, but the shape is only the outer case of a habit so old it no longer looks like care. What lives there is not a people you could count and not a spirit you could name. It is the continuation itself, sitting inside the stone the way water sits in a settled flag: present, shallow, and not going anywhere.

They have no single body. If you arrive at the hour when the seep is only a darkening of the joints, you might think the lanes are empty and that emptiness is the truth of the place. Stay through the turn of the light and you see that emptiness was only one of their postures. They assemble where the work of continuance requires a hand, and they disperse when the hand is no longer needed. A weight-stone that has crept. A slab over the cistern that must be lifted a finger’s width and set back true. A hinge that has dried enough to speak when a door moves. These are not errands. They are the places where the continuation becomes briefly visible, and then ceases to be visible again.

They dress as the climate dresses them: layers of dull cloth, close at the throat, the hems darkened where damp has climbed and then stopped. The cloth is not costume and not uniform. It is what remains when nothing brighter has been wanted for longer than dye lasts. Faces, when you are near enough to have them, are ordinary faces worn down to the same grain as the lintels. Not beautiful, not ruined. The eyes do the work of people who have looked at the same walls for so long that looking has become a kind of touch. They do not study you. They register that you are an object the lanes have not yet accounted for, and then they account for you by not altering their path.

Speech is rare because most of what needs saying was said when the drains were first cut true, and has been kept by use ever since. When they do speak it is at thresholds, under the covered way, in the low register the notch will hold without throwing it back. You will not overhear a plan. You may overhear a correction: the slab sits better if the near edge is lifted first; the bucket should be set on the flat, not the dish of the step; the tree behind the cistern is not to be cleared any closer than the clearing already is. These are not instructions passed from elder to child so much as the place reminding itself through a mouth. A child, when there is one, is not a beginning. The child is the continuation learning the groove: which stone takes the game of placing and replacing, which step is deep enough to be a seat, which doorway smells of the stores and is therefore not for lingering. Play here is repetition practiced until it no longer feels like practice.

They do not hunger in any way that would make the stores dramatic. Grain and dried things keep because the rock keeps a temperature, and the continuation eats what keeping allows. No one kills for the table in the lanes. If meat comes, it comes already become food, from outside the boundary, and outside is not a country they visit so much as a weather they receive. The birds that cut the air are not theirs. The lizard on the warm stone is not a pet and not a sign. Animals have mostly declined the invitation of a ground that offers so little disorder. What remains of creature-life is the continuation itself, which is animal only in the sense that it moves, rests, and returns to the same stones.

They are not guardians in the proud sense. Nothing here is treasure. They do not watch the boundary wall because they fear what might cross it. They keep the wall because a line that is allowed to lose its edge eventually stops being a line, and the notch has decided, long ago, to remain a notch. On the western side they do not fight the fan of stain on the cliff. The houses were built to it. Their work is to see that the drip still falls where the drip has always fallen, into the channel, not into a room. Maintenance, for them, is a form of agreement with forces that do not negotiate. Iron may rust to a certain redness and no further. Moss may not take the puddles. The tree may hold its few leaves and its wrist-thick trunk and may not become a shade. These are not laws they enforce. They are the terms under which they continue, and they continue by refusing every small departure that would require a new term.

Time does not pass through them so much as accumulate as fitness. A person who has lifted the cistern slab for decades and a person who lifts it for the first time this season perform the same motion, and the motion is older than either of them. You cannot tell, from the wear on a shoulder or the set of a hand, who is new to the work, because the work abrades everyone to the same contour. Death, if it comes, does not open a gap the lanes can see. A doorway stays shut a little longer. A bundle is carried by a different set of arms. The path does not wander. Grief, if they have it, is private the way voices are private: kept at the size the place will bear, and then absorbed into the next small correction of stone.

They are not lonely, and they are not accompanied in any way a visitor would recognize as company. They share walls, water, and the measure of the doorways. They do not share projects. There is no future improvement toward which they lean. The ambition that remains in them is negative and exact: that tomorrow’s seep will darken the same joints, that the dished step will be a little deeper and not suddenly sharp, that the public ground will still be wide enough to cross without two carts ever needing to occupy it. Wanting only that is a kind of wealth. It is also a kind of refusal. Anything that arrived with its own motion — a new course of stone, a brighter cloth, a louder game, a second tree — would be a rival continuance, and they do not host rivals. They would not drive it out with violence. They would simply not make room in the day’s motions for it, and the day here is the only authority.

If you stay long enough to be accounted for, they will make a narrow place for you the way they make a narrow place for the lizard: a stone you may warm, a threshold you may stand in, a drink from the cistern if you lift the slab correctly and set it back. They will not explain themselves. Explanation would be a new path, and they do not cut new paths. What you can learn you learn as they learned it, by repetition. Which flagstone holds water longest. Which hinge is allowed to speak. How far the clearing around the tree already extends, and why that far is enough. After some days your own breath will seem loud, and you will begin to hear the smaller sounds they live by: the tick of stone giving up the day’s thin heat, the thread of water when the seep runs, the single ring of a bucket set down on the flat and not the dish.

They do not vanish when you look straight at them, and they do not become more human if you look longer. What increases is the sense that the town is not the container and they the contents. The town is the visible wear of their long agreement, and they are the agreement still agreeing. At the middle hour, when no one crosses the open ground, they have not gone away. They are in the shut doors, in the weights on the roofs, in the dark under the cistern lid, in the fact that the stain on the cliff has not lengthened. If one of them steps out then, bundle in hand, it is not an entrance. It is the continuance remembering, for a moment, that it has limbs.

Leave by the eastern line, where the paving ends and the approach rock begins, and they will not watch you go. The boundary does not require a witness. Behind you the lanes return to their number, which is never quite zero and never quite a crowd. Ahead, the high country is only weather and distance. What lives in the notch remains where the weather is received and then put back into its channels: a people thinned to a function, a function thickened until it can walk, lift, correct, and be silent, keeping a small town exactly as finished as it has already become, and not one stone more alive than that.
