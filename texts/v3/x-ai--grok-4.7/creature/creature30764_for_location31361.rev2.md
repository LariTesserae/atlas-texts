What lives here does not announce itself by a door. For a long time the hollow seems occupied only by its own work: the wheel taking water, the rollers drawing withe, the racks losing moisture one bead at a time. Then a small correction appears in the work. A belt that has begun to glaze is lifted off the shaft before it can slip, turned, and set back with the worn face inward. The hand that does it is already gone from the gesture by the time you understand the gesture was recent. Bast dust hangs where the belt was handled. It settles on the mat in a paler streak that the next drip will erase.

They are few, and they do not move as a household moves. Three, most days, and a fourth who comes down the rim path when the race runs high enough to need the sluice boards seated by a shoulder rather than by the screw. You can be in the nave and not know which of them is with you until a rack-hook shifts or a gauge is read aloud in a voice meant for the mark, not for company. They do not call across the courts. Sound carries too well under the hornbeam, and they have learned the carrying as a kind of manners. What passes between them passes at the benches, at the tins, at the post where the numbers sit in scar: a nod, a turned wrist showing how far the press has come, the shared look that means the liquor has gone cloudy and the vat skin wants paring.

They are not young in the way the saplings were young when the vault was set, and they are not old in the way the iron is old. Their age is the age of people who have done one climate's work for most of a life and have not been asked to do another's. The skin at the wrists is bark-colored from tannin more than from sun. The hands are broad, the nails kept shorter than the work requires, because fiber catches under a nail and then the whole day's cord takes the snag. One of them favors the left knee and times the walk between halls to the wheel's slower note, so the crossing happens while the shaft is patient. Another can hear a belt drying before it cracks, and will be at the housing with a damp cloth while the motor is still only thinking about the pitch. The third sleeps lightly enough that the pre-light mist wakes them without a clock, and they are the one who walks the copper corridor while it is still a dark green suggestion, checking that nothing has leaned into the open and that the bitterness in the boundary bark is still bitter. They break a twig, taste the break, and spit. The spit is part of the inspection.

Where they sleep is not a separate idea from the halls. In the yew shed, above the line where condensation falls, a platform of live bough has been trained sideways and boarded with coppice slats that can be lifted when the yew wants to thicken. Bedding is retted fiber, beaten soft, stuffed fat, and emptied and rebeaten on a schedule written into the post with the rack numbers. It smells of the same leaf-rot as the vats, only drier. They do not all sleep there at once. One cot stays in the low hall, near the pumps, because a failure in the night is a failure of level, and level is the thing that keeps the pits from remembering they were ever eroding. A third place, barely a room, is a pocket in the hornbeam vault where a graft swelled crooked and was never corrected: a shelf of scar-wood, a lamp, a tin of the tea-colored liquor boiled down until it will keep, a cup that has been mended at the rim with a strip of willow so often the willow is the rim. Whoever is on the dark half of the year sleeps within reach of the switches.

They eat what the place can spare without becoming a different place. Not much is grown for the bowl. A strip of the sedge is cut and blanched in the press-liquor and eaten with a scraping of rendered fat that arrives in a sealed tin from beyond the rim, irregularly, whenever the fourth comes and remembers. Withe is not food. The rejected stems, the ones the beetles would take, are sometimes stewed long enough to give up a green bitterness that fills the stomach and does not pretend to be kind. Most of their calories walk in on the same path the root-fiber path has combed flat: meal, salt, oil, a paper of tea, once a cheese that went blue before it was finished and was finished anyway. They are not hungry in the dramatic sense. They are calibrated. A body that ate more would need more heat, and heat is the one thing the hollow refuses to host. They stay the temperature of the benches. Coats are worn at the rim and shrugged off at the first graft, because under the vault a coat is only a way to hold damp against the skin.

What they know is narrow and complete. Any one of them can pleach a replacement rib if a hornbeam fails, though a hornbeam has not failed in the years they count as theirs. They can read the race by sound: the culvert's pitch when it is only oozing, the knock when a stick has caught the wheel and must be lifted before it scores a tooth. They can tell which vat has begun to root through its lining by the way the draw runs silkier, and they will not call that a defect until the root starts drinking the liquor faster than the press can feed it. Defect and function trade places here often enough that they have grown suspicious of both words. A thing is kept if the keeping still yields cord, coolth, or a dry rack. When it stops yielding, they do not argue with it. They cut it back to a node and wait the season the node requires.

Children do not live in the hollow now. There were some, once. The evidence is not tender in the way stories want: a lower rack-hook, set for a shorter reach and never moved; a set of smaller marks in the post, healed almost flush, recording a year when the counts were kept by a hand still learning the shapes. The people who are here do not speak of where those hands went. Beyond the rim is a country that still wants other kinds of work, and the hollow has never pretended to be a destination for anyone who needs a crowd or a stove or a name that travels. The fourth, who comes for the sluice, is younger than the three and already has the rim in their gait — the slight hurry of a person who will walk back out before dark and sleep where the air does not drop a degree at the lip. They are learning the boards, the teeth, the taste of the boundary twig. Whether they will stay is not a question anyone puts into words under the vault. The vault has no ceremony for futures. It has seasons and shafts.

If you watch long enough, the three stop looking like keepers of a machine and start looking like organs the machine grew because some of the work wanted judgment. They are as continuous as the motors and less continuous than the trees. A belt can be replaced from the beater; a person cannot, not at this scale, not quickly. That is why the marks are burned into living wood instead of written on paper that the damp would take. The post remembers a hand after the hand has gone to the rim and not come back. The numbers do not care who reads them, but the reading still requires a throat and an eye, and so they remain, quiet, tannin-stained, timed to a knee and a mist and a pitch only one of them hears first.

They are not lonely in any way they would accept as description. Loneliness needs a picture of elsewhere being fuller. Their elsewhere is the rim, ordinary wet ground, a cart that no longer comes, a cheese, a younger pair of shoulders when the water is high. Inside, the company is the wheel, the person at the next bench, the wren if it stays, the drop off the yew hook, the shared fact of the temperature. In the afternoon, when the light is simply day, one of them will stand in the court with a coil of new cord and not be doing anything visible with it. The sedge is bright. The copper throws its thin green flash. They are only standing in the weather between halls, letting the cord's damp equalize before it goes on a shaft, and for that minute they look like what the hollow has instead of a crowd: a single figure, correctly scaled, necessary, and already halfway back into the work.
