They are not tall. Height costs breath, and breath is already a careful thing here. Most adults stand a little under the measure a lower country’s doors were built for, with ribcages that flare wider than their shoulders and a habit of speaking on the exhale, as if the words themselves must be rationed. Their skin takes the country’s colors early—salt-pale at the wrists and throat, then a dry bronze across the face and hands that is not quite a tan, more the slow staining of mineral dust worked into living grain. It does not flush easily. A person who has been running looks, for a long minute afterward, exactly as they did before, only brighter in the eyes.

They do not hurry in the streets. The cities are dense, but the pace inside them is the pace of the engines: patient, continuous, slightly under speed. You learn to read intention in the set of a shoulder or the angle of a tool rather than in footsteps, because footsteps do not carry. Children play in the lee of walls and under the warm bellies of parked wagons, games of balance and of matching the pitch of a pipe by ear. Their voices are the loudest things in a district, and even those drop off within a few body-lengths. A shout is an event. People turn.

Water is not private and not quite public. Each household keeps a sealed measure—enough for drinking, for the small washing that keeps dust out of the eyes and the seams of the lungs—and the rest lives in the civic tanks, counted in decades. A marriage is often sealed less by vows than by the joining of two measures into one larger vessel whose level both families can see. Children are not many. A city knows, roughly, how many new mouths the cuts and the foundries and the tanks can feed in a generation, and the knowing is not cruel so much as meteorological. People speak of a birth the way they speak of a good season in the ore: something that arrived, and must now be housed.

They work with their hands close to the metal. The old trades are not romantic and not despised; they are simply the shapes a life takes when the ground itself is the crop. Cutters, who walk the faces and decide where the next day’s bite will go. Sinterers, who read the color of a cooling pour the way other people read weather. Pipe-walkers, whose job is to know every joint in a district by the temperature of the air above it. There are also the counters—people whose work is to walk the tanks and the yards with chalk and a board, and whose word on a level or a weight is treated as a kind of law. Lying about water or about ore is one of the few things that will get a person walked to the edge of the city and left to find the next one on foot. Most do not try. The plateau is wide, and the roads are long, and a person alone on them is visible for hours.

They are not grim. The thinness of everything—air, sound, water, green—has made them attentive to small abundances. A new film of copper-green on a cut face will draw a knot of people at the end of a shift, not to cheer, exactly, but to stand and look, and someone will say how long it took the last one to reach that shade. Music, where it exists, is low and close: drums that are more frame than skin, kept against the body; flutes cut from pipe-stock, played into the collar of a coat so the note does not thin out and die. Meals are mineral and careful—salt-cured things, rendered fats from the road-animals when a string of them is culled, pastes of cultivated mats grown in warm dark rooms beneath the foundries, and, on certain days, a bitter bright liquor distilled from the same waste-heat that keeps the lizards on the pipes. People linger over it. Conversation happens in the space between sips, because the air will not hold a long sentence at full voice.

They keep time by the works, not by the sun alone. A shift is a shift; a pour is a pour; a decade is the span in which a particular face of ore is expected to give what it has been giving. Personal age is reckoned, but it matters less than how many cuts a person has walked, how many tanks they have sealed, how many times they have stood on the outer wall and watched the dust rearrange itself and still known the way home. The oldest among them have a particular stillness. Their breath is audible if you stand near, a soft bellows-sound, and they waste none of it on greeting. A nod is enough. They have already said, over a lifetime, everything the air would carry.

Out on the plateau, between the cities, you sometimes meet the ones who do not stay. Not exiles, exactly—messengers, surveyors, the small crews who walk the towers and the salt-roads and sleep in the lee of machinery that was abandoned when a cut played out. They are leaner, and their eyes are worse at close work and better at distance. They drink from caches whose locations are a kind of shared memory, and they speak even less, because there is often no one to speak to, and the wind takes what you do not keep. When they come into a city they stand for a while just inside the gate, listening to the hush of engines and voices, as if relearning the scale of sound. Then they go to the counters, report what the road has done since the last passing, and sit with their backs to a warm wall until their breath evens out.

At night the cities dim to a few necessary lights, and the people sleep in rooms that hold the day’s heat in their stone. Somewhere under the pipes the many-legged things farm the dust. Somewhere on the salt a string of long-legged animals stands sleeping, facing a wind that will not reach the sleepers indoors. And in the rooms the people breathe, slowly, spending the thin air with the same patience they spend everything else—already, without thinking of it as patience, because there has never been another way to continue.
