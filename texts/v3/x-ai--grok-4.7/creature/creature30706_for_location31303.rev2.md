They are the only wet things in the chamber, and even that is a courtesy of speech. Moisture is a rumor they carry under the skin and do not spend. You do not see sweat. You see the places sweat would have been, long since glazed over: the hollow of the throat, the inner elbow, a faint mineral sheen like the scale itself, paler, thinner, flaking at the crease when an arm bends too far. They drink by not losing. What little they take comes from the brass bladders, cracked with a flake and tipped so a warm, metallic sap runs into the mouth in a thread. It is not much. It is enough that the mouth stays able to speak, and speech here is a kind of work.

They are not many. A dozen would crowd the paths; there are fewer than that, and they do not keep a count you could ask for. Kin is a matter of who shares an ember-niche and who reties whose cords. Children, when there are children, are narrow and already careful with their feet, set down on the packed paths and lifted off them again, because a season of small wandering would lose a threshold to the mats and the household with it. The old are not bent so much as reduced. Heat has pared them. Cheek and knuckle take on the color of the orange scale, and the voice thins until it is mostly breath, which in this air still carries.

Faces do not read well. The copper leak from the walls catches a brow, the bridge of a nose, the wet of an eye, and lets the rest go. Eyes are there — the chamber’s claim otherwise is about everything that is not them — but they are deep-set and slow to track, tuned to the difference between a mat that has lifted and a mat that is only flexing. They look at hands more than at faces. A greeting is a flake held out, edge turned away, or a cord-end offered to be finished by the other person’s braid. Names exist and are used seldom. A person is the doorway they keep clear, the hollow they will not let the bladders roll into, the particular wrongness in the ticking that they are the one to answer.

Their hands are the finished part. Pads thickened past pain, nails worn to the quick and then past it, the skin cross-hatched with fine white cuts from the threads, which slice before they are twisted and become harmless once they are cord. They work close to the body. Trimming a mat means kneeling in the heat-shimmer against the wall, flake angled so the sheet lifts rather than tears, the spent scale gathered in a fold of the same fiber and carried to the ember-trench. Ash is not refuse. Scattered back, it seeds the next mat in the pattern they want: thick on the windward courses, thin over lintels, absent on the paths. They know the difference by the sound the flake makes. A good cut ticks. A bad one sighs, and they stop.

They do not hurry, and they are not still. The chamber’s clock has taught them a gait that looks like idleness until you watch the errands complete themselves: a bladder eased back from a hollow with the side of a foot, a cord checked by plucking it and listening to the pitch, a crawl to the far dark only long enough to feel whether the copied lacing has added a knot. They do not unmake that copy. Someone, once, tried. The filaments rebraided it within a sleep, knot for knot, and added a loop the hands had not yet invented. Since then the crawl is an inspection, not a correction. They come back with dust in the hair and a new hitch in the braid-work, learned from the dark, and the others take it up without discussion, because a pattern that survives where no one walks has already been tested by the only judge the place trusts.

Speech is short and local. A sentence that has to cross two hollows arrives thinned, the consonants eaten by the dry air, so they do not send sentences that far. What must be shared is left in the work: ash in a line means the trench was fed and the feeder has gone to sleep in the wall-niche behind it; a cord retied with the new hitch means the crawl was made and the copy is still faithful; a mat left untrimmed at a doorway is a person saying they are not coming back to that room, and the others let the scale have it, and do not cut a new path toward the sound of it being taken.

They sleep in the heat, not away from it. Niches sized to a body, floors worn to the shape of a hip and a shoulder, a skin of grown scale for a cover that does not warm so much as stop the small wind of the chamber from wicking the last water off the skin. Someone always keeps an ear on the embers. Not a watch, exactly. A person who is awake because the ticking changed register, and who will be asleep again when it settles. Dreams, if they dream, are not recounted. Waking is a hand already finding the flake.

They are not priests of the place and not its masters. The growth does not obey, and they do not petition it. What they have is a truce written entirely in maintenance: paths walked so they stay paths, bladders stacked so the migration has to begin again, fire fed with the dead scale so the living scale can be cut and not burned. In return the chamber does not close over them. It could. A season of lying down would do it. The mats would take the thresholds, the bladders would occupy the hollows they have been refused, the copied cord in the crawl would be the last pattern left, faithful and uninhabited. They know this the way a swimmer knows depth — not as a fear they speak, but as the reason the next small task is already in the hand.

When you meet one, the meeting is brief and unsurprised. They have heard you for some time. Footsteps in dry grit are a dialect they are fluent in. They will look at your feet first, to see whether you walk the paths or break them, and then at your hands, to see whether you are carrying a flake or will need one. If you are only passing, they step aside into a hollow and let the heat close behind them until you have gone, and the path shows no sign you were the one who used it. If you stay, they will not explain the chamber. They will put a cord-end in your fingers and wait to see if the braid you make can hold, and the waiting is the only welcome the place has ever had, and it has been enough to keep a people in a dryness that should have finished them, season after season, still not finished, still paring, still answering the tick with a smaller tick of their own.
