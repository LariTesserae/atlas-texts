The basin does not keep the animals I thought I saw. What I took for lizards on the new ledges resolves, when one of them turns, into something that has no reason to turn that way: a body like a wet pebble, four limbs only by courtesy, and a mouth that opens sideways along the flank because the head has not yet decided which end is for feeding. They hold the warm edges in the same crowded way, and when a plate ticks they all shift, but the whisper is not dry scales. It is a soft collective unsticking, as if a hundred small mouths had been sealed to the stone and chose, together, to let go.

The grain of wings is not midges. It is a weather of segments. Each fleck is a hard little comma, translucent, with a dark knot where a gut should be and often is not—some of them are empty, still flying, running on whatever heat they were made of. They do not land so much as adhere. A fresh glass face clouds over with them in the time it takes a seep to steam. They are born on the mineral slick, and they are the mineral slick: the iridescence peels up at its edges into bodies, and the bodies, when they die, slump back into the film without leaving husks. Nothing here has invented waste. The dead are the next pavement.

In the tea-colored sumps the flat blinking things are not frogs. They are the water’s own surface deciding, in places, to have an inside. A coin of warm pool thickens, develops a pulse, and that pulse is a life. Some last an afternoon. Some last until the pool sinks, and then they are only a darker stain, already being walked on by the pebble-bodies. Their eyes, where they have eyes, are not paired. A single wet lens migrates around the rim of the coin as the light moves, and when two coins touch and merge, the lenses argue for a while—orbiting, splitting, swallowing one another—until the larger water has one eye, or three, or none. I had called them stationed. They are not stationed. They are the stations, and the stations do not hold.

Higher in the rubble the pale scurrying is the hardest to keep false. At first it is only motion and the small collapse of dust, and that much was true. Then a piece of it stops on a block in full daylight and I have to admit the shape: a spine without a schedule, ribs budding and unbudding as it breathes, a pelt of short pale filaments that are not hair but the same filaments the clay grows when it is about to split. It feeds by lying down. Where it lies, the film beneath it disappears, and when it rises the stone is a cleaner color, polished by a belly that is also a mouth. They are thick between the plates. They do not flee the approach of a foot so much as incorporate the new shade, flowing into it, so that a shadow on the floor grows extra ribs until you move and the ribs remember they were not yours.

The sheets of birds are birds only in the use they make of air. Up close, at the scarp where they knit themselves out of the warm updraft, each one is a single stiffened membrane, veined, no feathers, the beak a fold in the leading edge that hardens when it needs to knock a fissure for the sweet-metal slick inside. They do not nest. They adhere to the unfinished walls in overlapping shingles, and the city I saw was a single repeated body, tiled, lifting all at once when the seep they were drinking from failed, wheeling as one membrane briefly plural, and settling where the noise of new water called them. Their shadows on the floor are not bird-shadows. They are soft-edged, slightly delayed, as if the light were reluctant to admit them.

Under all of this, in the clay that breathes beneath the glass skin, something is counting. I do not hear it with the noise of ticks and wings. I hear it when the other noise drops, in the moment before a plate moves: a slow interior syllable, wet, the size of the basin’s center. The pebble-bodies orient to it. The coin-lives pulse to it. The membrane-shingles tighten. It may be one animal, older than the floor, the original sump that never fully became a sump. It may be the floor, practicing having an animal. When a glass plate stars under a foot, the syllable stutters, and every small life in the basin stutters with it—wings, pulses, unsticking mouths—and then resumes, slightly rearranged, as if the count had been corrected.

The tools on the scarp are still there. The ring of slabs, the flakes, the cord, the scratched tallies, the hide shelter already walking apart. They are not older than the animals. They are the same age, which is no age, which is this morning and the morning the scarp split. Looking again, the flakes are not struck from stone alone. Some of them are shed. The cord is a filament like the pelt-filaments, twisted while it was still willing. The tallies on the glass plate are not marks. They are the places where coin-lives dried in a row, and someone—or some belly-mouth, practicing hands—dragged a finger through them before they set. The windbreak is a ring because a ring is how the membranes rest when they are not flying. Nothing with ordinary hands has been here. The basin has been trying on the idea of hands, and of leaving things, and of coming back to find them, and the idea is still wet.

So the inhabitants are not a population in a place. They are the place’s population of attempts. Each warm pocket tries a body. Each new edge tries a crowd. Each brief rain tries a generation that lives and dies inside the rain’s footprint, and the next rain, on the other side of the glass ridge, does not remember them. They are packed past touching because there is nowhere finished to spread into; the only habitable country is the country that is still becoming, and they live in the becoming, expired and replaced between one tick of cooling stone and the next.

What looks like hunger is closer to editing. The pebble-mouths unstick and reseal, taking film, taking each other, giving the taken back as pavement. The coin-lives merge and lose eyes until a pool has the number of eyes the light requires. The membrane-shingles lift and re-tile, and the city on the wall is never the same city, only the same appetite for a seep’s noise. The pale spines lie down and polish the clay, and where they have fed, the next glass is clearer, as if they were correcting the floor’s mistakes. Even the syllable under the center seems less a voice than a revision: the basin counting its own attempts, discarding the ones that do not hold, keeping—for an hour, for a breath—the ones that do.

And the rule that replaces the rule I had is this: nothing in the basin is a resident. Residence would require a ground that stays. What lives here is a series of proposals the cooling stone makes to the warm air, accepted for as long as the wet lasts, withdrawn when the plate drops or the sump skins or the daylight moves a hand’s width and the lens has to migrate or die. You can watch one begin. A dark map of vanished water thickens at its center. A pulse appears. A sideways mouth opens in a pebble that was, a moment ago, only a pebble. A membrane stiffens in the updraft and knocks once at the scarp, asking the rock for a sweetness the rock has not finished making. By the time you have understood the shape, the shape has been eaten by the next shape, or has lain down and become the cleaner color of the stone, or has wheeled away toward a noise that started while you were looking. The basin is not crowded with creatures. It is crowded with the act of crowding, repeated so fast and so close that the act has weight, and warmth, and a smell of metal and yeast, and a sound like a market that will never be built. Above the rim the finished bench keeps its ordinary emptiness. Below, the inhabitants do not live in the country. They are what the country is doing instead of being finished.
