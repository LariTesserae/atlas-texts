The crew does not arrive so much as continue. They came with the first barges of fill and the engines, and the ones who stayed have the look of people who stopped expecting a different climate. There are eleven of them on the books and usually nine in the rooms on the piers; the other two are always somewhere between the hall and the bays, or asleep through a shift they have traded. They do not resemble one another in any way a visitor would notice first. One is tall and narrow, skin the color of the wet clay, wrists already mapped with old burns from a previous plant. Another is broad, almost square, hair cut so short it looks like a decision made once and kept. A woman who runs the night wash line has a scar through one eyebrow and moves as if the steam were a room she already knows the furniture of. The youngest, barely past the age where the company will insure him, still startles when a heron claps off the canal, then pretends he did not. After a month the startle is smaller. They share the raised walkways and the hot pads where the cats sleep, and they share the understanding that the place does not rearrange itself around anyone’s mood.

Their rooms are single and identical: a bunk, a locker, a window that faces either the engine hall or the lagoon, both of which sound the same at night. Bedding never quite loses the damp. Someone has taped a photograph inside a locker door—an inland street, dry, with a tree—but the tape has yellowed and the image has curled at the corner no one bothers to press flat. Cooking happens in a common galley at the end of the row. Rice, dried fish, tinned things, once a week something green that arrives by the supply boat and is gone by the second day. The smells stay low. Conversation stays low too, not from sorrow but because the engines make a floor under every sentence and raising your voice feels like arguing with the floor. They talk about the bank that slumped overnight, about whose turn it is to cut it back, about the mullet that have learned to hold exactly where the return water is warmest. They do not talk about leaving in any tense that sounds like a plan. The contract is open-ended. The work is the same work.

What they do all day is keep the edges from remembering they are new. A man with a flat shovel walks the channel margins after each shift change and slices the clay back to the painted marks. The slice is clean; the water takes the cuttings and does not return them. Another checks the outfall circle with a pole, confirming the silt boil has not wandered. In the bays they fabricate the next length of pipe or the next section of walkway grating, because something is always about to need replacing even though nothing has failed yet. The youngest learns the valve order by standing beside the square-shouldered man and not touching anything until he is told. His hands stay clean longer than anyone else’s. At the hour the whistle blows they do not look up. Some of them eat. Some of them sleep. The engines do not notice which.

The animals have schedules that fit inside the human ones without consulting them. The cats—four, all descended from a pair that came in a crate with the first tools—own the warm concrete pads on the south side of the hall. They sleep in a loose pile through the hottest hours and hunt the piers at the shift changes, when boots dislodge the crabs that have not yet learned the new banks will not hold. One cat, black with a white chest, sits on the railing above the unused ditch and watches the frogs with professional boredom. The frogs themselves are loudest just before the whistle and just after, as if the sudden human noise were a cue to start again. They do not cross onto the maintained banks. The herons and the single egret treat the channel margins as a larder that restocks itself. They stand in the same three places, one leg tucked, and strike at the small fish that nose the algae mats. When a heron lifts, the sound is the loudest organic noise in the settlement, and for a second the midges scatter and reform. The swallows in the louvers come and go on a tighter clock: they leave at the gray that passes for dawn, hawk the columns of midges that stand over every dripping pipe, and return when the light fails by the same few degrees. Their nests are mud and fiber pressed into the metal gaps; the engines’ vibration is constant enough that the nests do not crack.

In the water the mullet are the most faithful residents. They hold in the outfall circle in a dark, slow wheel, hundreds of them, feeding on whatever the wash lines settle out. They do not school away when a shadow crosses the pier. A crew member can stand and watch them for the length of a break and see the same fish, or fish so alike it does not matter, turning in the same direction. Deeper in the mains, where the flow is faster, larger things move and do not show themselves—something that bends the algae mat from below and leaves a wrinkle that takes a minute to smooth. No one has netted it. The crabs are less loyal. Their burrows collapse by midday; by evening they have dug new ones a hand’s width farther along the cut. Pincers click against the clay in a thin, irregular static that you stop hearing until it stops. Dragonflies patrol in circuits so regular you could time a valve test by them. They land on the north-face biofilm, orange-gray against orange-gray, and disappear until they lift again, wings sudden and bright and then gone.

Nothing here is wild in the old sense. The frogs are loud because the ditch is undisturbed, and the ditch is undisturbed because it does not affect grade. The cats are fat because the crabs are many, and the crabs are many because the banks are soft and new and the crew keeps cutting them, which keeps them soft. The mullet stay because the return water is warm and fed and never varies by more than the width of the painted mark. The people stay because the contract pays and the rooms are dry enough and the work, once learned, does not ask them to invent a reason for the next hour. A visitor who stayed a week would begin to sort them—the tall man who prefers the night valves, the woman who can hear a bearing change before the gauge moves, the square-shouldered one who talks to the cats and not to the youngest—but the sorting would not change what they do. They move along the walks. They cut the banks. They sleep when the number of people in view is already correct. The engines run. The water sits at the marks. The steam closes over the roofs and opens again and closes, and whatever lives here has already arranged itself inside that opening and closing, and does not need the light to improve.
