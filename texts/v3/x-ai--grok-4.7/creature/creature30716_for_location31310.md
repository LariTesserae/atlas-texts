They are short in the way the lanes are short: nothing in them is wasted on height the cold would tax. Shoulders sit close to the ears. The neck is thick, and the head seems set into the body rather than carried above it, so that a person turning to look does so with the whole upper trunk. Skin takes the light the way the flags do — pale, a little translucent at the wrists and the temples, never quite dry. You can see the veins. They are not blue so much as a darker version of the water. Fingernails are worn square and grow fast; people pare them with a flake of roof-slate and flick the parings into the nearest channel, where the pale fish take them without rising.

No one here has the face of a visitor, because there are no visitors. The pass, if it is a pass, is a scrape in the rim that fills with rotten ice for most of the year and with a wind like a blade for the rest. What comes over it is goods, rarely, and people, more rarely still, and those people are already of the bowl by the time anyone bothers to learn their names — which is to say they have learned not to stand in a channel. The faces that are here have been here. Cheekbones are high and a little raw, the skin over them polished by glare the way the weep-grooves are polished by water. Eyes are narrow not from ancestry so much as from practice: everyone squints, even indoors, even at night, as if the brightness had taught the lids a permanent economy. Lashes are pale. In the oldest the whites of the eyes have gone the color of smoke that cannot rise.

They do not dress so much as wrap. Hide, felted hair from the few beasts that can be kept — small, sure-footed, resentful things stalled in the lee of the upper walls, fed on sedge and on the boiled skins of fish — and a cloth woven from a fiber that is not quite plant. It is teased out of the mats of black lichen where the lichen grows thick enough to peel, carded wet, and twisted while it is still slick. The yarn smells of mineral and dries the color of rust. Garments are layers that can be shifted as the drip shifts: a person walking the lanes is always adjusting, a panel lifted off one shoulder, a hem tucked, a wet side turned out to the air. Nothing is ornamental that is not also a fastener. Bone toggles. Cord. The brief brightness of a fish-scale sewn where a tie has worn through, not for beauty, though it catches.

They smell of the tanks. Cold fish, wet stone, the sour-clean of hide that has been smoked in a draft that will not draw. Up close there is also a sweetness, faint, like the dark smell of a dome after you have touched it and it has failed. Children carry it more than adults. It fades.

Speech is small and near. A sentence is finished inside the distance of an arm, and if it must go farther it is passed, person to person, the way a bundle is passed in a lane too narrow to turn around in. They have a great many words for water and almost none for weather, because weather is not what happens here; water is. There is a word for the sheet that skins a channel at its margins while the center still runs, and another for the moment the sheet closes, and another for the sound — that small sound — of the maul breaking it. There is a word for the left-lip hour and the right-lip hour, and people schedule by them without looking up. You will hear someone say they will mend a cistern lid after the switch, and everyone in earshot knows which switch, and how long they have, and whose fish will be inconvenienced.

They do not raise their voices to argue. Argument here is positional. Two people who disagree about a wall that is also a course stand in the lane until one of them is standing in the wet and the other is not, and the one in the wet has lost, or has chosen to lose, which is sometimes the same thing and sometimes a debt that will be paid in fish or in a turn at the upper tanks where the water is colder and cleaner. Grudges are kept in the masonry. A lip cut a finger’s breadth too low is a sentence that will be read every day for a century. People walk past such sentences and do not comment. The comment was the cutting.

Work is constant and looks like idleness if you do not know the water. A woman squatting at a tank with her hands in it is not resting; she is sorting the living from the dead among the pale fish, flicking the weak ones into a basket, leaving the strong ones facing the inflow. A man standing still in a lane with his eyes half-shut is counting the intervals between one drip off an eave and the next, because a change in the interval means a cornice above has begun to let go, and the carts must be walked before the meal of ice arrives and is gone. Children are put to the nets early. They wade the weep-channels to the knee — the channels are never deeper than that; the mountain does not give water in bulk, only in persistence — and draw the translucent jointed things up in fine cord meshes. The catch is not eaten whole. It is pressed. A gray paste, faintly sweet, spread on slabs of the fired clay and dried in the glare until it can be broken like biscuit. It keeps. It is the food of the middle day, when the domes are most numerous and no one wants to light a fire that will not draw.

The domes are not worshipped. That would be too settled a relation for something that lasts a minute or ten. But they are noticed, and noticing has hardened into manners. You do not set a basket in a pan that has gone coherent. You wait, or you use the next pan, and there is always a next pan. Children dare each other to breathe on them. A breath is enough; the dome collapses into water and the jointed things and that dark smell, and the child who breathed is briefly famous and briefly unpopular, because the paste-makers had their eye on that pan. Old people sometimes sit where a dome is likely and do not move until it has leaned and failed on its own. They say — quietly, at arm’s length — that the warmth off a body makes the standing wave last longer, and that this is a kindness, or a theft, depending on who is waiting for the pan. No one has settled it. The word that means a coin which spends once is also, in a slightly different mouth-shape, the word for a guest.

There are no priests. There is a person in each cluster of houses whose job is to know which channels are speaking honestly and which have been argued with in the night — a lip lowered, a slab shifted, a share stolen by a finger’s width of stone. This person is not elected and does not inherit. They are whoever has been cold longest without becoming careless. They walk the upper walls at the hour when the brightness goes edge-on and the town doubles in its own water-tables, and they read the reflections, because a stolen finger of flow shows in the doubled town before it shows in the real one. Their judgment is a tap with a maul on the offending slab. The tap is not repaired. Everyone hears it. Everyone knows whose.

Kinship runs sideways more than down. The houses share walls that are courses, and a child is as likely to sleep against the neighbor’s side of the wall, where the channel in the bond-line keeps the stone a degree less cruel, as against their own. Names are short and reused so thoroughly that people are distinguished by their water: Mar of the left lip, Mar of the tank that skins early, Mar whose eave drips onto the poultry-stall and is therefore owed a basket of the weak fish once a switch. The dead are not buried. There is no soil, and the glare would find anything put under a flag. The dead are taken to the rim, to the scrape that is sometimes a pass, and set in the rotten ice with their faces toward the bowl. In a season the ice has them. In a generation the weep has them, and they come back down the channels in no form anyone claims to recognize, and the fish are fatter that year, and nobody says why, and nobody needs to.

They are not unhappy. That is the wrong measure. They are occupied, in the way the bowl is occupied: fully, and inside a shape that has already been decided. What they watch for is not change but the next small rearrangement — the switch, the thaw-thread, the dome walking its hand’s breadth, the cart that must move before the meal of ice. A person who has lived here long enough stops looking at the rim. The rim does not do anything they can answer. They look at the water in front of their feet, and at the person an arm’s length away, and they speak in the voice that does not need to carry, and when the cold lowers itself over the bowl in the late day they break the skin of their tank and drink the black thread at the center, and it tastes, they will tell you if you are close enough, of stone and of the sweetness that fades, and of everyone who has already been drunk this way and did not mind.
