The floor takes a print and does not give it back. Whatever crosses a worn strip leaves a brief shine of moisture where the pad or the claw compressed the husk, and then the shine dulls, and the fibers ease toward the shape they held before, and by the time you have turned your head the passage has agreed to forget you. That is not softness alone. It is the rate of everything else. A run that is empty for the length of a breath is already being entered at the far end by something that uses it whether or not you are in it. Your print is overwritten by the next body, and that one by the next, until the only lasting marks are the ones made by the place itself: a lip of packed droppings along a wall, a polished arc where wings have struck the same curve of vine for seasons, a darker seam where water always falls.

They are many kinds, and the kinds do not share a project. That is the first correction the galleries insist on, once the noise has stopped being one sound. A weaver does not know the dam. The dam's makers do not know the thatch. The termites in a buried comb do not know the bird that carries a strip of their worked earth up into a nest and abandons half of it when a call goes off three ledges higher. What they share is the consequence. Each one's ordinary habit leaves a remainder, and the remainder is usable by something that was not asked. The valley is the remainder that did not weather away.

Low, in the runs, the mammals set the pace you can almost match. They are not large. The largest that keeps to the floor would sit in two hands if it ever consented to be held, which it will not. Coats run from a damp russet to a grey that is really the color of the husk they live in, darkened along the spine by the water they carry out of the pans. Their feet are used hard. You hear them more than you see them at first: a dry tick-tick where claws meet a shell-paved edge, then the softer double beat as they drop onto mat. They move in short rushes broken by stillness that is not caution so much as listening to a run you cannot hear the end of. Food is not a hunt for most of them. It is a picking. Seeds worked out of the packed floor with both forefeet, held, rotated, discarded if the shell is wrong. A beetle taken off a vine and eaten with the vine-juice still on it. Strips of fallen green pulled into a side pocket of the run and left there, not stored with any discipline you can admire, simply put where the body last paused. Half of what is put aside is found by something else before the owner returns. This does not seem to be a tragedy. The runs are full. What is lost in one pocket is gained in the next, and the accounting is done by the species, not by the individual that carried the leaf.

They nest in the walls where the worked earth is old enough to have set, and in chambers under the mats where an earlier collapse left a hollow. Entrances are round, or were round, and have gone oval from use. Around each one the floor is cleaner than the general matting, not because anyone sweeps, but because the traffic of coming and going grinds the husk to a powder that sifts downward into the next level. You learn not to put a hand near those ovals. The response is a face, abruptly, at a distance of nothing: eyes large for the skull, ears laid, incisors bare for a moment that is more announcement than threat. If you are still there after the announcement, the face withdraws and the alarm begins, a thin high thread that other faces take up down the wall. It dies within a short distance. The next district has its own alarms and does not borrow this one unless the thread somehow hits a frequency they already use. Mostly it does not. Mostly you are a local mistake, corrected locally, and twenty body-lengths on, life is as it was.

Above them, and in places through them, the birds occupy a volume rather than a surface. They are not one flock. The mistake is natural because the sound arrives as a mass, but if you stay and let your eyes adjust to the brown-dark you begin to sort the occupations. Colonial weavers work the mid-canopy thatch in clusters, a knot of nests hung against older nests, the newer ones greener, the older ones gone grey and still used because a used shape is cheaper than an empty gap. Their traffic is vertical. They drop to the pans, drink in a nervous row, rise again with a wingbeat that slaps the ceiling and sheds a small rain of the previous night's condensate onto whoever is in the run below. The mammals flinch and continue. The water was coming down anyway.

Other birds keep to the walls. Nest-holes in the packed earth, slightly larger than the mammal ovals, with a fan of whitewash below them that the rains never quite erase because the rains are drips, not a washing. These birds are quieter in the mass and louder in the single voice. One will sit in its hole and call into the gallery as if the gallery were a burrow it meant to fill with sound. The gallery, being built of half-tubes and accidental bends, throws the call farther than the bird's size would buy in open air. Somewhere down-current another of the same kind answers, and for a while the passage is a conversation between two holes, and everything else routes around the noise without appearing to mind it. They eat what the air gives them when the air is thick with termite flight, and what the vines give them when it is not, and they carry neither far. A caught insect is eaten on the perch. A fruit is pierced and left hanging, and the second meal belongs to whoever arrives next, bird or not.

Near the channel the long-legged waders should not fit, and yet a few of them do, scaled down by the roof rather than by their species. They stalk the pavements at the water's edge in a gait that is mostly hesitation. The water is shallow. Prey is the small swimming things in the pans and the larvae in the film of algae, taken with a stab that seems too violent for the target and is, apparently, correctly violent, because they continue. They do not join the colonial noise. They contribute single notes, flat, struck, easy to lose until you are listening for them. When the roof drips thicken they stop and become another grey shape against the shell-pavement, and you can walk past one at the distance of a stride before it decides you are worth the cost of lifting.

The builders of the water are less visible than their work. You see the work first and for a long time you see only that: a pool held a hand higher than the next pool, a seam of sticks and mud across a narrows, a notch where the seam has been opened and not yet remade. The animals themselves are low, heavy for their length, and wet to a degree that makes them continuous with the bank. They haul. That is the action you come to recognize: a body appearing at the edge of a pool with a length of peeled wood crosswise in the teeth, the wood too long, the grip adjusted, the descent into the water almost reluctant, the placement of the wood against a structure that already looks complete. Completeness is not the criterion. The criterion is whatever the water did last night. If it found a new way under, the way is filled. If it rose and left a mark on a wall that had been dry, the wall gains a course of mud. They work at the rate of the channel, which is slow, and they work at all hours, which makes them seem more numerous than they are. Stand long enough by one seam and you may see only two, or one, and the understanding comes late that two, continued, are enough.

They are not gentle with trespass on the seam itself. A foot on the sticks brings a surge from the pool, a slap of the tail that is not aimed at you so much as at the water, and the spray is the message. It reaches the galleries. It changes nothing in the galleries. The weavers shake and resettle. A mammal that was drinking waits, drinks, goes. The builder returns to the stick.

Insects are the part of the population you cannot refuse. They are in the air as grit is in wind, except that the air here barely moves, so they are a weather that stays. Moths in the upper dark, circling nothing you can name, their dust on your sleeves by the end of an hour. Midges over every pan, a standing column, rising and falling by a rule of temperature you feel as a slight coolness when you pass through them. Termites in their seasons, sudden, the galleries snowing with wings, and then the wings on every surface, and then the wings in the water, and then the next course of the floor made partly of wings. Ants on the food-trails, which are not trails you would draw on a map but darkenings, a continuous narrow traffic along a root, up a nest-string, across a sleeper's back if the sleeper is a log and not, just then, an animal. They do not investigate you unless you are sweet with fruit or cut. If you are cut they find the cut. It is not drama. It is another picking-up of what the place has offered.

Between the insects and the rest there is no stable peace and no war you could name. A gallery can be a hunting ground for an hour — a shrew-sized thing, sharp-faced, working the husk with a fever that makes the larger rodents look sleepy — and a nursery the next, and a road the hour after. The same vine feeds a caterpillar, a bird, and the beetle that arrives for what the bird dropped. Nothing in that sequence is arranged. Watching it, you keep wanting a manager. The pools look managed. The thatch looks managed. The terraces look like a decision someone made about grade. The want is yours. What lives here does not correct it, because nothing here has the spare attention, and because the resemblance to care is produced by repetition near water and food, not by a view of the whole.

There are predators, in the ordinary proportion. A raptor sometimes comes down the rare shaft where the roof has failed and not yet been grown over, drops, takes a body off a perch, and is gone before the alarm finishes its twenty lengths. You know it happened by the gap it leaves in a racket that otherwise does not have gaps. On the floor, a snake of unremarkable size uses the ovals in the wall and the hollows under the mat, and is flowed around by the traffic rather than fled from in any general way. Its kills are local and sufficient. It does not thin the runs. The runs cannot be thinned by one mouth. Higher, in the drier districts toward the rim-side warmth, something cat-small leaves prints you can distinguish from the rest because they are not immediately overwritten — a brief privilege of the hunter, whose pause empties a circle, before the circle fills from the edges as if the emptiness had been a smell. These are not absences in the valley's life. They are part of the thickness. A place this full without anything that eats would have eaten itself in another way: young smothered, runs packed past movement, pools fouled faster than the dams can lift them. The eaters keep the rate. They do not rule.

Young are everywhere and easy to misread as debris. A tremor in the wall of a nest is not wind. A cluster of pink-grey on a ledge, blurred by the low light, is not fungus, or not only. The colonial nesters raise their broods in public, in the sense that privacy here is a thickness of neighbors rather than a closed door. You hear begging as a register inside the greater noise, a finer grain, and once you have heard it you hear it in every district, day-side and night-side, a sound the adults answer without seeming to interrupt whatever else they are doing. Feeding is efficient and constant. A beak arrives, a mouth opens, the beak leaves, the interval is shorter than your attention wants it to be. Below, in the mammal chambers, the young are harder to see and just as present: a higher-pitched thread in the alarm-range, a scurrying that does not yet have the adult's economy, bodies that take the wrong run and are corrected by collision more often than by care. The place rears them by being unsurvivable at the margins and ordinary at the center. Most of what hatches or is born does not persist. Enough persists that the floor still takes prints and forgets them.

What they share, if anything can be called shared, is a tolerance for proximity that would scatter them in a sparser country. Here a body at your elbow is not a signal. It is the condition under which elbows exist. Fights happen, and they are brief, and they are about a hole or a strip of bark or a place on a seam, and they end when one animal leaves the disputed hand's-breadth, because there is another hand's-breadth just like it an arm's reach on, and the cost of winning the first is higher than the cost of taking the second. You will see blood. You will not see a battlefield. The wounded continue if they can, and the runs do not make a story of them. A bird that cannot rise is a meal for something that can, within the hour, and the perch is occupied again before the hour is out, by a bird that did not win anything except the timing.

They are indifferent to the valley as a valley. This is the correction that arrives last, after you have admired the roof and the ponds and the sense of a town. None of them occupies the town. Each occupies a nest, a pool-edge, a run, a comb, a perch from which the next perch is visible. The continuity you feel — the way a district gives onto another district, the way the channel organizes a descent you can walk in a day — is a continuity of leftovers touching. The roof is what remained of a thousand decisions to build where a previous nest had already broken the wind. The pavement is what remained of a thousand decisions to set a stick where the water was already going. You can walk from the central pools to the drier upper districts and back without ever entering a space that was planned for walking. The paths are simply the places where too many feet found the same efficient line, and the line, packed and repacked, became the only comfortable one, and comfort recruited more feet.

At the rim, where the made ground thins, you can watch the indifference fail in a small way and thereby see it more clearly. Construction does not stop out of respect for a border. It frays. A nest begun in a shrub that still has weather in it is abandoned half-woven when the night-cold arrives, and the half-weaving blows into the valley and is picked up and used in something else. A mammal run that ventures past the last mat comes back, not in a panic, in a simple failure to find food at the density the body expects, and the body, being a practical thing, returns to where the density is. The mountain, if it is a mountain, receives almost nothing from them. A few seeds. A little nitrogen in the rain that finally falls here as rain. The life you have been inside does not colonize outward. It deepens inward. Another course on a wall. Another pool lifted by a hand. Another generation of holes in earth that is already hole-riddled, so that the earth is more memory of animals than it is earth.

Stay long enough and the noise organizes you instead. You begin to move when the runs are between rushes. You stop speaking, not from awe but because speech comes back to you shredded, the gallery taking your voice and handing it to a hole that answers with a different species' alarm. You eat what can be taken without a struggle — fruit already pierced, water from a pan the waders have left — and you learn that struggle is costly here in a way it is not in open country, because every struggle is public, and the public is hungry, and the scraps of your effort are claimed before you have finished making them. Sleep, when it comes, is a shallow concession in a pocket the traffic has not yet found, and it ends when the traffic finds it, which is before you have dreamed. Waking, you are not rested. You are adjusted. The damp is no longer an insult. The dark is no longer an absence of day. A call that would have been alarm is information about a shaft, a pool, a rimward district where the warmth goes a little drier in what passes for afternoon.

Leave, and the valley does not mark the leaving. Your prints ease out of the husk. The notch you accidentally widened in a seam is found by a wet, heavy body and filled before the next thick dripping. The hole you breathed near returns to its conversation. In a season the place will have made, from the ordinary surplus of living, another thumb's width of itself: a lip on a pool, a rounding of a corner, a mat slightly deeper over a gallery that still runs hollow underneath, full of feet, forgetting each of them in turn.
