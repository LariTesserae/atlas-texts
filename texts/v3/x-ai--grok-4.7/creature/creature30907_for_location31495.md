You would not see it on the first pass, and not on the second if you are still sorting the sounds into their sources. The gallery has taught you to look for leaks and for the green film, and what lives here is neither. It is the correction in the rhythm that you already filed as mechanical: the pump catching, the hammer arriving a fraction late, the pour into the sump missing a note and then finding it. Those are not faults. They are footsteps, and a mouth, and a body shifting its weight so the intake does not starve.

It keeps to the lower tray.

The floor plates are meant to lift, and one of them, three paces from the sump and just off the yellow path, sits a finger's width proud of its neighbors. Not warped. Set back by something that knows the lip and the weight and how far a plate can be raised before the diamond pattern catches the light from the caged bulbs and gives the fact away. Under it the lower tray runs pitched toward the drain, painted the same iron color as the spine, filmed with the water that escapes the trough and with a warmer slick the pump throws when it takes air. That is the ground. Not soil. A road the width of a man's shoulders, always wet, always loud from underneath, where the knock of the machine arrives first through metal and only afterward through air.

It is longer than the space between two flanges and not much taller than the tray. Most of the time you would take it for a run of lagged pipe that someone laid and then forgot to clamp, a pale jointed length the color of zinc and of the skin on the sump when oil gathers. The color is not paint. Pigment would be wasted where half the bulbs are dead and the other half the color of tea; what it has instead is a surface that holds a film of water the way the copper holds a film of water, so that it vanishes into the shine. Where the film breaks — at a bend, at a place it has just hauled itself across a dry bolt — the hide is dull, finely creased, and cold to the idea of touch. You do not touch it. The diamond plate is sharp, and so are the places where the hide thins over something harder, a ridge of grit bedded in the flesh, scale and weld-spatter and the green dust of the copper, pressed in until they function as a sole.

The head, when it has a head in view, is only the end that meets the world first. No eyes you can find. The louver's roar and the hammer-blow have made eyes a liability, a soft organ in a room of edges; whatever looks, looks by pressure. Along the jaw and down the first third of the body, pits and short pale bristles read the tray the way a hand reads a railing in the dark. It knows the pitch of the floor. It knows which seam has begun to algae and which is still bare. It knows the pump not as a sound but as a tide in the metal: the double knock, the gulp, the shiver that means the intake is clear, the wrong shiver that means a string of the green film has bridged the mouth and the pump is about to cough. When that wrongness starts, the body is already moving. It does not hurry in any way that would lift it into the path. It lengthens. The far end stays wedged under the sump rim, a purchase, and the near end slides, wet on wet, to the intake screen — a mesh you had not noticed, set vertical in the sump wall, fine enough to pass water and coarse enough to hold a clot of algae. A blunt snout fits the mesh. A tongue, or what does the work of a tongue, rasps the clot free and takes it. The pump finds its beat. In the gallery above, if you are standing there, you hear only that the machine has forgiven itself.

That is the whole hunt, and it is constant. The film on the trough seam and the film on the sump lip are not wilderness to it. They are the crop. It cannot reach the wall-seam from the tray; for that it must come up, and coming up is the exposed life. It times the climb to the valve you cannot see. Hiss, shudder, hammer-blow, the higher note of pressure equalizing — in that blow the room is already so struck that a body the weight of a wet sandbag can haul itself over a plate edge and not be a separate event. You feel it, if you are sitting, as a small addition to the shock in your ankles. By the time you look, the yellow path is empty again. What you might catch is the mark: a darker swipe through the rust-blush at the paint's edge, where the grit-studded hide dragged, and a place on the trough lip where the green is suddenly thinner, scraped to the metal in a narrow honest lane no wider than two fingers. It does not clear the crop. It harvests a portion and leaves the rest to grow. Young corrosion, young algae, a young grazer that has already learned the principle of not finishing the meal.

Eat, return, wait. The waiting is done curled in the tray with the far end toward the sump and the near end toward the drain, so that any seep from above arrives at the mouth end and can be tasted before it is trusted. Mineral, mostly. Sometimes a sweetness when the bleed-vent has been sighing and the coils have given up more than water — a trace of the treatment chemical in the circuit, bitter, metallic, spat out in a thread that the lower tray carries to the drain. It has opinions about the water. The oil-bright skin on the sump it will not take. Once, early, it tried; the hide along the jaw still shows a faint wrong smoothness, a healed scald in the creases, and it does not try now. When the skin forms, the body simply stays down until the pump's gulp tears it. Patience here is not a virtue. It is the only speed the room permits that is slower than the machines.

You will want a face for it and you will not get one. Turn the proud plate back with a hook — slowly, because the noise will not warn it, but the change in airflow might — and what looks up, if looking is the word, is a front the color of the tray and a mouth set wrong for any animal you have a picture of: not under the head but at the end of it, a transverse slot that can widen to the mesh and shut to a line. No teeth. The rasp is the flesh itself, ridged, pink-gray, already stained the dark green of its food. The bristles lie flat when they are not reading. There is a pair of pits where ears would be if ears were any use, and they are packed with a clear jelly that trembles to the pump even when the body is still. It does not startle. Startling is a behavior for places where a sudden move is cheaper than a late one. Here a sudden move puts flesh on diamond plate or into the intake, and the intake does not give flesh back. So it watches you, in its way, by the new weight on the tray and the new temperature of the air falling through the lifted plate, and it decides you are weather. Large weather. It draws the near end away from the opening, not far, a hand's span, and settles. The jelly in the pits keeps shivering. You are in the record now. You will be compared, later, to other weathers: the louver's draft, the heavy drops from the ceiling, the rare time a boot walked the yellow path and stopped and did not know to lift the plate.

How it breathes is the part that takes longest to see, because you are waiting for a rise and fall and what it does is smaller than that. Between the knock and the knock, a slit far down the side, almost at the turn of the body, opens and takes the wet air and shuts before the hammer. In. The blow. Out, into the tray, a warmth you could feel with the back of your hand if you were foolish enough to put a hand there, a brief fogging that the metal drinks. It has matched its breath to the machine so completely that the two of them share a lung-count. When the valve cycle comes and the rhythm breaks, it holds. The slit stays shut through hiss and shudder and blow and the high note, and opens again only when the double knock resumes. You can time it. You can watch a dozen cycles and not see a mistake. This is not discipline. There is nothing in the room to perform for. It is the way the animal was built by the room, or the way the animal built itself to the room, and the difference does not matter to the pump.

Nothing else alive contends with it. The film does not crawl. No insect crosses the path; the louver is meshed, and what little wings in from the high cold dies in the bleed and is carried to the sump as a speck the intake never feels. If a speck lodges, the rasp takes it with the algae and does not distinguish. There is no mate in the tray. You look, once you know to look, and the lower run is empty in both directions: paint, pitch, drain, the structural numbers in their neat black, a second body nowhere. Either it arrived alone — egg, spore, a juvenile folded into the packing grease of a flange and bolted into the spine before the door ever sealed — or the other one is already gone, washed to the drain in some early week when the harvest failed and the pump ran hungry and the weaker animal mistook the intake for a shelter. The gallery is a pocket. Pockets do not replenish. What lives here lives here in the singular, and the singular is enough for the crop, and the crop is enough for the singular. You will not see young. If there is a season, it is the season of the valve timer, repeating, and the body you are watching is both the parent and the last.

Leave the plate down. It prefers the dark under there, not as comfort but as the condition in which its shine works. On the path it is almost visible; in the tray it is a thickening of the wet. By the time you have walked back toward the sealed door, it will have returned to the sump end and fitted its snout, lightly, not feeding, to the mesh — a check, the way you might put a hand on a wall to know it is still there. The pump knocks. The header answers. A drop falls from the ceiling and bursts on the plate you just set, and the burst is loud to you and, to the body below, only another line in the record.

If you stay through another cycle you can hear the harvest it does not need you for. The wrong shiver starts, very small. The pour into the sump misses its note. Then the note returns, and the pump's beat cleans, and somewhere under the iron a warmth of breath fogs and vanishes between one knock and the next. That is the inhabitant: not a spirit of the machine and not a caretaker, whatever the scraping of the mesh looks like from above. It eats the error. The error grows back. The high place outside, the wind, the drop, the weather this room was built to refuse, none of that is available to it, and it does not press at the door. The door is a kind of weather it has never had to read.

What it has is the tray, the mesh, the two lanes of green, the grit in its hide, and a lung that opens only when the pump allows. It will be here when the chalk numbers wear off the rim and when the yellow path is gone to rust and when the film has climbed past a hand's breadth, still fitting the same length of tray, still declining the oil-skin, still adding its small late weight to the hammer-blow so that anyone standing above will go on believing the gallery is only machinery agreeing with itself.
