The place is not a chamber that contains water. It is water that has learned the manners of architecture, and the architecture has learned not to interrupt.

If there is a population here, it is the only one, and it is not crowded. Emptiness is the medium it keeps. Between one presence and the next there is always more paving than body, more dimness than flicker, more unoccupied court than occupied one. They do not school in the way a living surplus schools. They occur the way a joint occurs in stone: necessary, sparse, exactly where the plan still remembers a turn.

What moves in the ankle-water is small enough to be mistaken, at first, for the water’s own unease. A paleness the length of a hand, almost unpigmented, the fin a thinner version of the same pallor. They hold to the shear where two skins of different cold meet, and they take what the shear suspends — grit, flake, the powdered edge of shell. They do not flash. Flash would spend light they do not have. They cross a court as a loosening of the gray, and the court closes behind them. If you stand still they will pass within a breath of your calf and not alter course for you. You are not a feature yet. You have not been here long enough to become load-bearing.

Lower, in the joints, a second scale occupies the hairline geometry the fish ignore. Things with too many legs for the dimness, flat, the color of wet mortar, working the seams where flag meets flag. They are not hiding. Hiding implies a watcher. They are simply the size of the work they do, which is to take the last softness off the stone and return it, in grains, to the slow bar the current is always building somewhere else. Lift one and the water under it is no different from the water above it. Set it down and it resumes the joint as if the joint had been the point.

The larger ones live where the old roofs failed into circles and the water stands over a standing figure’s head. You do not see them so much as subtract them: a darker turn inside dark water, a displacement that arrives, considers the column of your legs if you have waded in, and declines. They are cold in the way the deep courts are cold, which is not an absence of heat so much as a settled decision against it. They crop the gravel of shell and tooth. They crop, when the account requires it, whatever smaller pale thing has drifted too far from the shear. There is no pursuit you would call hunting. The basins are wide. Hunger here is a slow settling, the way a slab settles when it has been a wall long enough.

None of them need what is gone. There is no green to graze, no sun to pace a day by, no air-breathing chorus at the surface to mark dawn or weather. What they eat is the place itself, ground fine, and each other, and the small suspended wealth the turbulence keeps lifting off the paving and laying down again. The account closes. It has been closed for so long that abundance no longer looks like plenty. It looks like continuity: enough bodies to carry the mineral through another season of surges, never so many that a court fills with them.

What is stranger is how little they behave like residents of a ruin. A ruin expects its animals to shelter in the failure. These use the plan as a plan. The fish take the colonnade because the colonnade still shears the current into a reliable throat. The joint-dwellers take the hairlines because the masons, whoever they were, cut joints that still collect. When a course finally ceases to be a wall and a new cut opens between two courts, the life does not flee the change and does not celebrate it. By the next turn of the surge, a school has found the throat. By the season after, the gravel bar beginning on the downstream sill has its pickers. Creation and destruction are weather to them. They have had no other climate.

They do not mark you. That is the part that takes the longest to accept. In a green place, life orient to an animal presence — alarm, curiosity, the small renegotiation of distance. Here the renegotiation does not happen, or it happens on a clock you cannot hear. You are warmer than the water and you displace it, so you are, for a little while, a peculiar current. A pale school may bend around the warmth and resume its line. A flat many-legged thing may wait in its joint until the shadow of your foot passes, not from fear, from sequence. The large turn in the deep court may come near enough that you feel the shove of water off a flank, and then it is elsewhere, uninterested, because you are not shell and you are not the smaller pale thing and you will not, they seem already to know, stay long enough to become either.

If anything like mind lives in them, it is not the mind that watches a stranger. It is the mind that keeps a level. They know the courts that empty and the courts that take the spill. They know, without announcement, when the water at a sill is about to become the water over a sill. A school that has been holding in a dim colonnade will lift as one and slide into the next basin a moment before the surge arrives, not hurried, as if the surge were a sentence they had already read. They have been reading it since the stone agreed to stay.

Stand in one court long enough for the level at your calves to become the level at your knees, and you are among them without having joined them. They continue. The emptiness continues between them. Somewhere a wall you cannot see gives up a course, and somewhere a new throat opens, and the pale flick that is the only brightness in the dim will find it, because finding the new cut is what they are for — not as scouts, not as a people, but as the part of this place that is still willing to move when the masonry has another idea.

They are not the genius of the ruin. The genius, if the word can be used for something without a voice, is the plate itself: the held water, the unpaid plan, the storm that never raises its sound. What lives here is simply what remains possible once leaf and sun and noise have been subtracted, and what remains possible is precise, cold, and in no hurry to acknowledge you.
