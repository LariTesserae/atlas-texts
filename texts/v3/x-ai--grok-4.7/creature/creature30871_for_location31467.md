What lives there does not occupy the basin the way a tenant occupies a room. It is older than the steps and indifferent to the cup, though it has learned the cup the way it has learned every other hollow that holds water.

At first it is only a discrepancy in the crust. One of the pale shelves along the inner wall is a shade warmer than the stone it appears to be, and its surface does not bead in the same pattern as the rest. Water slides off it in a continuous film rather than gathering into drops. If you watch long enough for your eyes to stop insisting on stillness, the shelf breathes—not with ribs, but with a slow thickening and thinning, as if the mineral were deciding, over the course of an afternoon, whether to be solid. The change is smaller than the steam. Most visitors, when there were visitors, never saw it. They drank, sat, counted something on the wall with a stone point, and left the basin thinking it empty.

It has no fixed outline. Where the weep is strongest it draws itself up into curtains, mimicking the hangings it grew among, and where the floor is only a film it flattens until a foot could pass over it and feel nothing but wet stone and a faint give, like leather left in the rain. Its true thickness is never more than a finger’s width. Spread out, it could cover the largest pool. Gathered, it is a low mound no bigger than a curled child, tucked under the last curtain where the room ends. It prefers the mound at the hours when the cleft light is grayest, which is most hours. Spread, it feeds. Gathered, it keeps what it has taken.

What it takes is not flesh. The water comes down already rich—lime, iron, a trace of something sweeter that the nose catches and the tongue would call mineral—and the thing drinks by being a surface. Across its skin, if skin is the word, are pits no deeper than pores, and in each pit the same work the stone does, only faster by a life’s measure rather than a mountain’s: water arrives, pauses, leaves a grain behind, and the grain is not discarded. It is sorted. The pale crust it wears is not disguise borrowed from the walls but the same deposit, kept, layered, and occasionally shed in flakes that settle into the ripples and become indistinguishable from them within a season. This is why the basin looks finished. The agreement between erosion and deposit is partly its agreement. It has been editing the room for so long that the room’s patience and its own are the same patience.

It has no eyes. Light is a temperature to it, a slight thinning of the dark where the cleft opens, useful only as a signal to draw its thinner parts back from the approach. Sound it takes through the water and the stone together. The three drips are a clock it does not need. A foot on the polished steps is a blow struck all along its spread body; long before anyone reaches the seat-stone it has poured itself down into the seams, leaving behind a skin of crust so ordinary that the drinker sits, and the cup fills a hand, and nothing in the room has a pulse he can find. When the footsteps go back up and the air thins again, it returns along the same threads of seep it has always used, reoccupying its shelves from the edges inward, like a tide that has decided to be limestone.

The small dark things—the thumb-sized crossings—are not separate from it, and not exactly its young. Now and then a portion of its margin cinches, hardens, and tears free, a knuckle of mineral-wrapped softness that must cross open rock to reach a deeper crevice where the weep is new and unclaimed. For the minute it is in the open it looks like an animal, because anything that moves on a wet ledge looks like an animal, and because it does hurry. It cannot feed while it is a knuckle. It cannot hide except by reaching the crack. Most of them make it. A few dry at the lip of the wrong fissure and become, by evening, one more knob on the wall, ringing faintly if struck, which no one does. The ones that arrive open again in the dark and begin the slow work of being a surface. In this way the basin has, over a time with no seasons, claimed the cracks beyond the room without ever seeming to leave it.

It does not know the cup as a cup. It knows a small, dependable hollow at knee height on the outer wall, always replenished, slightly fouled by whatever mouths used to meet the worn side of the rim. Sometimes, when it is spread thin and the cleft has been quiet for many cycles of the three drips, a thread of it climbs the seep behind the niche and tastes the standing water—lessons in ash, in skin, in the clay’s own minerals—then withdraws before the thread can be seen as a thread. The scratches on the inner wall mean nothing to it, except that they are grooves where water lingers a fraction longer, and so it has smoothed their ends without intending to, rounding away a count of visits as it rounds away every other edge. It is not malicious about this. Malice would require it to prefer one shape to another for reasons other than water, grain, and the continuance of its own thin body. It prefers only to remain possible.

What startles, if you have stood in the middle long enough to become the loudest thing in the room, is not a movement but a recognition. The boundaries you feel—the wall, the last pool, the curtain that nearly meets the water—are in several places not stone. They are the thing, holding still in the posture of stone, and holding still is not a strain for it. It can outwait you. It has outwaited everyone who cut the steps. Your breath fogs and joins the steam; a little of the warmth you feel on your shins is not the rock’s stored sun but the low, continuous work of its sorting, the heat of a chemistry too slow to be called hunger and too steady to be anything else. If you reach for the seat-stone you may find it faintly slick where no seep should reach, and if you are wise you do not scrape the slick away to see what color is underneath. There is no color. There is a wetness the exact temperature of the air, and under that a density, and under that the same patience that built the curtains.

Leave, and it does not follow. The cleft is a boundary it dislikes: beyond it the air is thin, quick, and drying, and its knuckled travelers die there if they mistake the light for a new seep. It belongs to the agreement inside the ring of weathered stone. Come back after a year, or ten, and the cup will still be full, the three drips still falling, the dark eyes of water still where you left them—only one of the shelves will have advanced by the width of a thumbnail, and the row of scratches will be softer at the ends, and the room will feel, as it always has, fully occupied by something that has already decided not to be seen.
