The creature is a moose, and it is not a metaphor for the basin. The basin is cold, young, packed with its own civic density of canals and lodges and sedge; the animal that uses that floor is simply larger than the architecture, and has to live with that mismatch every step.

It is built like a body that was argued into a bad shape and then proved the shape. The frame is overlong in the legs, overlong in the snout, and the shoulder hump is not a proud saddle but a place where muscle has to live because the neck is too short for the head it carries. From the side the proportions read as a mistake that keeps walking: chest deep enough to disappear into a willow belt, hindquarters that look insufficient for the front, a nose that ends in a wet, mobile pad the size of a palm and works the air in small edits. The dewlap under the throat is a fold of skin that collects cold and drip and never looks like it was planned. In the open light of the floor it is dark, not the black of a shadow but the black of wet bark, and the coat is hollow-haired enough that water beads and runs off in strings rather than soaking through. Under the pelt the fat is a winter layer still not fully spent. You would not call it sleek. You would call it adequate, and then you would step back because adequate, at that mass, occupies a surprising amount of the street.

The head is where the mismatch becomes expensive. In season the antlers are a second skeleton carried outside the skull, palmate, broad as a door in the better years, each palm fringed with points that are not decorative so much as the edge the bone happened to take. They are heavy in a way the neck never forgets. While they are growing they are velvet, a live skin shot with blood, hot to a touch that never happens, and flies know it. The animal walks then with a particular care of the head, not pride but accounting: a branch that would be nothing to the shoulder is a wound if it takes velvet. Later the velvet strips, in rags, and the bone underneath is brown and ridged and absurd. It holds them through the rut and into the start of real winter, and then, on some morning when the rime is still on the south faces of the pans, they loosen at the pedicles and fall. One, then the other, or both close enough to seem a decision. The drop is a dull sound swallowed by wet stems, the same way a scarp-block’s fall is swallowed. What remains is a pair of raw pedicles and a skull that looks, briefly, too light. By the next day the lightness is gone and the winter head is just a long dark face again, browsing.

None of this is coordinated with the basin’s clock so much as endured inside it. The year turns and the body follows with a lag.

Through the late cold it stays on the floor because the floor is where the browse still stands above the sheets. Willow and young alder are a standing store, and the long legs are the tool that lets the mouth reach them without the chest having to invent swimming. It moves in the slots the smaller traffic has already cut, and the slots are too narrow, so passage is a continual shouldering: stems slap the barrel, rime showers off, the canal-edge mud takes a print the width of a plate and deeper than a vole’s world is tall. Lodges it steps over or around. A dam it may cross if the sticks will hold; sometimes they do not, and the break is a small catastrophe for the colony and a wet annoyance for the legs, already wet. It does not repair what it breaks. Repair is not in the repertoire. Eating is.

That eating is the day’s actual work, and it is slow. A head lowered into a willow belt, the prehensile pad and the incisors taking twig-tips and bark strips, a jaw that moves side to side more than a grazer’s would. A thousand small bites for a body that cannot afford to be theatrical about any of them. In the cold the browse is dormant and poor, so the animal is not, strictly, full. The winter fat is the difference between that and dying. You can see the bargain in the way it stands: weight shifted onto three legs for long minutes, the fourth cocked, ears working the muffled air, eyes half-lidded not from peace but from the arithmetic of conserving heat while the mouth continues. When it beds, it beds in the taller sedge or against a lodge bank, body axis along the wind if there is any, and the depression it leaves steams faintly and then rimes. Smaller traffic threads around the depression and does not investigate long. The smell is rank in a way the cold almost, but not quite, cancels: wet hide, fermenting browse on the breath, the sweet-dark note of a ruminant that has been eating wood.

The eyes are small for the skull and set so the view is wide and slightly downward. Predators of any seriousness are not the daily subject — not on this floor, not at this size, not once the animal is past its second year — but the ears are large and the caution is old. A branch calving from the scarp gets a full turn of the head. An otter in a canal does not. People, if the rim ever held any, would get the full turn and then a decision made at a distance the legs can still cash. The legs are the other half of the defense and the other half of the feeding. They are long enough that a drift which stops a deer at the chest is a wade, and they are jointed high, so the body stays above the sheets while the feet are already in the blue clay. The feet themselves are the detail the basin has to live with. Split hooves, splayed, the size of a small plate each, that spread under weight and fold when lifted. On the packed till of a rise they click and scar. In the muck of a spring ward they are snowshoes. Between those extremes they are simply large, and every trail it commits to becomes, after a week of its use, a trench. The civic lattice of canals was not dug for this gauge of traffic. The trench cuts a feeding platform, breaches a low wall, turns a careful head of water into a leak. By the following dusk the leaks are being mudded shut again by builders the moose will not look at. Neither party experiences this as conflict. It is weather of a biological kind.

Spring, when it reaches the floor — late, and more as a change in the standing water than as a date — is when the body spends what winter stored. The coat fades, rubbed and patchy, and the animal looks worse than it did in the cold, which is common and not a sign of failure. Antlers, if it is a bull and the year is a decent one, start as blood-hot bumps and then as the argument of velvet and bone already described. Cows do without that expense and put the same season into a calf, carried through the late winter browse as a quiet internal weight and dropped, when the sedges are at last green and the rime is no longer a nightly certainty, in some denser belt where the flies will be bad and the sightlines worse. The calf is all legs and a coat that has not yet learned to shed water. For days it does not meaningfully follow; it beds, and the cow stays in a radius measured by earshot, browsing a circle into the willow. Then it follows, and the trench gets a second, smaller print inside it.

The rut, earlier, on the other side of the year, is the only season the quiet of the floor is answered in kind. Not continuously. Bulls move more, and the movement is no longer only the commute between browse and bed. They cover ground that does not feed them, and they stop, and the call that comes out of that throat is a low, coughing, ugly sound, nothing like a horn and not loud so much as shaped to travel under the stems rather than over them. It fits the basin: a noise the cold air and the sedge baffle do not quite erase, so that another bull a ward away hears it as a direction rather than as a performance. Antlers, still on, are used. Mostly as display, the turn of the head that makes the palms read as width. Sometimes as contact, a push between animals that have decided the display was not enough, the sound of it again dull, bone on bone, absorbed by mud and water and the fact that neither of them wants a broken pedicle. A bull in that week eats less than the arithmetic requires. The fat from the summer pays for it. Some years the summer was short and the payment fails, and what the scavengers find in the first hard rime is not a mystery but a balance sheet.

Summer is the repayment, and also the season the floor tries to drive it off the floor. Browse is rich, the sedges are a green that means something, aquatic plants in the spring wards are worth sinking the whole head for — and the animal does sink it, ears last, coming up with a mouthful of dripping weed and a sheet of water off the dewlap. The same weeks the insect haze over those wards becomes a personal climate. Flies at the eyes, flies at the bleeding velvet if velvet is still on, a whine that the muffled air finally carries because it is an inch from the ear. The animal shakes, rolls in the muck of a wallow it has opened by the simple method of lying down and rotating until the willows around it are broken in a circle, and for an hour the coat is armor made of clay. Then it dries at the edges and itches and the flies find the cracks. Wallowing is not a ritual. It is maintenance, repeated until the season turns and the haze thins.

Through all of it the relation to the rest of the living floor is almost entirely one of mass and indifference, edged with the few attentions that matter. It will not hunt. It will not be hunted often. It competes, without acknowledging competition, for the willow and alder that also feed hares and beaver in winter, and because it browses high and strips hard it changes the height and the survival of the very belts the basin uses as structure. A winter of heavy use leaves a browse-line, as clean as a cut, above which the whips are untouched and below which they are antler-scarred stubs and peeled wood. The canals it damages it does not map. The lodges it beds against it does not distinguish from a bank. Birds use it sometimes — a back is a dry ridge in a country of wet ridges — and it tolerates them the way it tolerates rime, as a condition of the air. Its own calves it knows by smell and by the specific small shape at the flank, and a cow fresh from birth will charge what the basin would consider a serious predator and be correct to do so; the charge is not fast so much as unanswerable, a wall of shoulder arriving with the knees lifting clear of the sedge. Everything else it classifies, if that is the word, as not-food and not-threat, and walks through.

What it leaves behind, besides the trenches and the browse-line and the wallows that hold water for a week and breed their own flies, is the antler, and that is the one object that exits its body and stays useful to the city. Dropped in the sedges, brown, porous at the base where it separated, it is calcium the floor has not yet had centuries to accumulate. Within days it is being tasted. Porcupines, if the rim provides them, and squirrels, and the simple weather of wet and freeze, reduce it over a season or two to a lace of remaining points and then to chips in the muck. Nothing ceremonial attaches. It is a bone the ground received because a skull could not keep carrying it, and the ground’s smaller appetites spend it.

Seen across a day it does not look like a citizen of the place. The citizens are the ones whose works tile the floor edge to edge, whose repair outruns erosion, whose scale matches the canals. This one is a seasonal climate that happens to have hooves. It arrives along the same corridors each dusk and dawn, shoulders through what was maintained overnight, feeds for hours in a silence broken only by the wet tear of bark and the occasional cough of a call in season, beds, rises, and is elsewhere in the ward by the time the rime has burned off the south stems. The lattice closes behind it before noon. By the following night it is open again, because it came back, or another like it did — they are not many, a floor this size holds a few, spread so that even in the rut you can go a morning without seeing the second. The basin does not thin itself to suit them. They do not become smaller to suit the basin. The fit is the long leg in the cold sheet, the splayed foot on clay that has not yet decided to be soil, the head lifted, dripping, out of a spring ward that was a street until the mouth arrived and will be a street again when the mouth leaves.
