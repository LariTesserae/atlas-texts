The room had finished becoming itself long before any of you learned its name. That was the first thing the place taught: not a lesson, only a pressure, the way water presses when you stand in it to the chest. You could forget the pressure if you never left. Leaving was how you learned you had been breathing to a rhythm that was not yours.

She lived on the third platform up from the lowest, the one where the left-hand wall weeps a little faster. Her house was a single room with the outer wall left in the rock and the inner wall a slab set on edge and bedded in crust-mortar so long ago that the joint had gone the color of the rest. A curb no higher than her ankle separated her floor from the channel. She had never tripped on it. The hollow where she slept held her the way a palm holds a coin that has lived there.

Her name, among them, was a small downward sound, the way you describe a drop that has already landed. People said it without looking up. She answered without looking up. The looking was unnecessary. In a pocket that size, a voice already knew where it was going.

She was not young and not old in any way that showed. The damp kept skin from cracking and from blooming. Her hands were the part of her that had done the most living: the pads of the fingers flat from stone, the nails worn to the quick, a pale seam across one knuckle where a slab had pinched her once and the flesh had closed over the fact. She scraped crust on the days the channels asked for it. She did not decide those days. The sound decided. When a bowl went dull she heard it before anyone else, a thickening under the tick, and she took the thin blade of ground shell and walked the lip until the pitch came back. The scrapings went into the basket woven of older scrapings. When the basket was full she carried it down to the lowest platform, where the channels vanished under stone and took the soot and the grit somewhere no one followed. She emptied the basket into the dark and came back up. That was most of the work of her life, and it was enough work for a life.

The other work was listening for the ceiling-click.

Children learned the interval the way they learned not to step in the rills. She had learned it so long ago that the click lived in her the way a second pulse lives, slightly off the heart, refusing to match it. When she lay in the hollow she counted without counting. The click came. She turned her face a fraction toward the ceiling, not to see — nothing was visible up there, only the gray sheen of condensing water — but because the body, over years, makes a small courtesy toward whatever keeps time for it. Then she turned back. Sleep was not the absence of the roar. Sleep was the roar accepted so completely that the mind could walk around inside it without bumping the walls.

Once, years ago, the click came early.

She was awake. She was always more awake than she appeared. The interval broke the way a held note breaks, not into silence but into the wrong next sound, a half-beat soon, and then the true interval tried to reassert itself and failed, and failed again, like someone remembering a step and putting the foot down where the previous foot still was. She sat up. The hollow held the shape of her heat. Across the channel the next house was a darker slab and a darker hollow, and in it a boy who was then a child sat up at the same moment, because children are closer to the count than adults are. Their eyes met in the gray. Neither spoke. Speaking would have put a human sound into a measure that had never had one.

She crossed the water. The boy crossed from his side. They met on the curb that was nobody’s, the narrow dry between two rills, and they stood with their ankles in the thin moving air and their heads in the still air, and they listened. The click stuttered twice more. A drop that should have landed in the high bowl on the left landed instead a hand’s width off, a different stone, a flatter pitch, wrong. She felt the wrongness in her teeth.

Then it corrected. Not gradually. The way a stone that has been about to roll settles back into the socket it came from. The click returned to its count. The drop found the bowl. The roar, which had not changed its volume by any amount a stranger would have noticed, loosened something it had tightened, and the room was the room again.

The boy looked at her. She put two fingers against his wrist, not to count his pulse but to give him the interval back through skin, the way you return a tool to the hand that dropped it. He breathed out. They went to their hollows. In the morning no one mentioned it, because mention would have made it a story, and a story has a shape that wants to happen twice. It had not happened twice. It has not happened since. She still knows the exact wrongness of that half-beat, the way you know a scar by touch in the dark.

What she owned was little enough to list and not worth listing. The shell-blade. The basket. A cup of oil-fat that was not hers alone; the cups belonged to the niches, and the niches belonged to the pocket, and people filled them when a visitor came up the seam and did not go back out. Such visits were rare and grave and short. The visitor stood in the loudness and did not understand that the loudness was the house, and spoke too loudly, and was fed, and left carrying away a skin of water that would taste, outside, like ordinary water, and would not. She did not envy them the leaving. She had gone up the seam once, as a girl, because everyone did, once, so they would know. The world above was a blow of dry air and a light that made her eyes water and a silence so complete she thought she had gone deaf. She came back down before the turn of a day. The seam polished her shoulder on the way in, a little more of herself added to the curve. She has not wanted the surface since. Wanting is a surface habit. Down here the channels run at one volume and the wanting thins out to something more like fit.

She had a sister on the fifth platform and a brother who had died in his hollow with his mouth slightly open, as if still offering the interval a place to land. They wrapped him in a cloth brought down years before and already softened to the damp, and they gave him to the lowest dark, where the channels go. No one dug. The pocket does not give up floor. The water took what it was willing to take. Afterward his hollow remained, and for a while his heat remained in it, and then the stone forgot the heat and kept the shape, and sometimes a child from a fuller house sleeps there now and says the hollow is kinder than theirs. She does not correct this. Hollows are not kind. They are accurate.

On ordinary days she scraped, or she did not scrape. She crossed to her sister and they sat with their feet in the moving air and mended a lashing that had not quite failed. They spoke little. Speech in that roar is a thing you spend, not a thing you spill. A sentence crosses the water and arrives already partly claimed by the bowls, so you learn to send only what can survive the crossing. Her sister’s hands were like hers. Their mother had had those hands. The resemblance was not blood in the way surface people mean blood. It was repetition. The pocket repeats what works. Hands that fit the blade are kept. The rest is worn down to mineral and given to the rills.

Sometimes, rare as the early click, she felt the whole settlement breathe at once. Not the people. The fit. A moment when every curb was being stepped over in the same small way, every lamp holding its ration of light, every channel taking its thumb-drop across a lip that had been left sharp on purpose, and the ceiling answering, and the under-rush answering, and the pale many-legged things walking their undersides without deviation. In that moment she was not a woman in a room. She was one of the answers the rock had grown for itself. The feeling did not flatter her. It was heavier than pride and lighter than fear. It passed. She picked up the basket.

If you asked her what lived there, she would not say herself. She would tip her head, the small courtesy, and let you hear it: the roar that had stopped being sound, the click on its count, the left wall weeping faster, the water that never rose and never failed. She would let you understand that the inhabitants were not added to the chamber. They were the last tool-marks, softened at the edges, taken by the rock as its own. When she lay down, the hollow took her. The interval held. Somewhere under the lowest platform the dark accepted another basket of pale grit and did not give it back. The seam waited, narrow, polished, the whole world’s door, and almost no one used it. The room continued. It had already finished becoming, and still, under that, it was becoming, one drop, one scrape, one accurate sleep at a time, and she was how it did that, for now.
