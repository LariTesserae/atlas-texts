They are not a single people. The plateau keeps too many lives for that, and most of them never meet except as weight, or stain, or a brief heat that goes into the mat and does not come back. What lives here lives as the country works: slowly, in overlap, without a voice that carries.

Lowest, and first, are the ones you would not call animals if you were only passing through. In the water-film that never dries on the cushions, and in the hollows of stems that look solid until light finds them, there is a constant fine life that does not walk so much as circulate. It turns the iron in the seeps. It eats the sheen that rises on the pools after rain and is gone by afternoon. You see it as color moving on stone, as a clouding under still water, as the sweet-oil smell in the hollows that is not rot exactly and not clean either. It has been here longer than the herds. The peat is its body in another form, compressed, still working.

Above that film, the grazers. They are low because the wind and the wet have no use for height, and heavy because the mat is poor in any one mouthful and rich only if you take it all day. Their backs are the color of wet peat when it has just been turned, darker along the spine where the rain sits. Legs short, feet broad enough not to cut the cushions into wounds that stay. Horns, if they are horns, are worn down to knobs; nothing here needs a display that can be seen across a valley, and valleys do not exist in the way that would reward one. They move in lines because the gaps between scrub open and close with the fog, and a line finds the next gap where a scatter would stall and sink. A herd crossing a flat looks, from the ridge, like a darker current. Up close it is heat, and the sound of water squeezed out of moss under feet, and a smell of crushed leaf and cold dung that the rain takes before it can gather.

They do not lead in any way you could name a leader. The front animal is only the one that has not yet stopped to feed. When it stops, another shoulders past without dispute, and the line bends, and the bending is the decision. Calves walk inside the line, not beside it. The mat dimples under them and springs back before their mothers have gone ten lengths. They are born into wet and do not seem to mind it. Their eyes are small and set wide, more for the near stems than for the horizon, which is anyway not a thing you can use.

The birds treat the herds as moving country. They ride the shoulders until a shoulder shakes, then flick into the scrub and out again, a dark comma, a pale throat, almost no call. Nesting is a problem the plateau has already solved for them: not in trees, which drown their own forks in moss, but in the raised rinds of iron at the lips of pools, and in the old root-ridges that were trees and are now only firmer ground. Eggs the color of tarnish. Young that freeze rather than run, because running on this ground is a way of announcing yourself to whatever is already in the thicket, and freezing is a way of becoming another wet stone.

What hunts them does not course. Coursing needs distance and a dry place to spend it. Here the thickets at the rims of the bowls do the work of distance. Something long-bodied lives in that dripping dark, low enough that the crowns do not shake when it passes, patient in the way of an animal that cannot afford a second miss. You will not see the whole of it. You see the work: a cleared patch in the cushion where a grazer went down and was opened, the pale down of a bird scattered and already being pressed into the mat by rain, a trail of compressed plants springing back along a line that does not match the herds. Its feet are quiet because the ground is quiet. Its coat, where the light once caught a shoulder at the edge of a bowl, was the same oiled green as the hanging moss, not by mimicry so much as by the same water lying on everything. It eats, and then it is still for a long time. Stillness here is not hiding. It is how the place itself prefers to be.

There are smaller hunters that you do meet, because they come up at dusk to the skin of midges on the pools. They are hand-sized, dark, with mouths made for a surface rather than a chase. They work the skin until it is gone and then slip back under the peat-edge, and the only mark is a line of tiny collapses in the film, healing as you watch. Insects, for their part, do not swarm so much as staff the flowers: a dry whisper, continuous, never rising. At night, which is only a thicker version of the dimming, some of them carry a faint cold light, not enough to see by, enough to show that the mat is moving everywhere at once.

Nothing here builds. Nothing sets a mark meant to be read by another of its kind at any distance. What looks like intention — the even courses of iron, the gaps that are always where the herds need them, the fog that forms at the same hour — is the land repeating a rule until the repetition feels like a plan. The animals fit the rule. They do not write another one over it.

If there is a mind on the plateau, it is not housed in one skull. It is the line choosing the next gap, the long body in the thicket waiting out a rain-column, the film on the pool taking the sheen back into itself before afternoon. You can lie on the mat and feel the grazers pass as a change in the water under you, a cool pulse, then ordinary cold again. They do not investigate you. You are not forage and you are not weather, and those are the two categories that organize the day. When you stand, the place they pressed closes. By the time you have walked to the next bowl, the only proof they were a people at all is a darker current far off, already being rained on, already becoming the color of the grass they are eating.
