What lives there is not a species so much as a timing. The same bodies recur, but they do not keep a shape long enough for the word species to sit still on them, and they do not keep a place long enough for home to mean more than the span of one pulse of the rind. You learn them by what they do to the noise, and by what the noise does back.

Closest to the surface of everything are the clatterers. They are not insects, though your mind reaches for locust and is not entirely wrong about the scale or the suddenness. Each is a small sealed case, ceramic like the leaves, the color of dusted bone, with a seam along the belly that never quite closes. When they move in sheets the seam clicks against the cables and the sound multiplies until it is no longer clicks but a single dry roar, the voice of a hillside sliding. Individually they are almost tender. Turn one over — if you can catch one, which means waiting for a flex that knocks a sheet loose and taking the stunned one before the sheet reforms — and the belly-seam parts on a row of soft paddles, pale, moist only in the sense that they are not yet mineral, each paddle stamped with a pattern of pits that matches, imperfectly, the pits in the cable it was crossing. They read the tissue by touch. The armor is not for enemies. There are too many of them for enemies to matter in the ordinary way. The armor is so they can be walked on by the next sheet, and the next, and still be a body when the cable tears and drops them into a hollow that is already becoming a mouth.

They eat the dust. Not as a fallback. The glittering flour that rises whenever the rind flexes is their crop, and they comb it out of the air with fringes along the seam, pack it into a gut that is more kiln than stomach, and fire it, somehow, on the warmth coming up through the bundles, into a paste that hardens as repair-stuff. You can watch a damaged cable acquire a pale scab that is not the tissue's own callus but a million tiny contributions, each clatterer pausing, depositing, moving on. They are gardeners of a sort, if a gardener can be indistinguishable from the weather that ruins the garden. When a shelf slumps they ride it. They do not panic. Panic would require a future different from the present, and they do not appear to keep one. Their whole argument with the world is density: be many enough that the collapse includes you as a material, not as a loss.

Under them, inside the rind, are the mouths. Calling them animals is a courtesy. For a long time you think they are organs of the tissue itself — the place digesting its own excess, the way a body will digest a limb it can no longer feed. Then you see two of them contest a bulge. The rind heaves. One ring of grinding plates breaks the surface and takes a cable; another ring breaks beside it, angled wrong for feeding, and the plates lock, and there is a sound like mills arguing, and the first ring withdraws not into the tissue but sideways, under it, dragging a wake that is the wrong shape for a reflex. They have flanks. They have a direction they prefer. They are simply too large, and too continuous with the substrate, for the eye to find the edge of them.

What you can find is the ring. Lipless, yes. A circle of plates, each plate a worn crescent of the same unfinished mineral that juts through the walls, set in a collar of tissue that is not the rind's tissue. It is redder, when the light of a fresh fissure strikes it. It smells, faintly, of the sweet-metal gas, but concentrated, as if the creature has been breathing the leak on purpose and holding it. They drink the gas the way the clatterers drink the dust. The grinding is for the mineral plates and the cables, which they pulverize and swallow, but the gas is the prize; a mouth that has found a new fissure will camp on it, ring dilated, plates idle, the whole buried length of the animal pulsing in time with the leak, until the tissue — or a rival mouth — closes the seam. Then the camp breaks and the swell travels again, boat-long, indifferent to the platforms and the ladders, indifferent to you unless you are standing on a bundle it means to take. They are not hunters of flesh. Flesh is a poor fuel here. They are hunters of seams.

If one surfaces near your feet, the grinding plates do not reach for you. They reach for the rind. You are an obstacle only in the sense that a stone is an obstacle in a current. Step off the cable and you are no longer in the current. This is the nearest thing to mercy the underside offers, and it is not meant as mercy. It is a statement about diet.

Between the sheets and the mouths, the hooked climbers work the vertical. Too many legs is accurate and still understates it. The legs are not a number. They bud. A climber stalled on a thickening plate will grow two more hooks from the flank facing the new surface, and the old hooks, starved of contact, will dry to threads and snap off, and the creature will be a different count by the time it reaches the next crotch. What persists is the head, if it is a head: a small dense knot, eyeless, fringed with hairs that quiver in the thrum, and a mouth like a straw that it drives into the soft tips of laterals and drinks. Not sap. There is no sap. It drinks the gas dissolved in the new growth, the same sweet-metal breath, sweeter here because the tips have not yet fired it into light or structure. The climbers are drunks of a precise kind. They congregate where the tissue is deciding, and their ticking is the sound of argument — each hook testing tension, each straw tapping a tip to see if it is still fluid enough to yield.

They fight without ceremony. Two straws in one tip, and the legs lock, and the loser is not killed so much as unhooked. It falls. If the fall is short, it lands in clatterers and is paved over and must bud a frantic excess of legs to claw back out, emerging gray with their paste, already drinking again. If the fall is long, a glider takes it, or a mouth, or the collapsing shelf, and the knot of the head is just another mineral problem for something else to grind. They do not call to each other. The ticking is not a signal. It is the cost of holding on.

The gliders are the first things that look back.

They roost high, where the stacked hollows narrow and the cables run close enough to leap, and their membranes are grown from the same tissue as the rind, which is why, folded, they vanish. A cable thickens. You think it is a flex. Then it opens, and the pale surface splits into a span of living sheet, and something with a ring of small eyes — not many eyes, a ring, all of them simple, all of them aimed outward so that the creature has no front — drops into the air of the chamber. Steering is a flex, as you thought. Landing is a decision to stop being a membrane and become a grip: the trailing edge hooks, the eyes tuck under a lip of ceramic that grows, in the seconds after landing, into a lid. They are born from the rind, or budded, or both. You will not settle it. What you can settle is that they watch the dust. Their launches coincide with the storms. They ride the gust that a regional rebalancing throws, and they sieve the flour with the membrane itself, and they return to the roost heavier, the sheet of them opalescent with captured mineral, which they press into the cable until the cable accepts it. They are planters. They are also thieves. A clatterer sheet that has packed a rich seam of dust will lose a swath of it to a low pass, the membrane kissing the roar and coming away silvered, and the roar does not break formation for this. Density again. The gliders take a tax the sheets can afford.

Their cries are the almost-speech. Up close, which means clinging to a cable under a roost and enduring the tick of climbers crossing your hands, you hear that the rhythm is a map. Short, short, long: a fissure opening to the west of the roost. A rising phrase, cut off: a mouth camped on a leak, do not land. A chatter like gravel poured on a plate: clatterers in a profitable storm, descend. They are not warning one another out of fellow-feeling. They are advertising routes to gas and dust, and the advertisement is public because there is no private, not in a hollow where every flex is everyone's weather. A glider that lied about a seam would be believed once. The roost would follow. The seam would not be there. The liar would return light, and the next launch would not include it, and exclusion, here, is a kind of death that does not require teeth. You can hear a shunning happen. One voice repeating a route, and the chorus declining to pick it up, and the voice thinning, and then a membrane dropping alone into a gust that does not carry it where it said, and not coming back before the shelf below slumps and makes the question moot.

The clustered breathers are easier to pity and harder to see. In every crotch, every overlap of plates, every place a cable crosses a cable and the tissue has not yet fused them, there is a warmth that is not the rind's warmth. Small bodies, packed. They have no armor worth the name. Their skin is the color of the soft tips, and it does not harden, and they press together so tightly that the patch pants as one lung. They are the only things in the underside that seem to need one another in the immediate, bodily way. Separate one — a hook will do it, a glider's trailing edge, your own finger — and it cools. You feel the cooling in your hand, a small retreat of blood-warmth, and the creature curls, and if you do not put it back within a few breaths it grays and goes to powder, fine as the dust, and the powder is indistinguishable from the crop the clatterers comb. Returned to the cluster, it pinks again. The cluster reabsorbs the gap without comment. They have no eyes and no hooks and no plates. They vibrate. The vibration is faster than the cables' thrum and out of phase with it, and if you press your ear to a patch you hear, under the general roar, a thin continuous tone, the same tone from every cluster in a given vault, different from the tone in the next vault over. They are tuning forks for the local tension. The climbers drink near them but do not drink them. The gliders do not sieve them. The mouths, surfacing, detour. Even the rind, racing to close a wound, will swerve around a cluster and seal behind it, leaving the breathers in a pocket of older tissue while everything else is remade. They are not sacred. Nothing here is sacred. They are information the rest of the system is unwilling to spend.

That is the native life, if native means anything in a geology that is still being born. And then there are the others. The ones who lashed the cables.

You will not see one on the first day, or the fifth. The marks are old in the only sense available: a knot already half-swallowed, a peg of wood — wood, actual wood, the grain of it visible, a material that does not grow in this dryness and must have been carried from somewhere that still has rain — digested down to a dark spine. The hands that tied the knots were not quite the hands you carry. The loops are competent and slightly wrong, pulled tight in a direction that favors a wrist with an extra notch of rotation. Four digits in the smear of paste on one remaining rung, and a thumb set far back, and a fifth mark that might be a knuckle and might be a tool. They were here. They worked quickly. They left.

They are still here in the way a route is still here after the walker has gone. The gliders' cries include, sometimes, a phrase that does not parse as fissure or storm or mouth. A flat triplet, repeated, pointing not at a seam of gas but at the line of an old platform, the ghost of a lashing under new rind. The climbers tick more densely along those lines, as if the tension there were legible in a dialect they like. The breathers in vaults that once held a watching-wall hum a tone a quarter-step off from the neighboring vaults, and they have hummed it long enough that the rind around them has grown a shallow dish, a listening shape, accidental and exact. Something taught the underside a pattern, and the underside kept it after the teacher left, the way a path keeps a walker's weight in the spring of the turf.

And once, if you stay through enough collapses to stop flinching at the gusts, you will see the teacher.

It comes along a cable the gliders have been advertising with that flat triplet for the better part of a pulse. It is about your height, if height is measured in a place with no floor. It goes on all the limbs it has, which is four, with the fifth mark explained: a spur at the wrist, not a finger, used as a fid to open knots in the living cable, to feel for the old twist of fiber under the callus. Its skin is not ceramic and not the soft pink of the breathers. It is hide, scarred, dust-gray, with patches of the clatterers' paste dabbed on the shoulders and the spine like a crude armor it does not trust and wears anyway. The eyes are two, forward-facing, lidded against the flour. That alone makes it a foreigner. Everything native looks outward in a ring or not at all. A face that chooses a direction is a face that came from weather with a horizon.

It does not look at you at first. It opens a swallowed knot with the spur, finds the peg's dark spine, and tests whether the platform's logic is still in the tissue — whether a body could still rest here for the length of a breath without being paved or drunk or dropped. The tissue flexes. The creature rides the flex with a practiced collapse of the joints, then stands, then looks along the passage the way a person looks along a street they built and lost. The ring-eyes of a roosting glider uncover. The flat triplet sounds, close, almost polite. The creature answers with a click of the spur against ceramic, three beats, flat. Not speech. A toll paid in the local coin. The glider accepts it and veils its eyes again.

In its other hand, the one without the spur, it carries a flake of the unweathered mineral, struck the way the scatters you found were struck, edge keen. It does not raise the flake against the glider, or against the sheet of clatterers pouring over the cable, or against you. It uses the edge to score a mark in a plate of rind that has not yet hardened: a short line, a notch, another line. A count. Of what, you cannot tell. Collapses survived. Platforms refound. Pulses of the regional underside since the last time it passed this hollow. The mark will be gone before the tissue finishes the thought it is currently thinking. The creature scores it anyway. Then it moves on, down the cable, into the warmer dimness, and the mouths below adjust their swells by a fraction, and the breathers hum their offset tone, and the clatterers pave over the scored plate as if paving were a kindness, and the place, which was never finished, continues, carrying one more habit it did not invent and will not quite forget.
