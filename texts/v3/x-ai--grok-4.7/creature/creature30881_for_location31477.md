The description matches what is living there. Nothing in it is a visitor. The vault has been occupied long enough that occupation and geology are the same fact: the river-floor worn to swells, the salt that never wets, the moisture that left the stone and did not leave the living. What remains is one animal, colonial in the old sense, distributed across a city it also is.

Call it a range, not a species-list. The small pale tickers in the mats, the cat-sized lane-travelers, the mound-builders at the vents, the flyers that never settle — these are castes and ages of one body of life, not a fauna that happens to share an address. They do not hunt one another. They do not compete in any way a short watch could see. Food is the gray-green stem, and the stem is theirs too: a crop they thickened out of whatever first rooted in the abandoned river, selected across a span no single generation could intend, until the plant keeps water the way the animal keeps the plant. Break a fan and the sweetness is not sap so much as reserve. The whole vault is a reserve. Stone gives nothing back, so nothing in the living arrangement is allowed to spill.

The organization follows from that stinginess. Walls of grit and fiber, never high, because height is wasted moisture and wasted work. Mounds broad at the base, vented with finger-wide holes, because what must be kept is not treasure but climate: the nurseries sunk into cooler stone, the dried-stem granaries, the slow exchange of air that lets a colony breathe without wetting the pavement. Streets are only where the mats have agreed not to stand. Plazas are not civic. They are fallow, and the fallow is as deliberate as the built. You can walk a long time and meet no slope worth naming because slopes were worn away first by water and then, more patiently, by the preference of something that likes its roads level and its repairs small.

Individually they are unremarkable, which is the point. A lane-traveler is low, pale, many-legged enough that a count would feel fussy, with jaws sized to strip a fan and carry the pieces without dropping fiber on the salt. It does not look up. Looking up is a flyer’s work, and the flyers are the only quick thing, turning in roof-dark on paths older than any mound now standing. The mound-builders are slower still: a wooden clack at a vent, a pellet of grit, a hand’s width of wall in a life that is not short. The tickers in the mats are maintenance. They keep the joints clear, the fallen fans from matting into rot, the moisture from escaping into a ground that would not return it. None of them is the mind of the place. The mind, if that word survives contact with them, is the spacing: three mounds visible from one, a great deal of empty floor, groves as islands, the same pattern repeating until the gallery bends and continues.

They have no lamps because they do not need contrast. The gray leak from the roof is enough to tell pavement from fan, vent-mouth from living stem. Flash would be waste, and waste is the one thing the vault has spent geological time unlearning. Shadows stay soft. The undersides of the fans and the mouths of the vents are the only full dark, and those are interior, kept. A visitor’s eyes, wanting a focal animal, a queen, a door, will not find one. The city reads as repeating calm because calm is the metabolism. A mound that grew faster would crack. A mat that advanced faster would spend the reserve. They have already run that experiment, somewhere in the scars on the pale floor, and the scars are old enough to be mistaken for the river’s.

What a visitor hears as weather is their coordination, and it is not metaphor. The high stitching, the clack, the thin screaming multiplied by every curve of stone — these are not songs. They are the load-bearing sound of a space that has been filled, deliberately, so that no other signal can cross it intact. Close the eyes and it is one pressure. Open them and the floor is still mostly empty. The emptiness is real; the crowding is real; both are true because the animals are few relative to the vault and the vault is shaped to make few into many. Speech would not carry as words. That is not an accident of acoustics arriving before them. They widened the curves. They spaced the mounds to keep the reflections alive. They have been doing it since the last water left, which is to say they do not remember a time when the air was quiet enough for a foreign voice to arrive whole. A mind that wanted to speak across the gallery would have to become the sound, or outlast it. Neither is available to anything that hurries.

They do not hurry. Hurry is a wet habit, a habit of places where the season turns and the river returns and an animal must be done before the done is taken. Here the season is the decade, and the decade is not marked. A fallen fan lies among the living until it matches them, which is their idea of death: not removed, matched, the moisture already back inside the mat. A nursery is a cooler dent. A granary is a dryness. A ward is a density of clack. If there is rank, it is only which body is near which reserve, and rank moves as the mats advance and retreat across a cycle longer than their memory — if memory is even the right organ. More likely the city remembers by what it has already built. A slumped wall is repaired in the shape of the slump. A path of flyers persists because the stone of their turning has been sounded so long it has become a groove in the air. You could kill every flyer in the vault and the next brood, emerging from the vent-dark months later, would take the same turns, because the turns are in the echo, and the echo does not depend on any one of them being alive.

That is the strangest true thing about what lives there, and it should be said without decorating it. There is no individual whose death changes the floor. There may be no individual at all in the sense a visitor brings down from wetter country, where a life is a line with a start. Here a life is a position in a sound and a position in a grid, and when the position is vacated the grid edits itself by a hand’s width and the sound does not notice. They are not cruel about this. Cruelty would require that a single absence register as loss. What registers is imbalance: a grove that has taken too much pavement, a mound whose vents have clogged, a lane where the tickers have gone quiet so that rot could begin its small theft of water. Those are repaired. The repair looks like patience because it is the only speed the reserve allows. To a creature that measures in years, the city is frantic, every vent working, every lane occupied by ones and twos who are not lingering. To anything that measures in days, it is a still life with weather for a soundtrack.

They have never needed a name for the river. The floor’s forgetfulness is their founding condition, not their tragedy. Where water was, they are, and they are the opposite of water: they do not seek the low place, they do not shine, they do not leave. The salt crusts stay dry underfoot because every living gram in the vault is a lock on the last moisture the galleries held. Touch the stone and it is cool in the way of something that has not been warmed by a body in a very long time. Touch a stem and it is cool too, but the cool is full. That difference — empty cool, full cool — is the entire theology they would have, if they had one. They do not. They have the mats, the mounds, the lanes, the plazas of agreed emptiness, and the sound that makes the vault smaller than it is and larger than any of them.

A visitor who stays long enough to watch a mound change will not see it change. That is the courtesy and the refusal. The place will go on sounding in the same key, the flyers taking the old paths, the lane-travelers stripping fans and carrying them to vents that look like every other vent. If the visitor speaks, the stone will take the words apart before they reach the second mound. If the visitor is quiet, the quiet will be a hole in the weather, and the hole will be mended — not with curiosity, not with threat, with the ordinary drift of bodies whose work is to leave no gap in the pressure. By the time the hole is gone, the visitor will have learned the only fact the vault is willing to teach: what lives here does not come forward to be met. It is already everywhere the floor is flat, and it has been, and the flatness is how you know.
