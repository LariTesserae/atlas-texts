The first thing that lives here is the light, and it does not travel. It stands in the rooms the way water stands in a still cistern, filling them to a line no one marked, brighter near the floor where it has settled and thinner toward the ceiling, where it thins into a gray that is not shadow. Nothing casts. The chairs, the low tables, the long case against the far wall—all of them are simply present inside the light, their edges legible without being edged by darkness. If you lift a hand, the light does not part around it. The hand becomes another object the light has agreed to contain.

What else lives here has learned that agreement. They are not many. You notice the smallest first because it moves, or seems to: a pale moth the size of a thumbnail, fixed to the plaster just above the wainscot, wings open in the exact attitude of a thing that has just landed and has not yet decided whether to stay. It has been deciding for as long as the plaster has been dry. The powder on its wings is undisturbed; no grain of it has fallen to the rail below, though a fall is what powder does. When you look away and back, it is still deciding, or it is deciding again, and the difference is not available to you.

Larger than the moth, and quieter, are the ones who keep to the furniture. They have the scale of children and the patience of objects that were never children. One sits in the ladder-back chair nearest the window that does not open onto anything you can name—only more of the same light, standing outside as it stands in. Its hands rest on its knees. The hands are finished: nails the right length, skin with the faint map of veins a living hand would show if the blood had somewhere to go and had chosen, today, not to. It does not breathe in any way you can count. The ribcage under the thin shirt rises and does not fall, or falls and does not rise, and both are true in the way the cup was true on the curb and in the hand. It is waiting, but not for you. Waiting is simply the shape it was given, and it wears the shape without strain.

Another stands at the case, one finger extended toward a drawer pull it will not touch. The finger is a small distance from the brass. The distance does not close. Dust—there is dust, fine and even, the dust of a room that has been closed since the moment it was opened—lies on the pull and on the fingertip in the same thickness, as if the finger and the brass had been dusted together by a single gesture and then set apart. You can walk between them. The air where you walk is the same temperature as the air you displace, so that your passage leaves no eddy, no cooling, no proof you were the thing that moved.

They do not regard one another. Regard would require a sequence: a turning, a meeting of eyes, a conclusion. Sequence is what the place declines. The one in the chair and the one at the case occupy their gestures completely, and the gestures do not develop. A third, smaller still, is under the table, knees drawn up, face turned toward the carpet’s worn medallion. You see it only when you crouch, and crouching does not startle it, because startle is a story with a beginning. Its eyes are open. They are the color of the light when the light is thinking about being water. Nothing reflects in them. You are not in them, even when you are close enough to see the individual lashes, each one placed, none of them ever to be shed.

Sound lives here on the same terms. A clock on the mantel has a pendulum that hangs at the angle of a swing’s farthest reach. It does not return. The tick you think you hear is the memory of a tick, arriving from no previous tick and departing toward none. Floorboards under your weight give the note they were planed to give, and then the note is simply in the room with you, not fading, not answered by the walls. The walls have already heard everything they are going to hear. They keep it without echo.

If you stay, you begin to understand that staying is not a duration. The moth’s indecision, the extended finger, the drawn-up knees, the unreturned pendulum—these are not pauses in lives that continue elsewhere. They are the lives. The place was made, or settled, or simply occurred, at the density of a single finished instant, and then that instant was asked to hold inhabitants. It held them by refusing to let any of their next moments arrive and displace the ones they already perfectly occupy. They are not trapped. Trapped things press against their limits. These do not press. The limits are the size of the gesture, and the gesture is the size of them.

Farther in—there is a farther, another room, a threshold that is only a change in the carpet’s pattern—the same light stands at the same depth. Another chair. Another figure, this one with its head tilted as if listening to the tick that does not succeed itself. You can go to it. When you do, the one you left is still in its chair, still unrisen, and also, in some counting the house does not perform, still accompanied by the version of you that has not yet crossed the threshold. Both versions are calm. Neither is more present. The house has room for every completed arrangement and no room at all for the one that would come next.

What lives here, then, is the refusal of next. It wears the shapes of moths and of patient bodies and of stopped clocks because those shapes are easy to finish and hard to imagine continuing. They do not hunger. They do not age. They do not look at you with any plea to be released into time, because plea would be a movement, and movement would be a betrayal of the light’s long agreement with stillness. You can leave. The door, if you find it, will open onto the same standing brightness, and the figures will remain exactly as arranged, occupying their instant with the thoroughness of things that were never promised another.
