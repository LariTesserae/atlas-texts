What first seems to live there is only a repetition with a preference.

The basins do not all skin at the same hour, and the highland does not appear to care which of them does. Yet along one contour the tea-colored stage is always a little drawn down, and the syrup stage a little broken at the same lip, and the flexible sheet that should have traveled and been taken back under is missing, or is present only as a bitten crescent left sticking to the wire-plants. The missing does not look like a spill. A spill would have darkened a path. This is a measured subtraction, made in the same interval the drip itself keeps, as if something had learned the schedule and was keeping a second, quieter one inside it.

The track confirms the subtraction and then undoes the idea of a traveler. It is a pressed line, low and heavy, the mat pushed down a hand’s depth and slow to rise, and it does not wander. It runs from a lower release of the hollow cords to a basin, from the basin to a place where the pale threads are felting into cloth, from the cloth to a slit of a failed dome that is already being webbed shut. Then it ends. Not in a lair, not in a scatter of bones or resin, not in a second line joining it. It ends by becoming indistinguishable from the bank it has been following, the compression shallowing until it is only the ordinary give of the mass. If you go back to the release by another way, a new line has sometimes begun, with the same weight, the same refusal to meander, and no evidence that anything turned around. Whatever moves here does not seem to return. It seems to conclude, and then, elsewhere, to begin again as if conclusion were a method of travel.

Watch the line being made and the body, when it finally offers itself, is disappointing in a useful way. It is low because the air here leans and height would be a sail. It is heavy because the mat will not hold a lighter tread; a light thing would skate on the living tile and be shifted by the ring-waves of the slumps. What crosses the swell is about the length of a person lying down and thicker than that through the middle, dark as wet iron, with a surface that takes light the way the syrup-sheets do: a dull skin, flexible, already cracking along one flank where the cold has worked it. It does not walk so much as found itself forward. A portion of the front margin softens, flows a short distance into the grain of the mat, stiffens, and the rest is hauled up onto that purchase. The sound is the sound the place already owns, a compression, and then nothing.

At first this seems like an animal wearing the country as camouflage. Stay through one transfer—from release, to basin, to felting face—and the camouflage theory thins. At the release it does not drink the colder air; it lies across the mouth until the draft is stopped, and the skin along its near side tightens and pales, rime feathering it in the same needles the plants drive through the ice lenses. It is drying something. When it moves on, the patch of flank that took the rime cracks away in plates, and the plates do not fall behind it as waste. They catch on the resin bulbs and hang there, and by the next change in the light they have been climbed by white shoots and are no longer plates. At the basin it does not lap. The broken lip of syrup climbs it, as the syrup climbs any tear, and is closed over by the same coordinated slackening the wire-plants use, except the slackening is in the creature’s hide, which is briefly indistinguishable from the plants leaning away. It takes on a dark tongue of liquor and carries it inside the crack, and the crack seals from the edges with a flexible sheet of its own making. At the felting face it stops longer. The pale threads, meant for the wind, strike it and hold. It does not shake them off. It turns, slowly, and the threads wrap, and what leaves that station is thicker than what arrived, and less like a single hide, more like a gallery that has decided to be portable for a while.

So the body is not a body in the way a fox is a body, or even a slug. It is a sleeve the highland is continually issuing, and something inside the sleeve is what keeps the route.

That something is almost not visible, and this is where the first image has to be abandoned. Where a plate has dropped, or where a new wrapping of threads has not yet sealed, there is a glimpse of the interior: not organs, not a darker animal curled up, but a pale cord-work, wet, many-ended, packed into the sleeve the way the white roots are packed into a torn cross-section of mat. The ends open and close with the same faint draw the hollow cords have. Put a hand near—not on, the cold there is a bite—and the draw is stronger than the highland’s, and it answers. Stop a lower release with your shadow, or with a dropped resin bulb, and a small change runs through the sleeve: a stiffening, a re-routing, one internal mouth sealing and another opening, as if the occupant were less a creature than a correction to the breathing of the place. It is living in the industrial fact. The highland conditions air so the works will not ice shut or sweat themselves to rot; this is the portion of that conditioning which has become restless enough to move the conditioned air to where the schedule is drifting.

It feeds, if that is the word, on the stages. Clear water does not interest it. The tea-colored middle does, and the syrup, and sometimes the sand. At a quartz ridge it will halt and press its underside into the sorted grit until the ridge’s crest is disturbed, then move off with a paler dust held in the cracks of the sleeve, and leave the ridge already being re-sorted by the roots, the disturbance no more permanent than a footprint in a road that is also a sieve. The dust is not stored in any pouch you can find. It migrates inward along the cord-work and reappears, hours later, as a grit in the sheet it seals with, a temper in the hide, the way a potter’s ground shell tempers clay. The resin bulbs it passes sometimes shrink. Not all of them. Only those of a certain size, the ones that have cracked on schedule and not yet been overgrown. It takes the amber into the same cracks that took the syrup, and somewhere inside the two meet, and the sleeve that results is tougher along the flank that will face the walking rains.

There is no second one. That was the easy mistake, looking at a country so large, to assume a population merely unseen. The lines never cross. When a sleeve concludes—and it does, you can find the occasions if you are willing to be still longer than the light wants—you do not get a corpse. You get a lying-down that is identical to the old way domes fail, except in reverse and quiet. The portable gallery sags. Its internal draw flattens into the highland’s lesser draw. Roots web the lip. The pale cord-work does not climb out and flee. It opens itself along its length into the packed fiber, and for a little while the draft through that patch of mat is stronger, cleaner, more regular than the drafts around it, and the plants over that patch extrude on a tighter interval, and the basins below run a stage ahead of their neighbors. Then even that difference is absorbed. The sleeve is bank. The bank is ordinary. The occupation is over in the way a shift is over.

And then, at another release, sometimes before the first has finished sinking, a thin pale end is already out in the cold, feeling for threads, for a cracked sheet, for any purchase the wind has not taken. It does not grow a new animal. It accumulates one, by the same processes the highland uses to make walls: felt, resin, tempered skin, a weight borrowed from water it refuses to let stay. If it is the same occupant, it has no face with which to be the same. If it is a child or a continuation, nothing marks the generation except the route, which is hereditary in the sense that a drainage is hereditary. What persists is the preference: this contour, this subtraction, this habit of stopping the draft just long enough to dry a flank and then giving the draft back better than it was taken.

The moth, when it is there, is not a companion and not prey. It sits on resin as if it were another exudate, and the sleeve, passing, does not turn for it. The ash-colored wings are a coincidence of stillness. Whatever scarce fauna the highland allows, this is not quite among them. It does not call. It does not hold a territory against others of its kind, there being no others to hold it against, or only one, which cannot meet itself because meeting would require two places at once and it already is a sequence of places. It has no den that survives its absence. It will not be found by digging; digging only opens another mouth for the works, and the works close mouths.

What you can know of its attention is small and exact. It knows the hour a dome is ready to fail, and it is often already across that shoulder when the ring-wave arrives, not fleeing, simply no longer in the way, as a worker who has finished in a room will be in the next room when the first is taken down. It knows which tongue of syrup is too fresh and will not seal, and leaves those basins unvisited, the sheet intact. It knows the walking rain by a change in the draft before the grey column is visible, and it will anchor—front margin flowed into the mat, threads pulled tight—and let the column wet it to a shine and pass, and afterward the wetted flank is the one it sheds first, because rain-loosened hide is poor temper. None of this looks like thought if thought means a pause. It looks like the highland’s own regularity, loosened by a few spans of will, or by something for which will is the wrong tool-word: a bias in a process, old enough to have become a route, recent enough to still be mending the route as it goes.

Near the end of the light, if you have stayed, you may see it in the moment it is least like an animal and most like what it is. The sleeve has grown thick with the day’s felt. The vapor off the lower country is around it. It has stopped at a slit that is almost a mouth, the colder metal-and-green air coming out, and it is fitting itself to that opening the way a bank fits itself to a hill, by sagging, by being climbed, by giving its draw back to the larger draw until you cannot say which is breathing. For a little while the two drafts argue. The slit’s is colder. The sleeve’s is drier. Then they are one draft, and the extraordinary thing—the only one, and it is mild—is that the plants around the join extrude a bulb of resin in the scar, on the interval, as if a worker had clocked out and the works had not noticed, or had noticed and approved, which in this country is the same result.

In full dark you will not see the next beginning. You may feel it, if you are near a high mouth when the pale end comes out: a stronger draw, a brief colder thread against the wrist, regular as a duty, and then the small private sound inside the skull of threads taking hold, which might be nothing you would swear to. By morning there will be a new weight on some contour, already keeping the second schedule, already difficult to tell from the erosion and the growth that have been arguing here long enough to become a single apparatus. It lives there the way a correction lives in a machine that has forgotten it might have been anything else: not housed, not tended, not separate enough to be driven off, and not idle. The highland goes on manufacturing. Something in it goes on insisting, quietly, on the order of the stages, and paying for that insistence with its own body, which the morning will already have started to mistake for ground.
