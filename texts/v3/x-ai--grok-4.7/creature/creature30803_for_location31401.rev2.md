They are easiest to meet as a mistake of the light. A lane of cleared crust, still damp at the edges, carries a thin scatter of brightness that does not stay put—points of mica, you think, until one of them turns and the brightness has joints. The body is small enough to be mistaken for a seed. It is not a seed. It walks with the whole of itself, abdomen a little lifted, the six legs writing a dry, continuous tick that the hollow swallows before it becomes sound.

Up close the glitter is not metal. It is the cuticle, thin and slightly translucent, oiled by the same film that skins the silt at dawn. Where the dim green-gold strikes a flank, the body gives a pale answer and then goes matte again as it steps into its own shadow. The color, when it can be said to have one, is the color of the newest pellets: bone, bread, wet clay. Underground, in the breath of the shafts, even that color fails. They are the shade of the dark they work in. Only the ones who cross open crust take on the day’s thin gilding, and they do not linger in it. The light is a crossing, not a home.

The head is almost all work. A pair of jaws, not large until they are seen against a grain of silt, opens and closes with a patience that looks like thought and is older than thought. Between the jaws the mouth wets what it means to keep. There are no hands. There is no need. A pellet is a mouthful that has been decided: silt taken from a seam still soft enough to yield, turned, mixed with a thread of fluid from the body—clear, faintly sweet, stronger than it looks—and set. The setting is the whole technology. One grain against the last grain. A pressure. A release. The pellet holds because the fluid holds, and because the next pellet comes before the air can argue. You can watch a course rise the width of a finger between one thickening of the light and the next, and never see a gesture you would call a blow. Addition, only. The jaws that could cut do not cut the land. They portion it.

The legs know the crust better than the eyes, if the eyes are eyes. Dark beads, small, adequate for the difference between shaft-mouth and open lane, useless for distance. What leads them is closer than sight. A tremor in the pellet underfoot—another body passing, a course settling, the minute collapse of a drying skin—arrives as a direction. Humidity is a map. The warm breath leaving a shaft is a road with a temperature. The sweetness in the walls is a gradient they climb and descend without pausing to name it. Antennae write the air in front of the head, two fine strokes, constant, reading what the hollow has exhaled in the last moment: rain coming, a lens shrinking, a nursery that has grown a fraction too warm, a stranger’s chemistry on the lip of a hole. They do not look around. They arrive already informed.

Follow one and the singleness begins to fail.

She—the ones on the lanes are, almost all of them, the same unfinished sex, neither the buried abundance nor the brief winged weather that sometimes leaves the mound and does not return—she takes a grain from a damp seam where last night’s film still stands. The grain is smaller than her head. She turns it. The fluid goes on. She carries it not in a visible load but as a change in her outline, a slight laboring of the gait, back along the lane that other bodies have packed harder than the crust around it. She does not consult them. She does not avoid them carefully, either. When another meets her, antennae touch, a half-second, and something is exchanged that is not the pellet: a sample of the last chamber, the last alarm, the last sufficiency. Then each continues, as if the meeting had been a stitch in cloth rather than an event between two animals.

At the flank of the mound she finds the course that is still dark, still damp, still open to improvement. She sets the pellet where the edge is lowest. Presses. The fluid meets the fluid already drying in the course. She cleans her jaws on nothing, on the air, on the next intention. Then she turns and the lane takes her back. She will do this until the light fails and after the light fails. Night is not an interruption. It is the shift in which the open crust stops being a risk and becomes merely dark, which she already understood.

Inside, the body changes its job without changing its shape.

The shafts are narrow enough that two must pass by a practiced flattening. The walls are warm to a degree the plain never holds—heat of bodies, heat of the stacked gardens, heat of wet clay refusing to give up what the rain lent it. The air moves because they built it to move: vents aligned with the hollow’s slow breath, so that the dim warmth of the room is drawn through and tempered, never allowed to become the dead heat of a shut mouth. She enters that breath and is received by it. The cuticle that glittered outside goes dull. The jaws that portioned silt become, a few corridors in, the jaws that portion other things.

There are gardens. You smelled them from the hollow’s floor and could not place the sweetness—grain kept too long, a cellar, a sweetness without fruit. Here it has a face. Pale combs, finer than the masonry, ridged and branched, humid, tended, filling chambers that are not yet old enough to have sagged. On the combs a living rind, neither plant nor the builders, a softness that fruits in a pinhead abundance wherever it is kept in the right damp and the right dark. She does not eat the land. She eats this, or feeds it to those who cannot yet walk the lanes. Her mouth, which outside is a trowel, is here a farmer’s thumb: cropping, reseeding, stripping a spent patch, carrying a crumb of the living rind to a new comb still pale and empty. The fungus is not a crop in the way a field is a crop. It is a second stomach, installed in the architecture. The mound digests the hollow through it—fallen leaf, a little wood, the cellulose the jaws can scrape but not unlock—and the fungus hands back what a small body can use. Neither is the other’s tool. They are one metabolism wearing two shapes, and the shape that walks is the one you notice.

Deeper, the masonry changes grain. The nurseries are smoother, wetter, held at a warmth that does not fluctuate when the plain outside whitens between rains. Here the bodies that do not yet have their full commission lie in a pale clutter, each no more than a curve. She moves among them with a blunt gentleness, a mouthful of food already softened, a cleaning that is also an inspection. A larva that has cooled is moved. A pellet of waste is carried off with the same unemphatic competence as a pellet of clay. Nothing in her posture says tenderness. The tenderness is in the repetition, which does not fail when the light changes, which does not require her to be the same individual tomorrow. By tomorrow the body you followed may be spent. The nursery will not notice the difference. Another cuticle will glitter on the lane. The course will still rise.

Not every body is built for the lane.

At certain mouths, especially where the mound faces the open side of the hollow and the air is less certainly theirs, a different outline holds still. Larger head. Jaws that are not for portioning but for closing a gap—curved, darkened at the tips, held open or held shut for lengths of time that make them look like fixtures. The rest of the body is almost an afterthought, soft, pale, fed by the others, incapable of the long errand to the seam and back. These ones do not build. They are the sentence the colony keeps in its mouth for intruders: a brief, chemical, decisive no. Most hours they are unemployed. The hollow is not, this season, a battlefield. A mite wanders the wrong corridor and is ended without ceremony. A beetle tests a shaft at dusk and finds the shaft already occupied by a pair of jaws and a smell that means turn around. Then the fixture returns to being a fixture. The glittering traffic flows around it as around a stone in a path, using it, not consulting it.

Somewhere under the highest new courses, in a chamber smoother than the nurseries, one body has given up the lane forever. She is the abundance the others are the distribution of. The abdomen is no longer a walking part. It is a slow, pale length, maintained, cleaned, fed, too large now for the corridors she once used, if she used them—her life began in a flight you would have missed, a warm evening, a brief paired glitter over the plain, a falling, a sealing. What remains is rhythmic and immense for the scale of the room: eggs, in a steady issue, taken from her by mouths that know the difference between what must go to the nursery and what must be eaten to keep the rhythm from jamming. She does not direct the lanes. She does not need to. Her chemistry is already in the walls, in the fluid that binds the pellets, in the recognition that passes when antennae touch. The town is her body continued past her skin. She is not its queen in any sense that requires a gesture. She is its ongoing permission to exist.

Sometimes, when the air outside is thick and the dim sky lowers, the mound produces a different permission. Bodies with wings, dark and brief, gather in the upper chambers and then spill from the shafts in a thin weather, not a storm. They rise into the green-gold, glitter harder than the workers, and are gone over the plain—most of them food for the hour, a few of them the start of another room in another crease, if the rain and the silt and the luck agree. The heron on the lip does not bother with them. The bats, stitching the open side, sometimes do. By morning the mound looks as it did. The spent wings, if any fell back onto the crust, are portioned and disappear into the gardens like any other softness. Flight is not the life here. Flight is how the life risks a second copy. The life itself is the walking, the setting, the comb.

What they are together is not a crowd. Stand at the lip and the mound can seem sparsely used: a line, a scatter on the new courses, a few fixtures at the mouths, no mass that reads as swarm unless you kneel and insist on counting. The density is internal. In the corridors the passing is constant, a blood flow with manners. Traffic sorts itself by some rule of scent and yield. A body with a pellet has the center. A body returning empty takes the wall. No one directs this. The directing was done when the shafts were aligned, when the first comb was seeded, when the fluid in the jaws was given its strength. After that the town runs on contact and gradient, the way the hollow runs on films and lenses rather than a stream.

They drink as the place drinks. A film along the earthen wall is a long visit for a line of mouths. A droplet about to fall from a leaf is anticipated, or seems to be; they gather where drops have fallen before, where the moss cups keep a lens after the plain has forgotten the rain. No one opens the ground to find a river. There is no river to find. The water is a guest, and they receive it as guests receive: locally, without trying to become its master, and with the understanding that it will leave. When it leaves they spend the damp it left in the seams, and the gardens tighten their humidity inward, and the vents trade a drier breath until the next short rain writes its films again.

They have no devices because their devices are postures. The jaw is wedge, trowel, scythe, and spoon. The fluid is mortar and message and, in the soldiers, a sharper dialect of the same language. The wing is a season, not a possession. The mound is a machine that is also a body, also a climate, also a mouth. Nothing is fired. Nothing is smelted. Nothing rolls. If a course is wrong it is not demolished; it is buried in a righter course, the error kept as interior, the way a growing thing keeps its earlier knots. Subtraction is not in their grammar yet. The land around them still wears ripple-marks for the same reason: nobody here has learned to cut, and the century that will teach cutting has not arrived.

A single life is short and unremarkable. The one you followed this morning may be, by the warm edge of night, a body cleaned and portioned in a side chamber, her fluid and her grain returned to the combs, her last pellet already anonymous in a course that no longer looks new. Nothing marks the spot. The lane does not pause. This is not cruelty. Cruelty would require a witness who expected a story. What they keep is not the walker but the walking—the set of the jaws, the recipe of the fluid, the alignment of the vents, the warmth in the nursery, the recognition that passes in a half-second of antennae. Individuals are how the pattern touches the silt. The pattern is what lives here.

And the pattern is still young, which is the strangest thing about it. The masonry has soft corners. The highest courses are darker, damper, a day old or less. There are chambers roughed out and not yet combed. There are lanes that end because the decision to continue them has not yet been repeated enough times to become a fact. You can feel, in the sparseness on the crust, that this is not an ancient citadel running on inheritance. It is a first fluency. The hollow offered a room—contained damp, a lip of crust, leaves enough to soften the light, water that visits and does not rule—and something answered by becoming civic. Not by gathering into a heap. By dividing labor into bodies that cannot live apart, by installing a second stomach in the walls, by agreeing, without a meeting, that the land would be added to and not taken from.

At the dim end of the day the lane still carries its scatter of brightness. A pellet is turned. A film is drunk down to the clay. In the nursery a mouth delivers a softness to a curve that will, if the vents hold, be a glittering on a lane before the plain has grown a rill. The shafts breathe out, and the breath is theirs: warm, a little sweet, the only wind the room reliably has. The heron shifts on the lip and is not their concern. The bats stitch and unstitch the open side. Inside the unfinished town, in the dark that is not an absence but a material, the small jaws continue the only sentence they have—add, add, add—while the hollow keeps them, as it keeps its lenses, locally, and for as long as the damp agrees.
