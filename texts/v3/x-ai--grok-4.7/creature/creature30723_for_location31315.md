The place does not begin as a town. It begins as a hesitation in the weather: a place where the wind, which has been saying one long dry thing for days, says it a second time, lower, as if answering itself. You notice before you understand. The air has thickness without humidity. Scales of something finer than ash hang at a fixed height and do not settle, and under them the light arrives already used, gray-amber, the color of a lamp seen through horn. Nothing casts a sharp shadow. The plain receives you the way a held note receives another note — not welcoming, only acoustically available.

Underfoot the ground is not soil. It gives, a little, the way a drumhead gives when you rest a palm on it, and the give has a temperature. Cool. Not the cool of shade. The cool of a liquid that never reaches the surface. If you press harder the cool deepens and a faint ring starts in the bone of your wrist and runs off into the distance, not as an echo exactly, more as if the ground had decided your pressure was a phrase worth repeating to whatever is further along. There is a smell like warm metal and crushed sage and the inside of a violin case. No wet. The dust on your boots stays dust. You look down and the crust is a pale, tightly woven armor of ribs no thicker than a finger, the color of old bone shot through with tarnished copper, each rib edged with a wax that does not shine. Between the ribs the surface is felted, almost fabric, and if you kneel and look along it the felt runs to the horizon without a seam.

You stand up and the horizon is wrong in the ordinary way of high country — too close, too clean — and then wrong in another way. A swell that should be a mile off flexes, slowly, as if it had taken a breath. Not a mirage of water. There is nothing here that knows how to counterfeit water. The flex is in the scale-haze itself, a slow bend, and when it settles the swell is the same distance and a different shape. You try the other direction. Same. The plain is level in the way a conclusion is level: everything that could fall has already fallen, and what remains has agreed not to.

Walking, you learn the sound before you learn the layout. Each step is a tick with a tail. The tail is the tick traveling. After a hundred paces you can tell, without looking, when you have crossed from one body of the crust into another — the pitch drops a minor third, the tail lengthens, and the cool under the sole changes by a degree you could not name and could not miss. The change is not a border. It is a joint. The plain is one animal lying down, and you are walking the seam between two of its thoughts.

The animals proper come into the picture by subtraction. You had thought the plain empty. Then the ticking you had assigned to your own feet continues for a moment after you stop, and you see, far enough away that they are only a stipple on the copper, a scatter of plated things moving with the patience of erosion. They are not herded. They are distributed, the way leaves are distributed on a slow river, and their mouths are rakes. Each rake combs the bloom of shed scales off the living rib, and each combing is the bright tick you heard. They do not look at you. Looking is not their sense. When the wind changes they all lean the same way a moment before the wind arrives, which tells you that the wind is also a sound, and they heard it in the crust, and the crust told them.

Above them the air is stitched. Small membranes, no bigger than a hand, ride the static the scale-weather makes, and they speak to one another in hard cracks, the sound of a dry twig under a boot, except there is no twig and no boot and the cracks come in phrases. A phrase of three, a pause, a phrase of five. You count without meaning to. The phrases avoid the places where the plated grazers are thickest, and cluster where the crust is singing a little higher, which is to say where the oil in the tissue is moving a little faster. You do not see the oil. You never will. A cut — you try this, with a thumbnail, foolishly, on a rib — opens a dark line that does not wet your skin. The dark climbs back into the rib. The rib draws shut with a soft drumhead drag, and for a minute afterward that patch of crust rings a half-tone sharp, embarrassed or simply busy.

Something is listening to the ringing. You feel it before you see it: a subtraction of tick, a small area of the plain that has gone polite. Then a shape you had taken for a standing stone lowers a long head to the crust and holds it there, the way a person holds an ear to a wall. It is not looking at the cut. It is hearing the cut's aftermath, the way the half-tone travels, and it is deciding whether the rhythm is a grazers' rhythm or a mistake. You stop moving. Your heart, which you had not been counting as a sound, suddenly seems very loud and very regular, and you understand in a practical way why the people of this place sleep where the song is loudest. Quiet would be a kind of nakedness. The listener's head stays down. The half-tone fades as the rib finishes its work. The head lifts. The shape becomes a stone again. You walk, and you walk with an evenness you did not have before, and the evenness is not fear exactly. It is manners.

The town, when it arrives, arrives as a thickening of the same manners.

You see the towers first as pipes, pale, too far apart to be a skyline. They do not cluster. They keep a distance from each other the way singers keep a distance so their voices can braid instead of blur. As you come in, the crust under you changes its mind about pitch more often — every twenty paces, then every ten — and the tails of your footsteps come back sooner, as if the ground were more interested. The ribs are taller. Some of them have been persuaded to stand all the way up, and the persuasion is visible as a slight spiral in the grain, a torque frozen into the tissue. Between two such standing ribs there is a lane, and the lane is not paved. It is the same crust, only taught flat, and taught so long ago that the teaching has become the rib's own preference. You step onto it and the tick of your foot becomes a note with a floor under it. Civic. The word is absurd and accurate.

The walls have a second structure inside the first, and you see it only because the light is bad in the particular way that shows veins. Hair-fine lines, not metal, seated in the living tissue and running with it, branching where the rib branches. They are not bright. They are a difference in the dark. Where the lane has been walked into a polish, the lines near the polish are busier — denser, more cross-tied — and you have the unpleasant, then merely true, sense that the wall has been counting the polish. A ridge at ankle height, raised by centuries of the same routes, is brighter in those lines than the rib around it. The wall is remembering the walking by becoming slightly more itself where the walking was.

A door. You had not thought about doors. This one is not hung. It is an opening the rib agreed to and continues to agree to, held by a tension you can feel if you pass your hand through it: a soft resistance, like pushing through a membrane of static, and on the far side the air is colder by the same degree you felt between crust-bodies on the plain. The cold has a pitch. You could hum it. Someone inside is humming it, absently, the way people elsewhere hum along with a refrigerator they have stopped hearing. The interior is a throat. Hollow, stiffened, the walls curving in a way that returns your breath to you a moment late and a little lower. Light comes from the wall at the height of your calf, a vein-glow, honey through horn, enough to show the floor and not enough to show the ceiling, which goes up into a dark that is not empty. The dark is full of the tower's own voice, a standing resonance, the note this pipe was grown to hold. You speak, and your voice finds a shelf in that resonance and sits there. You stop speaking, and the shelf continues for a heartbeat, occupied by nothing.

People live in these throats the way vowels live in instruments: necessary, and not the point.

You meet them in the lanes more than in the towers, because the lanes are where the chord is. Mid-dim — you will not learn to call it evening for a while, because the sky does not perform evening; it only deepens — the western pipe drops its note by a third, and the market rib, which is only a rib that has been thickened until it can hold a crowd's weight without changing pitch, answers high and short. The answer is a signal older than any schedule. The bloom is shedding harder. The scale-weather is coming down from head-height toward the lamps. People adjust their work. A press of some kind, bone-colored, worked by a woman whose arms are mapped with the same fine lines as the walls — not tattoos; the lines move, slowly, when she shifts her grip — finishes its cycle and is wiped with a cloth that does not dampen. The cake she has pressed smells like the crushed-sage part of the ground, concentrated. She sets it on the rib and the rib accepts the residual solvent with a small satisfied tick. Nothing pools. A child near her spills a thread of lamp-oil from a badly closed vessel, and you watch the thread refuse the ground. It climbs the child's hem, finds the crust the hem has been brushing, and is gone. The child does not flinch. The child watches the oil go home with the expression of someone confirming a lesson already learned past the point of interest.

Lamps here are rude. You understand this when someone lights one at full wick and the scale-haze, which had been drifting in sheets, knots and swirls and the song of the lane goes thin, as if the crust had squinted. The lamp is turned down. The haze unkinks. The song returns to its proper density. The vein-glow is polite because it is the crust's own light, stored from the strikes and given back at a volume the plain recognizes as self. Foreign light is a false note. People carry it anyway, small and selfish, for the tunnels of the older ribs where the vein-glow has forgotten a turn. Selfishness, in this register, is only a light that does not consult the chord.

You stay long enough for a strike-season, or the season stays long enough for you. Time is not marked by dark. It is marked by what the towers report.

The first sign is the click-fliers. Their phrases shorten. Three-crack patterns become two, then one, then a long crack that is not a phrase at all but a measurement: the static is climbing past the level where language is useful, and they are spending their membranes on staying aloft. The grazers have already leaned. They lean for an hour before the sky does anything you can see. Then the scale-haze lifts all at once, not dispersing, lifting, as if the plain had inhaled, and the light goes the color of a bruise inside a shell. The towers begin to detune on purpose. You hear the western pipe flatten, then the next, then the market rib, a controlled sag, the civic equivalent of an animal lowering itself to the ground. People do not run. Running would be a stumbled rhythm, and the listeners are already closer to the lanes than they are in ordinary dim, drawn by the general rise in charge, hoping. People walk with the evenness you learned on the plain, and they walk into the throats, and the throats take them the way a held breath takes air back.

The strike, when it comes, does not come from a cloud in any lowland sense. The air itself becomes the cloud, a column of scale and charge, and the column touches a swell beyond the last persuaded rib. The light is blue-white and ends in the ground. There is no thunder in the interval you expect. The thunder is the plain answering, and it answers from underneath you, a roar that arrives in your feet already old and then rises into the air as the towers, all of them, at once, find the note they were emptied to receive. The chord is enormous and not loud in the way loud usually means. It is loud in the way a room is loud when everyone in it has said the same word. The lattices in the walls flare. For a moment you can see them all, a city's worth of fine memory, lit from within, and what they are remembering is visible on the terraces: ridges of footsteps blooming and fading, routes walked that morning, routes walked by people who are standing still beside you now, their own ghosts passing through their ankles and sounding, very softly, like someone still walking.

Nobody steps on the dreams. It is not forbidden. You watch a man wait, a sack of pressed cake in his arms, while a fading ridge of his own earlier path crosses the lane. He is not reverent. He is practical. The dream has a sound, and the sound is a rhythm, and a rhythm with nothing making it is exactly the sort of mistake a listener's head-against-the-crust is built to investigate. He waits the dream out. The ridge thins. The sound thins. He crosses. Behind him the vein-glow in the walls is running higher than you have seen it, honey gone toward amber, and the cool underfoot has sharpened, the oil moving fast, carrying the strike away from the swell and into the town's body and out again into the thin high air as a heat you never feel, because the conduits were built — were grown, were persuaded — to bleed it before it becomes weather.

Afterward the town is slightly more awake for a day. The lattices dream in public longer than usual. A terrace you had thought unwalked shows, at ankle height, the polish-ghost of a crowd that has not stood there since before you arrived, and the ghost has a song, and the song is the market chord from some other dim, displaced. People route around it with the same practical patience. A tower that has been adding a story — you notice the scaffolding of it only now, a centimeter of new rib, raw copper against the bone-color, creaking as the tissue decides whether to accept the torque — creaks more, fed. The grazers avoid the struck swell. The listeners do not. You see three of them, stones no longer, heads down in the blue-white scar, and you understand that a strike is also a wound, and a wound is a rhythm that has not yet decided to heal evenly, and uneven healing is, to a listener, a question worth standing over until the answer is dull.

You go out past the last rib, because the edge is not a wall and the decision it renews is legible only from both sides.

Beyond the town the crust is the same body, untaught. The lattices, where an engineer has laid a line toward a second market that will not happen, run under your feet for a season's length and then dull. You can hear the dulling as a detune: the path rings true, then rings true with a wobble, then rings at a pitch the towers do not answer. The oil has rerouted. The line is becoming plain again, not by dying, by losing interest. The grazers use it. They were using the plain anyway. You stand where the lattice gives up and you look back, and the town is a scatter of pipes on a shelf, far enough apart that the chord they make has air in it. They have been this size for longer than their records. The records are not kept in anything you would call writing. They are kept as resonances in the oldest throats, and occasionally a throat, in a dim when the charge is high and the bloom is heavy, releases one: a weather that is also a sentence. You hear one on your way back. It comes out of the western pipe as a pattern of sag and recovery that the people beside you do not discuss, because discussing it would be like discussing your own pulse in the middle of walking. Later, when you ask, a woman with lines in her arms that move when she shrugs says the sentence was about a second market. She says it was the plain, not the town, that decided. She says the deciding happened before anyone now living chose to live here, and the living is an agreement to inhabit a conclusion rather than an argument for a new one.

At night — the word is a loan; what you mean is the deepest part of the dim, when the scale-haze has settled to the height of your chest and the vein-glow is the only civic light left — you sleep in a rib, because you have learned the manners. The song is loudest there. Your heart, which on the plain seemed dangerously countable, disappears into a chord that has been counting better hearts than yours for a time that does not bother with numbers. The oil moves under you. You never see it. It is the cool that is not shade, the pitch of the cold, the reason a spilled thread climbs a hem and goes home, the reason a cut does not bleed outward, the reason the thunder is old when it arrives. It is circuit and nowhere. Above the hollow towers the platelets drift in their fixed layer, a weather made of the plain's own shed skin. The high shelf holds its shape. Somewhere on a swell a listener stands with its head to the ground, hoping for a stumble, and does not get one, and becomes a stone until the next mistake. Somewhere a grazers' rake ticks, and the tick travels, and the tail of it passes under your sleep like a sentence in a language you have started to hear and will not, if you are careful, need to speak. The town answers itself. The wind says the long dry thing once, and then lower, and the lower saying is the place, remembering that it is inhabited, and agreeing, for another dim, to hold the note.
