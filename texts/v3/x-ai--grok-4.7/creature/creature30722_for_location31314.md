They do not arrive. There is no track in from any larger dark, no season when the seam was thin enough to admit a body and then sealed. Whatever lives here was already in the mineral when the blister opened, held as a dryness inside the dryness, and the heat only gave it a room.

At first they read as weather. The floor’s restless dark, the ticking ceiling, the way a scrape anywhere brightens and then is answered from the far wall—these feel like properties of the chamber, the way steam is a property of a vent. Stand long enough for your own heat to become ordinary and the weather separates into bodies. They are small enough to lose against the grain of the stone, and none of them is alone long enough to be counted as one. A single animal is a correction the eye makes and then abandons. What persists is the mat: overlapping plates of them on the floor, a stitched hide on the walls, a hanging weight in the upper air where the heat is sharpest and the dust never falls. Disturb the mat and it does not flee so much as re-weave. The gap closes from the edges, not from a center. There is no center.

A body, when one can be held still by looking, is hard and slightly too heavy for its size. The young ones—if young is the word for a thing that has only just plated—are pale, the color of the sugary fracture they hatch against, and their shells still take a thumbnail’s scratch. They rasp the mineral film with mouthparts that are not jaws so much as fine mills, lifting curls of oxide thinner than the dust they work in. What they take in is not food in any green sense. It is film, salt crust, the metallic sweat the new rock keeps exhaling. Their guts are short and hot. You can sometimes see, in the palest of them, a dull traveling ember where the chemistry runs, a knot of warmth moving from mouth to vent and gone. They do not drink. There is nothing to drink. Whatever water their tissues locked in at the beginning is a hoard, and they spend it so slowly that a life can finish without a visible thirst.

The older ones have eaten their own color. Each generation of film they process leaves a little of itself in the shell—iron, a dark oxide, a glassing of salt—so that age is weight and ring and a dull metal sheen that takes the seam-light and does not give it back as brightness. Strike two of them together, as they do when the mat tightens, and the sound is not flesh. It is grit and coin. Their legs are many and short, built for surfaces that powder under a grip and for surfaces that refuse one. The claws are the mill’s smaller tools: they score, they anchor, they pass grains backward along the body to animals waiting in the layer beneath, a handoff so constant it looks like a current in the mat. Nothing is stored in a larder. The larder is the wall, and it replenishes on a clock none of them outlive.

They have no eyes that make pictures. A pale band runs the forward rim of each shell, and it is all heat: a blunt knowledge of which way the stone is newer, which crack has just spent its warmth, where a neighbor’s gut-ember is idling. The finer sense is electrical. The dust carries charge; the moving mat stirs it; the seams leak a faint wrongness into the air. They turn into that the way a burrower turns into firmer ground. Navigation is a matter of gradients. A body that drifts out of the mat’s field hesitates, rotates, and is pulled back not by loyalty but by the sudden poverty of signal, the way a hand jerks from a surface that has gone abruptly smooth.

The wings are a lie if you think of leaving. They are short, stiff, unfit for the open air this chamber does not have, and they are used as clappers. The industrial drone is not only labor. Under the scraping there is a phase, a lock, a mutual ticking by which a stretch of mat keeps time with itself. When the rock checks—a bright small crack, a slip of grit—the nearest clappers stop for less than a breath, and the stop travels. The whole chamber listens with its mouths still working. Then the rhythm returns, sometimes a fraction tighter, as if the new fissure has been entered into the work order. They do not investigate in the way a creature with a head investigates. The mat simply becomes denser along the fresh face, because that is where the film will be thinnest and, soon, richest.

For a long time this seems to be all of it: a single appetite distributed through thousands of hard small engines, a metabolism that looks like industry because it does not pause for night. Then the margin declares itself.

There is a place they do not cover. It is not large. A hand’s width, sometimes less, following the dullest red seam, where the rock is newest and the film is still more vapor than crust. Around it the mat keeps a clean edge, not a scared edge—no rearing, no agitated clatter—just a worked boundary, maintained the way a path is maintained, by countless small refusals to step over. At first this reads as caution. Heat that would warp a pale shell. Chemistry too sharp to plate. Stand with that explanation and it holds, and then it doesn’t, because the boundary is tended from both sides.

In the seam-margin the life has stopped being many. Or it has stopped being separable. A few bodies—older than the plated masses, darker, so mineral they barely tick when they shift—have ceased to travel. Their legs have lengthened into the sugary grain and gone rigid, not broken, invested. Shell fused to shell along the contacts until the seams between animals are readable only as faint ridges, like cooling-cracks in glass. What remains of their mouthparts works inward, into the softening face, not outward into the film the others harvest. They are still alive in the only sense that matters here: the gut-ember has not gone out. It has spread. A low warmth occupies the fused mass at a steadier burn than any single body could hold, and the mat at the boundary presses close enough to exchange without crossing. Grains move inward. A finer, altered dust moves outward, and the ordinary feeders take it with a speed that is not the speed they give the common film. The margin is a mouth belonging to something that used to be a crowd.

This is the part that will not settle into the first image. The small hard engines are not the inhabitants with a curiosity living among them. They are the mobile tissue. The thing that lives in the chamber, in the older sense of living somewhere—of holding a place, of being the reason the place has an inside—is the fused work along the seam, and it is still becoming. It does not have a face. It has a direction: down, into rock that has only recently stopped being softer than this, and outward through the mat, which brings it what the walls shed and carries off what it cannot use. When the mountain’s heat shifts, the fused mass shifts its burn to follow, and the boundary walks with it, a slow migration measured in cracks, not in steps. Generations of the pale ones plate, darken, and are spent in that service. Some, at the edge, stop traveling and are taken into the ridge of shells. The conversion is quiet. A clapper finds the new interval, locks, and is not heard as separate again.

Nothing here is waiting to become a swarm in a valley, or a bat, or a story with an exit. The wings will not open onto weather. If the blister closes—and the shove that opened it has not finished deciding—the mat will compress, the margin will narrow, and the fused burn will either keep a thread of seam or go out with the film. They have no plan for that. Planning would require a future different in kind from the present, and the present is already a full occupation: heat, oxide, charge, the tick of shells, the mill of mouthparts, the unhurried welding of bodies into a hearth that is not yet stone and no longer only animal.

Call it one life if you need a number. It will not agree with you. It will go on dividing and joining under the red stain of the seam, loud as a works, patient as the ridge, making more of itself out of a mountain that is still arriving.
