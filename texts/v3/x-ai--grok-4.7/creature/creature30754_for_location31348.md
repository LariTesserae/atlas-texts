They are not a people who arrived and then learned the bowl. The posture gives them away before the faces do. A stranger coming down the cut in the ridge walks as if the ground were finished, and has to correct, step by step, when it yields and answers back. The people of the bowl already know the answer. Their weight sits a little forward, knees soft, feet placed where the root-mat has been walked into a shine, and they do not look down to place them. Children learn this before they learn not to run into carrying-poles. An old man crossing a swollen rill does not hop; he waits half a breath for the board that is not quite a bridge to take his first foot, then gives it the rest of him as if he and the board had agreed on it years ago.

They are short-legged for the work, or the work has made them seem so: wide in the foot, thick through the calf, shoulders rounded from loads carried on a pole or a tumpline rather than in the arms. Hands are the other map. The skin of the palms is thickened in pads that match the cane—splitting, weaving, the turn of a lashing—and the nails are kept short because a long nail is a tear waiting in wet fiber. Many of the older hands have a permanent rosiness, not from cold exactly but from never quite drying, and a few are missing a joint to an infection that the damp would not let close, bound now in a scrap of wool worn smooth. Faces are windless faces. The rim keeps the hard weather off them, so what marks them is water and green shade: fine lines at the eyes from squinting into steam and into the bright lip of the falls, a sallowness under the color that clears when someone spends a day on the drying-terrace and comes down looking briefly borrowed from somewhere else. Hair is worn tied, braided, or cut to the neck, because loose hair in the slots catches on living walls and comes back full of midges. They oil it with something rendered from the goats, and in a crowd the smell of that oil sits under the wet wool and the leaf-rot like a signature.

Clothing is wool, because wool still works when it is damp, and everything here is damp. Undyed mostly, the color of the animals, with a few threads of a rust-brown dye that someone still bothers to make from a bark that grows only on the rim-rocks. Hems are short. A long hem in the lower town is a wick. Cloaks exist for the rare climb to the ridge and are otherwise pegged inside doorways, growing a little mold along the fold that nobody treats as a crisis. Feet are bare on the slabs and in the shallows, or wrapped in a soft hide sandal that is really just a sole and a tie, kicked off at any threshold because thresholds are already wet and the sandal would only make them wetter. Children go bare to the waist until the age when they start carrying, and then they look, abruptly, like smaller copies of the people they trail.

They do not have a word that separates house from household from the stand of cane it is made of. You ask where someone lives and you are pointed at a thickness in the green with a door-slit and a name, and the name is often the same as the lane, which is often the same as the person who last re-set the seep-channel there. Ownership, if that is the word, is the right to cut and the duty to replant, and it is argued constantly in a tone that outsiders would call furious and locals would call maintenance. Two women in the middle band can spend a morning shouting across a slot about whose roots crossed whose water, and part at the end of it to share a fish, because the shouting was the work and the fish is also the work. Disputes that cannot be shouted into shape go to whoever last cleared the main channel in that ward—not a chief, not quite an elder, just the person whose hands are currently on the town’s throat. The office moves. It is resented and sought in the same season.

Names are short, and there are not enough of them. You are distinguished by the lane, by the parent still living, by a scar, by the animal that follows you, by the mistake you are still known for. A man can be Three-Rill, or Maren’s-by-the-goats, or The-one-who-cut-early, and answer to all of them in a day without feeling that any of them is less his. Children are not named for weeks. The bowl has opinions about infants, and people wait to see if the opinion is favorable: the cough that the damp gives, the fever, the simple failure to feed. Those who stay are named for something that happened in the first month—a bird that nested in the door-pole, a flood that stopped at the threshold—and the name is allowed to be ugly if the thing was ugly. Survival is not prettied.

Work is not divided into the dignified and the not. Everyone clears. Everyone carries. The differences are in what else a body has become good at, and those differences are spoken of the way you would speak of a tool’s balance. There are splitters, who can open a cane pole with one draw and not spiral the cut. There are water-readers, usually older, who walk the seams at dawn and can say which channel will rise by evening from the sound in the rock, a skill no one has written down because no one writes. There are people the goats prefer, and this is treated as a trade. There are dyers, few, their forearms permanently barked. There are those who will go over the lip on a rope of root and hide to cut timber the bowl cannot grow, and they are paid in drying-space and in a certain silence, because the lip is where the town’s water becomes someone else’s weather, and not everyone wants a reminder that there is a below. Speech itself has a trade in it: callers, who can pitch a voice under the falls and be understood three lanes off, are borrowed for messages the way a mallet is borrowed.

They eat what the water and the rim agree to. Fish from the lower pools and from the fan of falls, dried on the upper racks until it is a board you can break with your teeth. Goat, rarely, and then all at once, a whole street smelling of it, the fat rubbed into hair and into lashings. Duck eggs. A bitter green from the lip-gardens, boiled until it gives up. Milk, thin, soured on purpose so it will keep through a warm stretch of days that is not actually warm. Grain is a visitor. When a party comes down the ridge cut with sacks, the upper terrace changes sound: more bargaining, more laughter of the kind that wants to be overheard, and for a week the gruel has a body to it that people comment on as if commenting were tasting. Honey happens, from a bee that nests in the rim-rock, stolen at a cost of swellings that are compared afterward with competitive calm. Salt is the true foreigner. A pinch of it is a gift that ends an argument or starts a marriage negotiation, and there are houses where a little leather bag of it has sat in the driest fork of the door-pole for longer than the children can remember, touched only to be sure it is still there.

They are loud at close range and quiet about the far. A conversation is conducted at a distance of a forearm, because the falls and the frogs and the goats will take anything softer, and so intimacy and business share a volume. What they do not do is speculate, much, about the country under the lip or the country past the ridge. They know both exist. Traders prove the ridge. The falls prove the below. But the bowl’s business is continuity of a degree that leaves little over for elsewhere, and elsewhere has a way of arriving already translated into sacks, rumors, and a cough no one in the wards recognizes. When sickness of that kind comes, they do what they do with a failed house: cut it back. The household is moved to the drying-terrace, which is drier and more public, and the living walls of their plot are opened to the air and the goats, and people bring fish and do not bring their own children, and the name of the sickness is refused as if naming it would give it a lane. Sometimes this works. The times it does not are remembered as years rather than as stories.

They play, because the body that only works in this damp seizes. In the last light, when the rim is gold and the lanes are already dark, the lower pools fill with swimmers who are not exactly swimming—holding the bottom, chasing a duck, arguing underwater with their eyes open in the peat-colored murk. Someone has a flute cut from a failed pole, three notes, and plays it badly on purpose until a child takes it and plays it worse. Wrestling happens in the slots, which makes it mostly a contest of who can brace against a living wall without breaking the living wall, an embarrassment that ends in laughter and a new lashing where a pole gave. Courtship is public because privacy is a wet fiction. Pairs walk the circuit of the rim path at the hour when the goats are coming down, and the town, without looking, counts the circuits. A third circuit is a statement. A stopped circuit, the two of them sitting on the dressed stone the roots have swallowed, is a decision, and by morning someone has already begun training a new door-pole at the edge of one or the other’s plot.

They are afraid of three things, and only one of them is spoken. The spoken one is a blocking of the main channel: a fall of rock from the seep-face, a refusal of the water to leave, the bowl filling not to a finger but to a knee, to a chest, to the doors. Drills are not drills; they are the ordinary clearing done a little faster when the rain changes its note, and children are taught the high paths the way other children are taught a prayer, by repetition until the feet know. The second fear is quieter: a season in which the cane does not take. A wall harvested and not replaced is a gap, and a gap is wind, and wind is the one visitor the rim was built to refuse. They do not have a ceremony for this. They have a habit of touching a new shoot when they pass it, a thumb to the node, which a stranger would miss and a local would deny and everyone does. The third fear is not a fear they would grant you the word for. It is the knowledge, sitting under the skill and the shouting and the shared fish, that the bowl is a mouth and they are what it has agreed to hold, and that the agreement is old, specific, and not written anywhere they could appeal to. They do not worship the rock. They keep it clear. They do not worship the cane. They replant it. They speak of the water as they speak of a powerful neighbor whose moods you learn because you share a wall. In the dark, when the frogs have taken the lower town and the falls have thickened, a person lying on a mat that is itself a mat of split cane can feel the seep traveling under the floor, a long slow pulse, and will shift in their sleep to stay in time with it, and call that nothing, and be right that it is nothing, and also be kept by it.

They bury in the rim, in cracks that are not quite graves, stopped with stones, high enough that the bowl’s water will not go asking. The body is wrapped in wool that will not be wasted longer than it must; after a season the wool is taken back, washed in the falls until it is only wool again, and returned to use. What stays in the rock is bone and a name that is already being worn by a cousin. Mourning is a lane gone quiet for a day, which in this town is a sound you can hear from the lip, a slot of stillness in the roar, and then the clearing resumes, because the channel does not keep the dead’s schedule. People say the dead like the height. They say it without conviction and without irony, the way they say the goats will come down when the light leaves the rim: not as a belief they are prepared to defend, but as the sentence that lets the work continue.

At full dark the bowl does not sleep so much as change its crew. Humans go in behind the living walls, and the walls tick as cane cools, and the water sounds nearer. Outside, the deer comes, and the rats, and the birds that hunt the rats, and the frogs say their structural thing, and the town’s dogs answer or do not. Inside, in the green-black, a household is a cluster of breath and wool and the small heat of bodies that have been wet since morning. Someone is always awake a little. A cough. A hand on a child’s back. The mental count of the channels, done the way other people count money, and then the slide back under, into the pulse in the floor. By the time the rim finds the first gray, they are already up, already in the lanes, already answering the ground. The place does not rise to meet them. They were never separate enough from it for rising to be required.
