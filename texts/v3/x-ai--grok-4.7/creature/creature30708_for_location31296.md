You notice the tenancy before you can say what holds it. Crew-prints close. These do not. Along the inner face of the north rim there is a run of damp at shoulder height where the casting has been leaned against so often that the weep-water parts around a faint oil of skin and finds its way down on either side, two clean threads, rejoining only at the floor. In the southeast thicket the jointed stems have been parted and have grown back as a corridor, not a trail: the passage is too narrow for a turned shoulder, and the stems at the entrance have learned to bow inward again after something passes, so that from the open floor it still looks like unbroken rank. The fern-mat at the end of it is pressed into a length that matches a body sleeping on its side, knees drawn, one arm up. It is not a nest built. It is a shape arrived at by returning.

For a while that is all you can be sure of, and the place is content to be read that way—as a set of preferences, a warm spot, a passage the garden consents to keep. Then the schedule gives the tenant away.

When a vent breathes and the table of fog forms at the northwest corner, something is already standing in the place the fog will occupy, as if the knock of the valve were a bell it has lived beside long enough to hear in the bones. It does not flee the fog. It lets the saturated air close over it and drinks, or seems to; the fog fails there first, a hole the size of a torso opening in the white while the rest of the bank still drifts. When the hole clears you have a body and then, if you have come at the wrong minute, you have only leaves again, because it steps sideways into the strap-growth with a movement so unhurried it does not trip the drip-line. The slap of condensate continues. The thicket does not advertise.

What you have seen, in the instant the fog fails, is not yet a settled thing. It reads first as equipment that has been left: a dull coverall the color of fiber lagging, darkened at the knees and the cuffs, a hood down, the fabric so rinded with the white feather of new mineral that it might be a spare gasket grown large. Then the coverall bends. A hand comes up—ordinary, five-fingered, the nails rimmed with the same chalk that comes off on a thumb—and wipes the mouth, and the wipe leaves a clean crescent that films over again before the next vent cycle. The face under the hood is a person's. The person has not been a visitor in some time.

They are not tall. The corridor would not allow it. They are made of the same wet warmth as the bay, so that standing still they do not steam, and moving they do not cool. The thin air beyond the rim is not a climate they enter. When they go to the rim at all it is to look down the drop the way someone looks at a road they have already decided against, and the brightness off the ordinary sky hurts them; you can see it in the squint, and in the way they turn the hood to cut the light, and in how quickly they come back to the pipe. Their hair, cut short enough to dry between cycles, carries a crust at the roots by afternoon. They brush it off against a wrist. The crust returns. They have stopped treating this as a defect.

The coverall was issued. A tab at the collar still shows half a size-mark and the ghost of a name written in the kind of marker that runs in condensate; they have not renewed it, and the letters are a gray suggestion, not readable, not mourned. Under it they wear whatever has lasted: a shirt gone soft, trousers rolled, nothing that flashes. Boots were abandoned early. Bare feet know the crust better—where it will sigh and flood, where a new skin of mineral has locked yesterday's litter into a slick. The soles have thickened. Not into hoof or callus of the ordinary kind, but into a paler set-layer, the same pallor as the surface that skinned before the body of the pour did, flexible, faintly warm, patterned on the underside with the swirl of whatever they stood in while it took. They walk on it without flinching. It is still their foot. It is also, already, a little of the floor.

They drink where the sheet stands thickest, kneeling, not cupped in the hands but lowered until the mouth meets the film, the way the roots meet it. The water prefers them almost as it prefers the felt. A thread will change course to reach the wrist they have set down for balance. They allow this. They have learned which pools taste of the sugar-breath of the open brackets and which taste only of salts and hot lagging, and they alternate, not from pleasure so much as from a sense that the bay has more than one kind of thirst and that matching it keeps the stomach quiet. Food is the other problem the place solved without being asked. The flower-brackets, shut, can be pinched free. Inside is a grain of paste, hot-sugar and mineral, barely a mouthful, and taking one does not seem to rob the cycle; another bracket opens in the gap before the lines ease. They eat slowly, standing, looking at nothing in particular. They do not hunt the mantis. The mantis sometimes climbs their sleeve during the vent-wait and stands at the shoulder with its forelimbs folded, patient, and they tolerate the small weight the way the pipe tolerates fern. When they shift, it shifts. Neither of them has improved the other into a companion. They share a height and a pause.

In the culvert they have an understanding that is older than the corridor and less visible. The first nights—if there were first nights; they do not mark them—they slept farther out, and the thing in the manifold-water came up as far as the lagging and rested its length along the outside of their calf, rubber-warm, not biting, not quite touching, a pressure through the film. They did not kick. After that it kept to the culvert but moved when they passed, a long flex under the surface, acknowledgment between residents of the same heat. They leave it the southwest corner at the hour the engine turns hardest. In return, on the mornings the sheet runs thin, a single fat bubble rises at the manifold lip and breaks with a residue they can lift on a finger and eat. They do not know what it is. They know it does not sicken them anymore, if it ever did. The not-knowing has become a kind of manners.

What they do all day is less like work than like the work's refusal to end. They recut the scratches on the inner southwest wall when the rind closes over them, not to keep a count—though the marks are in groups, valve-knocks, days, something—but because a closed scratch stops being a scratch and they are not ready for the wall to be finished in that spot. They turn the spare gasket by the rim, the one with the fern collar, so that a new face weathers, a small husbandry with no yield. They clear one industrial gutter with the side of the hand, and by the next fog it is felted again, and they clear it again, not in hope of drainage but because their hand has learned the radius. The work order under the scratched pane they do not try to save. They read it anyway, the running ink, the words that still hold: curing, interim, to be struck. Their mouth moves on interim. It is not devotion and not irony. It is the last legible sentence in a room that has otherwise given up language, and speaking it keeps a hinge in them from setting.

They were sent, or they stayed after being sent, or the difference ceased to matter when the test kept coming back unfinished and the shift that was meant to strike the bay was postponed and then forgotten and then contradicted by the bay itself. They remember the outside as a set of procedures: a ladder, a checklist, the bright edge in the chest after the climb, someone saying the pour would be cool enough to cut by Thursday. Thursday is not a unit the enclosure uses. They remember a name for themselves and do not use it; the marker ran, and replacing it would be a claim the rind would only overwrite. What remains of the errand is a habit of inspection. They press a thumb to the shaded faces. They note where the crust gives. They follow a new seam with a nail and find the root already in it, and they leave both where they are. Striking the bay is no longer a task they can imagine completing. Inhabiting it is not a decision they can remember making. Between those two absences they have a life, narrow as the corridor, paced by knocks and slaps and the soft successive click of brackets closing when the lines ease.

At night the casting contracts and ticks, and they lie in the fern-shape and tick with it—jaw, the small bones of the feet, a shiver that is not cold. Their skin, where the coverall does not cover, fats with droplets that travel upward along the hair of the arm and break against the stems. Sleep is brief and scheduled, a gap between one vent-round and the next, deep enough that the mantis can cross their throat without waking them and light enough that the engine's change of pitch brings them up before the fog arrives. They dream, if they dream, in mineral: surfaces skinning over, a ladder they cannot find the feet of, a voice reading the work order correctly, every word, including the ones the water has taken. They wake with the chalk on their lips and do not wipe it until the bay has had its drink of them.

They are not becoming the floor. That would be a simpler story than the one the bay is telling. The floor is younger than its garden; they are older than their errand and younger than their feet. Inclusion, not replacement. A spare life, as the gasket is a spare part, acquired by being set down and not retrieved, and now no longer spare in any way that matters. If a crew came with saws and a true intention to strike, they would hear the ladder on the rim and go into the culvert-water as far as the lungs allowed, and the thing there would make room, and the thicket would close the corridor, and the lean-mark on the north wall would be only a roughness, easily read as a flaw in the pour. They have practiced this without calling it practice. No crew has come with that intention. The pane still says interim. The tenant has outlasted the word.

Stand in the middle at the hour the lines come up and the brackets open and the sugar-smell lifts. You may see them at the pipe, hood back, face turned to the lattice-light, one hand on the lagging not for warmth but for the hum that is more feeling than sound. The pale hunter is a small folded figure at the wrist. Their eyes are open and not on you, even if you are the sort of arrival the place has not had. They are listening for the knock. When it comes they will move, unhurried, into the spot the fog is about to claim, and the bank will fail around a body that knows exactly how long it has, and the slap of a dropped droplet will mark the moment, and the tenant will be, for that breath, the clearest thing in the enclosure: wet, included, unfinished, and already keeping the only time the place believes in.
