What lives there is not a population in the ordinary sense, and it is not nothing. It has no border you can walk around. If you try to count it you are already using the wrong unit, because most of it is not gathered into bodies, and the part that is gathered into bodies does not breed a surplus and does not leave a gap when one of the bodies is still. The correct unit, if there is one, is the reach itself: the same closed accounting that keeps the crust from thickening and the pools from filling. Life here is one of the terms that already balanced, and it stays solved.

The first thing you will find, if you look where the water is warmest and least reflective, is the hand-sized thing the color of sinter. It sits against the bottom as if it had been set there by the same pour that set the floor. Its margin is a body only when the light strikes at a shallow angle and the edge lifts a little, a soft rind over a harder interior, the way a deposit of mineral can be both. It does not turn toward you. Turning would require a direction the pool does not have. It feeds by contact. Under it the mineral skin is thinner by the width of a film, and the film is being replaced at the rate it is taken, so the pasture never shows a bite. You can watch for as long as the fixed light will let you and not see a mouth. There may not be one. The underside is a field of slow adhesion, and whatever it takes up is already dissolved, already the temperature of the water, already the color that makes the body hard to see.

They are not the same, once you have seen more than one. The differences are small and permanent. One has a ridge along one side that fits a crack in the pool floor and will fit it as long as the crack remains, which is to say as long as the place does. Another is thicker in the middle, almost a dome, and the dome throws the same small shadow every hour of the unmoving day, a dark coin on the tea-colored bottom. A third, in a pool nearer a vent, carries a dusting of pale fallout on its upper surface and does not shed it. The dust is not decoration. It is part of the level of that pool: the same grains land, the same grains are cemented, the same grains are lost to the trickle at the outlet, and the body holds the middle of that transaction without ever becoming a mound. None of them move while you watch. If you come back—if “back” means anything under a sun that has not traveled—the ridge-fit one is still fitted, the dome still coins its shadow, the dusted one still wears the weather of its vent. They are not paused. A paused animal is between acts. These have finished arriving at their act, and the act is continuous, and the continuous has no phases you can name.

What they are made of is not flesh in any way that would rot if the balance failed. Cut one—and the thought of cutting arrives because the place is so like a works, and works invite sampling—and the interior is a dense, warm, faintly conductive gel, threaded with darker mineral the color of the wet iron pan. It does not bleed in a pulse. A slow clear seep meets the pool and is indistinguishable from the pool. The cut margin begins, at once, to be the next surface the gel can have. Whether that is healing or merely the same matched rate the crust uses when a plate is lifted, you will not be able to say. The distinction the place does not keep is the distinction between a wound and a joint. There is no hurry in the closure. There is no hurry in anything. By the time the margin has returned to the color of sinter, you will have forgotten the exact line, the way you forget which bead of condensation is the one you touched.

They do not meet. Pools that hold one do not hold two, not because of a territory you could map with threat, but because the film each one takes is the film the pool can spare while remaining the pool. A second body would be an unsolved term. You do not find the evidence of an attempt: no scar, no displaced grit in the shape of a contest, no smaller body starved at the margin. The accounting does not try and fail. It does not include the second. Where a pool is larger, the body is larger, still one, still the size the water can carry without a change in level. Scaled across the reach they repeat the way the vents repeat: not a herd, a spacing. Your feet learn it. After a day of walking—a day measured by your knees and not by the light—you begin to expect the next one at the next warm hollow, and the expectation is usually wrong, and when it is right the rightness feels like the ground’s, not yours.

There is another life, and it is harder to grant the name, because it has even less of a body. In the loud stacks, the hollow cemented chimneys that roar on a period you can time, the vapor is not empty. Hold a hand in the sheet—not long; the heat is the heat of a pipe that has always been hot—and the wet that condenses on your skin carries a slickness that is not only mineral. It smells faintly of the boiler and faintly of something sweeter, a sugar of stone, gone as soon as the air takes it. On the inner wall of a stack, where you can see in by lying on the crust and accepting the roar in your skull, the surface is not the same all the way down. Bands of a darker, glassier deposit ring the throat at intervals, each band the width of a finger, each band alive in the only way the place allows: it thickens on the windward side of the vapor and thins on the lee at the same rate, so the ring never narrows the throat and never widens it. It is a liner. It is also a mouth, if a mouth can be a circumference that never closes and never opens further. What it takes is whatever the vapor brings that is not the vapor itself—the finest fallout, the dissolved edge of the sinter, a metal the seam-current has put into the air as a taste. What it gives back is the roar’s steadiness. A stack without its rings would change its note. None of them change their note. The liner is the reason, or one of the reasons, and it has been the reason for as long as the stacks have had the height they mean to keep.

If you listen inside the period of a single stack, there is a softer click beneath the roar, regular, not mechanical in the sense of a part, mechanical in the sense of a stress arriving and leaving on schedule. That click is the liner adjusting by a thickness too small to see, taking up and laying down, the drive-belt of its own metabolism locked to the temperature the vent has chosen and will not revise. It does not know the hand-sized bodies in the pools. Nothing here knows anything else in the way that would require a message. The shared condition is enough. Both are solved terms. Both are warm. Both refuse a surplus.

The electrical seams have their own tenant, and this one is the easiest to mistake for a trick of the crust. Where the iron bites and the crackle runs steady, a dark thread lies in the wet of the seam, no thicker than a root, branching and rejoining in a net that follows the current and does not extend past the place where a wet finger would taste ozone. It is not a root. Lift a little of it—it comes up with the damp, not with a tear—and it is cool on one side and warm on the other, and the cool side is the side the current prefers, or the side that prefers the current; you cannot separate the preference from the path. Laid back, it resumes the net. It does not seek. Seeking would be a change of state, and the seam is already in its state. The thread eats the difference: the small fall from stressed crust to unstressed, the little voltage that never becomes a storm. In return it keeps the crackle from wandering. A seam without it would still bite, but the bite would drift, and drifting is not what this country does. So the thread is infrastructure in the same impolite way the pools are infrastructure, maintained by being what they are.

None of these—the pool bodies, the stack liners, the seam-threads—are the whole of what lives. The rest is thinner still, and you will deny it longer, because it has no edge to disappoint you with. In the permanent damp under the curled shelves, where the beads gather to the same weight and do not fall, there is a film on the mineral skin that is not the mineral. It does not green. It does not bloom. It takes the light at a different dullness, a half-tone lower than the crust, and only in the shadow, so that the sunlit tops of the shelves look empty and the undersides look occupied without ever looking inhabited. Breathe close and the air is the same warm pipe. The occupation is a staining that cycles: takes a little carbonate, gives a little carbonate, holds the bead’s chemistry at the point where the bead can persist as a bead. It is gardening of a sort, if gardening can mean refusing the next stage. This is why the damp pockets do not become marshes and the marshes do not become stands. The film is the refusal, distributed, unambitious, total. It does not want the country to stay as it is. Want is the wrong energy. It is the country staying, expressed as a stain in the shade.

You can live beside all of this and not be addressed. Nothing turns. Nothing startles. The industrial resemblance continues into the life and then stops, because a works has operators and a shift and a product that leaves. Here the operators are the process, the shift does not end, and the product is the same surface you are standing on, taken back as it is laid. If there is a consciousness in any of it, it is not the kind that collects a view of you and keeps it. The pool body does not store the shadow of your hand. The liner does not alter its click when the roar is joined by your pulse, loud in your own ear. You are a temporary term the accounting does not need to solve, because you do not stay at a rate that would unbalance a pool, and if you did—if you drank a hollow down, if you broke the film under every shelf—the place would begin, at the rate of the balance, to make your absence into the next correct surface, and the life would be in that making without ever having noticed the interruption as an enemy.

What lives there, then, is a set of closures. Bodies where the water can spare a film. Liners where a throat must keep its note. Threads where a current must not wander. A stain where a bead must not fall into a future. All of it rare, spaced, ungreen, warm, and finished in the way the shadows are finished: not waiting to become more, not remembering having been less, present at the volume the ground has settled on. If you stay long enough that your own hunger starts to invent a next stage for them—a mating, a season, a migration across the pans toward a wetter year—you will be describing a country this one does not touch. They have no year. They have the interval between one click and the next, and the interval is always the same length, and inside it they are entirely alive.
