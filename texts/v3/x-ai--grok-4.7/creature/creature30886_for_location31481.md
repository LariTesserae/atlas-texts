They are never all in one place at once, and they do not announce themselves as a people. You notice them first as absences in the weed: a lane parted and held open longer than a fish needs, a mat of milfoil folded back and weighted with stones so a skiff can lie in it without being combed away. Then a roof that was only tin shows a line of drying nets, and a figure is already down at the water before the figure has quite resolved out of the scrub.

They live on the knolls and the short stone causeways, never more than a few to a rise, and the rises themselves shift with the breathing of the water. A house is often two things at once: a room of boards and salvaged plate above the flood line, and a lower storey that is allowed to fill, where tools and smoked fish hang from the joists and the door is a hatch you climb through when the shelf is high. Paint is the same color as the stone after a season. They do not fight that. What they keep bright is metal that must turn—thole pins, the lips of bailers, the small brass of a lamp—and the pale inner wood of a hull, because a dark boat disappears against the weed and is run down by the next squall’s chop.

Their boats are short and double-ended, more tray than ship, with a scull over the stern and a pole for the mats. In the lanes between stands of pondweed they move by listening: the stem-tick changes pitch where the bed shallows, and a wrong note means stone. Children learn the note before they learn the words for depth. Adults still misjudge it when the turbines downstream are drawing hard and the whole sea sits an inch lower than the morning’s memory. Then a keel kisses the charophyte crust and the sound goes through the boat into the knees, a soft reprimand, and everyone shifts weight without speaking.

They fish the way the water allows, which is mostly by waiting inside the plant-life rather than above it. Wing-nets and long-handled scoops take the whitefish where a current needles through a gap in the milfoil; the fry-clouds they leave alone except in the one month when the birds have already had their glut and the smokehouses can use the surplus. Grebes are not quarry. Gulls are a nuisance and a calendar—when the gulls leave a slick, the upwelling has moved, and the boats follow the birds rather than the chart. Otters are neighbors with a treaty no one wrote down: a dock left clear, a fish head set on a flat stone, and in return the mooring lines are not chewed through more than once a season. Break the custom and the lines go, and also the caddis cases on your steps get kicked into the drink by small wet hands that are not quite hands. People talk about that without smiling.

The engines are theirs in the way weather is theirs. A few households keep the turbine hall and the pump houses, not as owners but as the ones who still know which belt sings before it breaks and how to clear a intake screen when the weed rafts down on a northeast blow. The work is loud and then suddenly intimate: inside the hall the pour at the lip is a body-sound, and speech is done with the hands. Outside, the same people are quiet again, poling home in the last light with grease to the elbow and a string of fish they did not take from the company’s water, because there is no company left, only the drop, which keeps its promise whether anyone is paid or not. They let the stack rust. They do not let the bearings run dry. When a gantry leg works loose in the slap, someone is under it at dawn with a wedge of shelf-stone and a patience that looks like sullenness from the far islet.

Speech among them is short and angled, full of directions borrowed from water. *Lee of the third knoll. Where the two currents argue. After the second squall, not the first.* They have names for the standing mist at the lip and for the particular hammered flat that follows hard rain, and they have a word for the minute at the hinge of the day when the small sounds come clear—drip, stem-tick, a fish turning—and another word for the feeling of having missed it. Strangers are not driven off; they are simply not explained to, and the lanes through the weed are confusing enough that most boats from lower country turn back before the first causeway, convinced the sea is shallower than it is, or deeper.

At night the knolls prick with small lamps, and the lamps do not try to outshine the water. Someone is always awake when the level is rising, because a causeway can go from dry to ankle to knee between one check and the next, and a child sleeping in the lower storey has to be lifted while still asleep, passed up through the hatch like a net, and settled again without the dream breaking. The birds shift and resettle. The engines keep their stroke. Rain walks the shelf and arrives as noise before it arrives as water, and under the roofs the people lie listening to both, counting, already knowing which boats will need bailing at first light and which weed-lanes will have closed, and which will have opened where no one has poled before. They do not call any of this waiting. It is only the way the floor of the world still moves, and the way they have learned to move with it, low, and never for long in a straight line.
