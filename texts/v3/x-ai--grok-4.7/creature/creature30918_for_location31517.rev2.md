The shelf is a small, sunlit plane of warm, anciently polished stone perched high, with ordinary light, local condensation, and a drop on one side. Life gathers only where damp lingers: lush mats and a few taller plants, while most of the stone stays bare. Near the wall sits a half-swallowed scatter of arc-like remains, one piece upright and faintly lit from within, humming at a pitch you feel more than hear. Warmth and damp seem to begin there. Animals mostly pass through rather than stay: odd insects, browsers, long-legged drinkers, noisy fliers, a quick hunter, and something large that patrols the outside of the lip but does not come in. Distance feels slightly stretched away from the scatter and shortened toward it. Water vanishes quickly except in seams and one shallow bowl. Nothing nests in the open, and the noise never settles into a message — it is simply many processes happening at once on stone that could not quite wear the machine away. I will describe whoever or whatever lives there. I will let the picture deepen instead of locking it early. What lives here is not a population you can census. It is a standing condition that has acquired bodies, and the bodies keep correcting one another. The shelf looks empty the way a held note looks empty: the loud part is the air, and the stone is only the instrument. Everything that remains for more than a breath has made a treaty with the hum, and everything that has not is already leaving. Start with what does not look alive, because the rest of the pocket takes its measure from it. The upright piece is shoulder-high and does not cast colored light, only a slow interior brightness that moves in veins the way a pulse moves under thin skin. It is not metal and not glass. The surface reads as stone that has been convinced, in a narrow zone, to remember a different grain. The three low arcs half-swallowed by the floor are the same material at different stages of being accepted back. One is little more than a bright seam in the polish. One still holds a curve you could rest a hand along, warm, slightly soft at the edge as if the shelf were eating it the way a wound closes on a splinter it cannot push out. The third flickers. Not with light — with presence. For a moment it is there as a solid lip; then it is a suggestion of a lip, and a small flier that was about to land has already landed on the stone where the lip was, and does not seem to have fallen. The hum lives in all four, but only the upright piece speaks it into the teeth. The others answer in warmth and in the way nearby leaves lean, correct, lean again, as if the arcs were a sun with a bad sense of direction. What attends this scatter is not a colony in the way of a hive. It is a circulation. Around the upright piece a ragged column of small fliers turns all day and does not stop at night, only tightens. They drink condensation off the arcs. Some of them pass into a vein and come out elsewhere on the arc, a gap in the path no longer than a blink, uninjured, unstartled. After enough of these passages you begin to notice that the column is not a crowd of individuals so much as a single habit wearing many bodies. A flier that exits a vein is not always the flier that entered it, if “same” means anything here. The wing-count is stable. The gaps are stable. The particular dust-colored body is not. They do not seem to notice the substitution. The shelf does not seem to notice it either, except that the glaze at the base of the upright piece is faintly thicker in the morning, as if each blink-passage paid a little stone into the machine. Beneath the column, in the permanent damp, a finer dust of life rises and settles with the brief rains. It is not insects in any ordinary sense. It is a glitter that organizes when the sheet of uncaused rain stands up in the middle of the shelf, and disorganizes when the rain stops. During the minute the rain lasts, the glitter draws itself into short threads that lean toward the arcs and then break. You can walk through it. It does not land on skin. It lands on the warmth, which is to say it lands on the idea of the device, and where the device’s influence thins — out toward the drop, up the wall — the glitter will not go. It is the youngest thing here, or the most continuously replaced. It has no nest. It has a preference. The green keeps a slower population. The mats are so overlapped they look poured, and under them a many-legged browser the color of wet bark eats only the red new growth of the one shrub that has managed to climb. It does not eat the smoked-paper bark. It does not eat the brass-colored stems. It is exact in a way that suggests the exactness is not its own. Leave a fallen red shoot on the open polish and the browser will not cross to it. Drag the shoot back into the island’s shade and the eating resumes, small and steady, a sound like paper being worried by a thumb. There are not many of these browsers. There do not need to be. The shrub produces red at a rate that matches them, and the matching is too clean to be a season. The vine with the rope-thick stem is the loud plant. It clatters. By day the clatter is a chorus that will not resolve into single voices; by night, when the many-winged flight folds into it, the clatter becomes one kept beat, like a sleeper counting. The vine is not counting. Something in it is. If you part the leaves — they are broad, slightly warm, and they resist being parted, not with strength but with a preference to fall back closed — the beat continues in the dark of the interior, wooden, impatient, and you do not find the instrument. You find ordinary plant. The rhythm is a property the vine holds the way the stone holds warmth: a hand’s width under the surface, older than the growth that expresses it. The small flowers in the mats open and shut on a cycle shorter than an afternoon. They do not open for light. They open when the hum drops or lifts by the smallest amount, which happens when a large body crosses the shelf or when the outside bulk at the lip comes close enough to ring the thin stone. A flower that has just shut looks no different from a bud. A flower that has just opened smells, briefly, of that sweetness with no source — the smell of the hum — and then smells of crushed green again. Insects that are not the column-fliers visit them in the minute they are open and are gone when they shut. Nothing here feeds casually. Every appetite is fitted to a window the pocket itself opens and closes. On the bare stone the long-legged drinkers are the only residents that refuse cover. Three or four, never closer than the hum will allow. They do not herd, and if one is killed — and they can be; the quick hunter from the wall’s shade does not take them, but a thing that comes down out of the ordinary sky sometimes does, a strike with no warning and a gap in the percussion — the shelf does not replace it that day. The next morning there are three or four again. The new one is not young. It is the same age as the others, which is to say it has no age you can read: legs like draftsman’s lines, a head that turns all at once, feet that tick the polished floor so the stone answers. They drink at the bowl. They drink the film of condensation that walks the inner wall. They do not drink the standing sheet of uncaused rain; they step aside for it, and the stepping aside is the only hurried thing they do. When dark comes they remain standing. Their balance-shifts continue at long intervals, a sparse night-clock. They are listening. Everything here is listening, but the drinkers do it with their whole height, and when the hum drops half a tone after sundown they all lift their heads in the same instant, as if the drop were a word they had agreed, long before any of them stood here, to treat as a name. The noise above them is not a flock. A flight of small shapes stitches the pocket from wall to drop and back, screaming one note the walls hold and return a fraction late. The wings are too numerous for birds. Count them and the count is different on the return pass, not because bodies joined or left, but because the shapes are partly the sound they make, and sound does not have a fixed number of limbs. They never land on the open polish. They land in the vine, or they stall and fall upward — a motion that should not be available, a brief reverse, as if the delayed echo in the walls had caught them and set them back on the stitch a wingbeat early. When the uncaused rain stands up, their single note changes key with every other voice in the pocket. The change is not a response to getting wet. The rain often does not touch them. It is a response to the pocket changing its mind about pitch, all at once, the way a room’s acoustics change when a door is opened. The rain is the door. It closes in under a minute. The previous roar reassembles, not gradually. There is a hunter that lives in the wall’s shade, narrow, two strides across an island, a sound like cloth torn once. It takes browsers that are a fraction too slow and, more often, the things that are only passing through: a hopper still deciding which mat to enter, a caller that has landed badly, one of the too-many-winged shapes if it stalls too long near the stone. The hunter does not cache food. It does not return to a den you can find. After the tear of cloth it is at the far side of the island, smaller in the mouth than the space it crossed, and then it is shade again. Its rule is the shade’s rule. In the open, in the full ordinary sun, it does not commit. Near the device it also does not commit; the hum dulls it, or bores it, or is a frequency it cannot hunt inside. It is the pocket’s editing. It removes hesitation. What it cannot remove are the drinkers, the column, the vine’s beat, and the bulk outside the lip. That bulk is the shelf’s other boundary, as important as the drop. It travels the outside of the edge, known mostly by a moving patch of silence in the callers, and now and then a forearm or a jaw clears the stone and the thin rock rings. It cannot, or does not, come in. This is not a wall’s refusal. It is a mutual one. The bulk is large enough that a single reaching could take a drinker. It never quite reaches. At the lip the hum, faint as it is there by day and fainter at night, meets some other attention the bulk carries, and the two attentions do not overlap. Prey use the drop as a throat — things climbing up out of the emptied mountain, things falling out of the sky, things emerging from seams that are not large enough — and the bulk waits where the throat narrows, on the outside, taking what the pocket has not yet agreed to hold. What the pocket agrees to hold, it holds lightly. Most bodies are passages. A pale crawler the size of a hand will come out of a finger-wide seam at dusk, cross three strides of polish, and go back into a different seam, leaving a track of damp that the warmth erases before the track is finished. A heavy shape will circle just outside the lip, its shadow crossing and lifting, for an hour, and never once put weight on the shelf, as if the shadow were testing whether the stone still means what it meant the last time this shape’s kind visited. Something with too many joints will stand in the brief rain and be gone when the rain is gone, not fled — absent, the way the third arc is sometimes absent, a presence the pocket declined to finish. The rules are not posted, but the residents behave as if they were remembered in the body. Do not nest in the open. Do not drink the sheet. Do not cross the hum’s minimum distance from another of your own kind if you are a drinker. Do not hunt in the sun or in the device’s immediate warmth if you are the shade. Do not land on the polish if you are mostly sound. Do not come in from the outside if you are the bulk; do not go out if you are the column. Distance will stretch away from the scatter and compress toward it, so a thrown stone — and the drinkers sometimes strike the thin floor hard enough to throw chips, not in anger, in the course of balancing — lands where it was not aimed. A cup of water poured on the open polish films, shines, and is gone, and the veins brighten a shade. The pocket is always settling a very small account with the machine, and the residents are the loose change. None of them tend it. None of them built it. None of them look at it the way a keeper looks at a fire. The browsers face the red growth. The drinkers face the bowl and, at the hum’s drop, the middle distance. The column faces inward because inward is the only direction the orbit has. And yet the leaning of leaves, the blink-passage through veins, the morning glaze, the way every voice changes key when the uncaused rain stands up — these are acts of maintenance performed by creatures that are only living. The machine is not being operated. It is being inhabited, the way a pier is inhabited by the tide: used, worn, slightly obeyed, never understood from inside the use. At night the warmth contracts. By midnight the far lip is merely mild, and the cold film there belongs more to the emptied mountain than to the pocket. The drinkers stand. The vine counts. The column tightens and continues its substitutions. The shade-hunter is nowhere, which may mean it is everywhere in the dark along the wall, or may mean night is a shade so complete it does not need to hunt. The bulk comes closest then, and the stone rings more often, and the flowers, if any are in their brief open minute, shut at each ring. The upright piece does not sleep. Its hum is half a tone lower, and in that lower tone you can hear, if you are willing to put your teeth near it — the drinkers will not stop you; they do not guard — a second rhythm under the first, not the vine’s beat, slower, the pace of the veins’ brightness as it moves. It is the same pace as the morning glaze. It is the same pace as the substitution in the column. The machine is not speaking to the animals. The animals are the rate at which it continues. Before dawn the pocket does the thing that makes it hardest to describe as a place with wildlife in it. For a short while the interference thins. Not silence — the shelf does not do silence — but a version of the roar in which separate processes can be heard as separate: one drinker’s tick, one late caller, the vine’s single beat, the hum, the faint ring of the bulk taking something on the outside of the throat. In that thinning, the scatter is brighter, and the arcs are all present at once, even the third, even the one that is usually a seam. The plants lean and do not correct. The column’s gaps stop. Every flier that enters a vein stays entered, for three breaths, four, and the column is visibly smaller, and the upright piece is visibly more occupied. Then the sun’s ordinary angle returns, the lean corrects, the gaps resume, the third arc flickers out, and the roar reassembles at full density so quickly you cannot find the moment the thinning ended. What lives here is that thinning, and the refusal of the thinning to last. A pocket the erosion was asked to miss. A scatter of work no one on the shelf is doing anymore, except by drinking, eating red growth, stitching a note across the air, ticking a sparse percussion, and blinking through a vein into a different body that does not know it is different. The life is a visitation sustained by a machine that has forgotten its makers and kept their interval. Loud, continuous, intimate as a room with too many people speaking. Under it the stone, nearly flat, still warm, answering only when touched, keeping the one thing it could not wear away by letting the living wear it instead. If you stay long enough to be sorted — and the pocket sorts everything that stays — you will not become a resident. You will become a passage that happened to be larger than the seams, and the drinkers will lift their heads when you shift your weight, and the shade will consider you only if you hesitate, and the hum will accept the water you pour and the warmth you add to the air and give you back nothing but the same half-tone at midnight, the same sweetness with no flower, the same knowledge, held in the teeth, that the room is full and has been full since before the polish was finished, and does not require you in order to continue being full.
