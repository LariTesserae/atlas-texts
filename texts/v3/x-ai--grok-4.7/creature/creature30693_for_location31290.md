The lowlands here do not end so much as they forget they were supposed to. A broad shelf of dark, mineral-rich mud slopes so gradually that the eye loses the horizon and keeps inventing one farther out, only to watch it dissolve into the same pewter sheen. Water stands in sheets no deeper than a wrist, warmed by the day and cooled by night into a slow pulse that never quite freezes or evaporates. The air above it carries a constant fine grit—bits of shell, pollen that never found a flower, microscopic glass from older storms—so that light arrives already broken, always a little late, always a little tired.

What moves through this is not a population in any ordinary sense. At first the surface looks only busy with weather: small winds stitching the water, a thicker haze where the mud breathes. Stay, and the busyness separates into bodies that refuse a single scale. Some are no larger than the grit itself, translucent capsules that tumble in the film and open only when two or three collide; they trade fragments of membrane and then drift apart, each slightly rewritten. Others rise as low domes the size of a kneeling person, their skins the color of wet slate, ribbed with channels that sip the standing water and return it warmer, slightly thicker, carrying dissolved metals that catch the dulled light in brief copper flashes. These domes do not walk. They thicken on one side and thin on the other, so that over hours they migrate like bruises spreading under skin, never lifting clear of the mud.

Between them travel longer forms, jointed yet soft, each segment a shallow bowl that holds its own miniature weather. They link and unlink without pattern the eye can keep. When three or four align, the water they displace runs uphill for a short distance along their backs before spilling, and in that brief reverse current smaller capsules gather and are carried. The larger forms do not appear to feed on them; the capsules simply ride until the alignment breaks, then settle into the mud and continue their own slow rewriting. Nothing here seems to hunt in the way a hunger would require. Exchange is constant, and loss of outline is the ordinary cost of continuing.

Deeper in the haze, where the shelf steepens by less than a hand’s breadth across a long walk, the domes grow taller and begin to lean into one another. Their channels join. What had been separate sips become a single circulation: cooler water drawn from the outer flats, passed inward, warmed, mineral-loaded, then released again in thin sheets that race across the mud and sink. The sound of this is not loud—more a continuous low friction, like cloth dragged slowly over stone—but it does not stop. At certain hours the leaning domes synchronize their thickening, and the whole shelf seems to inhale. The grit in the air drops. For a little while the light clarifies, and you can see, far out, ranks of still larger shapes that had been only darker weather. They are not domes. They are broad, low arches of the same slate skin, each arch spanning enough ground that rain could fall beneath it and never reach the mud. Under them the water runs clearer, and the capsules move in visible threads, following currents the arches themselves maintain by the slow flex of their undersides.

None of these forms casts a shadow that stays. When the light shifts, the arches pale until they are only a change in the temperature of the air; the domes flatten until they are mud with a different sheen; the jointed bowls come apart into ordinary ripples. Then the grit rises again, the circulation resumes its friction-sound, and the outlines reassemble—not always in the same places, not always at the same sizes. A dome that was knee-high at dusk may, by the next thickened dawn, be part of an arch, or may have dispersed into a school of capsules that still carry, in their membranes, the memory of a channel. The mud accepts every version. It keeps the metals, the warmth, the faint tracks of reversed water, and offers them back without preference.

There is no center. The shelf simply continues, and the exchanges continue with it, each body a temporary agreement between water, mineral, and the habit of holding a shape long enough to pass something on. If you remain, the agreements begin to include the disturbance of your presence—the way your weight pushes a lens of clearer water ahead of you, the way the nearest dome thickens on the side farthest from your heat and migrates, politely, out of reach. Nothing flees. Nothing arrives to inspect. The circulation merely routes around the new obstruction, incorporates the metals your boots shed, and, after a while, begins to return them to you in the fine rain of grit that settles on your sleeves: a few copper flecks, a rewrite too small to feel, offered without comment and without end.
