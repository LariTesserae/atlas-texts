The stem comes first, because everything else is only permitted. It is a cold green-black animal without a face, house-high and always a little wet, its rind tight as bark over softer pith. It does not think in any way a person would trust, but it chooses. It opens rooms when the water in it wants a turn, seals them when the traffic thins, swells a shelf if it is scored often enough, and lets a passage narrow until even the moths give it up. Its life is slow in the rind and sudden in the splits: a month to lean a finger’s width toward the wetter side of the basin, an hour to crack a new gallery with lips still sharp and shining. The city’s only true resident is this ongoing decision, made in sap and fiber, about which emptiness to keep.

Inside that decision the humans live sparsely, as if the dark were a commons too large for anyone to own. They are not many. You can walk a long vein of wall and meet no one, only your breath beading and then being taken by the air. Those who stay have learned to carry their lives in their hands. Their tools are small — bone awls, thumbnail flakes, cords of inner fiber — and their marks are lighter than scars. They do not cut rooms out of the plant so much as nag them into usefulness. A sleeping-shelf is pith persuaded, over a season, to thicken and lie flat. A cistern is a node the stem already meant to hold water, its lip packed with fiber so the drink does not all weep back into the wall. They name routes, not walls, because walls move. When a street closes overnight they follow the damp tracks of whatever found the detour first, and they give the new way the old name without ceremony.

They are thin people, most of them, not from hunger exactly but from the cold and the wet and the habit of turning sideways. Their clothes stiffen while they sleep in the lower third, where the air hardly moves and the water is nearest the skin of the earth. Higher up, near the youngest growth, they keep drier by a fraction and tell rough hours by the ticking of fibers in the walls. They speak softly, less from secrecy than from the sense that loudness has no purchase here. At the market — if the widened node deserves the word — they stand farther apart than a town would usually allow and trade fiber, flakes, and news of openings. Even then the place never fills. A person can choose the wrong moment, stretch both arms, and touch nobody.

What they fear is not darkness but sealing. The cords hung in the old central gallery, the one scar that would not heal, are their monument and their book. Knots under the fingers say who was lost when a chamber narrowed too fast, which splits drowned after rain, which circuit of climbers shifted and took three shelves with it. People come there less to remember than to check whether the stem has changed its mind again.

Around them, outnumbering them past any honest count, are the other citizens.

The moths keep to the upper seams, hand-sized, dry-winged, almost silent except for the tick of a mistake against the rind. They rest on the memory-cords and do not disturb the knots. Below them, in the water-films, pale woodlouse-things patrol in loose lines all day and are never, in any one stretch, a crowd. In still pockets between walls hang the slow colonies, nearer to seed than insect, pulsing once every few breaths; when they pulse, the tissue beside them loosens, drinks, and shuts. Blind ankle-high runners follow the newest splits because the freshest tissue is sweetest. Lean climbers, fur gone dark with damp, use the human shelves without seeming to notice that anyone sleeps there. And sometimes a heavy shape comes through that does not climb at all. It presses, the stem opens a temporary gallery around it, and after it passes the gallery is gone, as if the city had briefly made a throat and swallowed.

None of them announce themselves. Their overwhelming number is a fact you learn by patience, not by spectacle: a few in this passage, a few in the next, a few in every next, until the stem is revealed as a circulation rather than a container. They keep the paths clearer than the humans do. They find the new rooms first. They drink the surplus after rain and leave the city, by the following day, almost as quiet as before. If there is a civic order here older than knots and names, it is theirs — spaced, soft-footed, and uninterested in being seen.

Between these tenants there is no treaty so much as a mutual tolerance enforced by the plant. The humans score lightly and call it persuasion. The runners take the sweetness of a fresh split before anyone has decided whether it should be kept. The climbers shake rain from their coats onto shelves that were meant for sleep. The heavy shapes, when they come, rearrange the architecture without asking, and afterward everyone, human and otherwise, adjusts. Disputes among the people are small and local: who may drink before the patrols pass, who may worry a node into a shelf, whether a new split should be encouraged into a market or left to seal. Nobody disputes the stem. You cannot win an argument with a wall that grows.

So the life of the place, if it must be gathered into one picture, is this: a cold column in a young basin, full of spaced holdings and long empty dark, inhabited by a few careful humans and by a vast, nearly soundless traffic of other bodies, all of them moving through rooms that are only temporarily rooms. The humans think they live in a city because they have names, cords, and a schedule of trade. The moths, runners, climbers, and seed-colonies live in a body because that is what it is. The stem lives by being used. It persists the way a vein persists — not by loving what passes through it, and not by standing still, but by remaining just open enough, moment after moment, that none of its tenants can yet call it dead.
