They are not visitors to the stone. The stone is what they have not finished being, and what they are always on the way to becoming again.

The smallest of them never resolve into individuals for long. In the joints where night-water beads and thins, they exist as a decision spread across a million bodies: toward the damp, away from the open plate, never faster than cooling. Each is smaller than a seed and harder, a grain with a seam. Turn one in the light and the seam opens a fraction, drinks, and seals. They do not hunt and are not precisely grazed. The larger mouths take them the way a cloth takes dust, and the cloth of them simply closes. What looks like a river in the pavement is a preference moving. By afternoon the preference has shifted. By the next condensate it has returned, not because anything remembers, but because the water returns to the same places, and they are the sort of life that is mostly place.

Above them, the black freckles are a different patience. They sit where grit has stalled, no taller than a fingernail’s edge, and they are not plants, though the eye keeps loaning them that name for lack of a slower one. A freckle is a skin over a hunger that does not open. It stains the condensate dark and keeps the stain. When a grazer’s mouth passes, it gives up its outer face without protest and spends the next day building the face back out of sun and the thin metal smell of the rock. Nothing in them startles. Startling would waste a warmth they have spent all morning banking.

The domes are the country’s true population, and they are not all one thing, though they wear one color and refuse urgency in common. The low ones, ankle-high and numerous enough to become ground, are bodies that have chosen roundness as a way of lasting. Their warmth is not borrowed. Press a palm to one at dusk, when the stone has already given its heat back to the air, and the palm comes away as from a living flank: dull, steady, unwilling to spike or fade for your sake. A seam circulates in them, a darker thought traveling the circumference at the speed of a slow breath. It is not a pulse the way a heart is a pulse. It is the visible edge of a distribution. Whatever they keep inside themselves—they keep it distributed, so that no single injury is a death and no single feeding is a meal.

They do not speak. They do not turn to watch. If there is a sense in them, it is the sense of contact and of weather-that-is-not-weather: who is touching, where the water last was, how long the crown has been in sun. Where two have grown until they meet, they do not compete in any way a watcher can respect. They fuse. The seam between them becomes a joint that both of them drink from, and over years the joint rises into a low ridge with no destination, a hallway of shared skin. In the basin this has gone on so long that the ridges wander like frozen arguments. Nothing is solving them. They are the solution: more surface, more holding, less of the self that could be lost.

Some of the domes are not only themselves. Along the inner face, if a dead ring is split by weather or by the long patience of powder, there is a chamber no larger than a cupped hand, and in it a residue of paste gone to ceramic. The living ones still make this. It is not waste. It is a record and a reserve. In the dry months a dome can draw on its own interior the way the plain draws on night-water: slowly, without drama, becoming slightly less, and still round. The paste that leaks from the cracked crowns farther out is the same substance escaping a body that has grown too large for its own seal. It skins in the light. It hardens into fins that ring if touched. Given time, the fin is stone and the body is a rise and the rise is planed back into the table. This is not tragedy here. It is the ordinary completion. A dome that has finished is not mourned by the others. It is stood near. Warmth conducts. The dead rings above the basin are crowds that reached this completion together, still arranged as they arranged themselves when arrangement mattered—which is to say, when turning in one place for years could wear the floor smooth, and the wearing was a kind of company.

The birds know the difference between a living dome and a finished one, though they do not announce it. They are narrow, dark, almost instrument-like: a notch, a stitch, a weight the mounds accept. Flight is a correction they use when a dome cools or a grazer shifts or a boot becomes, briefly, the warmest fact in reach. Their feet are made for staying. The claws do not clutch so much as agree with the surface. They eat the freckles and the grain-life and, sometimes, the paste before it skins, taking it in quick, secret dips of the beak that do not break the quiet. They do not sing because nothing here returns a sound in kind, and they are not fools. A call would spend warmth and bring no answer. What they have instead is placement. A row of them along a spine, a single one on every third dome, is a sentence the country reads without hearing. When one leaves, another arrives in the interval of a breath, and the sentence remains grammatical.

The grazers are the only bodies that look, at first, like animals from a lower country, and the resemblance does not survive a long look. They are low, wide, colored exactly as the pavement is colored, and their shoulders move as if the bones had been argued down to suggestions, the same way the land’s edges were. Their heads stay down not from fear and not from stupidity. The food is on the ground, and the ground is not going anywhere, and lifting the head would be a kind of weather they do not practice. Between breaths the quiet becomes total again. A herd at rest is a change in rhythm, not a change in kind: more domes, slightly larger, slightly more willing to step. Their mouths work sideways. They take freckles, film, and the outer faces of the small round ones when the small round ones have grown soft with damp. The kiss they leave is pale. By the next day the freckles have begun to close it. The accounts are kept in seasons. No one here is rich by afternoon.

They use the domes, and the domes allow it, because a grazer’s belly is a moving shade and a moving warmth, and shade at midday is a mercy even a stone-bodied thing will accept. Birds stitch their spines. Grain-life settles in the damp prints of their feet and is carried, unintentionally, toward the basin. Nothing in this is kindness. It is adjacency so old it has become a metabolism.

In the basin the life changes phase without changing country. Under the water-skin, coin-sized and translucent, a thinner people travel without breaking the surface. They are almost not there: a wrinkle, a forgetting, a brief lens that bends the paste-colored light and then is only film again. They do not fuse with the ridges. They use the contacts where water lasts longest, and they carry something—spores, grit, the smallest of the seamed grains—in a fold of themselves that is less a pocket than a hesitation. Where they pass, the freckles establish a day sooner. They are not farmers. They are a delay in the water, and the delay is useful, and usefulness here is as close as life comes to a reason.

At the center of the dish of powder, the tracks do not belong to any of these. They are broad, shallow, and redrawn so slowly that to watch one lengthen is to watch nothing, and yet by evening it has lengthened. What makes them does not show itself in the midday inventory. It may not be separate from the powder. The powder is damp to a fingernail’s depth, ground by nothing you can see, and it prefers a shape: a path, a crossing, the memory of weight. Stand at the edge and the hairs on your arm do not rise. There is no malice in it and no welcome. Something large, or something that is largeness distributed the way a dome distributes its life, is crossing the dish on a schedule set by years. The grazers do not avoid the tracks. The birds do not perch in them. The coin-bodies wrinkle around their rims and go on. Whatever writes itself there is already part of the accounting, too slow to be an event, too persistent to be weather.

And there is one more presence, which is not a body and not quite an absence. In the rings where the dead domes keep their last posture, the air is no warmer and no colder, but a person who stops inside the smoother floor will notice, after a while, that their own breathing has lengthened to match a breath that is not theirs. The rings are finished. What finished in them has not entirely left the shape of the turning. It is not a ghost in any lower sense—no face, no grievance, no demand to be remembered. It is residual habit: the smoothness, the western break, the path of paler freckles that runs out and thins and vanishes. Follow that path and you will not find who made it. You will find the plain practicing flatness, and the living domes practicing not being flat yet, and a bird adding you, without comment, to the list of warm objects worth a short correction of flight.

They live by a few laws, and the laws are not virtues. Hold heat. Do not spend sound. Let water come to you. Become continuous with whatever you touch for long enough. Be numerous, because number is how this country survives a day that would starve a solitary mouth. Die into the surface so gradually that death is only the last planing, and the table remains what it has always been working to be: one pale pavement under an untheatrical sky, occupied so completely that emptiness is the illusion, and stone is only life that has stopped hurrying.
