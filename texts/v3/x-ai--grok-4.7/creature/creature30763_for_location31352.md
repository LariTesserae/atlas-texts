They came with the survey, most of them, and have not yet become older than the town. A few dozen households, never quite the number the plan drew lots for, spread through the sheds and the two streets of proper houses as if occupancy itself were still being negotiated with the ground. They are not pioneers in the old sense and not quite mill-hands in the old sense either. The work arrived already designed—shifts, whistles, belts that must be watched—and the people arrived already practiced at it, from other bowls and other stacks, carrying the habits of places that had time to round their edges. Here those habits sit a little loose on the body. You can see it in the way they plant their feet before a threshold, testing whether the step has settled, and in the way they leave doors ajar so the damp can do what it will without being argued with.

Mornings belong to the early shift. They come out in coats that never quite dry, collars darkened where yesterday’s condensation wicked up and stayed. Faces are pale in the ordinary way of people who work indoors under a bright sky they only cross. Hands are the part of them that has adapted fastest: nicked, stained at the nails with machine oil and with the green that comes off wet brick, warm even in the cool air because they have been near the housings. They do not hurry. The whistle has already told them when to be where, and the engines will be there whether they trot or not. Between the house row and the mill they walk the surveyed lines, stepping around the lenses of water and, more carefully than they would admit, around the reflection of the stack when it lies too convincingly in a drainage cut. Children going to the single schoolroom do the same, smaller versions of the same gait, satchels beaded, arguing in low voices that the frogs outlast.

The children are the first generation that does not remember another floor underfoot. They treat the sharp ledges as furniture. They know which poles the vines have claimed and which still bite with splinters. They fish nothing out of the standing water, because there is nothing in it yet that wants catching, but they float chips of pale stone on it and watch the chips refuse to hurry. In the lots the plan left empty they have made a geography the survey did not: a flat stone that is base, a willow that is out, a ditch where the ferns hide whoever is meant to be found. They are not wild. They come in when the second whistle goes, because supper is tied to the shift the way weather is tied to the bowl. Their knees are always damp. Their pockets hold screws, and seed-heads, and once in a while a beetle that has decided a coat is as good as a warm plate.

Inside the mill the adults become briefly exact. The broad note of the engine fills the chest and makes talk a matter of leaning close or not talking. They read the belts by touch and by a change in pitch too small for a visitor to name. When a belt slaps wet they do not curse the climate; they wipe, and tighten, and wipe again, and the wiping has become a kind of courtesy the machinery is understood to expect. The stack draws its pale column and they glance at it the way people elsewhere glance at a clock they already trust. Between tasks there is standing. Not idleness—readiness—with the weight on one hip, eyes on a flywheel, the body performing patience as a skill. Oil and water bead on their wrists in the same film. When the shift ends they leave prints on the railings that the moss will soften by morning, and they do not mind. The prints are not a claim. Nothing here has been held long enough for a claim to mean what it means on older ground.

Evenings are louder with voices and still somehow slow. Cooking smoke joins the stack’s column and does not outrank it. People sit on loading platforms with their feet clear of the green underneath, passing news that is mostly mechanical: a housing that wept rust, a pole that leaned a finger’s width after rain, a heron that stood through an entire shift and was therefore, by general agreement, employed. They drink what keeps, and they talk about the towns they left without nostalgia sharp enough to cut. This place has not yet given them a story they can prefer to those. It has given them sleep that comes easily, because the engines idle rather than stop, and the frogs keep a meter the body can fall into. Windows fog from the inside. In the fogged glass the room’s lamp and the stack’s distant glow become the same size, and nobody bothers to wipe a clear patch unless they are waiting for someone.

There are a few who do not keep the shifts. An older man keeps the drainage cuts, walking them with a hoe and a patience that makes the surveyors look hasty. He does not fight the water. He persuades it a few inches toward the courses already chosen, lifting a stone, lowering another, letting the lenses stay where the ground insists on holding sky. He knows the frogs by the ditches they favor and will not work a cut while they are calling from inside it. A woman keeps the schoolroom and the counts—who is on which shift, which shed still lacks a number that will not run in the rain—and she repaints the numbers higher each season, knowing they will sag, knowing the sag is not a failure of paint. She has started leaving the lower line, faint, so the doors carry both the intended address and the one the weather prefers. Between them and the mill-hands there is no real distinction of rank. The town is too small for rank to have anywhere to stand that is not also somebody’s path to work.

Animals live among them without petition. The starlings are acknowledged as neighbors with poor manners. The heron is left its post in the largest cut; people route the cart around it as they route themselves around the stack’s reflection. Dogs, a handful, sleep where the housings throw a dry heat and rise only when a shift changes, not from duty but from the pleasure of a moving crowd. Beetles cross tables and are brushed aside, not crushed, because crushing them leaves a smear that the damp will not forgive quickly. Nobody speaks of this as gentleness. It is the local economy of effort. Force spends itself against edges that have not yet agreed to wear down, and against water that will be back by night. What lives here has learned, faster than the stone has, to spend less.

At the far lots, where the plan thins into ferns and the poles stop, a smaller life goes on that is not quite separate. Trappers and moss-gatherers—two or three, no more—sleep in sheds the mill no longer needs and sell what the bowl offers: hides of the small animals that den under the platforms, bundles of fern for packing, willow withes for the baskets the houses still lack. They are the only ones whose work the engines do not time. They leave before the first whistle and return after the last, wet to the knee, carrying the smell of standing water into town like a second weather. Children watch them the way they watch the heron, as fixtures that might at any season decide to be elsewhere and so far have not. The gatherers themselves do not talk about staying. They talk about the cuttings, and about which north face has thickened enough to be worth a knife, and about the ledges that still flake if you trust them with your weight. They are teaching the children, without announcing it, which edges the rain intends to keep.

What binds all of them is not affection for the place so much as a shared tempo. They have matched themselves to a town that persists without ripening. Births happen, and are absorbed into the schoolroom counts. Deaths happen, rarely, and the ground takes them stiffly; the graves at the rim of the bowl are as sharp-cut as the ledges, and people visit them in ordinary coats, in ordinary damp, and do not linger past what the weather makes sensible. No one has yet been born, lived, and buried here. That absence is the quiet fact under the noise. They feel it without naming it, the way they feel the engines: as a condition, not a story. Until someone completes that span, the town remains a posting that has forgotten to end, and they remain its residents in the provisional tense—committed, useful, not yet ancestral.

If you met one of them on the open ground between sheds, you would not be struck. A person in a damp coat, pausing to let a cart pass, eyes already on the next housing, listening without seeming to listen for the note of the belt. They would nod. They might tell you the shift is long today, or that the heron is in its place, or that the numbers on the east sheds will need doing again after this rain. They would not offer you a history, because the history is still mostly a survey, and they are modest about surveys. What they would offer, if you walked with them as far as the mill, is the rhythm: step, avoid the water, avoid the reflection, wipe the hand, lean in, wait. It is a good rhythm. It fits the bowl. It does not ask the stone to be older than it is, and it does not ask them to be, either—only to keep the belts true, and the ditches persuasive, and the doors painted with both the address that was meant and the address the rain has chosen, until the ground, in its own time, decides which one to keep.
