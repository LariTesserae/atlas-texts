The knock arrives first, before any body, and it arrives the way a floor arrives under a foot: as something that was already doing its work and will not stop to be looked at. A soft hydraulic seizure low in the wall, a held second, a hiss of vapor let off into the warm wet, then the seizure again. You can count it against your own breath and lose. The period is shorter than a slow inhale and longer than a blink, and it has never missed.

What produces it is not a machine set into the plant. It is a swelling of the wall itself, a bladder the color of a ripe fig, fist-sized, nested where two wrist-thick stems have fused and then gone on growing around the fusion until the joint is only a rumor of a seam. The bladder fills from inside. You cannot see the water move into it. You see the skin tighten, the dull nap of its surface go glossy, a highlight walk across it the way a highlight walks across an eye that is about to blink. Then the skin loosens all at once, not collapsing, settling, and a thread of vapor leaves a pore no larger than a pinprick on its underside. The vapor is warmer than the room. It ghosts upward and is gone into the general wet before it can be a cloud. The bladder is already filling again. There are seven of them in reach of the eye, none of them in step with any other, and the knocks fall into a weave rather than a beat. Together they are the machinery. Apart, each one is only a fruit that learned to breathe on a schedule and never unlearned it.

The resident of the chamber is not any one of these, and not the sum of the birds and the stationed fish and the moths that trade places without changing the pattern on the wall. Those are attendants, or they are organs that have forgotten they are organs. The thing that lives here is the arrangement that keeps every interval exactly as wide as it is. It has no face. It has a posture. If you stay, the posture becomes visible the way a current becomes visible when you stop trying to see the water and watch what the water does to a leaf.

It occupies the middle, where the sheet is shallowest and a person could turn without brushing anything, and it occupies that middle by absence. There is a span of open water, root-ridges polished pale under it, wide enough for a body and holding no body. The span does not silt in. A film of fine green wants to close over still water in a place this warm; here the film starts at the edges of the span and is taken back, continuously, by something that is not a current you can see. Stand at the margin and the water against your ankle is a fraction warmer than the water a hand's width farther in. The difference is smaller than a fever and larger than a mistake. Something in the open span is spending heat, or receiving it, at a rate the rest of the room is not.

The roots under that span have been trained. Not carved. Trained the way a limb is trained by leaning on it for years: the same pressure, the same place, until the grain consents. Three ridges run parallel across the open water, low enough that the sheet covers them, and they are smoother than the ridges elsewhere, worn past the fibrous look into something that takes light like horn. Between them the grooves are deeper than use would make them, and in the grooves the water moves — not the whole sheet, only a skin of it, a finger-wide slip toward the left wall and back, on the period of the nearest bladder. In, when the bladder fills. Out, when it hisses. The groove is a duct that was never cut. It grew duct-shaped because whatever rests above it has been drawing and returning on that period for longer than weathering takes to finish a surface.

Nothing rests above it that the light can catch. The ordinary daylight comes through the blade-dome and lies flat, and in the middle of the span it lies a little less flat. There is a softness in it, a place where the thinned sun does not quite arrive at the water, as if it had passed through one more thickness that has no edge. The thickness is upright. It is about the height of a person who has never had to stoop, and it is narrower than a person through the shoulders, and it does not displace the air so much as agree with the air's own rising. Warm vapor from the bladders drifts. Most of it drifts at random. A portion of it, every few knocks, lifts in a column no wider than a wrist and then frays, and the column stands where a torso would stand if a torso could be made of the same wet the room is already full of. You see it only when a drop falls through it. The drop's path kinks, a correction, and continues. The kink is the outline. It is gone before you can want it to stay.

The attendants know the outline better than the eye does. The three birds on the far root-bench sit with their bodies touching and their heads turned three ways, and none of the three ways is toward the open span. They face the walls. Their chitter locks to the bladders and does not include a note for whatever holds the middle. The fish are stationed over their polished patches, and the patches stop where the trained ridges begin. No fish holds above the horn-smooth grooves. The boundary is not marked by anything sharper than a change in grain, and every fish observes it, pectoral fins ticking, as if the grain were a fence. The midges occupy their shaft of light and that shaft does not cross the span. It leans, very slightly, away. Even the moths on the weeping wall rest more densely on the near and far curves and more sparsely on the stretches of wall that would be closest to the middle if closeness were measured across the floor. The pattern looks even until you count. Then it looks like a deference.

So the resident is located by what declines to overlap it. This is an old arrangement. Deference is not fear; fear would fidget, and nothing here fidgets. Fear would also thin the sounds, and the sounds do not thin. The birds do not lower their voices when the vapor-column stands. The bladders do not slow. Whatever holds the middle is not a threat to the machinery and not a product of it. It is the reason the machinery has a room to run in rather than a choked mass. The stems rise, branch, fuse, and stop. They stop at the edge of the span. You can see the arrested tips if you look low, just above the waterline on the inner faces of the walls: ends that are not broken, not bitten, not scarred. They are finished. Each one has a cap of smoother tissue, rounded, the way a root rounds itself when it meets a stone it will not split. The stone in this case is not there. The refusal is. Growth meets it and turns aside and has been turning aside so long that the turn has become the architecture. The dome overhead is a dome because every blade that might have crossed the middle grew along it instead. The hollow does not widen because widening would be a kind of advance, and advance has been declined.

It does not eat, in any sense the stationed fish or the clicking beetles would recognize. There is no draw of small bodies toward the span, no thinning of the moths over time, no pellet of undigested wing in the grooves. The sheet stays clear. The sugar-smell of the room, the warm green voltage of sap moved under pressure, is the same at the margin as at the wall. If it feeds, it feeds on the difference it maintains: the fraction of heat, the kink in a falling drop, the fact that a film of green starts and is taken back. It spends what it takes on the same difference. A closed loop, smaller than the room's loop, nested inside it, running at the same rate so that neither loop pulls the other off its period. The deeper octave under the knock — the compressive sound of the greater mass outside, vast tissues leaning and not giving way — does not change pitch when the vapor-column stands or frays. The resident is tuned to that octave. It is not the mass. It is the pause the mass allows itself, given a body, given a height, given the habit of not being entered.

Touch is the wrong experiment, and the room suggests this without warning. The wall, if you put a hand to it, is firm and fibrous and faintly pulsing, and the pulse is the bladder's and not yours. Step toward the span and the warmth against the ankle increases by that same small fraction, and the trained ridges under the sole are smoother than they looked, slick in the way horn is slick, and they are warm from within, warmer than water can hold at this depth. The warmth is even. It has no center you can find with a foot. Set your weight as if something ought to be standing there to meet it, and nothing meets it. The water closes over the place your ankle was. The vapor does not kink around you; you are not the right shape for the kink, or you are too heavy a fact for a correction made of vapor. The fish do not relocate. The birds do not turn their heads. You are absorbable, a temperature, and the span declines you with the same courtesy it declines a stem: by being already finished, by having no seam where you could be included.

What it wants, if a held note can want, is the continuation of the intervals. Not company. The attendants are not company; they are the measure of the intervals, the way ticks on a glass are the measure of a liquid. It keeps them by the same means it keeps the stems arrested: a refusal distributed so evenly that it feels like a law of the place. A beetle that wanders into the span along a groove does not die and does not turn back in any startled way. It simply finds, after a few lengths, that the groove has become less interesting than the seam it left, and it is back at the seam without a moment you could call a decision. The resident does not pursue and does not receive. Its attention, if the word survives a thing with no face, is the equal pressure that makes pursuit and reception both unnecessary. Everything is already where the pressure puts it. The work of living here is to remain the pressure.

Over a long enough stay the outline accumulates anyway, against its own preference for not being seen. Not a shape. A habit of shapes. The vapor-column is slightly taller in the minute after three bladders have hissed in near-succession, as if the resident drew itself up on the richer pulse and then forgot, and subsided. The kink in the falling drops is not always vertical. Sometimes it leans, a few degrees, toward the left wall, toward the place where the larger thing under the water turned once and did not turn again. It leans back. The lean has the tempo of a listener. Whatever turned under the left wall is not prey and not a rival; it is a sound the resident has been hearing for as long as the grooves have been grooves, and the lean is the hearing made briefly spatial. Then the outline is upright again, and narrower than your shoulders, and the drops fall straight, and you would not believe the lean if the next one did not repeat it a quarter of an hour later, exactly, on no clock you brought with you.

There is no face for the lean to belong to, and inventing one would be a lie told against the room. The room has already said what a face would be for. Faces are for the attendants: the birds have the quick side-facing attention of birds, the moths have the eye-spots that are not eyes, the fish have the flat coin of a pupil held over an assigned patch. Those are faces because those are animals, bounded, able to be startled, able to be counted. The resident is not bounded on the side that matters. Its edge is the arrested tips of stems, the leaning midge-shaft, the deference in the density of moths, and those edges are the room's edges. To give it a mouth would be to pretend it takes from one place. To give it eyes would be to pretend it faces one direction. It faces the maintenance of a hollow. Its mouth, if the seven bladders are anyone's, is distributed low in the walls and breathes on seven clocks that only braid into one because it stands where their periods can be compared.

Light does not dramatize it, and this is the kindest fact available. The daylight stays ordinary, thinned by leaf, gold only where a blade is thinner than its neighbors. When the sun outside moves, the soft place in the span moves with it, a little, the way a faint spot on a lens moves when you tilt the lens. So some of the softness is only optics: wet air, a thickness of vapor, the ordinary trick of humidity. Not all of it. The optical part slides with the sun. The other part stays. You can separate them near the end of a long afternoon, when the beam has walked to the left wall and the midges have walked with it and the soft remainder is still in the middle, upright, unlit, no longer explainable as a column of brighter damp. It does not cast a shadow because it does not stop the light so much as decline to add itself to what the light is doing. A shadow would be an overlap. The resident does not overlap.

At the hour when the gold goes out of the blades and the chamber becomes one temperature all the way through, the knock continues, and the resident continues, and neither has acknowledged the change. This is the hour when a thing that slept would be caught sleeping, and nothing is caught. The birds chitter at the same lock to the bladders. The larger presence under the left wall does not turn for evening any more than it turned for noon. The vapor-column frays and re-forms, a little plainer now that the sun is not competing with it, and still it is only a correction waiting for a drop to make it visible. The resident was not waiting for dark. Dark is a condition outside the loop. The loop takes in heat and leaf-filtered light and the slow compressive octave of the mass, and it returns them at the same rate, and night is simply a lower term in a sum that stays balanced. If it dreams, the dream is the groove of water slipping in and out, in when the bladder fills, out when it hisses, a repetition so exact that variation would be the emergency.

You leave the same way the room allowed you to stand in it: by not being required. Pushing back through the thickness of leaf, you take nothing on your skin that the weeping walls do not also wear, no pollen of it, no scale, no scent that is not the general scent of sap and warm voltage. Behind you the thickness seals. The knock does not grow fainter in the way of a sound you are walking away from. It stays at full production until the leaf closes, and then it is simply elsewhere. The resident does not come to the margin to watch the margin restore itself. Margins are for things that end. It is still in the middle, still the height of a person who has never stooped, still narrower than your shoulders, still spending its small fraction of heat on the film of green that will start, at the edges of the span, and be taken back, all night, at the rate it has always been taken back. The attendants will keep their stations. The stems will keep their finished tips. In the grooves under the open water the skin of the sheet will slip toward the left wall and home again, on a period slightly shorter than a slow breath, slightly longer than a blink, forever, which in this chamber is not a dramatic word. It is the only available measurement.
