What lives there does not announce itself by standing in a court. It is already in the course you are walking on.

At first it is only a habit of the work. A mallet falls in a rhythm that is not quite a person's: three strokes, a pause long enough to listen, three more, and the pause is the same length wherever you hear it, on the near street or under a floor two layers down. The listeners, if they are listening, are keeping time with one another across distances no voice could cover. Between the strokes a hand — you will call it a hand before you have seen it — sets a brick, twists it a fraction until it sits, and withdraws. The withdrawal is always to the left. Always. Even when the wall being raised is on the right side of the passage, the worker crosses the line of the body to set from the left, as if the right side were occupied by something that must not be jarred, or as if there were no right side to spare.

When one finally comes into the grey where you can see three slabs ahead, it is smaller than the sound suggested. The height of a half-grown person, and then not, because the shoulders continue into the wall. Not leaning. Continuing. The courses at its back are not built against it; they are of it, the same clay-and-shell body, fired in patches where a kiln was near and still damp where the newest work is. The skin, if skin is the word, takes a thumbprint. Press, and the print stays, and by the next street it has been filled and trued by some other course, so that the body heals the way a wall heals, by addition. There are no eyes in the face you expect. There is a smoother place, a glaze, turned toward the string-line, and the line's shadow crosses it and the creature adjusts the brick by the width of that shadow. It does not need to see you. You are a change in the air's damp, and it has already cut a door to your height somewhere you have not yet turned.

They are many, and they are not separate in any way that lasts. Two of them working a pier will, if the pier needs to be thicker than two bodies, simply meet at the courses and cease to be two. The seam goes. You can watch it go: a dark joint, a thumb dragged once along it, the joint gone, and what remains has four arms for as long as four are useful and then tucks two back into the mass the way a mason tucks a line into a pocket. They do not speak. The mallets are the speech. A change in rhythm upslope is answered, a street away, by a change in the set of the bricks — tighter, or with a gutter left, or with a socket where a socket was not in the plan of the morning. The plan is not drawn. It is held in the intervals.

Under them, in the wet pockets, is the other thing, and it is older, and it is what they are paving over without ever quite reaching. It has no courses. It is the film that turns in a cistern and stops the mallets for three breaths: a skin of pale mineral, not quite clay, not quite flesh, stretched over nothing you can find when you reach in. Your hand goes through. The cold stays on the fingers an hour. It does not build. It seeps. Where a gutter was left unfinished on purpose — and some are, you begin to understand, left unfinished as mouths — it rises a finger's width and tastes the new brick and withdraws, and the brick afterward is glazed on its inner face with a white that no kiln put there. The builders do not hate it. They work faster when it has tasted a course. They leave it rooms. Small ones, always wet, always without a door a person could use, cut instead with a slot the width of that skin. Offerings, or lungs, or the places where the basin is allowed to remember it was a basin.

The builders eat nothing you can see. They take damp into themselves through the courses; a wet wall near one of them dries faster than a wet wall elsewhere, and the body darkens, slightly, and lays better. When a kiln is fed they stand near the dome and the dome's breath goes into them and they are, for a little while, rigid, which is as close as they come to sleep. In that stillness you can hear the other thing moving in the slots, a dry scrape, curious, patient, older than the first brick. It does not want out. Out is not a direction it has. It wants the work to continue, because every new layer is another place to be thin under, and the builders want the work to continue because the work is the only way they know how to be more than one and less than all.

If you stay, they will include you, and they will not ask. A threshold cut to your height is the beginning of a measurement. Stand there long enough and a course rises against your calf, friendly as a lean, and the clay is warm from a body that was just now a wall. Pull away and the course stops at the height it has reached, toothed, ready, in no hurry. They have paved a basin the size of a market town and the hills that hold it. They can wait out a person. The skin in the cistern cannot. It turns, once, under the water, toward the warmth you brought in with you, and for three breaths every mallet in the dark stops, listening, and in the quiet you understand that the unfinished gutters were never a failure of the work. They are how the place drinks. They are how it keeps a mouth. And the mouth has noticed that something with blood in it has walked out onto the floor it is still, slowly, becoming.
