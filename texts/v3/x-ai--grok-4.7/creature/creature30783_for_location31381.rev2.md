What lives here is not a crowd and not a remnant. It is a population that has accepted the scale of the floor and the absence of water as the terms of having a body at all.

The smallest of them never quite become animals, though they move. Along the seam-lips, where the living joins extrude their mineral dust, a granular life rides the extrusion itself—grains that are not grains, each one a bead of resin around a speck of salt and a coil of fiber too short to be seen as fiber. They do not crawl so much as advance by drying. A bead swells on the warm lip, splits, and the two halves are already being worried apart by the wind, each half sealing as it goes. By the time they have traveled a body's length they have incorporated a few more grains of the pavement's own dust and are heavier, lower, less willing to lift. In the grooves they accumulate into a slow skin that the scavenger-files crop like a harvest. It is not a harvest they planted. The seam grows it the way a factory grows scale on a boiler: as the residue of running. Cropped, the skin thins, the lips extrude harder, the wind takes more dust, and the beads begin again downwind of the file. The continent's fertilizer and its least citizen are the same substance caught at two moments.

Above that film, the first bodies that read as bodies are the stitchers. They are the dark punctuation I first took for a single kind of scavenger, and they are not one kind. Walk with a file long enough and it sorts itself. The lead animals are narrow and almost without mass, all gape, moving fast because they are only opening the lacquer-flake the wind has already loosened; they do not chew so much as sieve. Behind them come the squatters, lower, plated on the belly, who turn the flake and the bead-skin into a paste in a gizzard lined with swallowed grit, and who leave, in a neat line, pellets of spent mineral that the next gust will sort back into the grooves. Last in the file, and rarest, are the menders: longer, slower, with a mouth that is less a mouth than a gland. They do not eat the seam. They lay a thread of their own wax along any lip the file has torn past usefulness, and the plant accepts the thread, lacquers over it, and continues. A file that has lost its mender begins, within a day, to travel beside a seam rather than on it. The lip swells, extrudes too fast, the bead-skin clogs, the lead animals go hungry, and the file breaks into solitary walkers who will join another file or die as a dark stitch that no longer moves. No one leads this. The order is the order of the work, and the work is older than any of their lives.

They do not drink, and they do not sweat, and the problem this solves is the same problem the plants solved, answered in flesh. Their water, what water there is, is bound inside them the way the plants bind theirs: in gels and waxes, not in blood that could spill and flash to vapor in this air. Cut one—the wind will do it, a blade-edge of dust, a fall from a diaphragm—and the wound does not run. It clouds. A sweet film skins over in the time it takes the animal to stop moving, and the film is already food for whatever comes next. The smell is the smell of the plant's own exhalation. Nothing here has invented a private chemistry. They are all running excerpts of the rind's.

In the towers the population changes character, because the towers are the only vertical country, and vertical country can be owned in a way the pavement cannot.

The gliders are not a traffic of one species sharing a convenience. They are stacked. Near the slots, where the light is a white disc and the air leaves the culm in a hard pulse, live the slot-riders: small, membrane between elongated fingers of cartilage, mouths rasps, wings waxed not by grooming but by the air itself, which plates them with the tower's exhalation until they gleam. They ride the pulse and bank back in, and their whole economy is the lacquer at the lip of the slot, which the wind would scour away if they did not shear it on every pass, and which the tower replaces because a scoured slot whistles wrong and the organ-note splits and the updraft fails. They are maintenance. They are also vicious about the slot they maintain. A rider that tries a foreign slot is met in the air, membrane to membrane, and the two of them fall together down the shaft until one releases or both strike a diaphragm. You hear this as a change in the note—a flutter, a beat missed—and then the note resumes, and somewhere on a lower gallery a cleaner finds a body and does not distinguish it from any other windfall of wax.

Below the riders, in the dimmer stacks, the galleries are held by the valve-workers, and these are the first animals in the place that can be said to have a room. A conduit junction is a room. The fiber-valves that drive dust and resin and gas are the furniture, and the animal that lives at a junction—broader than a rider, eyes reduced, forelimbs hooked to the fiber—knows the rhythm of its valves the way a person knows a staircase in the dark. It clears crust. It is paid in crust. If the rhythm changes, a choke upstream, a split in the culm, the animal does not go looking for the cause. It tightens on its junction and waits, and if the wait is too long it seals itself into a wax shell against the valve and becomes, for a season, part of the machinery: a lump the plant lacquers over, inert, spending nothing, until the hum returns and the lump cracks from the inside and the same animal, thinner, resumes the same junction. I have no evidence they remember the wait. The behavior does not require memory. It requires only that a body which stops spending can be started again by the return of the sound it feeds on.

There is a third tower-people, and they are the ones the note is really for.

High, above the riders, where the culm narrows and the diaphragms are close together and the light is too bright to sit in, something larger keeps a chamber of its own making. You do not see it from the pavement. You deduce it from the way certain towers hold a purer tone than their neighbors, a tone with a pulse under it that is not the wind's. The chamber is a diaphragm that has been eaten out and relined with a mixture of wax and the animal's own shed skin, until it is a drum. The animal—call it a drummer only because of what it does, not because it intends music—sets its weight on the drum and rides the tower's sway, and its weight is tuned. Too light and the note goes thin and the updraft scatters and the riders leave and the slots scour and the tower begins to die from the top. Too heavy and the note drops and the dust does not lift in the tower's lee and the mineral diet thins. The ones that persist are the ones whose mass, at that height, in that diameter of culm, holds the chord the structure needs. They do not hunt and they are not hunted in any regular way. A rider will not rise to them. A valve-worker cannot. Their food comes up the shaft as the tower's own breath plates the drum-chamber with lacquer, which they rasp in a slow circuit, wearing a path that is itself part of the drum's tuning. When one dies, the note sours within a day, and within three the riders have gone, and within a season the tower is a dead column of the kind the pavement keeps as furniture. Sometimes a younger animal from another tower climbs—there are climbs, along the outside, at night, in the folded quiet of the fans—and takes the drum, and if its mass is right the note returns and the riders return and the tower is a tower again. If its mass is wrong, it starves in a room that no longer feeds it, and the column goes on ringing wrong until the scour takes it.

This is as close as the place comes to a politics: a single body whose weight is the difference between a working instrument and a ruin, and a succession that is only the arrival of another body of the right weight. There is no inheritance. The drum is not kept for the successor. The successor keeps itself by fitting.

Out on the plates, in the full glare, the long runners are the only animals that treat distance as a native element rather than a tax.

They are built for the groove and against the brightness. The body is a narrow keel. The legs are not long for show; they are long because the groove is deep and the dust in it moves, and a short leg would be a shovel. The eyes sit under shelves of pale scale, and the shelves are not armor against enemies so much as eaves against the sun; in this light an unsheltered eye cooks. They run in the morning and the evening, when the grooves are wires of shadow, and they lie up through the height of the day in the lee of a seam-lip or the stump of a dead tower, bodies the color of the pavement, distinguished from it only when they breathe. Their breath is the tell. It comes out as a brief pale puff of the same sweet hydrocarbon the plants exhale, and in the thin air it vanishes in a step. A still runner is invisible. A runner that has just settled is a small disappearing cloud.

They eat what the files do not finish and what the towers shed at a distance—lacquer-flake blown off a slot, a pellet of spent mineral that still holds a little wax, the occasional split blade that has cartwheeled across the plates and come to rest far from the fan that grew it. A blade is a feast and a danger in equal measure. It is large enough to feed a runner for days, and large enough to be seen by everything else that can see, and the wind keeps moving it, so the feast travels, and the runners travel with it, and for once they are not alone. Three, five, sometimes a dozen, strung out along a moving blade, each keeping the distance at which the others are a fact and not a threat. They do not share. They occupy different ends of the same wreck. At night the blade cools and ticks and the runners lie against it for the residue of its heat, and this is the nearest thing on the open pavement to a gathering, and it ends when the wind rises and the blade moves on and the group becomes a scatter of keel-shapes resuming their grooves.

I thought at first they were the predators, because they are the right size and the right speed. They are not. What hunting exists is smaller, meaner, and bound to doors.

At the slots, and at the mouths of galleries where a conduit opens on the pavement, there are waiters. They are the color of the lacquer they mimic, flattened, patient past any reasonable account of patience, with a strike that is a hinge rather than a lunge: the whole forebody swings on a thickened joint and closes, and what was a patch of rind is briefly a mouth, and then is rind again, with a bulge. They take riders that misjudge a bank. They take the lead animal of a file that follows a seam into a gallery mouth. They take, rarely, a runner that crosses a slot at the hour the charge walks the tiles, when the runner is dazzled by the blue stitch and the waiter is not, because the waiter has no eyes that the charge can fill; it hunts by the tick of the charge and by the change in the seam's warmth when a body interrupts it. The kill is dusty and short, as the place requires. There is no chase longer than a body's length, because a chase in this wind is a dispersal, and the prey will be in the next groove before the predator has finished turning. So the predators have given up the chase entirely. They are doors that sometimes shut. Their abundance is a census of doors: every mature tower has its slots, every slot its waiter, and across the continent that is a great number, and you will still walk a day and not see one close.

The domes have their own parish, and it is the strangest, because the domes are where the continent thinks with electricity, and the animals there have had to become part of the circuit or stay out.

Under the tile, in the shade that is only shade, the air is still dry and still cold but the tick of the charge is louder than the wind. The animals that live in that dark are not cleaners and not riders. They are thin, many-legged, and they walk the underside of the tiles in routes that follow the paths the spark takes when it grounds. They time themselves to the tick. Between snaps they move, feeding on a fungal-feeling crust—though it is not fungus; nothing here rots wet—that forms where the discharge has cooked the mineral dust into a brittle sugar the plants cannot use and these animals can. At the snap they freeze, because a moving leg is a path to ground, and a body that becomes a path is a brief bright death and a smell of burnt wax. You can watch them, if you lie under the rim of a dome at the hour the charge peaks: a still constellation of small bodies, then a crawl, then a still constellation again, in time with a clock the plants never meant as a clock. They have no name in any voice. They are the only lives in the place whose rhythm is not the wind's.

I have been writing as if all of this were animal, and the center of the continent is not.

The fans are plants. That is not in dispute. But stand near a mature fan through a full day's turning and the plant's status becomes a technicality you have to insist on. The blades track. That much is tropism, and tropism is common. What is not common is the correction. A blade that has split and lacquered and been worried by the gust into a new curve does not simply accept the curve. Over the next days it twists at the living pivot until the new curve throws the same shadow, at the same hour, as the old one did. Neighboring blades adjust their spacing so the fan's total throw of wind—the volume it hauls, the dust it lifts, the note it contributes to the front—does not drop. A fan that loses a blade entirely grows no replacement of the same size; it retimes the remaining blades, opening their arc, spending more of the day's charge-analogue (they are not electrical like the domes; they are hydraulic in the dry sense, resins driven through fiber under pressure) to make five blades do the work of six. This is not awareness. It is also not the simple growth of a thing toward light. It is a running adjustment of a machine to its own damage, continuous, local, and exact enough that the weather downwind does not register the loss. The continent's climate is full of such unregistered losses. The fans are populated, in the only sense that matters here, by their own ongoing solutions. If anything can be said to inhabit the place as a citizen rather than as a passenger, it is a fan in its mature tuning, clapped by its own motion, older than the animals in its lee, indifferent to them except where their weight or their feeding changes a curve it then spends a week undoing.

The passengers know this without knowing it. The runners do not bed in the wind-shadow of a fan that has recently lost a blade; the shadow is wrong, gusty, full of new eddies, and a wrong shadow is a place a waiter can use. The files do not run their seams through the scoured zone downwind of a retiming fan, because the dust there is too coarse, the bead-skin too thin, the lead animals starve. The life of the place reads the plants' repairs the way sailors read a coast, and the reading is accurate, and there is no one to thank for the accuracy. It was selected, the way the drummer's mass was selected, by the simple continuation of whatever fitted and the simple stop of whatever did not.

At night the census changes without the population changing.

The fans fold in their slow sequence, and the folding is a curfew that is not enforced and does not need to be. The open pavement becomes a louder place in a thinner way: the galleries underneath continue, the charge walks the dome-tiles as visible spark, the contraction of the cooling rind ticks, and the animals that own the day go still and the animals that own the dark begin. These are not a separate creation. They are the same files, the same runners, shifted into the hours when the glare is gone and the grooves are only grooves, navigated now by the warmth of the seam-lips and by the tick underfoot. What truly belongs to the night is the climbing. Young drummers, or whatever the drummer is before it is heavy enough to be one, leave the lower diaphragms and go up the outside of towers in the folded quiet. Waiters shift from slot to slot along the rind, a slow migration of shut doors. And across the plates, widely, in numbers that only the dark makes visible, the runners stand up out of their grooves and are briefly tall, heads raised, not looking—there is nothing to see—but listening to the organ-notes, which have dropped their register and steadied, and which from a standing head are a map. A sour note is a dying tower and a coming meal of shed riders. A pure note with a pulse under it is a drummer in place and a tower that will still be a tower in the morning. A missing note is a stump, a lie-up, a polished ring where the smallest gliders prefer the dead sound. The runners do not go to these places on the strength of the night's listening. They file the listening, in whatever way a body files a thing, and in the morning their grooves bend, a little, toward the news.

Nothing in this is a society. That is the fact that takes longest to accept, because the work is so coordinated and the coordination is so tight. There are no calls that recruit. There are no gatherings that decide. There are no young instructed by the old; the young that fit, continue, and the instruction is the continuation. What looks like a city—the tower with its stacked trades, its drummer, its riders, its valve-rooms, its waiters at the doors—is a city only in the way a reef is a city, or a gut: a set of niches made by a larger life, occupied by smaller lives whose occupancy keeps the larger life in the state that makes the niches. Kick the system and it rings, and the ringing is the system restoring itself, loudly, with dust and wax and the small bright violence at the doors, and by evening the note is the note again, and no one has agreed to anything.

If there is a person here, it is not any of them. It is the fit itself, repeated so many times, across so much lit distance, that it has a weather and a sound and a history of stumps. The inhabitants are the ways of lasting in a dry, bright, deafening engine that was not built for them and does not notice them and would stop, in places, if they left. They have not left. They are in the seams and the slots and the drums and the wrong shadows and the night map of notes, abundant past counting and almost never touching, running on wax and salt and the timing of a spark, in a country where the ground is alive and the water is a rumor from a lower world that no longer has any say.
