They are built for the thinness, though they would not say so. The chest sits high and broad, the ribs long, the shoulders a little forward from a life of carrying on the front of the body—jars, slabs, children, the folded weight of a hide. Hands are large for the wrists, not from any single trade but from every trade being a matter of grip: stone that does not forgive a slip, animals that lean their whole mass into a rope, clay that must be worked before the wind skins it. The face is wide across the cheekbones, the nose strong enough to warm what little air there is before it reaches the lungs. Lips crack. Everyone’s lips crack. The cure is fat, and fat is never wasted on vanity, so the cracks are a kind of weather people wear the way the scarps wear lichen.

Their color is the plateau’s color, which is not one color. Sun on stone all year turns skin the shade of the paler blocks after a season of wind—dusted, not burned in the lowland way, because the burn here is slow and total. Freckling and staining accumulate rather than fade. A person of forty carries a map of every ledge they have slept against and every cistern they have leaned over. The young look unfinished beside them, still the color of the inside of a jar.

They do not speak across distance. The air will not carry it, and the habit has gone deeper than necessity. Conversation happens inside an arm’s length, heads inclined, voices low and flat, the mouth doing more work than the breath. In a lane you may pass twenty people and hear nothing you can parse, only the soft grit of soles and the occasional click of a tongue that means move, or wait, or the animal is about to turn. Children learn the clicks before they learn the words. A shout is an emergency or a discourtesy; either way, people turn to look, not because the sound reached them but because a body changed its shape at the far end of the street, and looking is how news arrives.

Sight, in them, is a public sense. They read a figure at two hours’ distance the way other peoples read a voice in the next room: the set of the shoulders, the way a load rides, whether the animal is being led or is leading. Names are not called. A hand lifted to the brow—shade, not greeting—and the figure is known. Within the rooms the same habit persists. Faces are watched more than they are listened to. A lie is difficult not because people are virtuous but because the mouth and the eyes are always in the same small light, and the light is hard. They have a word for the particular stillness of someone about to refuse a request, and another for the stillness of someone who has already refused and is only waiting for the asker to see it. Both are used more often than the spoken no.

They live in densities that would smother a people with more air and more timber. A household is not a set of rooms so much as a thickness of bodies and stores sharing a heat. Grandparents, children, a sibling’s children, the sibling if the sibling’s wall failed in a hard winter, and always the animals that bed against the inner face of the south wall—two, sometimes three, the compact grazers whose breath is part of the night’s arithmetic. No one finds this strange. A room without an animal in the cold months is a room that has fallen out of luck or out of kin. Privacy is a matter of not looking at certain moments, which everyone can do, having practiced it since before speech. Couples turn to the wall. Birth happens behind a hanging hide with an aunt on either side and the herd shifting, uneasy, on the other side of the same stones. Death is slower and more public, because a body that has stopped producing heat must be replaced in the arithmetic before the jars freeze.

They count kin by walls more than by blood. Who shares your thickness, who has a right to the ledge where the dung dries, who may draw from the cistern that takes three days of cloud to gain a hand’s depth—these are the questions that organize a life. Blood matters, but blood can be walked away from, and walls cannot. A feud is not a shouting thing. It is a lane that two families stop using, a marriage that is not made, a share of milk that arrives late and a little short for three seasons until someone older decides the shortage has said what it needed to say. Violence is rare and ugly when it comes, because there is nowhere for it to be private and because the tools to hand are heavy. A killing rearranges a whole street’s heat. People remember the winter after, more than the blow.

Work is simultaneous and unremarkable. At first light the ledges are swept, the jars sounded with a knuckle, the animals turned out into the lanes that are also pastures. Stone is always being moved by someone, somewhere—a block walked on rollers of bone and hide across a roof, a course lifted a finger higher on a scarp that offered its back wall and is now being asked for a second storey. The pace is the pace of the fitting, not of any urgency. A badly set slab will announce itself in the first hard freeze, and the announcement will be a crack that runs into your neighbor’s room. So they fit, and fit again, and the young are not trusted with the final seating of a stone until they have spent years on the carrying. Metal, when a household has it, is kept in a recess cut for it, oiled with the same fat that goes on lips, and loaned more often than it is owned outright. A needle circulates through a street for a generation. Everyone knows whose grandmother brought it up from lower country, and the knowing is a kind of title.

They eat what the plateau permits, which is milk in four forms, blood drawn sparingly from the living herd in the months when the forage fails, meat when an animal is finished, and a hard bread made from the grain that grows in the few pockets where a wall throws a long enough shadow to hold moisture. The grain is a small, dark, bristled thing. It is never enough. Insects are not a famine food; they are an ordinary food, taken from the cistern throats and the under-eaves in season, toasted on a shard, salted if there is salt. Salt comes from a pan three days’ walk to the east, where a seep dies into a white crust, and the journey is one of the few that people make in numbers. They go looking, not listening, and they cache water in jars buried to the lip at intervals only the walkers can find again, because the plateau does not keep a path. A path would be a softness, and the wind erases softness.

Children are numerous and thin and sharp-eyed, and they are put to real work early because real work is mostly watching. A child of six can be trusted to see a herd begin to drift toward a cistern lip and to turn it with a click and a body set sideways in the lane. They play at fitting—small stones, careful, absorbed for hours—and at distances: who can identify a walker first, who can tell from the dust whether the load is jars or a body being brought back from the salt. They are not loud. A loud child is corrected not with anger but with the same gesture used on an animal that startles: a hand laid along the side of the face, turning the gaze, a low sound in the chest. By eight they can sit a grazer. By ten they know which roofs hold the day’s heat longest and will therefore be where the old women talk in the late light, and that those roofs are where news is finished, the way milk is finished, into something that will keep.

The old do not retire. There is no room in the arithmetic for a body that only consumes. But the work changes. An old man who can no longer lift still knows which face of a scarp will take a course and which will shed it in the first thaw, and his knowing saves a decade. An old woman who no longer walks to the salt remembers every cache, and sits on the south wall in the morning drawing the intervals in dust with a stick while the walkers kneel and memorize. Memory, here, is a structural material. It is why the very old are kept in the warmest thickness even when their milk-share is a courtesy and their breath fogs the same air as the herd’s. Lose them and you lose the cisterns that are not marked, the marriages that must not be remade, the year the cloud failed for forty days and which ledges still held a skin of frost on their shaded side at dawn.

They have no temple the way they have no trees. What they attend to is the water under the stone, which they never see and always hear if they put an ear to the right crack at the right hour—a faint, granular tick, more mineral than animal. Some people are better at finding the tick than others. They are not priests. They are consulted the way a needle is borrowed. A new room is not begun until one of them has lain with an ear to the foundation scarp and said whether the under-sound is a seep that will last or a seep that is only this year’s melt deciding, and failing. They are often wrong. The plateau is young, and its decisions are not finished. When they are wrong, a household spends a generation hauling water from a street away, and the person who listened carries that fact in the way their neighbors look at them, which is punishment enough, and specific, and does not require a shout.

They mark time by stone and by cloud, not by feast. A year is the interval in which a roof’s edge grows its small dune of grit and is swept clean again. A life is the interval in which a lane, walked every day, lowers by the thickness of a sole. They do not expect the horizon to produce anything. Riders appear; riders are identified long before they arrive; the city makes room as it makes room for a herd, which is to say it does not rearrange itself, it merely ceases, for a moment, to occupy the space the arrival needs. Strangers are a weather. They pass, or they stay and learn the clicks and the jars and the particular stillness of a refusal, and after a generation their children’s hands are as large for the wrists as anyone’s, and their dead are in the same walls, because the dead are built into the walls—bone pegs, and sometimes more than pegs, in the oldest thicknesses, where a scarp has been asked for so many storeys that the first rooms are now cellars of stone and the names have become the names of corners.

At night they close the lanes with their own sleep. The wind continues, small and persistent, moving its grain across the roofs. Inside, the arithmetic holds: breath, hide, milk-fat on cracked mouths, the animal’s heat against the south course, the tick of water somewhere under, still deciding. They sleep lightly, all of them, even the children. Not from fear. From the habit of a people who live by looking, and who know that morning will come as a change in the color of a crack in the wall, and that whoever sees it first is already, in the only sense that matters here, awake.
