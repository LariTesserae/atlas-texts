They are not one people, and they are not many in the way a census would prefer. What lives on the shelf is a thickness of kinds that only look separate when you stop moving and let the layers unstack.

Closest to the stone, and oldest in the only sense that matters here—settled first, before the halls had a reason to exist—are the clamped things. They have no name because nothing walks far enough from them to need one. A clamp is a body the size of a spread hand or, in the east cracks where the gel is richest, the size of a torso, pressed so flat to the fracture that the stone seems to have grown a hide. The upper surface is not a back. It is a field of low plates, russet, olive, the dull gold of cooling mineral, each plate edged in a fringe that is neither hair nor leaf. The fringe is how they breathe the air’s carbon and how they read the heat. Underneath, where you cannot see without lifting them—and lifting them kills the seal, and they do not recover a seal in a day—the flesh is a single continuous mouth, not open, not closed, a warm velvet that matches the gel molecule for molecule and draws it in without suction. There is no throat. The gel becomes them at the contact. A clamp that has fed well through a long season thickens; its plates stand a little prouder; the fringe darkens toward black at the tips. A clamp that has found a cooling crack pales and spreads, hunting laterally, a slow stain of life along the seam until it either finds warmth again or meets another clamp and stops, their fringes touching, a boundary written in color.

They do not move as a visitor means move. Across an afternoon you will not catch it. Across a span of days a clamp may have advanced the width of a nail toward the warmer side of its crack, and the plates it left behind will have been shed as thin mineral flakes that the smaller walkers collect. Those flakes are the first currency, if currency is the wrong word for a thing nobody owns and everybody uses. The clamps are the shelf’s wells. Everything else is downstream of their patience.

On them, and in the velvet between them, live the counters. They are the west-ward glittering, but they are not confined to the west; the west is only where the stone is new enough that their work shows. A counter is smaller than a seed, many-legged in a way that is really many-bristled, the bristles both foot and tongue. They carry granules—stone-dust, shed plate, a grain of resin crumbled from a pod—and they set them down with a dab of the fat their bodies make, the same solvent that stands in for water in every tissue on the shelf. One granule is nothing. A day’s granules, if the day’s warmth holds and the crack beneath is generous, become a wall you could read a vein through, rose when the ordinary light comes sideways. They do not plan courts. A court happens where three or four of them pause because the evening heat pools there, and pausing is laying, and laying for enough evenings is a plaza. Their exchange is positional. To stand where another stood is to receive the residue of that other’s fat, a trace of where it fed, which crack ran sweet, which lane above is shedding skin. Memory, at their scale, is a smear.

You could spend a life among the counters and never notice the walkers. The walkers notice everything, because everything is at their mouths and their hands.

The loaf-bodied kind of the east are the ones a first look takes for the citizens. Broad, low, many-padded, hides the color of the gel, mouths that meet a crack and leave only a tick of cooling stone. They are not citizens. They are a ward’s livestock and a ward’s architects and a ward’s dead, depending on the hour, and none of those words is right. A loaf is a stomach with a memory of direction. Inside, the gel is held warm—warmer than the crack, warmer than the air—and worked by a slow chemistry into the tallow that sleeves their muscles and, when they sleep long enough in one posture, shells outward through the hide and hardens into the shelves other bodies will use as rooms. They do not decide to build. They decide to stay, and staying is masonry. A cluster that dies in a ring—age, or a crack that failed, or the rare violence of one loaf pressing another off a seam in a season when gel runs thin—leaves an absence the living close over, and the absence keeps the shape of their circle. Those are the oldest chambers, and they smell of the sweet ward even after the bodies are gone, because the carpet of shed skin continues to be laid by the ones who remain, and the skin holds the resin-grammar of whatever was dabbed there last.

Their saying is almost all skin and stop. An arm flushing dusk means the crack is taken, or the body is full, or the lane should not be entered until the flush fades; the distinction is in how far the dusk travels, whether it stops at the shoulder or runs the whole flank, and in the sweet or salt of the dab left when they rise. Three of them blocking a lane is not a symbol. It is three stomachs that have agreed, by the simple fact of not moving, that what is behind them is not for the through-traffic of the day. Sometimes what is behind them is a new crack just beginning to gel, and they are keeping the counters and the moss off it until a clamp can seat. Sometimes it is a body that has shelled and not yet decided to wake. Sometimes it is grief, which on the shelf is a smell that does not fade on the schedule of other dabs, a resin that goes rancid-sweet and teaches the lanes to bend around a spot for season after season until the carpet is thick enough to bury it.

They do not fight with any part that was made for fighting. There are no claws, no teeth in the biting sense, no venom. When gel is scarce a loaf may lay its full weight across a seam and stay until the other leaves. That is the whole of their conflict. The one underneath, if it is a loaf, can wait as long as the one above; the loser is the one whose internal warmth drops first, whose tallow stiffens, who must go find a sunlit rim and be an engine again. The winners do not glow with it. Winning is only continued feeding. The flush, if any, is the ordinary gold of a full belly.

Above them, in the mid-shelf halls where the blades have locked and the light arrives amber, are the combers. Slender, hand-only, no hook in them. Their hands are the most precise things on the shelf: four digits and a pad, the pad always faintly tacky, able to lift a felt of dry moss without tearing the blade beneath, able to part an interlaced thatch and lay it back so the resin-weld does not crack. Combing is not grooming, though it looks like it, and it is not worship of the plants, though a visitor hungry for rites might call it that. It is the maintenance of volume. Uncombed, the parasite-moss felts a passage in a handful of days, and a felted passage stops being a lane, and a stopped lane stops being a way for loaves to reach a crack, and an unreached crack is a clamp that pales, and a paled clamp is a well that fails, and a failed well is a hall that starves from the floor up. The combers know this not as a chain of reasons but as a discomfort in the hands. A felting itch. They work it out hour after hour, silent, their throats sometimes gold when they pass one another in a lane narrow enough that shoulders must sequence, a brief acknowledgment that the work continues on the other side.

They sleep in the resin shelves the loaves have shelled, or in pockets of blade they have pinched almost shut, a living door. A comber’s youth is spent low, near the clamps, learning the tick of a good crack from the tick of a failing one by laying a palm on the stone and waiting until the heat writes itself into the pad. Their age, if they reach it, is spent high, where the canopy is oldest and the blades are thick as wrists, combing not passages but the slow knots where three halls want to become two. There is no rank in this. A high comber is not obeyed. A high comber is simply harder to replace, because the knots remember hands, and a strange hand can split a weld that took a season to make.

They eat the blades. Not the way a grazer eats, tearing and chewing and making a wet sound that could not exist here. A comber’s mouth is a flat press, ridged, and it takes a shaving from the matte surface of a leaf, a peel of wax and meat no thicker than a film, and the leaf skins the place over before the comber has moved on. A hall that is combed well is also harvested well. The plants are not diminished. They are polished. Where a comber has fed, the green is clearer, the resin smell sharper, and the next shaving, days later, comes away cleaner. It is a mutual trimming. The blades that lean over the rim and stop have never been peeled; they are bitter with unworked wax, and the combers leave them, a border of inedible green against the drop.

The fliers are not messengers. Nothing on the shelf needs a message carried farther than a touch, and the air is too still to be a road. They live in the upper lace because the lace is where the light is still ordinary, almost harsh, and their bodies are built to spend it. Light-bodied, fringed, a wingspan no wider than a comber’s hand, they are the shelf’s only true solar collectors that are also animals: a dark lattice under translucent skin, warm when you could catch one, which you cannot, because they do not land on skin if they can land on wax. They drink light and they drink the faintest volatiles the pods give off when they tick tighter in the afternoon. In return they carry spores of the velvet lichen, and pollen-analogues of the blades, and the eggs of the counters, which are smaller than granules and stick to fringe. A flier that crosses from the east ward to the west in the course of a long day is a migration of wells and of memory, unknowing. Their hush is the sound of that work. They do not call. A flush on a flier is a full-body dusk, visible only if the amber light catches it, and it means the lattice is saturated, the body must shade, the fringe must fold. Other fliers read it and do not crowd the same vein of sun. That is their entire politics: a distribution of light, negotiated in color, at a speed the loaves would find frantic and the clamps would not perceive at all.

There is one more kind, and it is the one the shelf is slowest to show you, because it does not keep to a ward and it does not keep a shape you can settle.

In the deeps of the sponge-partitions, where the sessile masons have grown a second and third story and the lanes become cellars, there are bodies that were loaves and are not loaves now, and were not combers, and are not clamps. They have given up the pads. The hide has thinned until the internal tallow lamps through it, a low continuous gold, not a flush—a flush ends—and the mouth has migrated, over a season of not feeding at cracks, to a dozen small contacts along the belly and the sides. They lie in the narrows and they are touched, constantly, by everything that passes: a comber’s shin, a loaf’s flank, the bristles of counters that have climbed a shed flake, the fringe of a flier that mistook a cellar for shade. Each contact leaves a trace of fat, and the trace is information—east-sweet, west-sharp, a rancid grief two lanes over, a new crack’s first tick, the amber quality of a hall whose blades are being peeled on schedule—and the lamper does not store it in anything like a mind. It mixes it. The tallow of a lamper is a blended solvent of the shelf’s last many days. When a loaf, uncertain, presses its side to a lamper’s side in the dark of a cellar, what it receives is not an instruction. It is a temperature and a taste of the whole. It leaves knowing, without a thought it could hold, whether to go east or to stay, whether the rim is worth the walk, whether the lane blocked ahead is a vote or a new well.

The lampers do not rule. They cannot move to enforce, and nothing on the shelf enforces. They are how the density thinks without speaking. A shelf without them would still be full; it would be full of local truths, each crack a nation, each hall a season behind the next. With them, a change at the rim—a fracture sighing, a new fan turning, a loaf gone to warm its belly in the harsh ordinary light—arrives in the cellars as a shift in the gold, and leaves again in the bodies that brushed past, and is combed, unknowingly, into the lanes by hands that only felt an itch.

They are born, if that is the word, from loaves that stayed too long in the cellars and were not dead. The shell hardened and then, instead of being kept as a room, softened again from within, and the body did not resume its pads. It is not a promotion. Most loaves never do it. Some combers, very old, have been found thinning in the same way, their precise hands gone to contacts, their gold dimmer than a loaf’s, salted with the amber of the halls rather than the sweet of the cracks. The lampers do not seem to notice a difference in origin. Mixture is mixture.

At the rim, in the middle of a day, you can see the whole dependence at once if you know to look for it and not for faces. A loaf, broadside to the drop, warming. Under it, in the crack the rim-stone keeps, a clamp that has followed the heat as far as the heat goes and stopped, fringes black-tipped, plates proud. On the loaf’s sunlit hide, a scatter of counters laying rose granules in the warmth, a tiny court that will not survive the evening, rebuilt tomorrow because the warmth will be there again. In the amber behind, a comber’s hands working a blade that leans and does not cross the edge. Above, a flier folding into dusk, saturated, choosing shade. And under the loaf’s far side, where it touches the carpet of skin that runs back into the lanes, a faint gold that is not the loaf’s own—a lamper close enough, in the first cellar, that the warming at the rim is already becoming part of the mixture.

No one of them is the inhabitant. The inhabitant is the contact. It lives in the near-touching, in the tick of cooling stone, in the dab that means closed and the dab that means grief, in the shaving taken and skinned over, in the granule set down where another paused, in the gold that does not end. The shelf is small enough to walk before an hour is gone, and it is inhabited entirely, to the last sharp inch, by a single slow attention distributed through bodies that cannot do without each other’s heat.

They will not look at you. Looking is not how they spend a face. If you stand long enough in a lane, a comber’s shoulder will brush you, and the pad, tacky, will take a little of whatever solvent you brought from elsewhere, and somewhere under the halls the mixture will register a taste it cannot file—wet, maybe, or cold, or simply wrong—and the lanes, over days, will bend. Not away from you in fear. Around you, the way they bend around a rancid sweetness, until the carpet is thick and the work continues on every side, and the shelf, recently emerged, already full, has included your strangeness by the only method it has: touch, and then the long patience of growing past it.
