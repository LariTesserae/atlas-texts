The crabs are the first to admit you, though admission is the wrong word. They do not guard. They audit. A hand-sized body the color of old bone and engine primer, legs jointed like folded calipers, each one carrying a faint oil-sheen where the shell has been polished by generations of the same route. They do not look at you with eyes so much as register your heat and your step-interval against a ledger they keep in the flex of the root-plate. If your weight arrives on a seam they have already marked for drainage, they stack—three high, sometimes four—until the lane clears, then unstack without ceremony and resume the circuit. Their traffic has no destination you can name. It is the maintenance of options: which cracks stay open, which fruit-knuckles get rolled under before the next pulse, which damp lane must be abandoned before the housing weeps again. When they die they do not leave shells in the open. The floor takes them the way it takes the green knuckles, and new crabs assemble at the margins of the clearings with the same pale finish, already knowing the yields.

Above them the moths are not moths for long if you watch past the first column. The wings are scaled, yes, and the color is the black-brown of used oil catching a tired thread of light, but the bodies are too cylindrical and the antennae too regular, as if a loom had been taught the idea of an insect and then left running. They do not feed on anything visible. They feed on the shear between temperatures—the half-degree where a vent’s exhaust meets the cooler drip from a bark-skinned casing—and their circling is the act of keeping that boundary from collapsing. When an engine loads and the pitch drops, a column shears, reforms, and for a moment you see the individual wings lock into a temporary lattice, a gauze that holds the vapor in place so the heat does not spike and cook the sap-crust. Then the lattice dissolves back into traffic. They are numerous enough to throw shadows and few enough that the sightlines stay open. No one brushes them aside. Brushing would be a kind of vandalism.

The glass-herd in the third clearing is slower to resolve because it does not want edges. Bodies like blown bulbs, organs of cold light stacked along too many legs, they complete their circuit around the dripping coupling and leave tracks that fade before the loop closes. What looks like drinking is closer to balancing: they take the condensate so the coupling does not flood, and they spend a fraction of their light back into the metal so the housing does not seize. The tithe from the ceiling is precise. The thing that lowers—never fully a tongue, never fully a root, something that learned both and kept the useful parts—does not hunt. It samples the surplus brightness the herd can spare without dimming the circuit. If the herd is thin, the tube withdraws early. If the herd is bright, it takes a longer measure and the largest engine, buried somewhere in the plant-mass, drops its idle by a fraction, as if acknowledging receipt. The herd does not scatter because scattering would break the books. Panic is a form of debt.

What flies in the lost ceiling never shows its whole outline. You get the leather slap, regular as the biggest piston, and occasionally the dent in the vapor when it feeds. Its wings are not built for this chamber; they are built for a longer dark that the chamber only borrows. It turns because the updrafts turn, and it times itself to the root-plate’s combined step the way a conductor times a rest. It is not the master. It is the part of the system that still needs open air in its idea of itself, so it stays high where the threads of light die and the overburden almost remembers weather. When true rain comes through the splits, its beat softens by a count or two, then locks back in. The others adjust without signal. Fluency again: the place has had time.

None of them are alone in their offices, and none of them are the whole inhabitant. The inhabitant is the agreement. The machines under the bark were cut and set by hands that are no longer here—the tool-marks survive as pale seams in the callus—but the growth did not erase them. It hired them. Housings became throats. Flywheels became weather. The crabs, the lattice-moths, the glass circuit, the high turner, the drinking roots, the fruit that is currency, the vapor that is both waste and medium: each one holds a clause the others can read. Load, freeze, tithe, drink, reform. A settlement of pauses rather than houses, loud because every office is working, open because crowding would be a failure of the grammar.

If something like a person still lives here, it lives as a pause that has learned to last. You feel it at the collarbone, where your sweat will not dry, and in the way your breath is already being counted into the choir. It does not wear a face. It wears the intervals—between gear-cycles, between wingbeats, between the moment a crab yields and the moment the floor drinks. Stay inside one of those intervals and the chamber will make room the way it makes room between trunks: not by emptying, by recognizing a step it can use. Leave, and the ledger closes over the absence without remark. The work continues. It has been continuing longer than the distinction between what was built and what grew up around the building and decided, quietly, to keep it.
