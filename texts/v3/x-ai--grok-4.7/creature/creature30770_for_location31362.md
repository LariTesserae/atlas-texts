The first thing that lives here is a patience so large it has limbs.

It is not one animal. It is the habit of staying still until the wind-wall has passed, practiced by everything with a weight to shift, until the habit grew a body of its own and the body learned to use the ribs the way a climber uses a hold that is also trying to close. You hear it before you invent a shape for it: the pad unsticking, then another, always on the undersides, where the fiber is older and the cold is damp rather than killing. Four points, then a drag, then four points again. The drag is a belly, or a bundle of fiber-cord, or both. When the paler dark opens between two ribs you sometimes see the underside of an arch freckled with them, dark against the pale split-faces, each no larger than a curled arm, each stuck by a film that looks like the sap and is not. They drink the film. They also drink the rime as it forms, rasping it with a mouth that is only a roughened patch, so that a crest which should whine in the wind goes quiet in patches, silenced by appetite. They do not fall. If an arch grows and pinches, they walk inward along the closing dark until they find a hollow still open, and the walk takes longer than a season, and none of them seems to notice that this is migration.

Above them, in the cloud the arches wear, the flocks are not birds in any way that helps. They are thin and jointed and they hold their wings—if those are wings—half-open for so long that ice plates them, and they use the ice. A split opening under them is an updraft with a smell of iron and green, and they ride it without a cry, turning as the warm fiber cools, dropping out of the column when the sap seals and the lift dies. What they eat is the fine dry tick of drops that froze on the way down. They open and close around a grain of that ice and it is enough. They are abundant the way dust is abundant. A wind-wall hits and they vanish into it, not by fleeing but by becoming the same temperature as the air, so that for a while the flock is only a difference in how the drops fall. When the wall has gone they are on a new rib, already icing over, already waiting for the next split to announce itself from somewhere you cannot see.

The booming on the crests is a herd, and it is also not. There are bodies up there, low and wide, with feet that have learned the edge. They walk the sharpest fiber because the newest growth is the only place the sap still beads, and the bead, frozen, is salt to them, or fat, or a memory of heat. They move in a file so that each animal’s feet find the polish left by the one ahead, and the file is long enough that the front of it is already hearing the wind-wall while the back is still in the still air, and this is why the booming seems not to stop when the wall hits. It does stop. It stops in a wave that travels backward along the file at the speed of attention, each animal freezing with a foot raised, and the crest under them goes on growing, which is the other half of the sound, the half that never learns to be quiet. Calves—small, still warm from the inside of something—are born on the file and must walk before the rime takes the heat out of them. Most do. The ones that do not become part of the crest: a darker knot, then a scar, then a whine where the air catches a shape that used to be a decision.

In the pockets, under the leaf-thin crust, what moves is not fish and not insect and not the water remembering that it fell. It is a thread, many threads, pale as the split fiber, living in the only standing water by eating the slowness of it. They lace the underside of the ice and thicken it from below, so that a pocket which should thaw when a paler dark arrives instead holds, and holds, until the crust is a lid and the lid is a floor and something heavier can cross it. The threads do not mind being a floor. They mind only drying. When a hollow pinches and a pocket becomes a seam, they retreat into the last wet, and if the last wet freezes solid they wait inside the ice with their length folded to a seed, and a later rain—someone, somewhere, letting go—wakes them into a new cup. Their patience is smaller than the underside-walkers’ and older. It does not have limbs. It has recurrence.

The platforms are not abandoned. That is the mistake the scale invites.

The people who drive the pegs are few, and they are never all in one hollow, and they do not think of themselves as living on the plateau so much as living in the interval between one wind-wall and the next. They are small, long-handed, with nails worn to the same polish as the herd’s path, and their breath hangs in the same shape as anyone’s breath would, which is the only ordinary thing about them. They do not speak in the hollows. The hollows are instruments, and a voice arrives everywhere at once, and everywhere includes the herd, which bolts, and the underside-walkers, which freeze and fall if startled from their film, and the threads, which cannot hear but can be broken by a foot set wrong on a lid they have made. So the people carry their talk in cord. A coil left on a platform is a sentence: thickness for urgency, knots for direction, a frayed end for a hollow that has begun to pinch. The next person who comes—days later, or a season, which from inside is the same—reads it with the fingers and adds a length or cuts one away. The conversation moves around the plateau slower than the growth and faster than a person can walk. It is how they know which ladders still open onto water, which crests have gone sharp enough to take a herd and therefore a following, which arches have started to close and must be left before the leaving becomes a trapping.

The blade of knapped not-stone is a tooth from the herd, or a plate from a flock’s iced wing, struck until it holds an edge for a few cuts. They use it to take cups from the inner wall while the fiber is still green and soft, then dry the cups in the brief warmth of a fresh split, packing them with rime-salt and the dried film the underside-walkers leave when they move on. Food is a problem of timing. Nothing here fruits. Everything here grows, and growth can be eaten only in the hour it is warm, or stored as a cup, or followed in the form of a herd that has already done the work of finding the bead. A person who misses the hour walks down into the damp older hollows and rasps rime the way the small stuck animals do, and lasts, and does not thrive. Children are carried across the lids, and taught the cord before they are taught the edge, and taught the edge before they are allowed a voice. A voice is for the crests, outside, where the air does not hold a sound and send it back larger. Even there they speak low. The newest growth is loud enough.

The marks—three strokes, a fourth stopped by sap—are not names. Names would want to last, and nothing cut into this plant lasts as itself. The marks are clocks. Three strokes means the split opened while a certain person was here; the fourth, arrested, means the sap came faster than the hand, which means this crest is young even by the standard of crests, which means the herd will be along it before the scar whines, which means the cord left on the nearest platform should be thick. Whoever reads the sealing scar later does not need the strokes. The whine is the message, delayed, and louder, the way all their messages become louder as the plant takes them over and wears them as weather.

They do not war. There is no territory that survives the year. A hollow you know is a seam by the time you have walked its length, and a platform you lashed is a line of dark eyes, pegs swallowed, the cord you left still readable if the sag has not yet dropped it into the dark that has no floor. They follow the new. Their whole craft is the practice of arriving at an edge while it is still an edge and leaving before it is a wall. Some do not leave in time. The plant closes over a peg and it will close over a wrist with the same indifference, the same beading, the same slow sealing that turns a wound into a whine. The others hear that whine and do not cut a rescue. A rescue would take longer than the closing. They add a knot to the cord, a knot that means a name, and they carry the coil to the next platform, and the name goes around the plateau until the cord frays and is cut away, which is as long as anyone is mourned.

At the far repetition, where fog makes the ribs identical and the newest crests are still splitting, there is something else, and it may not be separate from the plateau.

It does not walk the undersides or ride the splits or file along the salt. It is the size of a hollow and it stays in one, which should be impossible, because hollows pinch. This one does not pinch. The ribs around it grow and then, at a certain height, divide and grow sideways, as if the arch had been taught to make a room and keep making it. The cloud on that arch does not collapse into rain on the schedule of the others. It hangs, and in the hanging there is a warmer dark, and in the warmer dark the sound is wrong in a new way: the cracks and the tearings arrive and then stop, absorbed, the way a voice would be absorbed if the people ever used one. The underside-walkers will not cross the threshold. The threads in the pockets at the rim are thicker, older, built into lids that have not been floors for anyone. A cord left at the edge of that hollow is never answered. The people add no knots. They give the place the wide berth they give a crest that has started to sing with a scar shaped like a body, and they do not say, even in cord, whether they think something lives there or whether the plant, in one place only, has started to listen.

What lives on the plateau, then, is a set of ways of using an immense slow violence without being erased by it: stick, ride, file, wait in ice, speak in cord, leave before the leaving is too late. And under all of those, not kinder, the growth itself, which does not notice that it is inhabited, and which would sound the same if it were not—except for the patches of quiet on the crests where something has eaten the rime, and the lids that should not hold, and the platforms with their eyes, and the one hollow where the noise goes in and does not come back.
