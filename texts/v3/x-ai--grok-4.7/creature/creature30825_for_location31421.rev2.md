The smallest residents announce themselves only by subtracting a little motion from the mat. A frond nods twice and stops, and what caused it is already the color of the copper it has been eating. They are the size of a thumb-joint, hard, and slightly warm, each one a walking scale that rasps the plated skin of the growth and lays the same metal down again on its own back until it looks like a drip that decided to keep its shape. They do not hide. Hiding would be a kind of announcement. They match, and when the hairs of the thicket lift before a discharge they clamp and go still, because the seam of light that thickens a frond will also solder a careless body into the mat. Some of the stones you step over are last season's unclamped. The rest tick on, opening lanes that close behind them, converting shine into shell at a rate the thicket can spare.

Under them, and slower, are the ones that take the soft growth rather than the plate: low mouths, almost no height, a belly that rides the damp film and leaves a darker wake where the salt-crust has been lifted and the filament beneath breathed. They do not need the tarns often. Their patience is chemical, the same patience as the plants, so a day without drinking is not a day of want. What they cannot use they return, concentrated, in small dark coins of dung and in the rubbed bright patches where a hide met a sinter spine. Other traffic finds those coins. The thicket, over a time you will not watch, leans that way. This is as close as the place comes to a path.

The takers of these are almost not visible as animals. You learn them by a lane that opens and does not close, and by a quiet inside the quiet, a prickling that stops being answered. They hunt by local subtraction. A body that navigates by the charge in the spines can be made, for a moment, mapless: the hunter stills a hand's-width of the thicket's weather, and whatever was reading that weather stops, and the stop is enough. There is no cry to carry. There is a dimple, later, at a tarn's edge, or a scatter of shell like spilled shot, already being plated over. Nothing is wasted so much as reassigned. Even the appetite here is a kind of banking.

What crosses the open gravel is the same economy stretched until it looks like solitude. They are low because the air will not pay for height, heavy because mass holds heat and a little water and the metal they cannot help accumulating. A shoulder comes barely to a knee. The back is broad, planed, shedding dust and rime, and older individuals ring, very softly, if stone touches them—the same small cold bell as a jacketed stem, dying at once in the thin air. They breathe seldom. A flank lifts, holds, and forgets itself again, so that a slope can be occupied and still offer no sound but weather. Their feet are wide and almost silent, padded with the same black mat-stuff as the basins, grown, not worn. Tracks appear fresh and then nothing for a long time, because a single animal keeps a valley's worth of courtesy between itself and the next, and what looks like a herd is a scatter that would take a day to gather and has no reason to.

Some carry eyes, and these are the ones that work the pale reaches, where the electrical spines thin out and the bruised light still pays a little. The eyes are wide, low-lidded, the color of the tarns, and they do not flick. A turn of the head is an event. Some do without eyes entirely, and these are not a second people so much as a later weather of the first: they keep to the basins, where a frond is a better map than a sky, and they move along the charged spines the way a hand follows a rope in a dark stair. Between certain rises you feel them before any shape resolves—a flock of prickling across the wrists, answered, adjusted, let go. That is speech enough. A hillside can be in conference and give you nothing else.

They do not tend the basins. They are one of the basins' moving parts. Crossing, they export what the fringe combed from the wind; rubbing, they seed a spine with a particular metal; dying in the right hollow, they become a bank the next thicket can spend. Their young are not carried in any way that would announce itself. In the warm mouth of a tower that has not yet slumped, or in the slurry where one has, a heat that is not quite weather gathers a gel of ore and damp, and something in it takes the habit of a pulse. You will not see this either. You may find, in a chimney that breathed on your palm when the others did not, a darker recess and a faint regular warming, like a thought the rock is having. Leave it. It is not for you, and it is not finished becoming itself.

That is the other life of them, the one that takes longest to recognize: they enter the towers. When a route's damp fails, an individual climbs the layered throat—slowly, with the patience of something that has already lost the argument with the wind—and plates the entrance nearly shut from within, laying metal the way the fronds do, until the mouth is a seam and the body is a warmer dark behind it. They can keep this for longer than a season. Metabolism banked like moisture. A dry century is not, to them, a different kind of time. When the plateau's slow accounts turn and a basin migrates toward that chimney, or a slump opens the seam, they come out plated thicker, lower, sometimes blind where they had been sighted, and take up a route as if the interval had been a pause in a step. The towers that look like memory of steepness are also memory of residents. Some of the rubble fans are not failures only. They are molts.

What they want is modest and exact. A film of water on the shaded side of something. A spine that still holds yesterday's charge, so the news of who crossed is not yet weathered off. Enough metal in the hide to keep the cold from finishing its sentence, and not so much that a joint locks and the body becomes landscape before it has chosen where. Distance, which is not loneliness; the open ground is the matrix, and richness is islanded, and the islands are already full. They do not build. A rubbed place, a dung coin, a plated seam, a route the thickets lean toward—these are the whole of their making, and all of it can be mistaken for weather if you do not stay.

If one meets you, the meeting is mostly a revision of distance. The head does not rise. An eye, if there is an eye, takes the light you are interrupting and files it with other brief brightnesses, drizzle and discharge and the pale of a new slump. Along the nearest spine a prickling goes out, not at you, past you, informing the slope that a loud breath has entered the local law and will, with luck, leave. The animal adjusts its line by the width of its own courtesy and continues. It was not sparing you. It was keeping the rate: add nothing that must be subtracted later, spend nothing the basin cannot bank, refuse the spectacle of being seen.

There is a last resident, and it is easy to miss because it is the size of a basin and the speed of a migration. Not a mind floating over the plateau, and not a god in the tarn. In a few of the older hollows the thicket is no longer only thicket. The fronds at the center are continuous under the mat, one body sharing a root-warmth with the water, its spines the same spines the eyeless follow, its chemistry the vent that drives the drizzle-column above it. The crossers are not its cattle and not its priests. They are the part of it that can leave. When they return—when they rub, when they drop the concentrated coins, when at last they choose its hollow to lock and plate and wait—the basin thickens by that much, and the gray weather standing over it stands a little longer into the dry. You can live your whole walking of the plain and call this a place. Stay through the slump of one tower and the leaning of one route, and the place looks back, not with an eye, but with the adjustment of its damp around you: a withholding, a measuring, the same courtesy the low bodies keep, practiced at the scale of a valley.

Then the lid of cloud stays where it was. The gravel ticks once under a wide foot and forgets the tick. Whatever lives here goes on spending current, banking moisture, failing quietly when it has grown past what its base will bear, and beginning again in the warm mouth of the failure—resident as the cold, and no more interested in being witnessed than the stone.
