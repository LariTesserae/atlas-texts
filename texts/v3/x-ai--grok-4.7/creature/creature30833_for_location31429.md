What lives there has learned the shelf the way a hand learns a tool that still bites: by using only the parts that answer, and by never asking the rest to be kinder.

The first citizens are not the ones you see. They are a felt, a living film on the grain of the new rock, pale where the sun strikes and green-black where the seep keeps a face damp overnight. They drink light the way the lichens do, but faster, and they die back by noon into a crust the color of flour. By the time a boot finds them they have already spent their day. They are why the mats can root in a place that has not yet made soil. They are also why a palm set flat on the sunward bench comes away dusted, and why that dust, rubbed, smells faintly of hot stone and something sweet that has no flower.

The plants that follow them are not one community. Along the thread of water they are crowded, competitive, almost rude: cushions shouldering cushions, shrubs throwing short woody elbows into the light, leaves small enough to refuse the wind that rarely comes and waxy enough to refuse a sun that does not know how to be moderate. They flower in shifts, not in a season. A cloud is a winter. A clear hour is a June. Some of them set seed between one shadow and the next, and the seed does not travel far; it falls into the same crease, and the crease becomes a lineage. Off the water they are exiles, single plants in cracks, older than they look, each one a decision not to die that has been renewed every bright morning for years. Break one and the wood inside is dense, yellow, and dry. They are not lush. They are stubborn in a grammar the wet mats have not needed to learn.

The insects live inside that grammar and also inside the other. On the mats they are a traffic: small dark beetles that clip petals and do not fly unless touched; flies with bodies like beads of metal that stand in the flowers only while the flowers consent to be open; a moth, commoner than the daylight suggests, that shelters under leaves and flies in the minute after a cloud arrives and before the air cools, as if dusk could be manufactured by weather rather than by the turning of the earth. At the seep’s edge, spiders the color of grit wait with their legs drawn in, not because they are hiding from you, but because a web is a luxury the air will not support. They eat what walks to them. They are patient in a way that looks like stone. Higher on the walls, in the joints, a different insect drills the cushion-plants that have taken the vertical—a slow, almost inaudible industry—and the gray birds know the sound, or the result of the sound, and work those faces in spirals, eating as they climb.

The birds are the shelf’s only regular argument with distance. The small ones, gray-backed, thin-voiced, belong to the walls and the wet line. They do not sing. A tick, a softer tick, the tear of wings: that is their whole speech, and it is enough, because everyone who needs to hear it is already within an arm’s length in a place where sound dies. They drink in turns, as the mammals do, not from politeness but from a pact with exposure. One on the lip of the pocket means the rest may come. None on the lip means the pocket is a story you do not enter. They nest, if nesting is the word, in fractures still sharp enough to cut a breast feather, and they line those fractures with stem-wool stolen from the shrubs and with their own down, and they raise young that learn the pause-and-resume of cloud-shadow before they learn the shape of a hawk.

The broad-winged ones are only half residents. The shelf is their porch, not their house. Three perches, whitening underneath, a hierarchy enforced by short silent scrambles, a facing toward the notch when the day fails. They hunt the slopes below, where the mountain is already older and life is louder, and they return to sleep where almost nothing can come at them from above. The one that remains after the others have dropped into the valleys is not a sentinel in any tender sense. It is the bird that won the last perch. It will leave when the light on the upper wall is gone, or it will stay if the air on the slopes has turned against it. The shelf does not keep it. The shelf allows it.

The small mammals in the south wall are the nearest thing the place has to a people. Round-eared, thick-furred, economical with sound, they have taken the fresh joints because the joints are new enough to be clean and fractured enough to be deep. They harvest the seed heads at the edge of the mats with a fury that is only concentration. They dry stems in the upper cracks, where the sun stays longest, and the stores smell, if you put your face to the rock, of hay and cold mineral. One watches while the others drink. The watcher is not always the same animal; the office moves. Their alarm is a tick because a squeak would spend itself in the thin air before it reached the back of the colony, and because a tick is also the sound of the birds and of stone, and so it does not advertise its meaning to every ear. They know the five-toed hunter by the change in the birds, and by a pressure in the quiet, and they are gone from the seep before you would swear the hunter has arrived. They reappear starting with a watcher. The pact holds.

They have a burial the shelf did not ask for. In a crack too narrow for an adult, two low in the wall, there are sometimes small bones and a wad of nest-wool, and the dust of fresh fracture sifted over them by the mountain’s own restlessness. Not ceremony. Consequence. The colony uses the crack above and does not use the crack itself. That is as close as they come to memory.

The hunter is not of the shelf and is the reason the shelf’s manners exist. Low, thick-furred, colored to the broken rock, it crosses. It does not den here; the joints are too tight for it, and the shelf is too open, and the water is a thread rather than a living. It comes when the drinkers are many, drinks upstream where the thread is narrowest—upstream, always, as if it understood contamination, or as if the pocket were simply a place where prey looks at prey—and leaves by the scree, loosing a few stones that speak once and stop. Its tracks are sharp and temporary, which suits a life lived as a series of crossings. The claws prick deeper on the turn. It does not call. If it misses, it does not thrash the ground in frustration; the shelf would only swallow the sound. It goes down into older country, where a voice can be spent and partly returned. What it takes with it is a mammal, or a bird that misread the lip of the pocket, or nothing. The shelf refills either way. That indifference is the hunter’s true relationship to the place: it is fed or it is not, and the mats go on flowering by the hour.

And there is the other visitor, the one who stood the stones.

Whoever it is does not live on the shelf. The fiber cord says so: inner bark of a tree that needs a valley, twisted when it was wet, stiff now, tucked under the brace of the cairn as if the cairn were a pocket and the cord a thing you might want again. The flake of struck stone says the hands were competent and in a hurry; the edge is still mean, the bulb still clean, the work done here and the product carried away. The fire-ring says a night, or part of a night, and a meal of woody stems, and no second fire. The cleared grit has begun to slump back. Lichen on the cairn is a stain, not a claim.

What can be said of them is only what the shelf kept. They come from below, where there is bark to twist and time enough to learn a knapper’s break. They know the notch as a way, or they are trying to know it, and the three stones are for the hour when cloud fills the cut and the exit becomes a rumor. They are not herders yet; nothing has grazed the mats, and the mats would show it. They are not builders. They pause. They drink where the animals drink, which is why the fire-ring sits under the south wall and not beside the pocket—close enough to water to matter, far enough that a night’s sleep does not put a body between a colony and its seep. That placement is the most intimate fact the shelf holds about them. They understood, or they learned in one cold hour, that you do not lie down on the thread.

They may be one person, returning. The cord suggests an intention to return. Or they may be several, kin or strangers, each adding nothing the rock bothers to distinguish. In the angled last light their shadow, if they were standing at the cairn, would reach the seep. They are not standing there now. The shelf has already resumed them into absence.

Under all of these, and not separate from them, something else lives here that is harder to name because it has no body of its own. It is the agreement. Cloud crosses, and the shelf pauses: flowers concede, insects lift, drinkers finish, the watcher-mammal flattens, the remaining broad-winged bird does not shift its feet. Light returns, and a condition is met, and the same ground resumes without discussion. No one on the shelf is in charge of this. The film on the rock, the cushions, the bead-flies, the gray birds, the colony, even the hunter in the timing of its crossing—all of them have fitted their lives to a sun that is too strong and a cold that is too constant and a water that is only a thread, and to each other’s alarms. The young mountain is a partner in the agreement. It spends grain. It opens joints and closes, by collapse, the rooms it offered last season. The colony is already in a crack that did not exist when the fire was lit. The seep is already cutting a groove the color of the rock’s interior. In a few years the pocket will be stolen and the drinkers will have found, or failed to find, another pause.

What lives on the shelf, then, is not a population so much as a schedule. Brightness is currency. Damp is a street. Silence is the price of being heard by the few who matter and not by the one who crosses. Everything that stays has accepted a life measured in short summers manufactured by stone and cloud, and everything that does not accept it is only passing through—the hunter, the person with the cord, the broad-winged birds dropping toward older valleys where sound can live.

At dusk, when the rim alone is lit and the seep smokes where cold air meets water that crossed warm rock, the schedule is still running. Seed is being stuffed into a cheek. A spider is eating. A single stone in the gutter gives up its place. The cairn holds a cord no hand is reaching for. The shelf is almost entirely unfilled, and it is full.
