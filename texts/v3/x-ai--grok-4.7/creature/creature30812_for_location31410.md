What lives here does not stand up to be counted. The first occupancy is the mat itself, and it is not a covering so much as a body that has learned the shape of the dish. Moss and sedge and the low cushions do not merely grow across the rock; they hold the water that would otherwise sheet off and vanish, and in holding it they make the only soil the basin will have for a long while. Each season’s stems flatten into the fiber beneath them. What looks, from the shoulder, like a dull uniform green is, at boot level, a packed architecture of living tips, dead weave, and cold mineral film. It breathes in the only way such a body can: by taking light where the glare allows and by staying wet enough that the chemistry of decay never quite stops. Cut a rectangle from it and the edges weep for weeks. The scar does not heal by replacing what was taken so much as by creeping inward from the uncut margin, a slow anastomosis, the living part remembering the outline of the absence. Nothing in the basin is more permanent than this, and nothing is more indifferent to the noise.

Under the mat, and sometimes in the seams where the plants have been torn open, something smaller works without ever showing a track. It is not an animal you could name from a distance. It is the turnover itself—threads of growth and collapse so fine they register only as a change in how the water smells when you kneel and pull the fiber apart. Cold limits it. The rock offers almost no dissolved gift. So the work stays modest: a darkening, a slight give under the hand, the sense that last year’s stems are no longer quite last year’s. This is the population that does not migrate and does not fail to appear. It has no season of absence. When the cutters haul a sled of sod toward the mill, they are moving a piece of that population into the beaters, and the beaters do not distinguish between what was still organizing itself and what had already begun to come apart.

The people are not residents. They come up the shoulder while the glare is still climbing, and they go down again while the racks are still throwing blue bars, because there is no floor here that will keep a sleeper dry and no wall that will keep the roar out of a skull past nightfall. Their occupancy is a circuit. A crew of a few—never enough to fill the sightlines—moves between the marked plots, the sled path, the pumphouses, and the mill. They know the rotation of the cuts the way a herder knows which pasture was used last, except the memory is written in darker rectangles that close too slowly to forget. Hands learn the weight of a sod when it is full of water and the different weight when the press has taken the water out. Voices are saved for the lee of an iron wall or for the walk back down, where the noise finally thins. On the mat itself they work by gesture and by the agreed order of the day. A person standing still is usually listening for a change in the machinery, not for an answer from the basin.

They do not build toward staying. The cribs under the pumphouses are maintenance, not foundation. The cable is a tether to a generator that must not be allowed to drift, not a promise of lights in windows. Tools left out are lashed or stood upright so the mat will not close over them before the next visit; even so, a wrench set down in the fiber for an hour comes up filmed, and one forgotten until the following rotation comes up hosted, the moss already testing the metal for purchase. The workers’ marks are the survey laths, the poles, the corduroy that the plants are swallowing, and the pale sheets on the racks. When they leave, the basin does not look abandoned so much as returned to its ordinary rate. The machines continue. That is the point of how the place is run: the beaters do not require a watcher every minute, only a return before something binds or the penstock loses its feed.

What occupies the sound is not alive, but it is the most continuous presence after the mat. The mill does not rest at dusk. Belts keep their interval. The knock of the presses does not consult the light. Water in the wheel housing keeps the same thick note whether anyone is in the yard to hear it. Out on the open floor of the basin that sound is not background; it is the climate the other living things must endure or be unable to enter. The single bird that crosses the saddle does not land because there is nowhere the roar is low enough to make landing worth the risk, and nothing in the pools is large enough to repay the attempt. If there are insects in the cushions, they are the kind that do not need to sing. The workers’ ears adjust until a shift in pitch means a belt or a bearing, and until then the roar is simply the condition under which sheets accumulate and voltage holds.

There is a thinner life at the weeping lines on the knobs, where the rock gives water in steady stripes and the moss fans are brighter than the mat below. That brightness is occupancy too—growth that has found a reliable drip and thickened there, a series of small, repeated decisions written in color. Nothing grazes it. The fans stay until the rock face weathers enough to change the drip, which will not be soon. Farther out, the young seams are places where the mat has failed to hold and the water has begun to prefer a line. Their banks, such as they are, host the same plants in a slightly different posture, stems tugged downstream, roots testing whether the slot will stay. These are not yet habitats with names. They are arguments the basin is having with itself about where water will go, and the plants are both the material of the argument and the first responders to its outcome.

At the racks, for the hours the sheets are stiffening, there is a temporary population of surfaces that did not exist in the morning: pale, fibrous, still carrying the smell of the beaters, taking the glare and throwing it back harder than the wet mat does. They are not alive. They are the mat translated into a form that can leave. By the time the crew descends, some of them are already dry enough to stack. What remains of the living basin is what was not cut this rotation, plus the scars, plus the water still moving under everything with that sliding sound no engine quite covers. The people carry the day down with them in their clothes and in the small injuries of cold metal and wet fiber. They do not leave anyone behind to keep a lamp. The basin’s night, when it comes, is the same machines, the same seep, the same mat releasing heat it barely stored, and no voice.

If you ask what lives here in the ordinary sense—what eats, shelters, and returns—you will have to answer with a short list and then a longer one. The short list is the growth that makes the floor, the unseen turnover inside it, and the crews who borrow the daylight and refuse the dark. The longer list is the set of habits that have taken hold without requiring a resident animal: the way the plots are remembered by their scars, the way iron is raised on rock so it will not sink, the way the generator is trusted to hold one voltage through the hours no one is listening, the way a seam is checked each visit to see whether it has lengthened by a pace. Those habits live here more reliably than any person. They will still be operating when a crew is late, or when a season is poor, or when the bird crosses and does not come back.

Nothing in the basin hurries toward a fuller tenancy. The rock is too new, the water too poor, the noise too even. What has arrived has arrived as process: plants that store and creep, people who cut and press and leave, machines that do not know how to pause. Stay through the lowering of the glare and you will not be joined by anything that has been hiding. The pools stay empty of rising forms. The racks tick as they cool. The cable sags the same degree it sagged at noon. Whatever lives here is already visible, if you count the mat as a creature and the circuit of work as a kind of dwelling that does not include sleep. The rest of the ordinary living world is still on its way, or has looked in from the saddle and kept going.
