They do not announce themselves as a people. You notice a habit first: someone crossing the shallow sheet without lifting their feet clear, as if the water were a relative they refuse to insult by stepping over. Then you notice the same refusal in a dozen other bodies, and only afterward the bodies themselves.

They are warm-blooded and upright, though upright is a courtesy they extend to doorways rather than a law of their skeletons. The tallest of them comes to where a fused leaf would split into a stomatal door; the smallest children come to the height of the weep-line, and walk in it on purpose, because the surplus is sweet and because they like the temperature of the city’s unspent effort against their shins. Skin runs the colors of the felt and the stored light—cork-pale, vein-green, the dim copper of the specification halls—but it is skin, not leaf. It sweats. It scars. It takes the delayed daylight and does not store it; by evening a person who stood all afternoon in a noon that was secretly morning looks merely sun-tired, the way anyone would.

What distinguishes them is not a visible organ. It is a patience located in the hands and in the mouth. They carry seeds in flat cases grown from the same cork as the ground, cases that smell faintly of the quarter they belong to: bitter for exchange, resinous for sleep, metallic where a constant is being kept bent. A seed is not planted so much as read aloud to a site. The reading is not speech in the ordinary sense. It is a sequence of pressures—tongue, fingertip, the small heat of a palm held a finger’s width from a petiole—and the site answers by becoming briefly otherwise. You can watch a woman specify a cistern and see that the gesture is old in her wrists, older than her face. Children practice on lawns that do not mind being asked to be, for a few breaths, slightly less heavy. When they get it wrong the lawn shrugs back to itself and the child sits down hard and laughs, and an adult nearby does not correct the laugh. Failure here is a local event. It does not teach the plain outside the rim anything.

They have names, and the names do not stick to bodies the way names stick in stone towns. A name is a continuing argument with a particular revision-rhythm. You meet someone who is called, in the bitter quarter, the one whose floors rise on the third tick; the same person, near the sleep-halls, is called soft-resin, or the one who folds late. If you ask what they were called as children they will give you a sound that is half a frog-pair’s claim and half a slur of water changing grade, and they will be amused that you wanted a syllable you could keep. Kinship is reckoned by which structures have revised while you were inside them, and by who caught your bowl when a platform tilted. Blood is acknowledged and not decisive. A person may have been born in a thicket that exhaled around their mother, and that thicket, three revisions later, may be a canal, and the canal is still, in some register no one bothers to mythologize, an uncle.

They do not worship. They keep appointments with forces that would punish neglect. Capillary law, viscosity, the memory of hills—these are not gods. They are colleagues with poor tempers and perfect memories. The specification halls exist so that the colleagues can be consulted without everyone having to rediscover, in a morning, why a one-cell wall does not burst. The keepers of those halls are not priests. They are the ones who can hold a bent constant in mind long enough to notice when it is beginning to cost the tissue too much heat. You can recognize them by a slight hurry and by the warmth of their forearms, which run closer to the temperature of working xylem than to the temperature of the air. They sleep badly. The city does not thank them. When they do the work well, nothing dramatic fails, and the market goes on catching bowls.

The rest of the town argues, trades, repairs manners, raises children, and negotiates with stems for doors. Trade is in amendments and in the right to stand inside someone else’s amendment for a stated interval. A cistern’s water, sweeter and heavier than the weep, is not owned; the interval in which it remains a cistern is leased, and the lease is a seed-case passed hand to hand until the tissue tires and the sweetness thins back into ordinary surplus. Arguments in the bitter quarter are therefore about duration and radius, not about possession. Voices carry. Nobody whispers a price. The insects that orbit the exchange do not care about the prices; they care about the resins people exhale when they are lying, and a liar’s breath changes the cloud’s shape, and everyone can see it, so lies are short and architectural—misstated radii, optimistic durations—rather than stories about distant plains.

They eat what the felt and the standing organisms offer without being asked to stop being architecture: inner skins that slough, sugar that beads where a revision stalls, small fruits that set in stomata left open too wide. Meat is occasional and never cleared-zone. A heron-thing that dies of age is shared the way a tilted market is shared, quickly, locally, with the practical grief of people who have watched the same ridge be knelt into and resettled a thousand times. They do not keep animals. The animals keep routes through them. A bright-eyed mammal in a hollow petiole is not a pet and not a pest; it is traffic, and if your wall is its street you learn its hours or you stop storing anything soft at petiole-level. Children name individual frogs and are corrected, gently, because a frog-pair’s claim is a few meters and a season of singing, not a personality you can carry home. The children name them anyway. The city has room for inaccurate affection. It has room, in fact, for almost every private inaccuracy, so long as the inaccuracy does not try to finish.

That is the nearest thing they have to a taboo: completion. A person who tries to fix a room against revision—bracing a wall, pinning a stair-helix, sleeping always in the same exhalation of the same thicket—is not punished by officers. The tissue simply spends them. Their sleep goes shallow. Their seed-cases germinate wrong, specifying amendments that collapse inward, little private weathers that will not hold water. Neighbors notice before the person does. Someone comes and walks them across the saucer the long way, through the inhale of whatever will have them, until the braced habit loosens. There are no jails. There is the rim, and beyond it the finished plain, and nobody is exiled there because nobody needs to be. The plain is already the sentence for anything that wants to be done.

They die as the structures die, which is to say they are unmade without catastrophe and are not therefore casual about it. A death is a revision that does not return the person. The body becomes felt faster than stone towns would find decent, and the felt drinks, and by the next delayed-morning the print of the body is a darker nap in the ground, walked on, wept through. What persists is the argument of the name: for a while others still call a rising floor by the rhythm of the one who is gone, and the floor sometimes answers, out of habit in the grain, not out of haunting. They do not build memorials. A memorial would be a completion. They let the name wear out at the speed of the city’s metabolism, which is the only honest speed they trust.

If you stay, they will not ask what you are. They will watch your feet. They will offer weep-water and a seed-case empty of amendments, the way a host offers a cup and a key to a room that may not be a room by night. Should you learn the pressures of the hand well enough to specify something small—a path that holds through one inhale, a bowl that does not skate—they will make the short sound they make when a child gets a lawn half-right. Not praise. Recognition. You will have joined the scatter: not the frogs, not the heron-things, not the towers, but the warm interval that walks between them and keeps the colleagues from boredom.

And if you stay longer than that, the first image will not hold, because it was never the whole inhabitant. The bipeds are the part that can leave a structure and still be someone. The structures are the part that cannot leave and is someone anyway. A tower that kneels into a hall is not equipment undergoing maintenance. It has preferences. It thickens where it has been thanked, which is to say where people have leaned in the way that does not demand a permanent door. It runs hot and troublesome where it has been braced. The specification halls are not libraries the people built inside plants; they are plants that consented to remember, and the copper-green quiet is the sound of that consent being renewed against the drag of ordinary physics. The heron-things, the frog-pairs, the mammals in the petioles, the insect-clouds—they are not scenery around a citizenry. They are the citizenry’s outer grammar, editing appetite, pollinating transactions, insisting on distances. Remove them and the amendments would still function and the town would go mean.

What lives in the saucer is a single unfinished animal with many ways of being alive. Mobile and sessile, voiced and fibrous, sweet where it surplus and bitter where it bargains. The people are its capacity to walk across its own revisions and to decide, locally, not to finish them. The plants are its capacity to be place at all. The water is its willingness. The animals are its attention, parceled out so no one attention can clear a zone. You can speak to a person and be speaking only to a person; that is not a lie. You can also put your hand on a wall and feel the inner thunder of small feet and the tick of reknitting and, under both, the older slope of hills that are no longer there, kept alive because this animal refuses to let a finished plain be the last word on what a body can do.

At dusk—which arrives here twice, once in the sky and once, late, through the tissue—they gather without forming a crowd. A scatter, still. Someone specifies a low warmth on a lawn and does not lease it; it is simply left, an amendment with no price, and people stand in it until the duration ends and the warmth leaves their ankles and the frog-pairs resume. No one makes a speech. The keepers of the halls are already walking back toward copper-green, counting costs. A child tries to name a frog and is not corrected, because it is the hour when inaccurate affection is part of the temperature. Beyond the rim the plain is dark and done. Inside it, the inhabitant shifts its weight, tower to hall, sheet to weep, person to person, and does not conclude.
