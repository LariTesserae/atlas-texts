They are counted as people, and for most of the day that count is enough. A few dozen of them, never a clean number, because someone is always on the stair and someone is always in the workshed with the door half shut, and the child who was on the balcony at lamp-lighting is the same child who is under the pump house at what they call evening, and no one keeps a list. They dress for heat and for edges. Sleeves end above the elbow. Trousers are cut off and hemmed again where a vine or a plate corner has already done the cutting. Their skin takes the damp the way the stone does: it darkens in the creases, at the wrists, along the hairline, and dries pale where a lamp sits close. Hands are the part of them the town has finished. Knuckles mapped with small pale scars from rivets and from the unexpected teeth of new brick. Nails short, not from vanity. They do not wear gloves. The rails are too warm, and a glove, once wet, never quite returns to the shape of a hand.

They move as if the lanes had been measured to their shoulders. Two can pass in the green tubes without turning sideways, and three cannot, so three do not try. When they meet they do not stop for long. A nod, a name used once, a question that is also an instruction—the belt on the second shed is walking left again; the fern on the chapel sill has found the flue and will need lifting before it seals it. Then they go on. Their voices stay low not because the place is sacred but because a raised voice would still be in the lane an hour later, sitting in the moisture, and everyone would have to live with it. Children learn this before they learn the names of tools. A laugh is allowed. A shout is a kind of litter.

Work is what they do with their hours, but it is not the work of building. The town is already built. What remains is keeping. A piston that must be heard to breathe. A sump that must be looked at and not touched for drinking. Leaves that must be persuaded back from a hinge, a tread, a mouth of pipe, not torn out—torn, they come back thicker, and besides, the geometry likes them. So the people prune with a care that elsewhere would be called gardening and here is called maintenance. They oil what ought to turn. They wipe what ought to be readable: gauges, a chalk tally on the boiler-room wall, the shallow groove in the shear face where someone, early on, cut a mark to remember how high the daylight used to reach before the rim plants closed over it. The mark is still visible. The daylight is not.

They eat what the heat and the damp will allow, and what the upper town sends down the stair in a crate that always seems smaller than the people who receive it. Flat loaves that do not mold if you keep them off the stone. Salt. A jar of something red that tastes of sun and is rationed by the spoon. Greens they will not take from the walls. The wall-growth is not forbidden by a sign. It is simply not food. A woman tried, once, in the first year, and the leaves cooked down to a slick that coated the pot and would not scrub off, and the pot is still behind the pump house, green on the inside, kept as an answer. Protein is scarce and treated as weather: sometimes a tin, sometimes nothing, and no one speaks of hunting because there is almost nothing to hunt. The pale lizard is left on its rail. The moths are left to the lamps. When a child asks what the lizard eats, the answer is the same as the answer to where the sump water goes. A small shrug. A hand already moving toward the next task.

Sleep is taken in shifts that follow the piston more than any clock. When the long exhalation comes, some of them are awake to hear it and some are not, and this is arranged without being written down. The ones who are awake do not call it a watch. They sit. On a warm sill. On the chapel step, where the old boiler-room floor still holds the day's heat in a way the newer brick does not. They listen for the breath, and for the space after it, and they do not fill the space with talk. If the space goes on too long, they do not go down to see. They wait. The town has taught them that some absences are part of the cycle and some are not, and that the difference cannot be hurried into showing itself. When the breath returns they stand, not all at once, and go back to a hinge or a belt or a child who has migrated in their sleep to a cooler patch of floor.

They have a story of how they came, and it is short, and they tell it as if it were about someone else. The cut opened. The upper town looked in and did not like what it saw—the heat, the wet, the sheer—and sent down the ones who could live with all three. Makers, they were called, because the first task was to make a floor that would hold. They made it. The engines came after, or with them, or were already in the rock and only needed housing; the story frays there and they let it fray. No one now was on the first stair. The oldest of them arrived as a girl and remembers the walls still pale, the way a cut apple is pale, and the first vine no longer than a finger, and her mother saying they would not stay. They stayed. The girl is a woman with a scar across the web of her thumb from a plate that was sharper then. She keeps the chalk tally. She is the one who notices when the sump has drawn down, and she is the one who does not wake the others to show them. In the morning, which is only a change in which lamps are lit, she says the ring was there. They believe her. Belief, here, is a practical thing. It lets the day proceed.

If you stay long enough to be mistaken for one of them—long enough that someone hands you a cloth for a gauge without looking at your face—you begin to see the thing the census misses. It is not another body in the lanes. It is the way a lane is never empty of attention. A leaf turned against the plan of the street is turned back before noon. A drip that has found a new path is returned to the old one, gently, with a bit of tin slid under the stem. They do not speak of the town as alive. They would dislike the word. But they treat it as something that notices when it is neglected, and that returns the favor of care with a stability so complete it feels like permission to exist. The piston breathes. The sump holds. The plants keep the angles. And the people, who were sent down to make a floor, have become the part of the machine that can climb a stair, wipe a brow, and decide, each time the breath is late, not to go and look.

There is one more, and they do not count it, because it has no hour and no doorway. You will not see it if you look for a shape. Look instead at what never quite belongs to the hands that tend it. The reflection in the sump, sharper than the leaves. The warmth in the rail after the lizard has gone, which is the same warmth as before the lizard came. The moment after the piston's breath when the whole Floor seems to have leaned a fraction closer to hear whether it will be answered. No one answers aloud. Something answers anyway: a belt takes up its slack, a vine settles into a joint it has not been guided to, the water—ordinary to the touch, not ordinary in its timing—rises to the lip of the ring and stops, as if a level had been agreed. The people feel this the way you feel a room when someone has entered it behind you and has not yet spoken. They do not turn. Turning would require a direction. They wipe the gauge. They lift the fern from the flue. They go on living in the cut, densely, quietly, usefully, and the thing that lives with them—older than their arrival, younger than the rock, patient as heat—goes on living in the keeping, in the breath, in the water that is not for drinking, and in the fact that the town, which should by now have softened, still cuts the hand that is careless with it.
