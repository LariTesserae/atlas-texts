They are built for the missing air, and you see it before you understand it. Chest first: a keel of rib that starts higher than a chest should and keeps going, so that even at rest they look as if they have just drawn half a breath they do not mean to spend. The shoulders sit forward of the hips. The neck is short, almost an apology, so the head rides close to the work and does not have to be lifted into thinner atmosphere than necessary. When one of them straightens from a bench it is an event with a cost. Most of them do not straighten often.

Their skin is the color the mineral film would be if the film had circulation: a dry pale that goes gray at the knuckles and at the rims of the nostrils, where the air has been scrubbing the same small doors for years. It does not flush. Flushing would be waste. Veins show only where the work has thinned them—inner wrists, the web between thumb and finger, the soft place under a jaw that is more ledge than chin. The hands are the oldest part of any of them. Even the young have old hands. Pads of callus mapped exactly to the tools of a given bay: a lathe-hand is not a press-hand, and you can tell which workshop a person belongs to by which finger has been sacrificed to shine. They do not bandage small cuts. The dry cool closes them.

Faces are narrow, and the narrowness is not hunger so much as economy. Eyes large, set wide, lids heavy—built, like the small animals on the ledges, for a dusk that never fully becomes day. The pupils take a long time to shrink when a furnace door opens, and in that lag the whole eye looks briefly animal. Mouths are small and mostly closed. You will watch an entire transaction—a housing passed up, a count confirmed, a disagreement about a tolerance—conducted with the chin, the angle of a wrist, two fingers held against a throat to mean *wait, I am spending breath on something else.* Speech exists. It is rationed the way the drums are rationed. A sentence is a purchase. Arguments, if they happen, happen in the evening when the presses cycle down and the air in a closed bay can be allowed to go stale and warm with many lungs, and even then the voices come out low and late, arriving at the listener after the mouth has already shut, so that conversation has the manners of a correspondence.

They do not all look like one family, though the cleft is trying to make them into one. Some are taller than the walkways prefer and go through life slightly folded, crown brushing the soles above, a shine worn into the hair at the top. Some are the right height and move as if the place had been measured on their bodies, which it nearly was. Children—there are children, though you miss them at first because they do not play in any open way—are indistinguishable from the harnessed stock until one of them looks up with that slow pupil and you see the hands, already mapped, already deciding which finger will shine. They learn the routes before they learn the words for the routes. A child of six can cross three levels with a tray of seed-fastenings and never speak, never spill, never ask the air to carry a name.

Names are used, but privately, close to the ear, or written. Painted numbers on the bays are public speech; personal names are not. You hear one only when something has gone wrong enough to justify it—a hand in a belt, a drum seal failed, a birth—and then it comes out cracked, because the speaker is not practiced. What they call each other in the ordinary hour is function plus bay: the upper-press one, the hide-cutter, the drum-count. It sounds like poverty and is actually precision. In air this thin, a precise short call lands; a lyrical one does not.

They eat as if eating were a machining operation. Food comes in the same drums that bring everything else that is not rock—dense, dry, divided into portions that do not require chewing into spectacle. There is meat from the penned animals below, used the way a careful shop uses a billet: nothing ornamental left on the bench. Broth is a luxury because broth is water wearing another name, and water has a ledger. You see the ledger in how they swallow. A mouthful is held, considered, sent down. No one talks over food. The jaw is already employed.

What they drink is warmer than the air and smaller than thirst. A cupped measure, passed, returned. The person who pours is a kind of official, though no one would say so. Their hands are cleaner than the lathe-hands and their eyes are on volumes, not on faces. Around them the others arrange themselves without crowding, which is its own skill: bodies that spend all day in a cleft the width of a street have learned the exact distance at which another chest can rise and fall without stealing. Touch, when it happens, is brief and placed—a palm to a sternum to say *breathe slower*, two fingers at a wrist to count what the air is doing to someone who has been too long at a furnace mouth. Affection looks like maintenance. Maintenance is how tenderness survives at this height.

They sleep in shifts, because the industry does not believe in night and the cleft has no weather to argue with it. Bunks are cut into the older shear where the rock has stopped surprising them, stacked like the drums, each slot just long enough if the sleeper lies on a side and draws the knees. A sleeper's breath is visible for a moment and then not. They sleep with their mouths closed and their hands loosely curled around nothing, still shaped to a tool. When the shift changes, the exchange is silent and exact: a body rolls out, a body rolls in, the blanket—hide, again, always hide—does not have time to cool. Dreams, if they have them, are not recounted. Recounting would be a speech too long to be worth the breath, and besides, what would a dream offer that the cleft does not already repeat?

The small animals are not separate from them. This is the part that takes longest to see, because the eye wants a line between keeper and stock and the cleft has rubbed that line thin. The furred ones know individual hands. They veer toward certain people and away from others with the confidence of creatures who have been correcting a route for generations of both species. A person will pause, barely, to let a shoulder of them pass, and the pause is not courtesy so much as grammar. Some of the animals sleep in the bunk-slots when a slot is between shifts, packed against a human sternum, lending heat, taking heat, their tiny harnesses unbuckled and hung on the same peg as a work-apron. No one remarks on it. Remarking would make it a choice, and it is older than choice.

The larger animals below are different. They are not companions. They are inventory that breathes, and the people who tend them have a heavier quiet than the rest, a quiet with guilt braided through it so evenly that it no longer reads as guilt—only as the particular slowness of someone who has to kill carefully because nothing can be wasted and because the pens are close enough to the walks that a bad death would be heard, late and incomplete, by everyone. Those tenders wash more than the others. Their forearms are the palest skin in the cleft, scrubbed back toward the mineral color, and they do not eat in the same bay as their work. That is the nearest thing to a ritual you will find. The rest of religion, if it exists, has been compressed into the way a hand is laid on new rock when a fresh face shears open overnight—acknowledgment, not worship. The mountain is not a god. It is a landlord that has not finished the lease.

They make decisions faster than they speak, which is why the settlement can edit itself hourly without argument you can hear. A bench needs six inches; two people glance, the glance is the vote, the bench moves. A motor needs a ledge; the animals are shifted before the family that sleeps nearest has fully woken, and the family, waking, reads the new absence and adjusts their knees. Authority is real but local, and it lives in whoever has been longest in a given bay, which is legible in the callus map and in how little they look up when spoken to by gesture. Newcomers—there are some, ferried in with the drums, still coughing the thick air out of their blood—are not initiated with stories. They are put on a route. If the route accepts them, they stay. If their chests cannot learn the ration, they are sent down again before the cleft has spent much water on them. This is not cruelty in their register. Cruelty would be letting a wide-lunged body take a bunk a narrow one could have used.

You begin, after an hour, to hear them anyway. Not words. The rhythm under the presses: a cough suppressed into a shoulder, the particular scrape of a certain pair of boots that everyone on the middle walk already knows means the drum-count is coming, so the measures should be ready and the mouths closed. A woman on the upper level—she has the folded height, the crown-shine—sets a housing down harder than she meant to, and three people in adjacent slots adjust their grips without looking, the way a school of fish adjusts to a shadow. They are entangled. The thin air did not make them kind. It made them so mutually dependent that kindness and competence have become difficult to tell apart, and they have stopped trying to tell.

If one of them were brought down into ordinary air, the first thing to return would be sound, and it would hurt. Voices would arrive on time. Their own breath would boom. The small precise calls would turn into shouts they did not intend. They would hate it, probably, for a day, and then the body would greedily learn waste again—flushing, talking, standing straight, spending a sentence on nothing—and the hands would remain old, and the eyes would take too long to shrink in full sun, and at night they would sleep on a side with the knees drawn, waiting for a sternum against their back that was not going to come, listening for a press-cycle that the lowlands do not know how to keep.

Up here they do not imagine that. Imagination of elsewhere is a long sentence. They have the cleft, the young rock still filming itself in chalk, the animals threading their ankles, the yellow slots of work that do not travel far. They have each other's distances, measured to the inch. When the shift-bell—a mechanical clack, not a ring; a ring would spend itself in the thinness—passes down the walks, they are already turning, already spending the next breath on the next exact thing, already themselves in the only size the mountain has so far agreed to hold.
