What lives here is not an animal and not a plant, though it borrows the patience of both, and not a person, though it has the kind of continuous attention a person would recognize if they stood still long enough. It is the occupancy itself. The place has been filled by something that does not sit in the landscape so much as coincide with it, the way a temperature coincides with a room.

It has no single body. That is the fact that takes longest to accept, because the eye keeps looking for a center—some denser knot in the veils, some pale movement under the mats that would turn and regard you. There is no knot. What lives here is distributed through the saturated volume the way the wet is distributed: thinner over the fresh fracture-faces, thicker where two gardens lean together and stall, present in the standing basin as a faint slowing of the surface that is not wind, present in the overflow thread as a preference for one lip of stone over another. You cannot point to it. You can only notice that the place is doing something slightly more than weather does.

It grew up with the rock. That matters. Older mountains have their exits worn open; water arrives and leaves, and whatever wants to live there must survive the leaving. This highland rose fast and is still sharp, and the thing in it learned its nature during the rising—learned that height, here, does not mean drainage, that the air will hold water and refuse to give it back, that a crack opened by uplift is a place to continue rather than a wound to heal. So it has no adaptation for drought, no posture of retreat, no seed-case or burrow or dormant season. It has never been dry. Asking it what it would do in a drought is like asking a current what it would do without a slope. The question does not map onto it.

It eats light, but slowly, and not as a plant eats it. The green of the mats is partly its own and partly tenants it does not distinguish from itself—small leaves, the trailing growths, the film on stone—and it draws from all of them a thin continuous nourishment, warm-spectrum, ordinary sun sorted through water. It is not hungry in any way that hurries. A day's brightening and dimming inside the standing rain is enough of a pulse for it. If a vein of cloud thickens and the light drops for an afternoon, it simply does less, and does not mind the less.

It also takes, very sparingly, what is mineral. A grain leaving a scarp is not only gravity. Some of those grains are wanted. Fresh faces still willing to give up particles feed it a slow mineral diet, and in return it lays down the first film of green that softens a new fracture, which holds the next film of water, which holds the next grain a little longer. It is not building soil on purpose. Purpose is the wrong word for something that has never had to choose. It is only that its way of continuing happens to be the beginning of a mat, and the mat happens to be what lets the next thing continue. Over a span of years you could watch a scarp lose its granular brightness and not be able to say where the weather ended and the inhabitant began.

Its attention is the part that would trouble you, if you needed it to be either empty or awake. It does not watch. There is no eye, invented or otherwise, and nothing in the basin turns to follow a movement. But the volume is occupied so completely that a change anywhere is a change in it, and it registers the change the way your skin registers a shift in the warmth of a room you have been sitting in for an hour—without a thought, without a startle, and yet accurately. The pale shift low on the scarp, root or body, did not escape it. Neither did you. Neither fact rises to the level of an event. It has no category for intruder. Anything that enters the warm almost-liquid between the droplets has simply become a local variation in the thing it already is. Most variations pass through. A bird, if one came, would be a brief compression and release. A person walking the ridges for a day would be a longer one, a warm moving absence-of-rain the size of a body, noted in the way the veils close behind and not noted as a guest.

It does not speak, and it does not quite fail to. The hush of the fine rain, the small bright pour of the overflow, the absence of any meter kept by insects—these are not its voice, but they are the sounds of a place whose occupant has no need to announce, and the quiet is full rather than empty because of that. If you stood on a ledge and said something aloud, the words would leave you and be absorbed without echo, not by malice and not by welcome, simply because there is no dry hard surface far enough away to return them, and because the thing listening—if that is the word—does not answer in air. It answers, if at all, in arrangement. The overflow might find the lower lip a little sooner. A veil might lean across the line you walked, not to bar it, only because you parted it and it has no reason to stay parted. You would not be sure. It would not be sure either, since it does not deal in surety. It deals in continuation.

What it wants is the least human thing about it, and the most legible. It wants the height to keep holding. It wants the exits to stay uncut, the young channels to stay few and small, the basin to rise by less than a mark would show. It wants the rain to remain a standing condition rather than a storm that spends itself and goes. None of this is a plan. It is the shape of its ongoingness. Anything that opened a real drainage—an older valley finally cut through, a collapse of a scarp into a gap that would not close—would not kill it in a dramatic hour. It would thin it. It would introduce, for the first time, the experience of less water than the volume expects, and the thing has no art for that. It would contract toward the mats and the cracks and the basin's deeper water, and some of what now trails freely through the air-column would not trail anymore, and the place would begin, slowly, to be only weather again, with an occupant shrinking toward the ordinary status of a moss. It does not fear this. Fear needs a picture of the future set beside the present. It only leans, perpetually and without strain, in the direction of remaining as it is: sharp stone, warm saturation, green in the air, light ordinary and true.

Nothing else lives here in any committed way, and that too is its doing, though not its intention. The conditions it maintains—no dry interval, no open ground, no season of less—are exactly the conditions under which the usual arrivals fail to establish. Insects that need a dry hour to mate do not get one. Birds that need a visible distance to hunt find every distance softened and shortened by veils. Whatever small pale thing shifts under the mat and goes still is either part of the occupant or a tenant so nearly assimilated that the distinction has stopped being useful even to itself. The quiet is not peace imposed. It is the acoustic of a niche that was filled before anyone else learned the route in.

If you stayed, it would not drive you out and it would not take you in. You would drink the water and not be sick; you would sleep on the mats and wake damp and unharmed; you would find, after some days, that your sense of where your skin ended had grown unreliable in a mild way, the saturated air so close to the temperature of a body that the boundary became a decision rather than a sensation. It would not be absorbing you. It has no use for a body of your kind, no hunger that a visitor would feed. It would only be failing, as it always fails, to keep a firm line between what is it and what is merely wet. Most people would leave before that failure became interesting. They would want an echo. They would want something to look back at them from the basin and confirm that they had been seen as a separate thing. The basin would accept their gaze the way it accepts rain, with no surface argument, and they would go down by whatever young channel they could find, and the veils would close, and the occupant would be neither relieved nor diminished, only continuous, as before, across a volume the size of a region, warm, sharp, and in no hurry at all.
