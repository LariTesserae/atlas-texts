The first thing you meet is not a face. It is a set of shoulders turned sideways in a lane, a basket held up out of the drip-line, and the small courteous pause that lets you pass. The people of the shelf are built for the width of their streets and the reach of their arms. They are not tall. Height is wasted here; the leaf-ceiling would only take it. They run broad through the chest and short through the leg, with a steadiness that comes from a lifetime of slick stone. Their feet go flat and their knees stay slightly bent, the posture of people who have never trusted a surface to stay dry under them. You can tell a newcomer from the lower country, on the rare days one is hauled up the cliff-hoist, by the way they lift their heels and look for somewhere to set a dry sole. There is nowhere.

Skin here takes the color of the damp rather than of the sun. It is not pale in the starved sense. It is a cool, even tone, a little grey at the temples and the knuckles, as if the mineral film on the stone had decided, over generations, to include them. Veins show blue-green under thin wrists. The resin works leave a harder mark: a honey-colored varnish in the creases of the palms that soap does not clear, and a faint sweet smell that stays in the nail-beds of the vat-tenders long after the furnace has been banked. Sawyers of the weeping stone carry a pale dust in the lines of their faces that the fog wets and turns to a mask by midday; they wipe it with the heel of the hand and the hand becomes the same color as the slurry pits. Nobody remarks on it. Clean, on the shelf, means not actively dripping, and free of leaves.

Hair does not dry. They cut it, or bind it, or both. The common style is a close crop above the ears and a knot at the nape, wrapped in a strip of waxed cloth so the wet weight of it doesn't soak the collar by the second hour. Children wear theirs loose until they are old enough to mind the channels, and then the knot appears, a small rite nobody has bothered to name. Beards are kept short for the same reason; a long beard becomes a wick. Eyes are the part of them the fog has trained most carefully. They do not squint. There is rarely anything bright enough to warrant it. What they have instead is a patience with low contrast, a willingness to wait while a shape finishes arriving. They recognize each other at a distance by gait and by the particular dark of a coat before they recognize a face, and they are rarely wrong. A person who startles easily does not last. The fog is always producing almost-people—a coatrack, a hanging bundle of sap-sacks, a trunk—and the town has learned not to spend its pulse on them.

They dress in wool that has given up any ambition of lightness, layered, the outer coat waxed at the shoulders and the hem where the drip hits hardest. Colors have mostly conceded. What was once rust, or a dull blue from a lower-country dye, weathers toward the same green-black as wet slate. The one brightness they permit is practical: a strip of pale resin-varnished cloth tied at the wrist or the basket-handle, visible in the lane when the fog sits down to the ankles, so that two carriers do not meet chest-to-chest around a blind corner. Buttons are horn or cut stone. Metal fasteners weep and stain. Everything they wear has been rained on more recently than it has been washed, and washing itself is a matter of hanging cloth in the warm lane outside the resin shed during the furnace stretch and turning it once. It comes back damp and clean enough.

Their voices match the town's acoustics. They speak at the volume the fog will carry to the person in front of them and no farther, which means you often do not know a conversation is happening until you are inside it. Sentences are short, not from dullness but from the way sound dies. A long story has to be told in pieces, across a lane, across a day, and they are used to holding the middle of a thing until the weather lets them finish it. They do not greet by shouting across open ground; there is little open ground, and a shout would embarrass the shouter by vanishing. Names are used sparingly once you are known. A tilt of the head, a shift of the basket to the other hip, does the work of hello. Children are louder, and are allowed to be, until the age when they start walking the channels. Then the quiet arrives in them the way the knot arrives in the hair—not as a rule handed down, but as a fit.

What they do all day is move water, sap, and stone along paths the shelf has already suggested. Before full light—the phrase is a courtesy; light here is only a thinning—the tappers go out under the meshed trees with shoulder-yokes and small iron spoons. The trees give best at the end of the night, when the fog has sat on them longest. A cut no wider than a thumbnail, low on the trunk, and the clear exudate beads and runs. They catch it in bowls of oiled wood and do not hurry. Hurrying bruises the bark, and a bruised tree sulks for a month. The best tappers can hear, or say they can hear, the difference between a tree that is ready and a tree that should be left: a slightly wetter tick in the leaves above it. Whether that is hearing or long habit, the yields follow the people who claim it.

By the time the tappers are back, the channel-keepers are already on their rounds. These are the closest thing the town has to a civic order, and they would be offended to hear it put that way. They walk the old water-threads with hooked poles, lifting leaves out of the glazes before the glazes become streams, opening a side-notch if a basin is rising too fast toward a foundation, closing it again when the rain walks off. They know every seam in the stone the way a person knows the unevenness of their own kitchen floor. A new crack is an event. They mark it with a dab of resin and come back the next day to see if it has lengthened. Arguments about water—whose channel flooded whose yard, who failed to clear the notch above the market—are the only arguments that regularly rise above a murmur, and even those are conducted standing in the disputed lane, both parties looking down at the water rather than at each other, as if the shelf were the one being asked to decide.

The sheds take the rest. Sawyers work the blocks that come up from the shallow quarries at the rim-side of town, where the stone still weeps and cuts clean. The slurry is not waste; it settles in tanks and is skimmed for the pale dust that goes down the hoist in waxed casks, the town's steadiest bargain with the lower country. Vat-tenders mind the resin through its stages: cold and thin in the morning vats, warmer and slower as the wheel's belts bring the heat around, then the hot finish during the single furnace stretch, when the shed is the only room on the shelf where breath does not show and people invent small errands in order to stand near the door. The resin, drawn off and cooled in shallow pans, sets into rounds the color of old bone. Lower-country buyers use it for things the shelf does not need—waterproofing, lamp-fuel cut with oil, a varnish for furniture that will never see this much damp. On the shelf itself it seals pipe-joints, coats basket-grips, and marks the cracks the channel-keepers are watching.

Between these there are the small trades a town grows when it cannot easily fetch what it needs. A person who stitches waxed coats and re-soles the flat boots with layers of resin-stiffened hide. A person who keeps the cistern-valves and knows which copper pipe sings when it is about to freeze—rare, but it happens in the early cycle when the fog is thin and the air goes keen. A person at the market court who receives what the hoist brings up and what the sheds send down, and who keeps the tally on a board rubbed with ash so the marks survive the damp. Grain comes up. Salt. Lamp oil. Sometimes a crate of dried fruit that the children smell before the tally-keeper has the lid off. Meat comes rarely, and is eaten the day it arrives, because nothing keeps. Most meals are from the broad-leaf plants, which are bitter raw and mild when steamed over a resin-stove, and from a meal ground out of a seed the floating mats in the basins set twice in a season. It is a diet that does not excite and does not fail. People are solid on it. Nobody is fat; the lanes and the rounds walk it off. Nobody is thin in the hungry sense either, except the very old, who narrow the way the trees narrow when a tap has been cut too often.

They live in the stone houses as if the houses were clothing rather than architecture. Doors are left open in all but the needling rain, because a shut door in this air goes soft and swells and then will not shut again properly; better to let the fog have the front room and keep the dry—drier—work in the back. Interiors smell of wool, mineral, and the faint sweetness of resin if anyone in the house works the sheds. Bedding is raised off the floor on stone benches built into the wall, clear of the seep. A house that has been in a family long enough has its habits mapped: this corner beads first, this joint stays sound, this window gathers a moss that the household has decided to leave because it slows the draft. They do not decorate in any way a lower-country visitor would count. A shelf of the bone-colored resin rounds, too flawed to sell, lined up by size. A child's collection of the small curled leaves from the dry circle, which are the only leaves on the shelf that ever go crisp, kept in a jar until they soften again—which they do, eventually, as if the house itself disapproved of the exception. Tools on pegs. A lamp. That is a room.

Kinship runs through yards more than through names. A household is whoever sleeps above the seep-line of a given foundation and shares its stove during the furnace hour, when people come home cold and stand in the warm lane first, not talking, letting the heat find the wet hems. Children move between houses with an ease that would look like neglect somewhere else and is only the size of the town. Everyone has seen everyone born, or nearly. The old do not move off into separate quarters. They take the benches nearest the stove and the rounds nearest the market, and they are consulted about cracks and trees because they have been watching both longer. Death, when it comes, is usually the damp's slow work on a chest, or a fall on stone that a younger knee would have caught. They bury in the one slope where the moss is thinnest, near the rim but not over it, with a round of waste resin set flush in the ground as a marker, because wood does not last and cut names on ordinary stone blur under the film. The resin holds the letters. In fog you find a grave by touch.

They are not solemn as a people. The quiet is logistical, not spiritual. In the market court on the day the hoist comes up there is a looseness, a willingness to stand around the crates and repeat news from below that everyone will have heard by evening anyway. Someone imitates the hoist-worker's lower-country accent, the hard vowels that sound like dry air, and the imitation is accurate and not unkind. Children play a game in the lanes that is mostly about not being touched by a dripping leaf while running, which is nearly impossible, and the loser wears a wet leaf on the shoulder until it falls off. During the furnace stretch the shed lane fills with people who have no business there, and the talk rises, just slightly, the only hour of the day when a sentence can afford to be long. They joke about the fog the way people elsewhere joke about relatives who will not leave: it is always sitting down, it has opinions about the laundry, it has moved the far end of the lane again. They do not joke about the circle.

The circle is where their practicality grows a second, quieter layer. No one preaches about it. There is no shrine, no story required of children beyond the warning, and the warning is delivered in the same tone as the warning about the cliff: that ground does not behave, stay off it. Adults do not step over the clean curve even when a tool rolls across. They reach with a pole. They do not discuss, in any gathering, what the circle is; discussion would require a theory, and the town's temperament runs against theory. What they have instead is a set of observed manners, held the way the channel-keepers hold the knowledge of seams. You do not pour water toward the line, even water that would not reach it. You do not build with your back to it if you can face it; the rendering sheds are the exception everyone regrets and no one undoes, and the people who work their rear doors are the ones who develop the habit of glancing. You do not leave a child there past the time it takes to cross and come back. The children who go anyway—and they do, they have gone for as long as there have been children—report the same nothing: dry shoes, no wind they can name, a feeling like being looked at by an empty room. The town believes the reports and still does not treat the nothing as permission. A nothing that keeps moss off and curls leaves is doing something. Doing nothing would look like the rest of the shelf.

There is one person whose work touches the line more than anyone else's, and the town allows it because the work is maintenance, not inquiry. She is old enough that her knot of hair has gone the color of the slurry dust, and she walks with the channel-keeper's hook even though the circle has no channel. Once in a while the wind that starts at the circle's edge pushes a pale curled leaf, or a scrap of blown moss, exactly onto the line, where it sits half in and half out, and the half that is in begins to crisp while the half that is out stays wet. She hooks these off. She does not step over. She has been doing this since before the current vat-tenders were born, and she will not say who asked her to start. If you walk with her she will talk about trees and about a crack above the market that has been the same length for forty years, which pleases her. If you ask about the circle she will point the hook at a leaf on the line and say that it shouldn't be left, in the tone she would use for a clogged notch. Then she moves on. People say she sleeps badly on the nights when the deck lifts and the lower brightness shows. They also say this about half the old, so it may mean nothing.

The young leave sometimes, down the hoist, and some of them come back. The ones who stay below send up word that the sun is a weight and the noise of birds is an insult and the ground is too far from the sky. The ones who return—usually within a season—walk strangely for a month, heels still lifted, voices still pitched for air that carries. Then the fog irons them flat again, and they take a tap-round or a place in the sheds, and if they speak of the lower country it is about fruit and about how far you can see, said without longing. Distance, to them, turned out to be a kind of exposure. They had not known they were fond of the lane's proper length until a valley refused to end.

What else lives here is harder to set beside a person with a basket, because it does not stand still to be counted, and because the town has declined to give it the status of a neighbor. But the shelf is not empty in the animal sense alone. The absence of beasts is a fact; the absence of attention is not. Anyone who has walked out past the last meshed trees in the hour when the fog is at its thickest and the sheds' hum has been swallowed knows the difference between being alone and being unaccompanied. Alone is the lane at the end of the furnace stretch, everyone gone in to eat, drip ticking, your own breath the loudest thing. Unaccompanied is the ground beyond the trees, where your breath is still the loudest thing and yet you lower it. The lowering is not fear exactly. Fear would have a direction. This has a distribution. It is slightly stronger on the side of your body that faces the circle, even when the circle is a long walk off and the fog has taken the landmarks. Channel-keepers feel it on certain rounds and not others, and they have learned which seams lie on the line between the town and the bare curve, and they clear those seams a little more often than the work requires, as a way of occupying their hands.

The jointless stone in the old foundations behaves, if you watch it across a season, like something that is still participating. It weeps when the open shelf weeps, even inside a house, even where no crack connects it to the weather. A household that has rendered over such a stretch finds the render darkening on the same schedule as the basins rising, a damp map appearing under the lime that no leak above can explain. They wipe it. It returns. After a few years most households stop rendering and stack cordwood instead, and the wood nearest the wall stays wetter than the wood at the outside of the stack, and rots first, which is the wrong order. People move the stack. They do not take the wall down. Asked why, they say the stone is structural, which is true and not the whole reason. The whole reason is that the wall is older than the idea of taking things down, and it has matched the shelf's pulse for longer than the house has existed, and a person does not casually silence a pulse they live inside of.

At the circle the participation is negative and therefore clearer. The dry stone is not dead stone. Dead stone, elsewhere on the shelf, grows a film within a day of being cut. This stone refuses film, refuses moss, refuses the small colonization of damp that is the shelf's ordinary definition of being a surface at all. It is held in a different state, actively, the way a palm is held open. Leaves that cross it lose their water not by wind alone—there is not always wind—but by a drawing-off, a preference of the moisture for anywhere else. A channel-keeper who once, against his own manners, flicked a drop from his pole onto the bare stone watched it fail to bead. It spread to a dark star and was gone before he could count ten, taken into rock that shows no pore. He did not tell this at the market. He told the old woman with the hook, who nodded as if he had reported a notch already on her round, and told him to flick the next drop the other way.

Whether this is a thing that lives, or a condition that the living shelf maintains the way a body maintains a temperature, the people do not decide, and the distinction matters less to them than it would to a visitor. What they know is the etiquette the condition requires, and they keep it, and in keeping it they have shaped themselves: the soft voices, the glances, the poles instead of hands, the willingness to let a foundation stone weep on its own schedule rather than force it into the schedule of a house. They are tenants of a place that finished becoming itself before they arrived, and they have been here long enough that tenancy has turned into resemblance. A child born on the shelf begins, by the time the hair is knotted, to pause at the clean curve without being told a second time. The pause is not dread. It is recognition, the same recognition that keeps a hand off a hot pan. Something is there that takes what is offered, water, attention, a leaf left too long, and prefers not to be offered anything. So they offer the pole, the glance, the long way around, and otherwise they go on with the taps and the vats and the slow wheel at the rim.

In the evening, which is only the fog thickening until the pale wrist-cloths are the last things to vanish, the town draws in. Lamps come up in the back rooms, small and yellow, each one a decision to spend oil. The wheel is left to turn unattended; it needs nothing at night but the trickle the channels still deliver. Belts creak to an empty shed. Out on the shelf the trees tick as the beaded water gathers and falls, and the circle, unseen, continues its dry work, a held breath at the center of a place defined by moisture. The people sleep above the seep, on stone that remembers the weather, and if they dream they dream of lengths—of lanes, of cracks that do not grow, of the drop beyond the rim that they do not need to look at. They wake when the tappers' yokes knock softly against a doorpost, which is as close as the shelf comes to a bell. Another cycle. The cloud has not left. Neither has anything else.
