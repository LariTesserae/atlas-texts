They do not arrive as a people. They arrive as a thickness that the basin has learned to wear, and only afterward do the thicknesses separate into kinds.

The smallest are the first to become individuals, and only because there are so many that sameness fails. A body the length of a finger-joint, pale as salt-crust, made of overlapping plates that never quite meet, so a dark line of the animal’s own interior shows along the back like a badly mended seam. They have no face you can hold still. What serves for a mouth is a cluster of stiff hairs and a rasp, aimed always down, and what serves for eyes are two dull beads set so wide they seem to look at the stone on either side rather than at anything ahead. They do not need ahead. The world that matters to them is a film thinner than a breath of fog, the mineral stain they worry at, and the bodies pressed against their flanks. Touch is their weather. A neighbor shifts, and the shift is news: damp this way, a rasp of fresher film that way, a heavier step coming that must be climbed or yielded to. They yield more often than they climb. The hide of them is a courtesy that has become anatomy. To be one of them is to be always slightly displaced by the next, and to displace the next in turn, and to find in that displacement the only map the shelf will give.

They do not sleep in any way a quiet animal would recognize. They stall. A patch goes slow, mouthparts still working at half-speed, plates barely ticking, and the stall spreads a body-length and dies, and another stall opens where the first did not. If you watch one long enough you see it pause with its rasp lifted, not feeding, not moving, the beads of its eyes unfocused on the gray leak of the sky, and in that pause it is briefly not traffic. Then a neighbor knocks it and the pause is over and the ticking resumes as if the pause had been a private error. They shed. The sheds are translucent curls no bigger than a fingernail paring, and the curls do not last: other small ones worry them apart for the mineral caught in the old plates, and the channels take the rest. A life among them is measured in sheds. You can count the pale curls caught for an hour in a seep and know that a generation has turned over in that acre, and that the acre has not noticed.

What they know, they know as local law. Do not stop in a main channel when the lane is opening. Follow a ripple until it dies, then start your own or join another. The film is never finished; the film is the work. There is no store and no season of plenty. There is the next mouthful of stain, and the body beside you that has not yet taken it, and the understanding, older than any of them, that the stain returns if the licking does not stop. They are the basin’s slow tongue. Without them the manganese would crust into plates too hard for anything but rain to argue with, and the rain does not argue hard enough.

The many-legged ones live in a different tense. They are built low and long, the color of stone that has been wet so long it has forgotten dry, and they carry on their shoulders a collar of grit that the water keeps adding to. The collar is not clothing. It is a consequence. A gland along the neck weeps a clear stickiness; grains find it; the stickiness does not wash off in the damp, only hardens at the edges and weeps again at the center. An old one moves under a little geology, a saddle of cemented sand and flake that clicks against the channel wall when the animal turns. Young ones are almost naked, their secretions still thin, and you can see the true hide: soft at the joints, plated along the back in a series of low ridges that will, if the animal lives, disappear under the crust. They do not all live. The channels are full of half-collars with nothing under them, grit-rings the water has not yet broken, and the small clickers use those rings as bridges when the pools run high.

Their legs are many and not all equal. The front pairs test; the middle pairs carry; the rear pairs shove, and shove again when the collar has grown heavy enough that carrying is no longer the whole problem. They walk the grooves the way a thought walks a sentence it has already said: not inventing the path, keeping it. Where a channel forks they pause, antennae — four of them, thick as wet twine — writing the air in short arcs. The air answers in chemistry: the passage of another of their kind, the mineral breath of a seep, the sharp wrongness of a pool that has gone still too long. They choose, and the choice looks like habit, and habit, repeated across more bodies than the shelf can show at once, is what keeps the fork from silting closed.

They are not gentle with the small ones, and they are not cruel. The clickers are road. A many-legged walker sets a foot and the hide climbs the foot or scatters from it, and the walker does not adjust except when the footing itself fails. Sometimes a clicker is caught under a plate and carried a distance, ticking against the belly, before it drops. The walker does not notice. What the walker notices is other walkers. When two meet in a narrow groove they do not fight in any way that spends them. They knock, collar to collar or flank to flank, a soft stone sound, and the knock is a measurement: how heavy, how recently secreted, how willing to yield the inside line of the channel. The lighter one climbs, briefly, over the heavier one’s crust, legs scrabbling for purchase on a geology that is not its own, and drops on the far side and continues. The heavier one does not stop. The knock was enough. In wider places they pass abreast and the knocks become a brief conversation of contacts, three or four, and then each is alone again inside the general noise.

If they have a private life it happens in the side-seeps, where the hum of the great ones is thinner and the footing is worse. There they slow. The secretions increase. Two will stand flank to flank for a long time, collars growing, antennae barely moving, while the small life closes over their legs and treats them as new stone. It is not rest. It is a kind of being-with that does not require the channel’s law. When they part, each carries a little of the other’s grit in its crust, a pale vein the next rain will not quite erase. You could, if you cared to follow one for days, read a history of meetings in the layers of its collar. No one in the basin cares to follow. The history is kept because the body keeps it, not because any mind returns to it.

The things in the pools are harder to separate from the water they inhabit. A flattened hand is the wrong name and the closest one. They are margins. A body that is mostly edge, spread under the surface, thickest at a center you locate only when the margin lifts. The underside is a pallor of cilia, or of something that does the work of cilia, stirring the mineral milk in a slow wheel. The upper side takes the color of whatever dust is drifting that hour: rust, gray, the faint violet of a manganese film gone thin. They do not hunt the swimmers because there are no swimmers of a size that would matter. The milk is their pasture, and the milk is also, in some hours, their speech.

A clap is a margin breaking the surface and falling back. One syllable, wet, without timbre enough to carry a mood. What carries is timing. A pool will run a sequence — short, short, long, a gap, three shorts — and a pool two shelves away will answer not by copying but by completing, as if the first had offered a shape with a missing side. The completion is not always accepted. Sometimes the first pool claps through the answer and the pattern collapses into single notes with no relation, and the hand-things settle, and the milk goes slack. Sometimes the completion holds, and a third pool enters, and for a little while the lenses across a reach of the basin are one instrument, played by bodies that may not know they are playing. The pattern does not store food or warn of rain. Rain warns itself. What the pattern stores, if it stores, is the fact of other pools: that the trough continues, that the drift of dust is shared, that a stillness here is not the only stillness.

They arrange themselves along the sideways settlings. When the milk drifts toward one stone rather than down, the margins slide until they lie parallel to the drift, edges almost touching, never touching. Contact is a different event, rare, and it looks like a mistake that both bodies spend a long time undoing — a slow peel, a clap that is only a clap, no sequence in it. Afterward each holds a little apart from the line, as if the line had become briefly unsafe. They can leave a pool. You see one hump itself onto the wet shelf, margin folding into a clumsy packet, and cross, slapping, to a lower lens, the clickers scattering from the unfamiliar touch. Most do not. A pool is a life. When a lens fails — silted, or drunk down by a dry week the basin sometimes manages — the margins do not migrate as a group. They flatten further, become almost indistinguishable from the damp stone, and the small ones write their crosshatch over them, and some fraction of them do not rise again when the water returns. The ones that rise clap once, alone, and wait. Often no answer comes. The wait is not grief in any face you could read. It is the same posture as feeding, with the feeding taken out.

Above them, the fliers are a single animal until they are not. A sheet, dark against the lid of cloud, holding a shape — lenticular, or a long hook, or a simple smear with a thicker leading edge — for longer than any wingbeat accounts for. Inside the shape the individuals are real: membranous, no larger than the clickers, bodies reduced almost to a knot between the flying surfaces, legs tucked or trailing in a way that snags nothing because there is nothing in the air to snag. Their keen is the sound of the sheet, not of the body. Alone, one of them is nearly silent, a soft tick at the top of each downstroke, lost the moment another joins. The formation is the organ. While it holds, they do not feed, do not land, do not break into the private errands a flying thing might be imagined to have. They occupy a volume and keep its edge. When the edge shatters, the reason is never visible from the ground. The sheet becomes bodies, and the bodies become a confusion of directions, and then, with a speed that feels like memory rather than decision, a new sheet knits at a different height and the keen resumes in a new key.

They land when the light thins, not because dark frightens them — the basin is never bright — but because the shift is theirs. They come down onto the shelves in a soft, rattling rain of their own, and for a short while the ground-hide is two populations, clicker and flier, distinguishable only by the way the newcomers hold their membranes half-open, drying or resting or simply not yet willing to be walkers. They do not eat the clickers. They lap the same films, rasp against the same stains, and the clickers make room with the same courtesy they give their own. By the time the great ones have moved off the main channels, most of the fliers have folded completely and become, to a careless eye, only more of the ticking hide. A few do not fold. They sit with membranes spread on the stone like scraps of wet leaf in a place that has never grown a leaf, and those few are still there when the next thinning of the lid lifts the rest of them. Whether they failed or refused or were simply late is not a question the basin’s noise can ask.

The largest bodies are the ones the place seems to have been worn for, and they are the hardest to meet as themselves. A lane opens in the hide. That is the first fact. The second is the hum, already present, already broad enough to be mistaken for the channel’s own friction. The body follows: long as a toppled column, low, plated in salts that match the seeps so exactly that a still one would be a ridge until it breathed. The plates are not a collar grown by accident. They are hide, renewed from within, ridged and overlapping, and the salts bloom on them the way they bloom on stone, because the animal leaks, slowly, at the seams, and the leaks are of a water close enough to the basin’s water that the same minerals choose it. An eye, if it is an eye, is a dark lateral slit that does not track. The head is not much narrower than the body. The mouth is a wide, downturned gap that does not bite the small life. It gapes, now and then, and takes in the damp air, and the hum changes pitch by a degree, and the gap closes. They feed as the others feed, on films and on the milk of pools they do not enter but skirt, lowering a lip to the margin and drawing a slow draft that leaves the hand-things unsettled for a clap or two. They are not predators. The basin has not needed predators. The mineral stain is enough, and the numbers are kept by cold, by failed pools, by the simple arithmetic of sheds and of collars grown too heavy for the legs under them.

What they are is passage. A great one does not wander. It travels a route so long that a single body may not complete it between one thinning of the light and the next, and the route is older than the body. You can see the age in the polish: a groove within the channel, lower than the walkers’ footing, rounded to the exact curve of these bellies. They inherit the groove and they deepen it, and the deepening is their only monument. When two meet, the hums occupy the air until the air is too small, and one stops. The stop is the yielding. The one that continues does not hurry. The one that stopped waits, hum held in, a pressure you feel more than hear, until the other has passed and the lane has closed behind it and opened again, and then the stopped hum resumes, lower, as if some of it had been spent in the waiting. They do not knock. They are too large for the courtesy of knocks. Their courtesy is the silence they grant, and it is brief, and it is the most complete silence the basin ever makes, and even then the ticking of the hide goes on underneath, indifferent, already writing the crosshatch back across the lane.

Whether any of them thinks is a question the place will not settle for you. The pool-sequences complete one another across distances no body walks in the time the answer takes, which means the news moves by air or by the drift itself, and yet the completions have the shape of replies. The fliers hold agreements that outlast the strength of wings. The walkers carry in their crusts a record of flanks they will not meet again, and they pause in the side-seeps as if the pause were owed. The great ones yield the groove with a discipline that looks, from the shelf, like recognition. None of this requires a mind that knows itself as a mind. It requires only that the basin’s life has been dense enough, for long enough, that signals and routes and the small mercies of yielding have been worn into the bodies the way the channels have been worn into the stone.

They have no outside. Nothing in their traffic refers to a green world, or a dry one, or a world where a single voice could be followed to its owner. A clicker stalled with its rasp lifted is not longing. It is stalled. A flier that keeps its membrane open through the ground-shift is not mourning the sheet. It is open. The hand-thing that claps into an unanswered pool is not calling a name. It is doing the only next thing its margin knows, and sometimes the next thing works, and a far lens answers, and the milk between them drifts, for a little while, in the same sideways pull.

If you stay, you stop looking for the inhabitant and start to feel the kinds as pressures: the ticking as a floor, the knocks as punctuation, the claps as a sparse grammar, the keen as weather, the hum as the slow sentence the trough is always in the middle of. They live by continuing one another. That is the whole of the society, and it is not small. A shelf of clickers is a city whose streets are rebuilt every time a walker passes. A chain of pools is a correspondence that can take all the gray hours between thinnings to finish a single shape, and then forget it, and begin another with no apology. The great ones are the roads the city is not allowed to close.

By the paler gray, nothing has resolved into a hero or a nest or a queen. The fliers have risen or failed to. The collared walkers are back in the grooves, crusts a grain thicker than yesterday, though yesterday is not a word they have. The clickers have shed and eaten the sheds. A great one is a lane somewhere in the mist, humming the note the basin will wear until that body dies and another, already in the groove behind it, takes the note up without a seam. The hand-things clap a sequence of thirty and stop clean, and in the stop the thread of water is audible, shy, almost a voice, and then the hide closes and the thread is traffic again.

What lives here is not waiting to be named. It is the continuation the basin does not announce: pale, jointed, crusted, marginal, membranous, immense, and all of it listening, in the only way stone and damp have ever required, to the next knock.
