They are fewer than the paths suggest, and they do not fill the place so much as keep it from closing. You meet them first as interruptions in the sound: a hand-cart that changes its knock where a plank has been replaced, a voice that does not rise above the beaters because it does not need to, the brief metallic answer of a wrench on a coupling that has been tight and loose in the same place for years. Only after that do you see a person, and usually only part of one. An arm in the gap between bales. The back of a neck at the dye vats, darkened where the steam condenses and runs. Bare feet on the store step, because the planks outside are wetter than the floor inside and no one tracks the difference on purpose.

They are not a people set apart by dress or by any story they tell about themselves. Cotton worn thin at the elbows, a strip of the same fiber they cut tied at the wrist against the chafe of the carts, a boiler-man’s shirt gone the color of the brick he leans on. The young ones and the old ones share the same short walk and the same habit of not looking up unless a ridge light has cracked or a boat has come in off schedule. Children grow up knowing which boards give and which piles sing when the river is high, and they are not picturesque about it. They are underfoot until they are tall enough for a cutter’s hook, and then they are simply another pair of hands on the morning roster.

What they share is a competence that does not announce itself. The woman who keeps the tally also keeps the store, and she can tell, from the drag of a sack across the threshold, whether the fiber inside was cut too green. She writes in a book that has swollen at the fore-edge from years of damp thumbs, and she does not trust the book more than the sack. In the pump room a man listens with his palm on the housing, not because the gauges lie but because the gauges tell him what the water is doing and his hand tells him what the machine has decided to do about it. He has a bad knee from a fall on the lower walk in a wet month nobody else remembers as special. He still goes down. Someone else would have to learn the particular cough the second pump makes before it loses prime, and there is not, at present, someone else.

In the quarters the walls are close enough that a cough in one room is a fact in the next. People sleep in shifts that follow the vats more than the sun. A lamp is left for whoever comes off the night boilers. Meals are rice, river fish when the bend is willing, greens cut from the same margins as the cane, and a sour pickle that lives in a crock behind the store and is never allowed to finish. They eat quickly and not in silence. The talk is small and exact: whose hook needs a new ferrule, which sluice is taking leaf, whether the boat expected Thursday will make Friday if the lower channel stays as it was at dawn. Argument happens, and debt, and the ordinary meanness of people packed into wet heat with the same three songs. It does not become a history. The place is too narrow for a feud to find a second room.

There is a person at the clinic who is not a doctor and does not pretend. She has iodine, gauze, a kettle, and a talent for boils, crushed fingers, and the cough that comes when someone has stood too long in the vat steam with his mouth open. She sends the bad cases upriver when a boat is going, and she has learned which injuries can wait for that and which cannot. Her sink is the cleanest metal in the settlement. People wash there even when they are not hurt, because the other water is brown and this water, for a few minutes, is not.

The cutters are out before the shed is fully loud. They work the beds on a rotation older than any of them, leaving stands to thicken while they take the ones that have reached the right height and the right give under the thumb. They do not clear. Clearing would be a kind of boast. They take what the machines can eat before the next wet rise, and they leave the wall of cane where it is, because the wall is also the reason the river has not yet decided to straighten the bend and take the piles with it. Their hands are cut across the pads in pale crossways lines. They wrap them and keep cutting. A new worker from upriver will try, for a week, to stand on the root-mat as if it were ground. Then he learns to walk on it as the others do, with the weight a little forward, ready for the give.

At the wharf the boatmen are half of this place and half of the river. They know the depth of the channel by the color of the boil off the pile closest to the current, and they will not bring a loaded hull in when that color is wrong, no matter what the tally says is waiting. When they are ashore they sleep in a lean-to that is technically a storehouse, and they eat with everyone else, and they do not quite belong to the quarters. Their children, if they have them, live farther up where the banks are higher. They speak of that farther place the way the mill people speak of the city the fiber eventually reaches: as a true thing that does not require visiting to remain true.

No one here is waiting to leave in the way stories prefer. A few do leave. A girl goes upriver to learn a trade that is not fiber and comes back twice, then not again. A man takes a boatman’s wage for a season and discovers he cannot sleep where the pumps are not running, and returns, embarrassed by a reason he will not say aloud and that everyone understands. Most stay because the work is steady and the debt at the store is survivable and the bend is where their dead are, under markers that sink a little every year and are lifted back by whoever notices. The cemetery is a strip of slightly higher ground behind the quarters, roots kept cut, names painted on wood and repainted when the paint fails. Grief is not hidden. It is simply not larger than the day. Someone dies; the beaters stop for an hour; the pumps do not; the hour ends.

In the late afternoon, when the brick gives back its heat and the condensation ticks on the tin, they are most themselves. A cutter sits on a coil of hose and sharpens a hook he has already sharpened. Two women at the dye shed compare the stain on their forearms and decide the blue has taken. A child is sent for the tally-keeper’s book and walks back slowly, reading nothing, because the book is heavy and the walk is the errand. The boiler-man smokes in the one dry corner of the pump-room step and watches the weir as if it might, for once, do something new. It does not. He is not disappointed. The surprise he is built for is smaller: a change in pitch, a hairline at a joint, the day the fig root finally lifts the bolt he has been watching since he was young enough to think the tree might lose.

They do not love the wet, and they do not hate it. They dry what can be dried and let the rest stay dark. They speak of the cane as a crop and as a neighbor, without sentiment in either direction. If you asked them what lives here, they would look at you as if you had asked which way the river runs. They live here. The machines live here. The figs live here, and the reed, and the brown water under the planks. None of these is visiting. The balance they keep is not a virtue they would name. It is the sum of a thousand small repairs, and of the decision, renewed every morning and never discussed, not to take more board, more brick, more bed, than the bend will quietly allow them to keep.
