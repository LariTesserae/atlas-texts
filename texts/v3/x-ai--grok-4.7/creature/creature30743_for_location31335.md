At first it is only a change in the quality of the damp. The crust ahead of a boot looks the same as the crust behind it—pale rind, dark varnish, the small polygons of salt—until the varnish lifts at one edge and does not fall. A film the color of wet iron peels a finger’s width, holds, and settles again, slower than gravity would prefer. There is no sound with it. The flies on that patch do not rise. They walk the lifted edge as if it were still ground.

It is not one thing. For a long time it is only the habit of the margins.

Along every dike where warm seepage weeps, the dark slick is thicker, and thicker in the same places day after day, even after the machines rake the windrows and the rain skins everything equally. It prefers the lee. It prefers the temperature a hand can bear but not enjoy. Where a grate lets water out of a sump, the slick climbs the concrete a little higher than the splash line and stays, drying at its upper edge into a brittle lace that the next seep softens and claims again. People who work the ponds call the high line the tide, though nothing here has a moon it can see. They wipe it off railings without looking and it is back by the next shift, not as a stain exactly—stains do not choose the warm side of a pipe.

If you stay, the choosing becomes harder to file as chemistry.

A section of crust between two ponds, no wider than a road, is bare in the morning and occupied by afternoon: not by birds, which space themselves with the economy of the gantries, but by a continuous dark that was not poured and was not grown in the way the brine organisms cloud a pond. It has an edge. The edge moves. Not fast. A person walking the service road can outpace it without noticing they are in a race. By the time the shift lamps make their small pools, the edge has reached the culvert and stopped, as if the culvert were a thought it had already had. The flies are thickest just behind that edge, walking, not clouding. They do not feed on it so much as keep it company. When a gull lands inside the dark and shifts its feet, the surface gives like skin over shallow muscle and then is only wet mineral again. The gull does not startle. Nothing here startles easily. The gull steps back onto pale crust anyway, the way an animal steps off a thing that is not ground and not water and has not yet decided which lie to tell.

In the sumps the concrete-colored fish hold in their slow schools, and under them, on the floor, something paler moves with them and not with the current. It is the color of the fish until a lamp finds it, and then it is the color of the lamp, poorly, as if light were a language it had learned late. It does not school. It spreads when the fish spread and gathers when they gather, a patient underline. The crabs on the outflow grates pick at it and find nothing to pick. Their china-click goes on. One crab, older than the others by the size of its pale barnacle of a shell, will not cross a certain warm plate on the grate even when the plate is the short way to the spill. It detours. The detour is older than the crab. Other crabs use it. The plate stays bare on top and dark underneath, where no claw reaches, and the dark underneath is warm when everything else on the grate is only wet.

People notice the detours before they notice the reason.

A technician who has worked the eastern gantries for years stops using the third stair on the north approach. She cannot say when she stopped. The tread is sound. It is warmer than the others by a degree a bare hand can feel through a glove, and the warmth is not the pipe’s warmth, which is honest and steady and comes from below. This warmth arrives as she puts her weight down and leaves when she lifts her foot, a half-second late, like a breath held and released by something that does not breathe on its own schedule. She takes the long way along the catwalk. Others have started to also, without a memo. The third stair grows a skin. Maintenance scrapes it, logs the scrape, and does not schedule a replacement, because the steel is fine. By the next rain the skin is back, thinner, as if it had learned that thick is what gets a chisel.

It learns thickness elsewhere.

In the lee of an evaporator hall, where the windrows of brine-fly casings pile to their preferred height and smell their preferred smell, the rake passes on its day and leaves the usual flat. Overnight the flat is no longer flat. Something has drawn the casings into a low ridge that follows the wall, not the wind. The ridge is warm. Flies walk it in numbers that make it look furred, then, when a truck door slams three ponds away, the fur exhales—every fly up at once—and what is underneath is not casings. It is a continuous dark the width of the ridge, already sinking back into the crust, taking the shape of the wall with it as a memory of cover. By full day there is only the stain and the flies resettled and the rake’s old tracks. The operator who drives the rake begins to leave that lee alone. He tells himself the bearings dislike the angle. The ridge, unmolested, grows honest. Knee-high. Then not a ridge: a fold, as of something large lying along the foundation and keeping the hall between itself and the open flats.

The hall’s concrete sounds different when struck. Crews know this without measuring. A healthy wall rings. This one answers short, the way a wall answers when earth has been packed against it, except nothing has been packed. At night, with an ear to the stain, there is the pump three kilometers off and, under that, a slow internal weather: not a heartbeat, nothing so borrowed, but a circulation, thick, pausing where the valves in the building pause, resuming when they resume. It has matched the plant. Or the plant has been matching it so long the difference cannot be heard anymore.

Birds will not roost on that wall. They use every other roof, every transformer yard, every pale stretch of crust. The fold’s length of foundation stays empty of them, a gap in the middle distance a person can learn to expect the way they expect a road. Waders on the dike-crest opposite face the other way. It might be the smell, which is the smell of the basin concentrated—metal, warm ocean, the sweet thin rot of casings—or it might be that the fold is simply occupied, and occupation here is respected in the same spacing the gulls already practice with each other.

What lives in the place is not separate from the place’s work.

It drinks the seep. It drinks the schedule. When a valve opens somewhere with the sound of a swallowed word, the fold along the hall answers in a movement too slow to see and too large to be anything else: a settling, a taking-in. The ponds do not drop. The pumps do not strain. Only the high line on the concrete climbs another finger overnight, and the flies stitch a new altitude, still a hand’s width above whatever surface it is willing to be that hour. It can be surface. It can be film. It can be the warmth that arrives under a boot and leaves late. On the open flats, where the horizon is only where counting stops, it rarely bothers. The chemistry out there is already a decision. It prefers the repairs, the lee, the undersides of gantries where condensation has learned its stains. It prefers to be mistaken for maintenance.

The people who remain are not many, and they have made a small religion of not naming it, which is not the same as not knowing. They wipe rails. They take the long catwalk. They rake everywhere but the preferred lee. New hires are told the third stair is closed for inspection, permanently, and the lock on the chain is real, and rusted, and warm. No one writes down why. Writing would require a noun.

At the hour the shift lamps fail to defeat the dimness, the basin’s pale burning belongs to the ponds, and the fold belongs to the wall, and between them the service road is empty in the way the air above shoulder height is empty—heat, haze, the tick of cooling metal. Something crosses the road then without crossing it. A darkening of the crust that begins on the hall side and completes itself on the pond side, the middle never visibly traveling, as if distance were a formality it keeps for the sake of the machines. Flies rise in a sheet over the place where it would have been if it moved like an animal, and settle on nothing, and rise again, confused by an absence that is still warm.

By morning the road is only road. A tire track passes through a darker polygon of salt and comes out pale. The driver does not remember the dark. The polygon remembers the tire: the print softened at the edges, rounded the way the old patches on the pump houses are rounded, as if years of warm wet air had worked on it in a single night. In the print’s shallow basin, not water exactly, a skin. It holds the sky’s tin color. When the first flies find it they walk the curve of the tread as if it were ground, which by then it is, and also is not.

It will be thicker tomorrow where the truck idles. It has learned idling the way it learned valves. The city continues its slow work, and the work has a second pace now, matched so closely that only the detours give it away: the stair avoided, the lee unraked, the crab’s long way across the grate, the gull’s step back onto honest crust. Whoever keeps those detours is what lives here. Not the flies, though it wears them. Not the fish, though it underlines their schools. Not the crews, though it has learned their hours better than the roster has.

It is the thing the basin has been concentrating all along, past salt, past mineral, past the point where a pond should precipitate and instead continues, quietly, into intention. Heat from underneath. Weather that never clears. A lid of haze the color of old tin. Under that lid it spreads when it must and folds when it can, and in the long quiet between pumps it holds still enough to be mistaken for the stain it used to be, before staying still became a choice it could unmake.
