It does not announce itself. For a long time you would say the chamber holds only the fish and the mussels and the pulse of the machine, and you would be almost right. The thing that lives here is distributed through those facts so evenly that it reads, at first, as weather.

What you notice before you see a body is a correction in the water. The spiral at the center of the room is not only the culvert’s work. Every few turns it tightens a degree, as if a hand had closed somewhere inside the flow and then remembered itself and opened again. The schools answer it. They do not flee. They revise their wheel so that a clear lane remains along the west wall, a lane that is empty even when the lamps show nothing in it. The eels under the grating go still in that same interval, not hiding, only declining to move until the correction has passed. Then the ordinary traffic resumes, and you tell yourself you imagined the lane.

Stay through another cycle of the valves and the lane fills.

It comes up from the culvert the way a length of heavier water would come, not swimming so much as arriving. The first part of it is almost the color of the tea-brown distance: a broad, low shape pressed to the slab, wide as a door and no taller than the grating’s clearance, so that for a moment you think a section of the floor has detached and is sliding. Then the edge lifts. There is a margin where the body is thinner, almost translucent, and the lamp-amber passes through it and shows the brick courses beyond, slightly displaced, the way a windowpane displaces what you see through it. That margin ripples, not as skin ripples, but as a sheet of finer current would, and it sets the near fish a hand’s width aside without touching them.

More of it follows. It does not have a front in the way a fish has a front. The leading mass thickens as it clears the culvert mouth, gathering upward into the mid-water until it occupies a volume you could walk into, if walking were possible here, and the water inside that volume is simply slower, denser, a fraction darker. In the darkening you begin to make out structure, and the structure refuses to settle. There are folds. Some of them behave like muscle and some like membrane and some like the standing wave that forms over a submerged lip of plate. A fold along what might be a flank holds a row of pale discs, each the size of a coin, each opening and closing on its own schedule, not together. They are not eyes in any ordinary arrangement—they do not track you as a pair would—but when one of them passes across your shape it pauses, and the pause travels down the row, and the whole mass eases a little farther from you, politely, without hurry.

It is old in the way the room is old. Not ruined: finished into a form it can keep. Along its thicker parts the surface carries a scurf of the same brown filaments that cling near the lamps, and in places a mussel has seated itself and been overgrown, the shell still there, a hard oval under the living sheet, incorporated rather than eaten. A fish-scale glints and then does not; the scale is not the animal’s. Things settle on it and are either shed, when the next pulse comes, or kept, if they do not interfere. Near what passes for its underside, where it rides just above the grating, the body has taken on the grid: faint square impressions, pressed in and released and pressed again over years, so that the tissue there is crosshatched, a memory of the floor written into it.

You look for a head because looking for a head is what you know how to do. There isn’t one, or there are several that are not heads. At the end nearest the culvert a collar of thicker tissue narrows into the darker water of the intake and does not end where your sight ends; it continues down the throat of the machine, out of the chamber, into whatever shaft feeds this room. It is not visiting. Part of it lives in the pipe the way the rest of it lives here, and the surges you feel in the grating are not only the impeller. When the pitch of the roar drops, the collar tightens, and a long shiver runs the length of the visible body and goes out through the thinner margins, and the fish-wheels scatter and reform farther off. When the pitch recovers, the shiver stops. The animal and the machine have been sharing a pulse for so long that the valves knock in the same grammar as its own contractions. You cannot tell, after a while, which of them is keeping time for the other.

It feeds without drama. The column of translucent specks near the south wall bends toward it when the collar loosens, and a portion of the column simply ceases to be in the open water. There is no strike. The denser volume inhales, and the specks are inside the volume, and then they are not visible. The small pale fish are mostly left alone; they school against its margins the way they school against the walls, using it as architecture. Now and then one of the blunt gray ones drifts too close to a fold and is folded. That is rarer. You see it once: the fish continues its diagonal, the membrane lifts with the same unhurried correction you mistook for current, and the diagonal ends inside. A few silver flakes spin out and are taken by the ordinary flow toward the north throat. The mussels, the eels, the rest of the school do not change their business. This is not a hunt that organizes the room. It is a tax the room already priced in.

If it has a preference, it is for the warm layer under the vault. After it has fed, or after the impeller has run hot for a long knock-cycle, the mass rises. It thins as it rises. The door-wide slab of it becomes a canopy, then a haze, then something you would miss if the lamps were better. Spread against the roof it covers half the chamber, and the coins of daylight from the bull’s-eye ports fall through it and come out tinted, greener, moving more slowly than the light alone would move. In that state the pale discs are scattered widely, a loose constellation on the ceiling of the water, each one opening on the ports, on the lamps, on the dark between. It is not sleeping. The collar in the culvert still answers the machine. But the greater part of it is at rest in the only warmth the room offers, and the fish pass under it as under a second, softer roof.

Touch is not necessary, and would not tell you much you do not already know from the way the water behaves. Where a margin brushes the back of a hand it is cool, as everything here is cool, and slightly slick, and then it is gone—not withdrawn in fear, simply flowed onward, the way a current gives up a shape it was holding. There is no startle response you can provoke that is larger than that small courtesy of distance. It has lived with divers, or with the memory of the ladder and the dry phase, long enough to have categorized an upright disturbance as something that passes. The oval scars on the north wall, the missing rungs, the smoothed numbers on the bulkhead: none of that was its doing. It does not break things. It occupies what continues to function.

What it is, in the older sense, is the chamber’s animal: the one metabolism that learned the schedule thoroughly enough to become part of the schedule. The fish are numerous and the mussels are a crust and the eels are a flicker under the bars, but they could be exchanged for others of their kind and the room would sound the same. This could not. Take it out and the spiral would loosen, the clear lane would fill, the warm layer under the vault would be only a degree of physics, and the culvert would roar without anything in the pipe answering back. The machine would go on. The water would go on. Something specific would have left.

It does not look at you for long. One of the discs finds you, pauses, passes the pause along, and the canopy, if it is up, or the denser mid-water mass, if it is not, eases its center a body’s length away and resumes the slow turning the culvert taught it and that it has since begun to teach the culvert in return. In the amber, against the riveted plate, it is almost possible to lose it again—almost possible to go back to believing the room contains only ordinary tenants and a turbine that has not yet failed. Then the valves knock, three times, and the shiver runs the length of what you can see, and out into the dark of the intake where the rest of it lives, and the silver rope of trapped air climbs the wall through a body that is not quite water, and does not break until it has passed beyond it, into the still layer under the hatch that no longer opens.
