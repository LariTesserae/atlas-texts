What lives here does not live in the bowl so much as in the interval between its sounds.

You would miss it at first, because it is not a shape against the stone. It is a refusal of certain echoes. Stand in a brief empty and clap your hands once: the nave answers as it always does, the western crown, the rim, the fault-crack, each return arriving in its known place. Then one of the returns is late by the width of a breath, and when it comes it is duller, as if the stone had put a hand over its mouth. That is the animal. It is holding a patch of air the way a person holds a breath, and the held air eats a sliver of the report before letting the rest through.

It is not large. A body you could circle with your arms, if it would allow itself to be circled, which it will not, because circling is a sound and it spends its life spending as little sound as the place will permit. The body is a loose bundle of stiff fans, the same horn-stuff as the plates but thinner, almost cloth, arranged not as wings—though they can be wings—but as baffles. Between the fans are gaps no wider than a finger, and in the gaps the air does not quite flow. It pauses. The animal tunes those gaps the way a hand tunes a drumhead, tightening one, slackening another, so that a clap arriving from the west is swallowed and a clap arriving from the rim is passed. It can, for a few heartbeats at a time, become a hole in the bowl’s grammar. Other animals, banking off echoes, bank off the absence and find themselves somewhere they did not mean to be. It feeds in the correction.

What it eats is the mistake. A sheet of membrane-fliers that has trusted a false wall will shear, and in the shear a few of them tumble into the lower third of the corridor, where the nail-runners are, and where they do not belong. The fans open then—not a clap, never a clap—into a soft chute, and the tumbled fliers, already wrong-footed, are guided rather than struck. They do not die of the guiding. They die of the landing, on stone that the chute has chosen, a particular polished swale under the western crown where the wax-mist is thickest and the fliers, dying, shed the mist they had not yet swallowed. The animal does not take the bodies. The nail-runners take the bodies, as they take all powder, and pay no attention to whose corridor was lied to. The animal takes the mist that would otherwise have been carried out through the crack by the next gust. It presses the fans flat against the swale and draws the sweetness through the gaps, a slow filtration, wax settling on the inner faces in a film too thin to drip. Later, in the dark that is only a change in heat, it folds and scrapes itself clean with a motion so small the stone does not report it. The scrapings are its meal and also its tuning: a film too thick and the gaps stop pausing the air; a film too thin and it cannot hold a silence long enough to be useful.

It has no fixed address, which puts it at odds with the growths and in agreement with everything else. But it is not a commuter. Commuters arrive and leave by the crack. This one has not left. You can tell by the quality of its mistakes, which are local and improving. A newcomer fliersheet banks off the real wall and learns. This animal has been teaching the same swale for longer than the tally scratches have been readable, and the western crown has leaned, over generations, a hand’s breadth toward the place where the mist is most often spilled. Whether the crown knows the animal or only knows the wax is not a distinction the bowl keeps. The cable under the swale is warmer than the cables around it. The animal returns to that warmth the way the growths return to heat, not out of loyalty but because the film dries fastest there, and a dry film is a precise film, and precision is the whole of its hunting.

Seen in the dust-light, when it fails to finish a silence and a fan-edge catches the rumor of gold, it is the color of the plates a season after they have powdered: paler than horn, darker than the floor. It has no eyes you would point to. The whole outer surface is a listener, and a listener in this nave does not need a face. What it has instead, low on the bundle, are two hooks of the same stuff, small, and with them it anchors to a cable or to the lee of a living plate while the fans work. Anchored, it can hold a silence longer. Unanchored, drifting in the upper third, it can only snatch one, and the snatch is riskier, because a drifting baffle sometimes becomes, to a sheet of fliers, exactly the kind of gust they were born to ride. Then it must open everything at once and fall, not in a dive but in a collapse, fans cupping air just enough to keep the hooks off the runners below. The sound of that fall is the closest it comes to being known: a soft, wrong-shaped whump, like a sack set down rather than dropped, and the animals that know the bowl do not bank off it. They have learned that this particular wrongness does not mark a wall. Newcomers sometimes do bank, and that is another way to eat, a worse way, because the correction happens too high and the mist disperses before the fans can be pressed to anything. It prefers not to. You can hear preference in how rarely the whump comes. Most hours it is only the late echo, and then not even that.

It does not clap. It cannot; the fans are built to unmake reports, and a report of its own would undo the hour’s work. Its mating, if the dry systems here still use the word, is a matter of two silences occupying the same corridor and failing, very slightly, to cancel. Where they overlap, a thin band of ordinary air survives, and in that band the wax-dust that both have been carrying on their inner faces meets and is not scraped away. The pair does not touch. They hold the overlap as long as the nave allows, which is never long, because a third echo will arrive and one of them will have to spend its silence on not being flown into. Afterward the dust that was not scraped has a different grain. It is carried, stuck to a fan-edge, until the carrier finds a crack in the rim where the scale-spill from the larger shifter has made a pocket of still air. There the dust is left. It does not sprout in any way you would call sprouting. Over a season the pocket grows a stipple of soft fans, miniature, useless for hunting, occupied entirely in learning how long a grain of quiet can be held before the rim’s chatter shakes it apart. Most stipples die at the first real spill of scale. One in a long while does not, and climbs down, already listening, already refusing a particular return from the western crown. The parent, if it is still in the nave, does not acknowledge the climb. Acknowledgement would be a sound.

It has no quarrel with the rope-animals, which pour too low and too evenly to be lied to; their sack-of-beans report is continuous, and a continuous sound cannot be made late. It gives them the floor and they do not look up. It has a careful truce with the nail-runners. They clean what it spills, and in cleaning they sometimes cross the swale while the fans are pressed flat to it. A runner that steps on a feeding fan is not attacked. The fan simply ceases to be a filter and becomes, for one step, ordinary stone-colored stiffness, and the runner’s nail skids on the wax-film and the runner corrects without ever knowing the correction was offered. In this way the animal pays for the bodies it does not take. The bowl’s rule of circulation is honored: the mist was going to leave, and has been delayed; the body was going to be powder, and is powder; nothing pools except the warmth under the cable, which was going to be warmth anyway.

The old hand-marks mean nothing to it, except as texture. The stacked wind-break is a good anchor, the lowest course already half-swallowed by the cable, and on still days the animal hooks there and practices a silence so complete that a person standing at the crack would hear the rim and the crowns and not hear the small gap where the stack’s polish should have returned a thin, bright tick. The flaked point in the niche is occasionally crawled past and never moved. The tally scratches are a ridge under the fans when it travels the wall, a faint interruption in the stone’s agreement with itself. It does not count them. Counting is a sequence, and sequence is what it hunts by breaking.

If you stayed through an acoustic hour, when the crowns clap in order and the nave fills until your teeth know it, you would feel the animal as a small sickness in the order. Not a missing clap—the growths are not its instrument—but a missing piece of the return, a tooth-width of the rolling report that arrives at the ear and does not arrive at the bone. You might think the stone had a flaw there. The stone does not. The flaw is alive, and it is hungry in the only way this place permits hunger: by arranging for someone else to be briefly, usefully wrong, and by taking, from the correction, nothing that would have pooled if left alone.

When the hour ends and the corridors shine empty again, it is already gone from the swale, the film scraped, the gaps retuned to whatever the next sheet will trust. The western crown ticks as its stored heat shifts. Somewhere in the rim a stipple holds a grain of quiet a little longer than it held one yesterday. The bowl does not record the difference. The animal does, in the only ledger it keeps, which is the set of echoes it can afford, tonight, not to be.
