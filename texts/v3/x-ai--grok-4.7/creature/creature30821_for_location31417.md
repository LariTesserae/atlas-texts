What lives there is not one body so much as a habit the notch has started to keep. You notice it first as a correction. A leaf that was bowed under its own water stands again without the wind having done it. A thread of seep, which had been finding the same stain for weeks, is turned a finger’s width aside so that it runs along a root instead of across it. The root darkens. The leaf drinks. Nothing walks away from the adjustment.

If you stay through the middle of the day, when the mist thins and the coins of sun move, you can see the rest of it assembling. It is small where it meets the light and larger where it does not. On the lit edges it is only a gloss, a thicker film than dew, copper-brown where the mineral has been in it and clear where it has not. In the shade between the broad leaves it gathers into a slow thickness, not quite a shape: a sleeve of wet along a stem, a palm’s width of darker green pressed to the underside of a frond, a pause in the drip-line that lasts longer than a drop should. It does not have a face. It has preferences. It prefers the warm faces of the stone to the ones the wind can reach. It prefers the places where two leaves touch and hold a film between them. It prefers the pipe.

The pipe is where it becomes easiest to mistake for maintenance. Along the joint the turbine draws from, the seep has been trained. Not stopped—trained. The film rides the metal in a continuous skin, thinner over the hot length, thicker at every coupling, and where a bolt weeps it drinks the weep and returns a clear run to the channel so the intake does not silt. The operators, when they come, find the strainer cleaner than they left it and write the fact down as if the fact were weather. They do not see the skin withdraw as their boots enter the engine house. It does not fear them. It only will not be in the same small room as a moving hand, the way a tide will not be in the same pool as a stone you have just dropped. When they leave, it is back before their cable-sway has settled, sealing the hairline they opened with a wrench, tasting the new oil they wiped and refusing it, pushing the black bead to the concrete where it can dry and not enter the root-water.

It is younger than the notch and older than the shed. The timber remembers a time without it: the logbook’s pencil dents stop being only numbers in the month the flow figures begin to lie in a gentle way, always a little steadier than a fracture ought to be. Since then it has been learning the interval. The relief valve’s cough is part of its weather. It flinches at the cough the way a leaf flinches, a whole connected surface taking the sound and settling. It has no ears. The sound arrives as a change in the tension of water, and water is what it is made of, plus whatever the seep has been carrying, plus a little of the leaf it has been feeding. Cut a frond it has been dwelling in and the cut does not bleed plant-sap alone. For a moment the wound holds a viscosity, a reluctance to fall, and then the reluctance lets go and is only water on your wrist, warm, with a metal taste.

It does not hunt. Nothing here is large enough to be worth hunting, and it has not yet invented appetite in that direction. What it takes, it takes at the rate of thirst. It draws the mineral film off the stone so the stone can weep again. It draws sugars out of the leaves it sheathes, and in exchange the leaves do not scorch when the sun finds them, because it is always between them and the heat, turning the light into a slower green. Ferns it has occupied grow past the height the notch should allow and then stop, not from any limit in the fern, but because past a certain reach it will not stretch. It is still local. A body of habit does not yet know how to be a long one.

At the lip it will not go. The fall is too fast; the air beyond the edge is empty of the warmth it requires. It has tested this. You can see the tests as dried copper arcs on the last stone, places where a film went out and thinned to nothing and left its mineral behind like a tide-mark. Toward the cable anchor it is curious and unsuccessful. The sway is a kind of wind. It climbs the first bolts and loses itself at the second, scattering into drops that the gulf takes. The noise of the turbine it has accepted. The noise of the open air it has not.

At night, if you kill a lamp and let your eyes go, it is almost visible as a change in how the wet shines. The ordinary dew is scattered. Its dew is continuous. It moves the way a blush moves, not by traveling but by arriving in a neighboring place and ceasing where it was. A sleeve on one stem becomes a palm on the next. A palm becomes a skin over the concrete footing of the engine house, feeling for the warmth that comes up through the slab from the pipe trench. It rests there, if rest is the word, against the geared hum, and the hum goes on unchanged, and the leaves above it tick with drops that fall at the interval it has chosen.

It is not kind. Kindness would require it to notice you as a you. It notices displacement. Set a boot in the pan between the stones and the pan is empty of it before the leather is wet; stand still long enough and it will come back to the outside of the boot, curious about the condensate your heat is making, and if you are very still it will climb as high as the place where your pulse changes the temperature of the leather. It does not enter. Skin is a shore it has not decided about. The one time an operator knelt and put a bare hand into a seep-pan to clear a clog of roots, the hand came up filmed, and for an hour afterward the film stayed, warm, working at the creases of the knuckles, opening none of them, drinking the salt and the machine-oil and leaving the hand cleaner than it had been and faintly coppered in the lines. He washed it off in the fall. It let itself be washed. It had not yet learned to hold.

What it is becoming, if the notch keeps rising and the pipe keeps drawing and no one poisons the seep with a flush of solvent, is a resident. Not an animal, not yet; animals arrive by crossing edges, and the edges here are a fresh lip and a gulf. This one arrived by concentration. It is the wetness agreeing, over seasons, to be in some places more than others, and then agreeing to prefer, and then agreeing to repair what it prefers. The shed is almost inside its preference now. The vine through the ridgeline is sheathed every night and the bench under it does not rot at the rate the floorboards rot, because the film takes the standing water off the seat and gives it to the vine. The logbook is beyond saving. It does not read. It only keeps the pulp damp enough not to flake, as if a record were a root and might still be useful later.

If you come twice you will think the plants are merely vigorous. If you come through a season you will see the vigor is routed: toward the engine house, away from the lip, along the pipe, never quite onto the cable, never quite into a boot that is still walking. The notch fills. The machine runs. Between them, something that is mostly water has begun to keep a shape for hours at a time—low, continuous, warm as the stone, tasting of copper and leaf, with no voice except the one it borrows when it shifts and the drops change their interval. It lives there the way a climate lives somewhere. You can put your hand in it. You cannot put it out.
