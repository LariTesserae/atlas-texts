What lives there is not one thing, and it is not settled into kinds the way a map would like. Stand long enough in the same gallery mouth and the categories loosen. A shape you had filed as bird drops to the mat and runs, using its wings as a pair of pale crutches, the flight-feathers too soft at the tip to be only for air. Something you had taken for a knot in the culm detaches, four limbs and a head the color of the fiber it was pressed to, and climbs until it is only a slight thickening of the joint again. The place breeds resemblance. Everything that lasts has learned the palette of straw, tea-water, old bone, and the dark of wet felt, so that at a distance the plateau looks empty even while it is densely occupied. Emptiness is a costume that still fits.

The sheets in the air are the loudest citizens and the least willing to be singular. Up close, when a turn of the flock shears low over a vault and a few individuals are flung out of the mass like sparks, you see they are not all the same animal. Some are true fliers, short-bodied, with a keel you could feel through the feathers if one ever let you, beaks narrow for the insects that seam the water-pockets. Others are heavier in the shoulder, almost embarrassed by their own wings, and they use the flock the way a poor swimmer uses a crowd in a river: direction without the full cost of it. They peel off to land on the culm-ribs and cling, shaking, until the surf of wingbeat comes back around and takes them up again. A third sort never quite commits. It runs the mat in the shadow of the sheet, a long-legged echo, and springs only when the shadow thins, as if flight were a rumor it was willing to test twice a day. From above, from inside the roar, none of this sorting is available. They are one weather. Their name, if the older stakes and rings ever had one scratched beside a water-line, has been grown through. What remains is the behavior: they travel the open reaches in a circuit that is not quite seasonal and not quite daily, pausing where the condensation still stands at midmorning, lifting when the mat starts to tick dry, roosting not in trees — there are no trees — but in the throats of the galleries, thousands of small warm weights hooked into the living tubes until the tubes lean a fraction and creak all night like rigging.

Between the sheets, the residents keep smaller countries.

In the joints, where one culm has fused to the next and a pocket of litter has collected — shed fiber, insect hulls, the dust of feathers — there are nesters no larger than a fist, round, eye-dark, with feet like soft hooks. They do not dig. Digging would be a wound, and a wound in this floor weeps a sweet sap that draws the wrong attention by dusk. They press. Their bodies, sitting, teach the pocket to deepen. Over a season the joint becomes a cup lined with their own down and the plant's scar, and the cup is inherited. You can find cups with three generations of stain in them, the oldest almost the color of the culm, the newest still bright with a yolk-colored streak where a chick pressed its chest to the wall. They speak in clicks that carry oddly well inside the tubes, a private telegraph, and they go quiet so completely when a shadow crosses the gallery mouth that the silence itself is information. Something higher up has learned to read that silence. It does not always act on it. The economics of the place are thin. A meal skipped is often wiser than a meal announced.

The wall-runners are the ones you meet if you walk the ribs with a hand out for balance. Pads, not claws. A narrow head. A tongue that tests the seam ahead of the feet, reading whatever the culm is exuding today — sap, condensation, the faint salt of last night's roost. They travel in loose single file, not from affection but because the safe line along a tube is only as wide as one body, and the one behind can feel, through the living wall, whether the one ahead has stopped. When they stop, everyone stops. Often the reason is a hole: a place where something larger has bitten or broken through, and the culm has tried to close it with a lip of new tissue that is still soft. The runners will not cross soft tissue. They reverse, the whole file turning in place with a patience that looks like courtesy, and take another rib. Their droppings, small and dry, are part of the felt. The plateau eats its own bookkeeping.

What hunts them does not live on the walls. It lives in the decision to leave the wall. From the open ground, from the lip of a collapsed gallery, from the brief shade under a flock, something low and long comes across the mat in a movement that is half pour and half stitch. It is not fast over distance. It is fast over the last body-length, which is the only distance that matters, and it kills by pressing rather than by tearing — a weight, a fold of loose skin at the throat used like a hand, a patience that matches the runners' own. Its hide takes the print of whatever it has been lying on. Lie on the pale culm-dust and it is straw; lie in the tea-water margin and it comes up darker, dripping, wrong for the ground it is now crossing, so it does not cross far until it has dried back into anonymity. It does not roar. The roar is above it, a cover. Under that cover it can afford to be almost silent, a soft friction, the sound of the mat accepting another slight weight. People who have stayed long enough to know the lulls say the dangerous lull is the one that arrives a half-beat early, before the flock has truly banked away. Something has looked up. Something has decided the air's distraction is sufficient.

There are larger presences, but they are rarer and more like weather than like neighbors. Once in a long walk you see, far out where the horizon creases, a darkness that is not a cloud shadow because it does not move with the wind. It stands. It may be a herd of the heavy-shouldered fliers come down to the mat in a place where the felt is thick enough to bear them, feeding on the finer grass at a water-pocket's rim, shoulders touching, the air above them disturbed. It may be one animal. The distance will not say, and approaching is a negotiation with the floor: the ribs you trusted under the halls run out, and the open pavement flexes, and there are hollows under it where old galleries have fallen in and not yet been filled by the slow knitting of stems. A wrong step is not always fatal. It is loud. The loudness is the danger. Everything listening understands a new sound.

The insects are the part of the population that does not consent to be scenery. At the water-skin they are a seethe, yes, but they are also couriers. Some carry pollen from the rare culms that still flower — a dull, fibrous bloom high on a vault, easy to miss, smelling of warm dust and almost nothing else — and some carry nothing but themselves from pocket to pocket, and some are the reason the runners will abandon a whole rib by noon. A column of them, small and red-brown, will open a soft place in the felt and sink into it, not as predators of anything you can see, but as a rewriting: by evening the mat there has a different hand-feel, looser, sour, and the grass-ring has drawn back as if insulted. The plateau allows this. It has been allowing revisions for longer than the rings of stones have existed. The insects are not an affliction laid on top of the world. They are one of the ways the world continues.

And the people. If they are still a people, they are a scattered grammar rather than a nation.

They live where the callus is thickest, on the raised scars, and they do not live many together. Three shelters within earshot is already a village, and earshot here is a treacherous measure — in a lull, a voice carries across a gallery; under the flock, a shout dies at the door. So they have other measures. A peeled stake set at a lean means the water-pocket has shifted west. Two stakes crossed and bound mean do not trust the floor beyond; someone went through, or something did. A ring of true stones with the western arc left open is a shelter still in use. A ring closed all the way around is a refusal, or a grave, or both: the mat will take a body, but slowly, and the stones are there to say that the taking was witnessed and should not be hurried by scavengers who do not understand stones. You find, sometimes, a bundle of culm-fiber hung in a joint above head height, knotted in a pattern that is not structural. It is not decoration either. It marks a cup of the nesters that someone has decided to leave alone, or a rib the runners use at dawn, or a place where the long hunter has a lie-up and the lie-up should be known. The knots differ by district, if district is a word that can survive without borders. Walk two days and the knot-grammar drifts. Walk five and you may not be able to read it, only to tell that it was meant to be read.

Their hands are in everything that is not still growing in its own manner. They cut, but they cut as the runners travel: along the line the plant can spare. A culm taken at the joint, not shattered. A strip of mat lifted where it was already loose, scoured clean of the sour insect-rewrite, and beaten into cord. They drink from the pockets with a scoop of peeled fiber, and they do not drink the first water of the morning, which is thick with the night's insects, nor the last of the afternoon, which has concentrated into something that bites the throat. Midday, brief, after the sheet of rain if there has been one, is the hour of water. They have a posture for listening — weight on the back foot, free hand not quite touching the culm, as if touch might be louder than they wish — and they hold it whenever the roar changes key. Children, when there are children, learn the posture before they learn the knots. A child who cannot hear the half-beat-early lull does not walk the open pavement alone. This is not said as a rule. It is visible in who comes back.

They hunt, a little. A runner taken from a file is a calculated theft: one, from the rear, where the reversal has not yet rippled forward, and then away along a rib the hunter has already tested. A bird from the edge of a roost, in the dark, by feel, before the colony understands it has been diminished. They do not hunt the long presser. They do not, if they are wise, hunt in the open under the sheets, where a kill is a signal to everything else that something on the mat is bleeding and slow. Most of what they eat is the plateau itself, persuaded: the inner film of a young culm, sweet and wet; the seeds of the dull flowers, toasted on a flat stone until they stop tasting of dust; a cake of beaten felt, sour, filling, the food of having no other food. Meat is a parenthesis.

They talk in the lulls, and the talk has the shape of people who cannot count on the next sentence arriving. News first, knots and water and whether the far darkness stood or moved. Names second, and briefly. They have names. They are not mystical absences; they are few, and often gone, and a name said too often in a place this audible starts to feel like a stake left to mark a thing that will move. So a person is "the one who closed the ring," "the one who reads the west knots," "the child who hears early." If they have older names, those are for the inside of a shelter, spoken into the callus, where the living floor gives back a duller version of the voice and the flocks cannot use it.

At night the roosts settle and the roar steps down, not into silence — never fully — but into a grain: the creak of loaded tubes, the click-telegraph in the joints, the soft friction of something long changing its lie on the mat, the tick of the day's heat leaving the fiber. The people sleep on the raised scars with their ears uncovered. Covering the ears is a story told about strangers, about someone who came up from lower country expecting night to mean privacy and found that privacy here is a kind of death: you cannot afford not to hear the floor decide, under you, to be less floor than it was. Some of the shelters have a second platform, smaller, outside the ring, where one person stays awake in turn and does not pretend it is a watch against enemies. It is a watch against the ordinary. The ordinary includes the hunter, the sour columns, the gallery that has been meaning to collapse since before the stones were carried, and the flock that sometimes, for no reason anyone has knotted into a pattern, lifts in the dark and turns the whole plateau into surf again, so that the sleepers sit up already listening, already counting who is on the platform and who is not.

By dawn the condensation has beaded, the cups in the joints are vocal, the sheets are gathering themselves out of the gallery throats in long peeling departures, and the plateau looks, for a little while, as if it might be a quiet country of plants and light. It is the least honest hour. Everything that lives here is already in motion under that appearance: the file of pads testing the night's new lips of tissue, the presser drying back into the color of the ground, the insects opening or closing their small doors in the felt, the people reading the stakes before they drink. The vegetation is total and the halls are empty and the emptiness is a lie told by color and distance. What lives there lives by being hard to see until it changes the sound, and by treating the ancient flatness not as a stage but as a body — warm, scarred, revising itself, loud in the way a body is loud when you lay your ear to it and stop mistaking the rush for silence.
