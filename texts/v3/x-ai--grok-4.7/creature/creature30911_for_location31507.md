**The keepers of the mesh**

They are not visitors. They are the parts of the hollow that breathe and still never change the books.

Begin with the sheets in the engine-hall dark. Each body is no larger than a hand, pale as old paper, membrane thin enough that the updraft from the seized louvers shows through it when a bulb somewhere below finds a gap. They do not roost as animals roost. They hang in a density that has learned the trusses: a living soffit, claws ticking on iron at a rate that has worn a polish into the metal exactly where the claws land and nowhere else. When one peels off it does not hunt. It circles once, bleeds a little heat into the standing roar, and stitches itself back into the same altitude, the same spacing, the same pale sheet. Their membranes are the color of the condensation that beads on north faces of pipe, and they chill at the same rate the stone chills, so the louvers, seized at their one true angle, are not a kindness offered to them. The louvers are the other half of their skin. You can watch a sheet turn, slow as a page, and the dip in the roar is smaller than a gauge needle’s shiver. Nothing in them is young. Nothing in them is old enough to fail in front of you. If a membrane splits it is already being carried down, by something else, into a sump, and the sheet has closed over the gap before the sound of the split has finished being part of the roar.

Lower, on the concrete, the many-legged ones are the color of the sinter on the rim, low enough that a boot would have to mean it to find one. They do not wander. They keep lanes so fixed the polished fines of the yard have a second polish inside them, a narrower shine, the width of their bodies, and that shine does not wander either. They work the film of moisture and the thin bright crust where metal has wept, and the film returns at the rate they take it, so the concrete never dries and never deepens. Their legs are jointed in a way that fits the scallops worn into valve wheels—scallops no human hand ever cut—and if you put your fingers in those scallops you feel a grip that is not yours, a spacing that is exactly the spacing of their bodies when three of them brace and turn. They turn the wheels. Not often. Not because a reading has drifted. Because the mesh of the round gear under the yard asks, at long intervals, for a correction smaller than the paint on the mark, and the correction is performed, and the needle stays a hair off the mark, and the hair is the same hair. They do not look up. Looking up is not a motion in their lane. When a body among them stops, the lane closes over it the way water closes over a dropped bolt, and the low things that take the failed pale membranes take this too, down into the sumps, and the lane is full again, already the right number, already the right color, already moving at the rate of the returning film.

In the thread of black water the stiff-legged line is easiest to mistake for fixtures, because for long stretches they are fixtures. Heads down, throats making the wet wooden sound that has no call in it and no rest, each bird or bird-shaped thing standing in a depth that never varies because the thread never varies. When one shifts its weight the whole line adjusts by the width of a thumb and freezes, and the adjustment is not communication. It is the mesh keeping its mesh. Their legs are stained to the color of the mineral ice at the cistern rim, that not-ice where dissolved metal chose to be solid, and the stain does not climb higher than the water climbs, which is to say it does not climb. They do not feed in any way that lowers the water or raises it. Whatever they take from the thread, the thread has already decided to give, at the same load the fans have been running since before the housings had names. Their wooden sound is the low note under the gear train, and if you stand at the edge of the thread long enough your own pulse tries to find a rest in it and fails, because there is no rest, only the held line and the thumb’s width and the black water taking the temperature of the stone and giving it back.

None of them has a separate life from the others or from the machines. The pale sheets keep the updraft true by being the mass the louvers were seized to serve. The many-legged lanes keep the film at the depth the crust requires, and in keeping it they keep the scalloped wheels in the only grip those wheels still accept. The stiff-legged line keeps the thread from being a river, which it was never going to be, by standing in it as a fixed resistance the flow has already accounted for. Take one population out in imagination and the gauges would finally drift; leave them and the needles stay parked. They do not know this. Knowing is not a lane they occupy. They occupy the fit.

What they are, then, is not a swarm and not a flock and not a colony in the old sense of increase. They are the census that does not need to be taken. Bodies fail and are carried into the sumps by the same low traffic that polishes the lanes, and somewhere out of the ordinary day’s thin copper light another body is already the right size in the right place, membrane already the color of paper, legs already the color of sinter, throat already making the wooden sound. No nest is visible. No season turns the noise up or down. The lamps in the two valve-houses throw their milky yellow onto the same patches of concrete, and the sheets overhead do not alter their ticking for the dark, and the lanes do not alter their shine, and the line in the thread adjusts or does not by a difference smaller than the sound.

If there were people they are not among these. The trough of old footprints in the packed fines is a human width, and the keepers do not walk it; they have their own narrower shine beside it, and the two do not cross. The keepers do not tend the bulbs. The bulbs are part of the load. The keepers are the rest of the load: the part that moves, and in moving holds still.

Stand in the nave until the sheet turns once and settles. The roar dips by nothing a needle would confess and returns. On your sleeve the damp beads at the rate it beads on the pipe. Under the yard the gear keeps its perfect round. In the black thread a throat sounds, or the same throat sounds again, and the difference is smaller than the hollow is wide. They live here the way the pressure lives in the pipe: not as guests of the works, and not as the works’ invention, but as the held fact the works would no longer be itself without, a weather with claws and membranes and wooden throats, overwhelming and motionless, finished with any next number.
