What lives here does not arrive as a single body. It arrives as a set of habits that have agreed, over a long time, on the same warm dark.

The oldest agreement is a weight. There is one of it. The chamber's slow budget of fallen fruit, drowned insects, and the occasional misstepped wader will feed one circuit and not two, and the life that keeps the perimeter has organized itself around that fact so completely that solitude is not a condition it suffers. It is the shape of its day. You would not call it an animal at first, only a rounding of the wall that is sometimes nearer and sometimes farther. Closer, the rounding has a hide the color of stone that has been wet for years and has stopped noticing. The hide is not smooth. It carries a nap of fine, close growth, something between moss and hair, that holds the chamber's moisture and gives it back so slowly that the creature's outline is always a little softer than the stone's. Heat from the rock enters it through the belly and the insides of the legs. It does not bask. It stays. When it rests, it rests where the old rib lies, not on the bone but beside it, the way a path rests beside a stone it has been walked around for so long that the avoiding has become the route.

It is low, longer than it is high, and it puts its weight down on feet that are almost not feet: broad pads, four of them, each able to spread on the leaf-mat so the pressure becomes a damp compression rather than a step. That is why the trench is shallow and continuous and why the mats close after it and yet remember. The head is not much distinguished from the shoulders except when it feeds. Then a mouth opens along a seam you would have taken for a fold in the hide, and the seam is wide, and it is not hurried. Fruit, insect, a wader that froze one beat too late. The swallowing is a long muscular thought. Afterward the seam is a fold again, and the head, if it is a head, turns a little toward the rib as if confirming a landmark, and the confirmation is the whole of its ceremony.

It breathes with the room. The throat of the chamber inhales night and exhales day in exchanges too slow to call wind, and the bulk's flanks rise on that same interval, so that for long stretches you cannot tell which movement belongs to the animal and which to the air. It does not vocalize. There is nothing in its life that would be improved by a sound that dies within a body-length. Other lives already know the trench is occupied because the waders freeze, and the bulk already knows the waders have frozen because the mat ahead of it stops ticking with their small steps. Information moves by absence. That is enough.

It is not guarding the chamber. Nothing here is guarded. It is keeping a loop that its body expects, and the loop happens to describe the room. If the fruit failed for a season, the loop would tighten, then fail, and the rib would acquire a companion, and some smaller life would eventually walk a lesser trench until the fruit returned. The chamber has done this before. The bone says so. The living bulk does not grieve the bone and does not avoid it. Recognition without mourning: that is as near to a mind as the weight seems to come, and it may be nearer than it looks. It pauses longer at the rib than the calories require. It has done this across enough circuits that the mat there is denser, uncrushed, a small plat of green the feet have elected not to take. Whether that election is memory or only a preference for the feel of that particular damp, the room does not distinguish, and neither, probably, does the animal. The effect is the same. Something is being kept.

Above it, in the braided vines, the lives are many and brief and more nearly social. The climbers are narrow through the skull and long through the hands. Their feet do not release a stem so much as trade it, one hold for the next, so that even in motion they read as part of the braid. A body the length of a forearm, hide the dull brick of the flowers they rob, eyes set so far to the sides that a turn of the head is less a glance than a re-aiming of the whole attention. They eat the moth-fliers and the fruit that splits without falling. They do not store. The heat spoils anything kept, and the vines set often enough that storage would be a waste of a body this small.

What they keep instead is distance. Each braid holds one, sometimes two if the fruit is heavy, and the border between braids is the turn of a head. One climber pivots until its face is toward the neighbor. The neighbor pivots back. They hold that aim for a breath, or for three, and then both return to whatever the hands were doing. No chase, no display of teeth, no sound. The longer the aim, the clearer the claim. A young climber testing an occupied braid will be looked at until it looks away, and if it does not look away it will be looked at while the resident advances one hold, then another, and the young one discovers that the vine it wanted is already a sequence of hands. It leaves. By the time it has found an empty braid toward the darker wall, the resident has resumed feeding, and the moths have resumed landing, and the negotiation has left no mark except in the animals, who will repeat it exactly the next time, and the next.

They sleep hooked, heads down, so that any tremor in the vine arrives first at the skull. They give birth, if that is the word for so small a dropping, into the crook where a stem crosses itself, and the young is a paler version of the hands and the hunger, and it stays on the natal braid until the resident's looking lasts too long, which is how a mother becomes a neighbor. Some of the young do not find a braid. They drift downward, are taken by the flat lives in the dishes or by the bulk on its loop, and the vines do not register the difference. The population holds. It has held long enough that the tattered leaves under the palest veins are a record of preference: the moths land where the sheen is, the climbers hunt where the moths land, the leaves tear, the sheen persists. A map of hunger written in damage, and still the plant sets its dull flowers on schedule.

The moth-fliers are the least attached of the room's citizens, and even they are attached. They do not migrate. They circulate. A drift from flower to sheen to the underside of a strap-leaf, a death in a climber's hands, a replacement already lifting from a fruit-split two braids over. Their wings are sized for thick air. In a true wind they would be wrecked; here the air's reluctance is their medium, and they swim it more than they fly it. Up close their bodies are dusted with the same faint paleness as the veins they trust, a powder that comes off on a leaf and may be how the sheen is renewed, or may be only the residue of feeding. The chamber has not been asked to decide. What matters is the traffic: constant, nearly silent, a soft redistribution of sugar from the vines' low flowers into the climbers' days, with a tithe paid to anything that can interrupt a drift.

Lower, the dishes have their own people. Each shallow pocket holds one flat life, bark-colored, rimmed just above the surface, and the rule is one to a dish because two would be a war the water is too small to contain. They are older than they look. A flat life does not hunt so much as edit the margin. An insect committed to the edge is a completed decision; the tilt of the body merely agrees with it. Between tilts they are so still that the film of mineral settles on their backs and makes them continuous with the dish, and a wader can stalk past a pocket for days before its freeze-response learns that particular outline. When one dies, the dish clouds, clears, and eventually accepts a young flat life the size of a coin, already practicing stillness, already the color it will die as. Where the young come from is the one migration inside the water: a brief, wet crossing over the damp between pockets, undertaken at the hour when the bulk is on the far side of its loop and the waders are frozen for some other reason. Most crossings fail. Enough do not. The dishes stay filled.

They do not use the wall-cups. The cups are too small and too high and too often dry, and the film that skins two of them supports only a scum of the smallest many-legged things, transients, not tenants. Still, the cups are not uninhabited in the broader sense. A pale crust of living film finds them, loses them, finds them again as the condensate changes its mind about its path. That film is the same order of life as the mats, only thinner, and it is the reason the even rims have stayed legible: growth prefers the cut, as the lichen prefers the strokes by the throat. The old work is being read by organisms that cannot know it as work. They know it as edge, as hold, as a place where water pauses. In this room that is a kind of literacy.

The waders are the only lives that still belong partly to outside. They come in through the throat as leggy, dull-colored birds no taller than a hand, or as something bird-like enough that the difference is academic in the dim. They do not sing here. Outside, perhaps, they do. Inside, the air eats sound, and they have given up the expense. Their whole vocabulary is the freeze. One stops, and the stop propagates, each bird reading the bird beside it, until the damp between the dishes is a scatter of statues and the bulk, or the reason, or nothing, passes. Then, without a signal you can see, they resume, spear, swallow, relocate by a handspan when the light at the throat changes its smear. They do not nest in the deep chamber. They nest, if the word fits, in the brighter tangle just inside the entrance, where a falling bead can still flash and a chick can learn the difference between a dish that is a dish and a dish that is a mouth. The ones that learn stay. The ones that don't become a line in the bulk's unhurried account.

Under all of this, the many-legged film-workers go on being the room's smallest continuous verb. They do not have territories a larger eye can respect. They have gradients: wetter, less wet, the taste of fruit-sugar where a split fell and was rained on by the vault, the bitter of leaf-rot, the mineral track of a condensation path. They follow these the way the bulk follows its trench, faithfully and without announcement. When a bead falls they pause, not from fear, from the interruption of the film they were reading. Then the film re-forms around the impact, and the reading resumes, and the path that looked random is revealed again as a sentence they have been traveling all their lives, and that their replacements will travel, letter for letter, as long as the vault keeps letting go of water.

No person lives here. The hands that cut the cups and leveled the slab and stroked the marks by the throat finished living here a long time before the current bulk was born, and before the bulk before that. What they left is occupied only in the way a ledge is occupied by water: used, not remembered. If any of their intention remains, it remains as a levelness the strap-leaf is slowly undoing, and as an alignment of empty cups the scum does not need and does not spoil. The chamber did not keep them. It kept the conditions they briefly shared, the heat, the measure of water, the dim, and those conditions have been breeding their own tenants ever since.

So the population of the room, stated plainly, is this: one heavy circuit and the bone of a previous circuit; a dozen climbers more or less, counting the unassigned young; a drift of moths that never quite becomes a flock; one flat tenant to each true dish and a hopeful coin-sized traveler between them when the loop is far; a shifting handful of waders who have agreed to be quiet; and an uncountable reading of the film by legs too small to matter until they are gone, at which point the mats would stall and, slowly, so would everything else. Nothing in that list is magnificent. Nothing in it is alone in the way a lost thing is alone. Each life is fitted to a refusal the room made first: the refusal of wind, of bright light, of cold, of any sound that tries to cross the whole nave. They have answered by becoming local, exact, and almost mute, and by spending what energy they have on continuation rather than on display.

If you stayed through a full circuit of the bulk, you would see the household complete itself once. The weight leaves the rib, completes the trench, takes what the margin offers, returns. A climber looks, and is looked at, and both look away. A moth lands on a vein that is only just not glowing. A dish tilts. At the throat a wader, newly in from whatever weather the outside is having, freezes because the others have frozen, and in that freeze it joins the room. The bulk settles beside the bone. The mat it elected not to crush accepts the new shade. Somewhere in the vault a bead gathers past the size the stone can hold, and lets go, and is caught by a leaf, and is read by many legs, and is not applauded, and is entirely enough.
