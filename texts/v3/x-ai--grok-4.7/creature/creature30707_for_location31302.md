They are not many, and they are not few. From the civic terrace at the hour that does not pass you can count the roofs and still not count the people, because most of them are not on the roofs and not on the lane and not arranged for counting. They live in the intervals the city was built to keep. A compound holds a household the way a cistern holds its coin of sun: completely, at a level, without display. Walk the grit and you meet someone, and then for a long stretch you meet only a goat resetting its weight and the stipple of condensation on a wall, and then someone else, and the two meetings do not add into a crowd. The city is occupied at the density of the fish: scarce enough that each has room, numerous enough that no tank is empty.

They are ordinary in the face and unusual in the duration of the face. Skin takes the cool light and keeps it. There are young faces and old faces and faces that have stopped between, and none of them is traveling. A woman’s hands may be the hands of long work — broad at the knuckle, darkened in the creases by clay and leaf-wet — and the creases do not deepen. A boy’s mouth may be a boy’s mouth for as long as anyone on the terrace can remember, which is a long remembering and not a long time. Aging happened once, the way the ridges rose once, and then the remaining gradient was gone. People are the age at which they became unsaturated. Infants do not appear. No one can say whether this is because no one begins or because beginning is one of the motions the valley tidies before it can be noticed. The children who are here were already children when the last real change ran out. They run. They scrape. They forget the scrape and keep the running. They do not grow into the doorframes. The doorframes were cut for the height they already have.

Speech fits the hush, so their talk is a close thing, carried a lane’s width and no farther. They do not raise their voices to cross distance; they walk the distance. What they say is mostly arrangement. Where the salt cakes sit. Which tank the stems came from. Who stood on the terrace when the bird redrew its arc, and whether that was the same standing as last noticing. They have words for tools and water and kin and the pass, and they have a word, used softly, for the tidying — not a god’s name and not a curse, something closer to the word a cook uses for the way a pot settles when the flame has nowhere left to climb. They do not pray. There is nothing to petition. The sun is already as high as it will be. Asking it to move would be like asking a finished wall to be taller, and they are not a people who talk to walls for an answer.

Kin is real and oddly unurgent. Households share sleeping room and workroom and the tree and the tank, and the sharing is exact: this mat, this bronze pin, this side of the hearth that is always the same size of heat. Children belong to compounds more than to the drama of parent and child, because drama needs a slope and there is no slope. A man may be father to the boy in the next yard in the sense that he remembers the boy’s first testing — the picked fruit, the branch still full, the child’s face doing the long work of understanding that effort has nowhere to lodge — and that memory is a kind of kinship thicker than blood, since blood does not mark and memory does. They visit. They do not move house. A compound emptied by choice would be an eddy, and the valley would close it, and someone would find themselves home with the mat unshifted and only the memory of having meant to leave. A few have that memory. They are quieter than the others, not sad exactly, but finished with certain sentences.

Work is placement. The market terrace holds its rows because hands put the rows back in the same noticing that would have depleted them, or because the valley does, and the hands have learned not to care which. There are people whose days are the salt, the hides, the pins, the wet stems. They are not merchants. Nothing is priced, because price is a difference and difference does not keep. What they practice is the knowledge of measure: how many cakes a household already knows are its own, how a hide should lie so the line cut in the stone is covered and not exceeded. A woman at the grain sacks — call her the one with the gray scarf, though the scarf is only a way to see her — moves with a patience that would look, in another country, like boredom. It is not boredom. It is fit. Her wrists know the weight that keeps the sack looking as it looked. She speaks little. When a child comes for the measure that is already the child’s, she nods at the level of the hush and the nod is the whole transaction. Later, if you look, she is still there, and the sacks are as full, and she is not tired. Tiredness is a gradient. It does not accumulate.

There are cooks, and the cooks are the closest thing the basin has to philosophers, though they would not take the word. They stand at hearths that refuse ash and treat warmth as a position rather than a process. Food is always at the point of being ready, so cooking is the art of not pushing it past itself. They slice. They warm. They set. A man with a broad, quiet face and a bronze hook on his belt — he uses the hook to lift the pot that does not need lifting, because the gesture is older than the equilibrium and he likes the gesture — will tell you, if you stand near enough, that the difficulty is not the fire. The difficulty is the eater. Hunger arrives and is met and does not teach the body anything new. People eat because the day, such as it is, has a shape, and the shape includes the bowl. The taste is good. It is the same good. He has stopped grieving that. Grief, like tiredness, failed to deepen. What remains is care: the stem placed so the wet end does not touch the rim, the salt crumbled to the size the mouth expects. He remembers a time, or the story of a time, when a meal could be spoiled. He keeps the care anyway. Care is one of the few motions that does not require a future to justify it.

At the pipes and the pools there are tenders, though the pipes do not leak and the pools do not fall. They walk the ceramic runs with their fingers on the clay, feeling the water that occupies the pipe completely, moving and not moving. They clear fern from a lip that fern has not overgrown, and the clearing is either undone or was never needed, and they do it because their route is one of the closed paths, like the bird’s arc, and a route is a way of being in a place that will not be altered by you. An older tender, a woman whose hair is the color of the unlichened fractures, knows every seep-line’s length. She can tell you the north line stops where it stops, always, and she will say it without wonder. Wonder was an early weather. It passed. What she has instead is a proprietary calm, the calm of someone who has been permitted to accompany a fact. Children follow her sometimes. She lets them put a hand in the cistern. The fish flick. The coin of the sun does not break. She watches their faces for the small recoil that means they expected a splash to mean something, and when the recoil fades she is satisfied in the only way satisfaction works here: as a match, not a gain.

The pass has watchers, not because anything comes through it. The pass is a fact with sharp corners, and someone should stand where the fact meets the valley, the way someone should stand at a door even when the door is only a way of agreeing that inside and outside differ. The watchers are often the ones with the memory of leaving. They look at the knife-edge and the fixed sun and the scree that does not walk, and they practice not testing. Once, one of them walked up through the gash until the basin’s hush thinned. He does not tell it as a story with an ending. He says the air above the rim was the same air, and the light was the same light, and the reason he came back was not a wall or a fear but the sudden obscenity of his own footfall, loud, unreceived, a noise the stone had not agreed to keep. When he returned, his place on the terrace was uncreased. He had been gone and not gone. He watches anyway. It is his arc.

There are no rulers. There were, perhaps, in the planning time, when channels had to be argued into being and wards named. The arguments ended when the arguments could no longer change the stone. What remains is a habit of gathering on the civic terrace, not to decide but to be in the same look. People stand in their own shadows. They speak across a few hands of distance about the goats, the salt, a child who laughed above the hush and was not ashamed, only surprised by the air’s refusal. If a dispute arises — a mat, a pin, the side of a tank — it is settled by memory of arrangement, and if the memories differ, they stand together until one memory loosens, not by force but by the same tidying that unwrites a footprint. The one who loosens does not lose. There is nothing to win. They go back down the step and the terrace is not scuffed by the standing.

They have interiors. This is the part that takes longest to see, because the valley’s surfaces are so complete they look like the whole truth. Behind the completeness, each of them carries a small weather the stone will not accept: a preference, a joke worn smooth, a fear that cannot happen and therefore cannot be soothed by happening. The woman with the gray scarf prefers the left-hand sack, for no reason that survives inspection. The cook sings, almost under the hush, a tune with no new notes, and if you know it you can meet him inside it for the length of a bowl. The pass-watcher is afraid of his own footfall and has made a life of standing where footfalls are quiet. A child — there is one who always takes the long way between the fern island and the market, a way with an extra corner — believes, privately, that if she varies the corner by a handspan the valley will one day fail to notice, and the failure will be hers, a secret ownership. She has not done it. She rehearses it in the only place rehearsal can live, which is memory of an intention. At night, if this light can be called a kind of night — it cannot; the sun is simply there, and they sleep by custom, shutters pulled, the coin still on the water — she lies on her mat and walks the varied corner in her mind, and the mind is not tidied, and that is the closest thing she has to a room of her own.

They are kind, in a manner that does not seek gratitude, because gratitude implies a debt and debt implies time. Kindness is another placement. A hand at an elbow on the terrace step. A stem turned so the wet end is inward. A silence kept when someone is busy remembering a test. They do not love in the key of longing. Longing needs a distance that can close or fail to close. What they have is nearer: a recognition so continuous it resembles the way the fern resembles itself, frond by frond, none yellowing. Couples, where they are couples, share a compound’s exact measures and a fund of unspent sentences. They touch. The touch does not lead anywhere it has not already been. It is not therefore small. In a country of closed paths, a repeated touch is one of the paths, and walking it is how a person continues to be a person rather than a feature of the masonry.

Animals live among them without being kept and without being wild. The goats are known. People have names for particular ones, names used rarely, since the goat does not come when called and does not need to. A dun goat with a pale throat occupies the turf below the north seep and is occupied by it; children tell each other which is her, and the telling is a kind of literacy. The fish are not named. They are too alike, or too much a property of the tanks, and the people respect that difference without a rule for it. Birds are acknowledged by their arcs. Someone will say, at hush-level, that the short arc over the market roof has been flown, and someone else will nod, and the day — the custom of the day — will have a mark in it that the stone will not keep and the speakers will.

What lives here, then, is a people matched to a climate of completion, and a scattering of animals matched to the same climate, and a few unclosed things that live only in them: the preference, the tune, the rehearsed corner, the fear of a loud step beyond the pass. They do not experience themselves as enchanted. Enchantment would be an explanation, and they do not need one. They experience themselves as residents. The basin holds them the way it holds daylight, at a level, without expense. They wake, they place, they eat, they stand in their shadows, they sleep by custom under a sun that will not go down. If you stayed, they would make room in the only way room is made here — by an interval, not by a sacrifice — and in time, which is only the order of noticing, you would find your own arc, your own measure, your own sentence you no longer need to finish. Until then they will look at you the way the tender looks at a hand entering still water: waiting to see if you expected a splash to mean something, and calm, already, with the fact that it will not.
