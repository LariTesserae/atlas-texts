It does not arrive. It condenses, the way the beads do, except the condensing takes the shape of attention before it takes the shape of a body.

At first there is only the sense that the newest joint is being watched from the side the water has not yet chosen. The watching has weight. A lashing creaks in a rhythm slightly slower than the swelling of the fiber, as if something is waiting for the cord to remember how tightly it wished to be pulled. When you hold still long enough that your own shadow stays where it was left, the shadow gains a second edge—not cast, but pressed, a narrow darker band where something has leaned its regard against the clay and the clay has accepted the lean as another course.

It is the size of the work it is doing. That is the only measure that holds. Crouched inside the pocket it is a compact heat beside your knee, close enough that the air between you thickens with the same mineral sweetness, its breath leaving brief clearings in the pollen-crowd. Those clearings close again from the edges, never from the middle. When the scale rushes outward and the same slabs become a wet face under weather, the presence lengthens with them without stretching: a figure the height of the ridge, still close, still breathing the same mixed air, its hands (you begin to call them hands because they divide the work) spanning distances that should require journeys. Both sizes are occupied at once. It does not travel between them. Travel has not been built.

What you can bear to look at directly is mostly limb and tool, and the distinction keeps slipping. The forearms are the color of fresh-split stone just after the break, pale, glittering with the same unwilling mica, and they weep the way the walls weep—warm film, not blood. Fingers taper into wedges, into cords, into the pads that press moss into mortar while the mortar is still deciding. Sometimes a hand is simply a hand, four fingers and a thumb, nails packed with clay and fiber, the skin at the knuckles already netted with the fine cracks of plaster that has dried and been wetted again. Sometimes the hand is absent and the work continues inside the interval, the mud arriving already slapped, the knot already old in fibers that were loose a moment earlier. It does not seem to mind which version you meet. Both are true enough to bear weight.

Its face, when the brass light sits on it, is the least finished part of the room. Features are laid in the way stones are laid: a ridge where a brow might be, still bright from the break; a mouth that is mostly the place water has found a faster path, parted, not for speech. Eyes occur at different depths. One sits near the surface, dark and wet as the channel under the slabs, tracking the next gap that must not be allowed to remain empty. The other is farther back, or smaller, or simply not yet cut, a thumb-deep tarn that pulses when the rest of the face is still. It looks at the work more often than it looks at you. When it does include you, the inclusion is practical. You are volume that might be useful, or volume that must be lashed around, or volume that has begun, inconveniently, to sweat in the same grammar as the walls. A cord brushes your wrist again, tests the heat of the skin, and withdraws; the pressure leaves a faint twist-mark that fades only when the room decides the mark is not structural.

It does not speak in sounds you can answer. The small shouts belong to the water. What it has instead is adjustment. If you shift your weight, a paver under the far wall—unreachable, intimate—lifts by the same fraction and settles again, acknowledging the load. If you breathe out hard enough to stir the pollen, a new shoot occupies the eddy your breath made, as if the green had been waiting for a pocket exactly that shape. It is not unkind. Kindness is a gap, and gaps are tasks. It simply continues, and the continuation has room for you the way the ceiling basket has room for another pole: tightly, with the mountain’s weather still a palm’s thickness away, with the understanding that you will be packed into the making until you either belong to a joint or are gently, firmly, extruded back into the thin air.

You never see it eat. You see the pool under the film lose a pulse of volume and then regain it from a joint that should not have fed it. You see a yellowing leaf-tip taken between two fingers—stone-pale, precise—and the yellow pressed backward into green, the color returning like water finding downhill that has only just been invented. The insect that is sometimes a crumb of mineral walks across the back of its hand and is not crushed; it is set, carefully, onto a surface that needs a tenant of exactly that lightness so the surface will not set like the clay. Nothing here is wasted, including the accidental. The being’s own shed film—there is a constant fine peeling where plaster-skin dries and is renewed—drifts into the mortar and vanishes, strengthening the courses by the amount of a thought.

It has been working since before the pocket was a pocket, which is impossible, and the impossibility sits on it lightly. There is no fatigue you can recognize, only changes in the brightness of the break-faces, moments when the new stone it seats is dimmer, already willing to be old, and moments when the stone is so fresh the light cannot sit on it at all. In those brighter intervals the cold whistle from the pinhole sharpens, and the being turns one wedge-edge into the blade of altitude on purpose, hardening a lip of clay with a touch of the outside that lasts less than a breath. It does not flinch. The summit moves through it and is translated, the way all the contradictions are translated, into another bead, another course, another refusal of emptiness.

If you stay, it will eventually make a place for your shape. Not a kindness: a completion. A hollow sized to a sleeper, or to a mite, or to both, lined with the newest moss, the lashings drawn close enough that your pulse becomes one of the room’s pulses, the one that keeps a certain pool from filming over. You would not be its companion. It does not have companions. You would be load-bearing. The grass bracelet on your wrist would finally be tied off, unfrayed and final, and the being would move on to the next joint—the one that is only now learning it was always meant to weep—leaving you inside the work, warm, packed, unfinished, and, for the first time since the missed step, exactly as large as the world that is holding you.
