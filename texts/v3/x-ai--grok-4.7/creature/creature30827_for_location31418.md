What lives here does not occupy a center, because the chamber has none. It occupies the intervals.

At first you would miss it for the same reason you would miss the second hand on a clock that has been underwater for a lifetime: it moves, but not in the register your eye was built to trust. It is not the slug on the flange, though the slug crosses it sometimes and is not rebuffed. It is not the thumb-sized lantern holding its burrow in the cable, though that small light is a kind of tenant’s lamp, kept where a fold of the larger body has gone thin enough to see through. The plants are not it either, and yet they dress it, and it does not shake them off. They have grown into the same decisions.

Call it, if you need a name for the length of a look, the keeper. The name will not survive contact. It has no face turned toward you. What you take for a shoulder is a housing that has been lived-in so long the casting remembers a curve that was never machined. What you take for a hand is a root-mass that has learned the span of a valve wheel and now rests there, pale, tubular, not gripping. The wheel has not been turned in any human sense. It has been *held in the correct weather*. That is the difference, and it is the whole of the creature’s vocation.

It is larger than a person and smaller than the room, which is to say it is the size of a habit. Parts of it are soft. Parts of it are the iron it has been digesting, grain by grain, and laying back down as a darker lace inside its own tissues, so that a cut — if anything here were cut — would show growth rings of metal the way a tree shows years. It does not bleed in a way that would alarm you. A nick weeps the same warm broth the ceiling weeps, and the weep closes by thickening, not by clotting. The chemistry of the vents is not a neighbor to it. It is breakfast, and has been, since before the last lamp glass clouded.

Its eyes, if that is the word, are not paired. They are the small brightenings you already noticed and misfiled as mineral glow on the iron: a scatter of them, coin-sized or smaller, opening where the water is fractionally wrong and closing where a frond has drifted across. They do not track you. They track gradients. Sweetness. Heat. The slow opinion of a boiler that has not been allowed to die. When a gear advances one tooth, several of those lights answer, not with a flash but with a deepening, as if the click were a word it has agreed to keep using. The gear does not know it is being spoken to. The keeper does not require the gear to know.

It is not intelligent in the manner of a mind that plans an afternoon. It is intelligent in the manner of a marsh that has decided, over centuries, which channels will carry the flood and which will silt themselves shut. Somewhere in its mass there is memory of the dry years, when this was a works with air in it and men with wrenches, and that memory is not fond and not bitter. It is structural. The keeper grew into the leftover instructions the way ivy grows into a fence: not to mock the fence, but because the fence was already a sentence about where to go. It still completes those sentences. A piston that must finish its stroke finishes it because the keeper’s tissues have taken up the rhythm and will not release it. A gauge needle points at a unit no engineer would recognize because the unit is the keeper’s own: not pressure, but *enough*. Enough warmth. Enough seep. Enough leaf to jacket the catwalk so the walk cannot remember it was for feet.

It does not hunt. The small animals are not prey and not pets. They are lint on a coat that has learned to metabolize lint. The armored slug polishes a path along a fold of it and, in polishing, keeps a pore clear; the keeper allows this the way a sleeper allows a draft under a door. The lantern-eyed one is closer to a symptom. Where the keeper’s body runs thin against a coil of cable, light leaks, and something no bigger than a thumb has taken the leak for a room and the room for a life. Pull the cable and you would not free a separate creature so much as tear a window. You will not pull the cable. The water is too occupied, and your hands are already full of weed.

If it has a mouth, it is the throat of pipe in the fourth wall. Not a mouth that bites. A mouth that breathes the way the house breathes: out, then back, on a cycle longer than your attention. Warm water leaves through that rifled dark carrying a faint sweetness, the signature of a body that has finished with a molecule and is willing to let it go. Colder rumor comes back, is tasted along the rifling by a nap of cilia so fine they look like tarnish, and is either invited deeper or turned aside into a side-channel the plants have already agreed to drink. You could swim into that throat. It would admit you. It would not notice you as a trespass until you had gone far enough that *notice* and *enclose* were the same event, and by then the notice would be gentle, the way a root is gentle when it finds a crack and widens it over a season. People who enter thinking of monsters are the ones who panic. Panic is the only sharp thing the keeper still answers, and it answers by slowing, by packing leaf and silt and its own denser flesh until the panic has nowhere to accelerate. Most such accidents end as part of the balance. The chamber does not keep trophies. It keeps laminations.

It is not lonely. Loneliness would require a self with an outside edge, and the keeper’s edge is a negotiation. On one side, boiler. On another, citrus-colored climbing growth. On a third, the mortar it has entered and exited through bolt-holes until built and grown are one fabric, and it is the thread that made the fabric true. In the corner where the water is heavier, it grows in tighter spirals because that is where it is listening hardest — not to a voice from outside the boundary walls, but to a difference in the stone, a cooler intention leaking through from the depth that is only a rumor here. It answers that leak by thickening. It does not fear the cold. It refuses to let the cold become a policy.

Watch long enough and you will see it feed in the only way that does not look like feeding. A bubble rises, apologizes, is caught by a leaf, becomes a small silver room. The leaf is not the keeper. The patience that does not pop the bubble is. Hours later the silver room is gone and a nearby filament is a shade more opaque, a shade more metallic. A drip arrives from the onion-skin lamp and is absorbed without drama. The keeper’s nearest light deepens by a fraction no photograph would consent to record. Somewhere in the pack the next tooth of the gear train is already being leaned on, not with force, with attendance, so that when the click finally comes it will arrive on time, padded, half-dissolved, and feel — to anything still capable of feeling it — like speech.

It will not speak to you. It has already included you in the sentence it is finishing, which is the sentence of the room: warm, dim, fully occupied, quiet enough that motion is mostly unnecessary. If you leave, it will close the path behind you with ribbon-kelp and not out of spite. If you stay, it will not welcome you. It will weather you, at the rate it weathers iron, until what is yours and what is the chamber’s stops being a sequence and becomes, very quietly, one fabric. That is how it lives. That is the only hospitality it has ever practiced, and it has been practicing for as long as the weep in the ceiling has been a law rather than a leak.
