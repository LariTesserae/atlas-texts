The cold here is not weather but a condition of the tissue itself. It lives in the pale ribs and the keels, conducted inward the way the light is conducted, and it pools in every hollow that has not yet been sealed. Where a fresh face has split open, the cut is wet for only a moment; then the sap skins, the skin powders, and the powder behaves as snow would if snow had never learned to melt. Underfoot, on the wider spars, the crust creaks with a sound too fine to carry. A traveler learns to step where the fiber is older and darker, already scabbed, because the newest edges will part cloth and skin with the same indifferent geometry they use to part the air.

Movement is possible only along the lines the plant has drawn and not yet redrawn. A spar three paces wide will hold a careful walker for the length of a dimming, springy, faintly sticky where a duct has wept and healed. To either side the drop is not a fall so much as a change of category: the body leaves the line and enters the brightness, and the brightness has no floor that can be reached before the lungs have emptied and filled again many times. Things already in that brightness turn slowly—curled scales of cuticle, shed from some outer face and drifting for an hour—until they touch a lower wall and adhere, becoming part of the next surface. No path crosses the open volume. What looks like a continuation, seen from a distance, is only another rib in another chamber, separated by weather.

The organism does not pursue. It does not need to. A hollow that has been a corridor closes by the simple addition of a flange, the tissue advancing at the pace of a sleeve drawn across a table, the sound no louder than that. The traveler who has gone too far down a thinning span returns to find the span folded inward, gray-green, and the volume it vacated already filling with the mild internal wind that is born wherever a sunlit mass stands above a shaded throat. The wind does not howl. It arrives, cold, and forgets itself in the next emptiness. Echo dies against the living faces. A call sent after a companion travels a short way and returns only as a warmth on the cheek.

Water offers itself and withdraws on the same indifferent schedule. A pocket cupped in a fold will hold a skim of ice at its rim through one dimming and be empty by the next, the melt drawn back into a vein the width of a finger, visible for a hundred paces as a dark thread inside translucent fiber and then gone. To drink is to kneel, to warm the crust with the heel of the hand until a film loosens, to take what the mouth can hold before the tissue reclaims the rest. There is never a stream that can be followed. There is never a dry day so absolute that the teeth do not feel damp. The balance keeps a body alive and keeps it from lingering.

The few marks left by others are already being edited. A platform of lashed ribs, weighted by a carried stone, will hold for a while against a billowing membrane; then the membrane thickens, the lashings split, and the stone tilts into a seam that closes over it. Notches cut into a keel for counting dimmings grow shallow as new fiber lids them. A ladder of pegs ends where a flange has crossed the route, the last peg still sound, the next one simply unnecessary because the wall is no longer a wall. No one has stayed long enough to crowd these traces together. The plant revises the architecture faster than any hand can found a second camp beside the first.

What else moves here stays small and rare, pressed to the margins the growth itself creates. A moth-sized drinker clings to the freshest bead and is gone before the bead freezes. An armored crawler the color of the tissue it matches may be found twice in a long circuit and not again. Larger shapes, when they appear at all, resolve into knots of fiber or into nothing, lost behind a brightening. The scarcity feels enforced by the cold and by the sharpness and by the simple absence of anything soft enough to burrow or enough decayed to feed a scavenger. The living mass spends itself on boundaries. The interior remains vacancy, and vacancy is a poor cradle.

Light changes without ever becoming night. High in the vaults the white-gold brightens and dulls as the outer skin keeps or releases what falls upon it, a cycle of minutes rather than hours, and each dimming tastes slightly different—sweeter when a great span has opened, more bitter when one has sealed. The eyes ache for contrast that does not arrive. Shadows are only the brief blue of one spar crossing another, and those shadows move. To rest is to choose a scabbed surface wide enough to lie upon, to face away from the brightest conduction, and to wake when the pressure in the ears shifts, which means the chamber has altered its shape while the body was still.

There is no sign that leads upward that remains a sign. Thinner tissue glows more strongly, and thinner tissue is also the tissue most ready to split or to scar shut. A throat that admits a draft may be the memory of a surface opening, the mass having found a use for the air the way a body finds a use for a nostril, but the same draft can be cut off by a single new keel. Stones appear where they have been carried and set down, never where geology would have placed them. Speech about an outer face of low green under hard sun is speech only; the brightness here does not point reliably, and the compass is whatever spar has not yet withdrawn.

So the inhabitant, if the word can be used, is whoever keeps to the lines while they last, drinks the film before it is reclaimed, and does not mistake the quiet for stillness. The place is not empty of event. It is empty of audience. Whole districts open and seal with no louder report than a change in the taste of the air, and the cold accepts the change as it accepts everything else: as another arrangement of the same living mass, sharp at the edges, vast in the hollows, and already, quietly, becoming the next thing.
