It does not keep a den. Dens are for places that stay still.

What lives here learned the basin by its interruptions. For a long time—long enough that the concrete still showed clean aggregate in every square it crossed—it was only a habit of arrival. A change in the pitch of a gate train, a cooler thread in an otherwise even column of water, the first yellow-white lamps clicking up under a squall: these were enough to bend its path. It came in from the shallows at the edge of the fill, where the new sea met older shore and the water was already thick with harbor things, and it did not turn back. The empty squares did not frighten it. Emptiness, here, is scheduled. You can cross a mile of it and know, by the organ-note under the wind, which bank of engines is loaded and which return culvert will be dumping by the time you arrive.

It is large enough that the bait schools register it, and not so large that the works have built a protocol around it. Inspectors have logged a shape. Tug crews have throttled when something longer than their lamp bar slid under the keel and kept the trench instead of bolting. None of them agree on the outline. In the green-silver noon it reads as a darkness with a motor in it, too continuous to be a seal, too unhurried to be a shark working a boil. In the trenches, where the light fails to working dusk, it is mostly a displacement: the inspection cage on its rail shivers as if a current had shouldered it, and then the rail hums alone again. Near the lamps it will hang just outside the glass, where the small silver fish pulse and break, and the lamp does not catch an eye so much as a wet slope of hide, pale where the mold-ridges of a column would have left a shadow, dark where the hide has taken on the soot-film that settles under the foundry stain.

The hide is not scales and not quite skin. It takes impressions. A bolt-head, leaned against for the length of a closed gate, leaves a shallow dimple that lasts until the next long crossing of open water rubs it out. A stripe of ceramic dust from a pipe gallery shows on its flank for a day, bright as a survey mark, before the scheduled lane-river washes it. Algae does not fur it. The water is too new, too turned-over, and the thing itself does not hold still on the pilings the way the mussels do. What grows on it grows at the joints of its motion: a thin green at the crease where a fin—or the fold that does the work of a fin—meets the body, a few mussels no bigger than thumbnails on the trailing edge of a scar that has not yet decided whether it is healing or becoming structure. The scar is from a grating. Early on, before it understood intakes, it followed a school into the dark of a pipe and met the rush that feeds a cooling root. It came back out. The bolts at that grating still show a scrape of something that was not paint.

It feeds where the workers of the sea already gather, and it feeds on the schedule as much as on the flesh. When a return culvert dumps its warmer, particle-rich water, the gulls work the surface boil and the seal-shaped bodies turn in the upwelling and the silver fish stack themselves in the light. It rises into that stack from below, not fast, and the school opens the way a shift opens at a gate: a gap, a hesitation, a closing-over. It takes what the gap offers. It does not empty the light. An emptied light would be noticed; a thinned one is only weather. In the open squares, where the fauna is a traveling cloud and then nothing for an hour, it will follow the cloud until the cloud decides, or until a gate complex opens and the lane becomes a river. Then it lets the cloud go. Rivers here are not to be argued with. It has learned the sound of a train of gates the way a harbor animal learns a diesel: not as threat, as timetable. Twice a day, or on the cycle the works actually keep, it is already in position at the margin of the lane, holding against the draw, watching the paint flakes and the rafts of wire-weed and the occasional lost fender go past in a path that does not wander. Sometimes a stunned fish comes with them. Sometimes it does not feed at all and only rides the residual eddy after the gate closes, under the industrial lamps, while the fish that missed the change hesitate and the pumps drop into their lull.

Sleep, if that is the word, happens in those lulls and in the lee of caissons where silt is allowed to become a flour. It does not lie on the bottom. The bottom is a plate, and plates are for machines. It hangs, slightly negative, in the dark slot of a joint wide enough for a boat, or in the ring of columns under a cooling tower where the current dies and the beard of algae ticks against the mold-ridges. The piston thud comes through the concrete into its body in threes. It has arranged itself so that the threes match a slow flexion along its length. Whether this is comfort or merely how a body built for conveyance rests when conveyance pauses, there is no one here to ask. The dry galleries are not for it. It has nosed the stair towers where they march down into the column grids, tasted the sweet-chemical edge that leaks at a seal, and turned away. Oil and hot metal are information. They are not habitat. Above the certain line where the valve halls stay dry, it does not exist, except as a disturbance in a log.

It is not alone in the way a last animal is alone. The basin is already full of users: the mussels on the lower bolts, the harbor fish at every lit grating, the seal-shaped bodies, the gulls whose shadows cross the columns, the tug with the lamp bar, the cages on rails, the crews who speak in short bursts because the halls are louder than speech. It fits among them as another user of joints and lights and scheduled flow. What distinguishes it is the range. The others keep to a work, or a trench, or a surface boil. It crosses the orthogonal ghost of the grid when the squares are empty, and the crossing takes the time it takes, and in that time the only company is the sum of the machineries singing across each other, unresolved, and the slow crumple of the bright ceiling. It has begun, on those crossings, to prefer certain routes—not marked, not the same twice, but biased: the upstream face of a particular caisson, the cable on the bottom whose jacket is still unbitten, the square where a squall reliably pools fog over a cooler sluice so that the lamps below come up early and turn the installation into a brighter fact than the sun. Preference is how a place starts to have an inhabitant rather than a visitor.

Young as the sea is, the inhabitant is not older than the fill. It did not come up from bedrock. There is no bedrock. It assembled itself out of what the first water brought and what the works keep introducing: harbor blood, pipe-rush, the habit of reading a roar for distance, a body willing to be impressed by bolts and to forget the impression by the next long fetch. If you met it in the green-silver of midafternoon, mid-square, with the formwork lines still sharp under you and the wind building a short sea overhead, you might think you had seen a remaining piece of the coast that used to be here, a large simple animal crossing a bay. Stay with it. The coast-animal does not adjust its stroke to a piston thud in threes. It does not abandon a school because a gate train, still invisible, has changed its mind two kilometers off. It does not wear, for one day only, a pale stripe of ceramic dust like a number stenciled on a piling, legible at depth, before the lane-river takes it back.

By dusk the lamps are the true geography again. It will be near one of them, or in the trench that is also a duct, or holding in the dark of a joint while the engines load and the lull ends. The bait will pulse. Something will break from the school. On the surface a squall may be walking the fetch, gulls riding the gray wall, and the thing below will not know the birds except as shadows and as competitors at the boil. It does not need to know them. It needs the floor that remembers the molds, the currents that stop being rivers when the gates say so, the volume no one else is using, and the noise that never resolves into a single engine. That is enough to live on. It is already more itself than it was on the day the basin finished filling, and it is not finished. The corners of the sea are still sharp. So, where it leans, is it.
