The first signs are not bodies but absences that refuse to stay empty. A path through the high grass narrows overnight and then widens again by noon, the blades bent in two directions at once as if something passed both coming and going. Dew collects in the bends and holds the shape of a weight that was never quite heavy enough to crush. By the third morning the grass has learned the route and parts of its own accord, a pale seam running from the tree line down to the shale beach where the lake used to reach. The lake is only a darker green now, a skin of algae stitched to the mud, but the seam still ends there, and whatever uses it stops at the edge and turns back.

They do not come out while the light is hard. What moves in the early hours is smaller and quicker: beetles the size of thumbnails ferrying flakes of lichen from one stone to the next, always uphill, always in lines that break and re-form when a shadow crosses them. The stones themselves have been turned so their damp faces stay down. After several days of watching it becomes clear the turning is not random and not done by the beetles. A stone left deliberately wrong-side-up is corrected before the next dusk, and the correction leaves a print too broad for any beetle and too shallow for a hoof—five pads, the outer two longer, the center one carrying a crescent of grit that matches the shale. The print fills with water that does not sink. Something has sealed the ground under its step.

At the margin where the grass gives way to a stand of black alders, the bark has been worried in horizontal bands at about the height of a low window. The wounds are old and new together; some have healed into ridges of scar that catch the moss, others still weep a clear sap that tastes, if you are foolish enough to taste it, of iron and green walnuts. Hanging from the lowest branches are curls of the same bark, shaved thin and pierced at one end with a hole too neat for teeth. They turn in the wind and do not fall. When the wind drops they keep a faint ticking, as if something inside the curl has not finished drying. Under the trees the leaf litter is swept into low ridges that follow the same seam the grass learned. Between the ridges the soil is bare and warm to the touch even at dawn, and pressed into it at intervals are the five-pad marks, deeper here, and beside them smaller prints that are only three pads and a drag. The smaller ones never appear alone.

What lives in the alders comes down to the seam just after the light fails, and it comes in a loose file rather than a crowd. The tallest walk with a forward tilt, shoulders rounded under capes of woven grass that are not clothing so much as another layer of the slope they left. The capes are damp on the inside and dry on the outside; when one of them pauses to listen, the grass of the cape and the grass of the hill move at different speeds. Their hands—if the word holds—are long and the same rusty color as the alder leaves, and they use them to lift the bark curls one by one, holding each to an ear that is only a fold in the side of the head. Some curls they replace. Others they strip further with a thumbnail and tuck into a sling that hangs across the chest, already heavy with similar pieces. They do not speak above the tick of the bark. The sound they do make is a soft click at the back of the throat, answered from farther up the file, never from beside them.

The smaller ones travel inside the file but not in it. They move on all fours until they need height, then rise and balance with one hand on the hip of the nearest adult, the other hand carrying a single stone turned right-side-down. Their faces are narrower, the eyes set farther to the side, and they watch the tree line more than the path. When an owl crosses, the entire file stops between one step and the next, capes settling, and the smallest press themselves flat against the warm soil until the wing-sound is gone. Only then does the clicking resume, a little faster, as if counting the ones still present.

They reach the algal edge and do not cross it. Instead they kneel—the tallest first—and begin to peel the green skin back in strips, rolling each strip into a tube and weighting it with a shale flake so it will not blow into the grass. Under the skin the mud is black and smells of rot that has not quite finished. They work a trench the width of two hands, parallel to the old shoreline, and into the trench they press the bark curls they carried, standing them upright like pale commas. The smallest are sent back along the seam with empty slings to fetch more. They return at a trot, capes shedding dew, and the trench fills until it is a low palisade of bark that will, by morning, have drawn a thin line of real water out of the mud—not enough to drink, enough to darken the soil and keep the algae from sealing over again.

While this is done, one of the tallest remains standing, facing the dark water, and unwraps from inside the cape a bundle the size of a folded shirt. It is not bark. It is a piece of something that once had a straight edge and a printed mark, now soft with damp and chewed at the corners. The figure holds it open toward the lake for the length of a long breath, then refolds it smaller than before and returns it to the cape. No one else looks at it. The clicking passes over the gesture and continues. When the trench is finished they smooth the rolled algae strips into a single mat and weight the near edge with the stones the smallest brought, so the mat will not creep back overnight. Then they turn, the file reversing without shuffling, and take the seam uphill again. The last to leave is always one of the narrow-faced ones, who pauses at the first ridge of litter and sets its three-pad hand flat on the warm soil, holding it there until the heat matches on both sides. Only then does it follow.

By full dark the seam is empty and the grass has begun to stand up, but the ticks of the remaining bark curls are louder, and under the alders a new warmth is spreading outward from the places where the tallest knelt. If you wait long enough, and if you do not bring a light, you can hear the smaller sounds start: the dry shift of capes being shaken out and hung, the soft percussion of stones being stacked into low walls that will be gone by morning, the occasional cough that is answered by a click and then by silence. They are not far. The alders are only the gate. Behind them the slope steepens into a series of shallow terraces held by roots and by the same five-pad weight, and on the terraces the capes are spread to dry in a wind that does not reach the beach. Fires are small and smokeless, fed with the inner curls of bark that failed the listening, and the light they give is the color of the sap. In that light the faces are visible at last—not hidden, only accustomed to being missed—and they are neither long nor short, only tired in the particular way of people who have been correcting the same stones for longer than the lake has been gone. They eat the rolled algae cold, and they pass the folded bundle from cape to cape without opening it again, each holder weighing it a moment in the palm before giving it on. When the last holder has weighed it, the bundle is tucked into a crack between two roots and covered with a stone turned right-side-down. The stone will be wrong by dawn. Someone will already be awake to turn it back.
