What lives there does not stand in the court so much as occupy its tolerances.

You will not see it on the stair. The moss has no track that is not also the track of water. You will not hear a footfall under the engines; the knock already owns that register, and anything lighter is absorbed by leaf and brick before it can become a step. If you wait where the amber bulbs make a corridor of the middle catwalk, what arrives is not a figure entering a scene. It is a correction. A sight-glass that had begun to film over is clear again, the wipe still wet at one edge, perfectly oval, thumb-wide, with no thumb in it. A valve that had been sighing a half-note flat seats itself. A vine that had been testing the lip of a winding withdraws one tendril, not torn, not burned—simply no longer interested, as if a boundary had been restated in a language roots understand.

It has a body, or it keeps one. The body is the size of a person who has spent a life ducking casings and does not remember standing straight. Shoulders slope to the habit of the galleries. The hands are the clearest part: long, stained the color of the water, nails worn square against metal and soft against petiole, the pads mapped with old burns that never quite finished healing and never quite needed to. Skin elsewhere is harder to hold in the eye. Where the steam hangs, the outline thins. Where a fern presses close, the outline takes on the fern’s serration for a moment and then lets it go. Clothes, if they are clothes, are the same dun as the brick at the waterline—canvas gone to felt, felt gone to a second moss, pockets weighted with washers, twine, a folded rag that is always damp and always clean enough for glass.

It does not eat in any way you would call a meal. It drinks at the weir the way the engines drink, a mouth to the sheet, a swallow that does not interrupt the fall. What it takes from the plants is smaller than grazing: a pinch of new growth from a place that would have crowded a gauge, a bitter leaf chewed once and spat into the sump, a sweetness licked from a split stem and then forgotten. Nothing in the court is food for it, and nothing in the court is threatened by that. The absence of animals is not its hunting. It is older than the absence, or the absence is its shadow. Moths do not come to the bulbs because the bulbs, under its attention, burn at a frequency that is light to iron and nothing to wings. Rats do not nest in the sump because the sump is already occupied—by temperature, by a low continuous tremor, by the knowledge, somehow distributed through pipe and root, that a warm dark hollow here is not empty.

Its work is the settlement, kept without improvement. It does not garden toward beauty and does not maintain toward efficiency. It walks the three layers of catwalk on a round that is not a clock-round and not a whim: sight-glasses, glands, the places where a root has begun to think itself a gasket, the bolts whose rust-fans have started to crawl again after years of stillness. A hand on a casing is a listening. A hand on a stem is the same listening in another key. Where heat is loaned too generously and a brake-fern crisps at the margin, it turns a damper an eighth, no more, and the next day’s growth comes in green. Where a pump lifts a little less than the court requires, it does not open the machine. It clears a single strand of algae from an intake with a fingernail, and the knock downstairs finds its old interval again.

It speaks, but not to visitors, and not in sentences that leave the mouth. The schedule of the valves is its speech: open, hold, shut, the sighs arranged so the vines receive steam when they can use it and are spared when they cannot. The slap of the belt is punctuation it allows, a looseness it has decided is better than a tension that would fatigue the splice. If you stand long enough in the lower gallery you begin to hear the round as grammar. A downpipe clears its throat because it was asked. A leaf nods because the receipt was witnessed. Production continues at a room’s worth because a room’s worth is what the body can feel all at once—every winding within the reach of attention, every weep-hole still granting water, every lamp still owed its cage.

It does not remember arriving. Memory, for it, is patina. There was a street above, once, and weather that fell instead of condensing, and a shift that ended. Those facts have the status of fittings left in a wall after the machine they served was scrapped: true, load-bearing nothing, rounded by the same water that rounds everything else. What it knows freshly is local and exact. Which coupling weeps on the humid side of the week. Which fern will try the winding again before the month turns. How long a drip must hang before the leaf is ready to take it. It knows the court the way a callus knows a handle—by repetition worn past noticing, and by the flare of noticing when the handle changes by a hair.

It is not lonely in the way a person is lonely, because the register of animals was subtracted so long ago that companionship is not imagined as fur or voice. It is accompanied by continuity. The engines answer. The weir does not finish its sentence. The green presses, and is answered, and presses again. When it pauses at the head of the iron stair—at the one place where day reaches, gray and rationed—it does not look up toward the street. It looks along the diagonal of mossed treads, counting nothing, confirming that the stair is still a stair and still a softness, both, and that this is correct.

If you remain past the point where your own pulse tries to match the knock, it may include you in the round the way it includes a new drip: not welcomed, not refused, noted. A rag might be left where your sleeve brushed a casing, erasing the human oil before it can pit the plate. A vine near your shoulder might be pinched back, not to clear your view, but to keep your view from becoming a gap the green would have to learn as permanent. It will not ask what you want. Want is a pressure it does not service. The court is already at the volume it can bear.

Should you leave, the settlement closes over the place you stood with the same patience it uses on a bolt. Moss does not hurry. The sight-glasses stay clear. Somewhere under the lamps the body returns to its tolerances—shoulders sloped, hands mapped, outline thinning where the steam folds back into the leaves—and the work goes on at the rate of water finding a channel it decided centuries ago, and of something that decided, more recently and more quietly, to remain inside that decision and keep it true.
