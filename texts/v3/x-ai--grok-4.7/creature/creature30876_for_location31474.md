They are easiest to miss where they are most present, which is everywhere the ground has finished deciding what it is. At a distance the first impression is not of life but of a mistake in the ridgeline: a length of darker stone that should not have survived the planing, a hump the weather forgot. Walk toward it and the mistake does not correct itself. It only becomes warmer. The air a body's length out is a degree less honest about the cold. There is a smell like a whetstone just wetted, and under that a sweetness so faint it might be the lichen warming in your own breath. Then a plate along the spine lifts the width of a finger, settles, and the ridge is an animal, and has been one since before you chose a direction.

What lives here is mostly that animal, repeated at intervals insulting to any idea of a herd. Call them engines only after you have stood under one long enough to feel the knock; before that they are simply the large life, the life that makes the plateau's silence have a weight. A grown one runs the length of a fallen hall and not much higher than a laden wagon. The legs are short for the mass, set wide, ending in pads as broad as doors, ridged underneath in chevrons that grip shelves without scraping them. The head is the indecent part: small, almost an afterthought, set low, with a wide lipless mouth and eyes the color of wet slate set so far to the sides that it is never quite looking at you and never quite not. The ears are shallow cups filled with pale down, more for the change in pressure before a rain than for any sound another creature might make. There are no horns, no mane, no display. Sex, when it can be read at all, is a difference in the hang of the belly plates and in a faint musk on the windward flank that the cold keeps local. You can pass a dozen and not be sure you have seen both kinds.

The hide is the argument they have with the stone, and they are winning it slowly by becoming it. Plates overlap from the poll to the root of a tail so short it is only a rudder for lying down. The plates are grown, not carried: keratin gone mineral, grit worked in season after season until a thumbnail raises dust the color of the shelf they last rested on. At the joints the crust is thin and the true color shows, a dull liver-dark, damp, a little paler than blood. Along the belly the gaps are wider. Press a wrist there and the heat is immediate, indecent, a hand's span of summer kept for a purpose. The purpose is the mill. You hear it if you are willing to waste an hour with your ear against a plate: not a rhythm, a decision. Silence, then one low knock, as of a stone turned over in a barrel, then silence long enough that you decide you imagined it, then another. The knock is the gizzard, or the gut, or whatever organ has given up the distinction. It grinds lichen, the grit the lichen bound, the stiff pale stems when a basin still holds them, and a steady ration of flour the wind has already milled and simply not sorted. Heat is the solvent. Charge is the leftover. On the driest weeks the rime on the windward plates grows faster along one side of the spine, and if you carry metal in your mouth you will feel it, a prickle, not a miracle. They do not use the spark. It leaks, the way heat leaks, and the cloud takes both.

They feed by lowering the whole front of the body until the mouth can work the crust without the neck having to admit it is a neck. The mill takes what the tongue — broad, rasped, dark — can rake. A day's grazing is a shallow brightening of the shelf, lichen pared to a film, not stripped to bare. They are not in a hurry to impoverish what impoverishes so slowly. When the mouth lifts, threads of mineral slime bridge the lip and the ground and break without sound. Hours later, or the next day, the casting comes: a friable pale rope, already half stone, laid in a line so straight it looks surveyed because the body that laid it does not fidget. Rain sorts the rope. Clays drift a hand's breadth and stop. Heavier grains stay — dark, sometimes green, sometimes a dull brass — and become, over a life longer than counting, the seams that run the shelves like the memory of roads. They are not roads. They are appetite, filed.

What else lives here lives in their margin, and would not survive the margin's closing. Under the drip-line, where breath and flank pull a little water out of air that had sworn it had none, there are shrew-things: palm-sized, warm, almost odorless, fur the color of rime so that stillness makes them stone. They drink the condensate and pick the organic fraction the mill did not finish from the edges of fresh castings. They do not squeak. A boot can pass over one and register only gravel; the trick is that the gravel was watching, and then was not interested. In the same margin, beetles with matte shells work a slower clock, legs too cold to tick. Near the rare stands of stiff stems a web of pale threads binds stem to grit — fungus, or a truce that only resembles fungus — and is taken, when taken, as a single mouthful in a day. Wingless moths the color of frost cling to the leeward plates and do nothing visible except not die. None of this small life is abundant. Abundance here is vertical: one great body, and the minute republic that can fit in its weather.

They do not speak. Nothing here does. A person who shouts hears the shout taken apart within a few lengths, not by echo but by a thinness that will not hold shape. The large life gave up voice so long ago that the throat retains no bellows for it. What passes between them, when anything does, passes by presence and by the seams. A shelf already threaded with old castings is a sentence another can read with its mouth: this was grazed, the mill here ran rich in brass-grain, the body that worked it stood a month and then turned toward the basin that is now only a stain. You will see two of them a valley apart adjust their headings over the course of a day until their paths, extrapolated, would meet next season in a pocket both have reason to expect will hold water. They are not going to each other. They are going to the same fact. If they arrive together they may stand flank to flank for a week, sharing a warm cylinder of air, and part without having touched. Calves — the word is wrong; the young are already the size of cattle and the same color as the shelf they were dropped on — stay inside that cylinder until their own mill knocks true, then drift, not wean so much as become a separate weather. There is no herd defense because there is nothing on the plateau that has ever needed defending against. You are not a predator. You are a temperature that will leave.

A life is a sequence of shelves and one or two basins that held, once. They lie down by folding, not collapsing: the pads set, the mass lowered in a controlled failure, the near side plates lifting to vent heat if the mill is running hard. The oval polish on the oldest shelves is that gesture, repeated until the stone agreed. They sleep with the eyes half-lidded and the knock continuing, slower. Dying, when it happens, is a longer lying-down. The mill's interval stretches. The flank cools from the spine inward, last heat held in the belly like a coal in ash. The shrew-things remain until the drip-line fails, then scatter to the next engine or dry into the cracks, indistinguishable from lichen. The plates dull, crack along the growth lines, and in a decade are a low scatter the wind sorts into the same seams the mouth once laid. Nothing scavenges in any hurry. There is no hurry. The cloud, robbed of one flank's breath, does not noticeably thin. Only the local rime comes in a little less faithfully, and the seam ends, and another animal, months or years off, reads the ending and does not turn that way.

If you stay, the rules they follow become the rules you follow, not by instruction but by the air. Do not spend sound. Do not count on a pocket of water to be there because it was there; they know the pockets by a greening you will learn, and by a wetter cold, and they are more often right. Do not cross close under the belly when the knock is frequent — not from danger, from the rudeness of standing in a factory while it works. If you are in a path, the path will bend, over a day, by the angle required. There is no malice and no curiosity sharp enough to be called interest. A head may lift — the only quickness, and it is not quick — and the side-set eye will hold you the way it holds a stain where water stood. Then it lowers. You were not feedstock. You were not weather. You were a brief warm mistake in a census that has already decided the important masses, and is content, for the length of your staying, to count you as part of the dimness.

What lives here, then, is a patience with a mouth. It is also the patience's dependents, and the cloud those bodies accidentally keep, and the seams their leavings become when rain is brief and local and done. Stand long enough and the first image — a ridge that was wrong — gives way to the second: a continent being chewed, warmed, sorted, and laid back down by things that will not call it work. They are still there when you stop looking. The knock comes once. The light does not change. Somewhere a casting cools into the color of the shelf, and the shelf, which had thought itself finished, accepts one more line.
