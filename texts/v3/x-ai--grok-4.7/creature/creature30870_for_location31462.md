They are not one animal wearing many bodies. The floor holds a company of kinds that have worn each other into complementary shapes, and the company only looks single because nothing here has room to stand apart.

The smallest are the grain. They are flatter than they first appear, coin-thin when they turn edge-on to the light, and their shells are not armor so much as a crust they have convinced the mineral to grow for them—salt and silica and a little iron, laid down in faint rings that can be counted if a shell is cracked, which almost never happens while they live. Under the shell the body is a soft hinge. They do not have a face you would call a face. A seam opens along the forward rim and a tongue of rasping plates works the ground the way a file works soft stone, never biting deep, only taking what has already begun to loosen: the bloom of oxide, the sugar of salt, the dust that other feet have ground one grade finer. Their legs are many and nearly invisible, a fringe that does not lift them so much as row them. When the light is ordinary and even, as it always is, you can see the fringe only as a slight blur at the margin, and the blur is how you know which way a patch of floor intends to go.

They do not hunt one another. Hunger here is not a chase. It is a position. To be on top is to meet the crust before it has been worked. To be underneath is to take what the upper mouths have missed, and also to be shielded from the dry rasp of air that moves—barely—when the mass turns. So they stack without agreement and without resentment. A body that has fed enough simply slows, and the ones behind it climb, and the slowed one becomes floor for a while. Some of them never speed up again. The shells of those stay in the mosaic until the rasps above them thin the shell itself into grit, and then they are gone in the only way anything is gone here: by being made smaller than the mouths that follow.

Among them, and slower, move the ones the upper layer rearranges for. These are longer, still hard, still matte, but their shells are not grown from the basin so much as assembled—plates of older shell and flakes of machine-metal seated into living seams, so that a large one, if you could lift it, would show a patchwork of pale mineral and dull industrial gray, the gray always a little warmer where the oxide has not yet been licked back. They are not predators in the clearing sense. What they take is already being taken. They work the denser seams: the bolt that has not powdered, the tooth of the wheel where grease once lived and left a stain the mineral still remembers, the black glass where the fused floor is thick enough to repay a deeper cut. Their mouths are narrower and more patient. They leave grooves you can follow with a fingertip, and the grain comes after them and softens the groove into the general braid.

They do not speak. What passes between them is pressure and pause. A large one turning under the pile makes a hole that seals, and the sealing is the message: here the floor is occupied from below; pass over; do not pack. Where two large ones meet in a pipe’s shadow they may lock their forward seams for a moment, plate to plate, and trade nothing you could call food—only a grit of metal-dust that one has concentrated and the other lacks. Afterward both move off at angles, and the grain fills the place they vacated as if the meeting had been weather.

There is a third kind, rarer, and easy to miss because it does not add to the rattle so much as change its grain. These are the still ones. They look, at first, like housing half-buried, or like a knot of grain that has died in a heap and fused. If you watch long enough the heap breathes: a slow lift, a slow settle, timed to nothing in the light. Under the borrowed shells a body larger than the large ones holds itself almost closed. It does not rasp. It takes what falls into the seams of its disguise—salt hop, rust flake, the occasional grain that slows too much and is not climbed off of in time. It is not cruel about this. The grain that slow are already on their way to becoming grit. The still one only chooses the moment. When it has taken enough it may shift a hand’s width toward a pipe or a wheel, and the company flows around the shift without alarm, the way water accepts a stone it has always known.

None of them sleep in a den. Sleep, if that is the word, is a local thinning. At no hour does the floor go quiet, but there are stretches where the pitch drops and the braid of tracks grows faint, and in those stretches a layer of grain simply stops rowing and lets itself be walked. Their rings do not advance while they are still. When they start again the new ring is paler, as if the mineral they laid down in stillness were cleaner than what they lay down while moving. You can read a life in a broken shell that way, if you are willing to break one: long pale bands for the times the mass rested, narrow dark bands for the times it worked a fresh oxide or a tooth still sharp enough to cut.

They have no memory of water. Whatever in their making once required it has been replaced by the salt itself, which they move through their fringes and their seams the way other animals move blood. Moisture, if it ever enters the pocket—and the sealed rim makes this almost a contradiction—would not be a gift. It would cake the dust, dull the rasps, glue a generation to the floor until the ones above ate the glue. So the dry is not a hardship they endure. It is the condition their mouths are shaped to. The rattle is the sound of that condition being satisfied, over and over, at the scale of coins and knuckles.

Toward the rim, where the mineral lip is barely higher than a hand, the company thins. Not because the edge is dangerous. Because past it there is no floor continuous with this one, and they do not cross discontinuities. A grain that is pushed to the lip by the pressure behind it will turn and insert itself back into the mass rather than go over. The large ones never reach the lip at all; the pipe and the wheel and the housings are nearer the center, where the old work sank, and they orbit those the way the grain orbits them. The still ones prefer the lee of metal. So the rim stays almost clean, a pale border, and the border is the nearest thing they have to a skyline.

If you stay, the early picture—a swarm, a rattle, a floor being worn down—gives way to a slower one. You begin to see the divisions that are not kinds but times of life. The newest grain are paler, their rings few, their rasps quick and wasteful, lifting more dust than they swallow. The middle-aged carry darker bands and walk lower, economical, taking the film and leaving the stone. The oldest of the grain are nearly the color of the basin and move so little that the large ones sometimes plate over them, mistaking them for shell-stock. The large ones themselves show age in the metal they have kept: a young one is mostly pale, with one or two gray seats; an old one is a quilt of bolts and flakes, heavy enough that the grain must detour, and when it finally stops for good the quilt does not scatter. It becomes a housing. Later a still one may roof itself there. Later still the grain will climb it and the rattle will include, for a season, the faint tick of living feet on a shell that no longer answers.

They will not finish the basin. The stone is deeper than their patience, and their patience is only hunger distributed across many mouths. What they will finish is every loose film, every powdering bolt, every tooth that can be rounded, every shell that dies in reach. The machines will shrink into rumors of shape. The wheel will become a low oval, then a stain, then a taste in the bodies that worked it, passed along in those brief plate-to-plate meetings until no one is left who carries the taste, and the meetings continue anyway, trading other dusts. The rattle will not stop when that happens. It will only lose a pitch it had, the small bright one that comes from feet on cut metal, and settle deeper into the hiss of mineral on mineral.

That is what lives here: a metabolism with no outside, dressed in many shells, loud because dryness carries sound and because there is nothing else to interrupt it. Not a people, not a nest, not a last survivor pacing a ruin. A floor that learned how to move, and then learned how to be several ages at once, and then forgot there had ever been a difference.
