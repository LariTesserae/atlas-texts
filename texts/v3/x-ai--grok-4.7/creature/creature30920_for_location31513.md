The first thing that lives here is not a body but a habit of remaining. Men come down from the rim in small parties, never alone, because the noise eats a single voice before it can call back, and they stay longer than the shift-board says they will. Their boots are already the color of the clay by the time they reach the first pump-cage. They do not hurry. Hurrying slicks the footing and the basin punishes a fall by keeping whatever you dropped—tool, glove, the small dry thought you meant to carry back up. So they move the way the water moves: finding the next lower place, occupying it, waiting to see whether it holds.

They live in the sheds and in the gaps under the idle belts, not because the sheds are dry—nothing is—but because a roof, even a leaking one, gives the condensation somewhere particular to fall. A man sleeps with his head on a coil of hose and wakes with the pattern of the braid pressed into his cheek and a film of warm water in the hollow of his ear. He does not shake it out at once. It will only return. Their food is what they carry down and what they can keep above the sheets: tins, hard bread, a sack of rice that has to be hung from a rafter or it wicks itself into paste. They cook on a small stove whose flame is always a little blue at the edges from the damp in the fuel. The smell of the cooking does not travel. It sits in the shed with the ammonia and the warm stone and becomes part of the ordinary.

They speak little, and when they do it is into each other’s collars, mouths close, the words shaped more by breath than by sound. Names shorten. Directions become points of the hand. A man will tap another’s shoulder and point at a pipe that has begun to knock in a new rhythm, and that is a sentence. They know the basin by its failures. This pool was a hand lower yesterday. That roof-plate dropped a thumb of grit in the night. The electric pump on the western reach has started to sing in a higher key, which means the bearing is taking water again and someone will have to go out with the grease and the spare and stand in the sheet up to the knee while the midges find every margin of skin. They do not curse the work. Cursing takes air they would rather spend on the next step.

Among them move others who are not on any board. A woman keeps to the older spoil heaps where the reeds have taken, and she is known because she is the only one who walks the margins without a pump to tend. She carries a pole and a flat basket and she lifts the algal skin in places, looking, setting it back. The men leave her the small dry rises. She leaves them the sheds. Sometimes she sets a knot of green reed-root where a berm is slipping, and by the next season it has held or it has not; either way she does not explain it. Children come rarely, and only with the supply parties, and they are kept to the spur where the rails still show. They throw stones into the sheets to watch the waders lift and settle, and the stones vanish without a splash you can hear. After a while the children stop throwing. The fullness does not reward the gesture.

The animals do not share the place with these people so much as continue through them. Frogs will sit on a boot if the boot stays still long enough. A wader will stalk the length of a pipe-bridge while a man crosses it the other way, each adjusting by a finger’s width, neither yielding the line. In the deepest pockets the fish rise to the drip from a man’s cuff as readily as to a fly. The midges do not distinguish. They take the warm film off skin and off stone with the same appetite. Nests appear in the folds of spare belts, in the mouths of pipes that have been capped and forgotten, in the crown of a hard hat left on a valve wheel. The people clear what they must and leave the rest. There is no perimeter that would hold. A shed that is swept in the morning has a new line of droppings by dusk and a frog in the corner that was not there when the broom passed.

At night—which is only a deepening of the same gray-amber, the splits in the crust gone black, the open reaches losing their far edges—the basin does not empty of work. The pumps keep their note. Someone always has a lamp, a small battery light hooded with a hand so it does not throw too far and call every insect in the reach. They move between the known failures, checking levels with a stick, listening to bearings with a cheek laid to the housing. Sleep happens in pieces. A man will doze against a warm pipe and wake when the knock changes, already on his feet before he has decided to stand. Dreams, when they come, are of drier sounds: a door closing in air that does not drink the latch, a voice that carries across a room. They do not speak of the dreams. By morning the film has rebuilt on every surface, including the dreamer, and the day is only the night with a little more gray let down through the cracks.

What lives here longest is the attention. It does not belong to any one body. It passes from the woman with the basket to the man with the grease to the child who has stopped throwing stones, and it stays on the water, on the fresh faces of clay, on the small motions that add, over years, to the large ones no one will see complete. They are not waiting for the basin to finish being born. They have understood that their lives are inside the birth, and that the birth does not consult them. So they occupy what they can, yield what they must, and keep the pumps turning, not because the turning will win, but because a stopped pump floods the shed by morning, and the shed is where the rice hangs, and the rice is what lets them remain another day inside the noise and the wet and the slow arrival of the floor.
