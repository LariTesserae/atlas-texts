What lives here does not arrive. It is already accounted for, the way heat is accounted for, and the only introductions are births into a census that has no spare line. You learn them by the interruptions they make in a system that otherwise continues, and then you learn that most of them make none.

Closest to the ground the life is not separate from the works. It is the works wearing a skin for a while. In the litter, bodies no longer than a finger-joint travel corridors that exist only because other bodies traveled them yesterday and the day before, a traffic of chewing and carriage whose routes are older than any one traveler. They do not explore. Exploration would be waste. A grain of softened wood, a thread of fungus, a bead of condensation in a fold of leaf: these are known addresses, and the small things move between them with the irritable exactness of clerks who have held the same desk too long. When one dies it is not mourned and not wasted. It is folded back into the same traffic by something slightly smaller, or by the same something, later. The dark at that height smells of them: a dry, heated, slightly sour smell under the sweeter rot, the smell of many mouths working at once and none of them open to the air.

Above that, in the root gaps and the collapsed mouths of old burrows, live the keepers of still water. They are not large. A hand would cover most of them. Their virtue is that they do not spend. A pocket of water under a root is a fortune in this basin, and they sit in it or beside it with their bellies against the cool and their backs against the warm, and they alter their position by the width of a leaf when the day shifts, and that is their whole diplomacy. Skin drinks. Skin also listens. You can pass within a stride and not see them, because they have arranged themselves into the grammar of damp: a darkening, a gleam, a shape that could be a stone that never quite dried. Only if the water itself is threatened do they move like animals, and then it is a short, ugly, efficient motion, and afterward the pocket looks as if nothing had ever claimed it. They have been claiming it for longer than the tree above them has been standing.

The things that use the trunks live vertically, which in this country is a kind of wealth. Horizontal space is argued over. Vertical space is rented from the bark. Some are flat and patient, pressed so long against a tree that the tree has grown a lip around them, a living bezel. They feed on what travels the surface: the slow syrup of sap where a crack has opened, the traffic of smaller bodies, the film that condensation leaves and the heat thickens. They have mouths but you will not see a mouth. You will see a place on the bark that is cleaner than the bark around it, a small polished absence, and if you watch that absence for the length of time the basin considers reasonable — a long time — you may see it advance by the width of a fingernail. Others are the opposite: all grip and angle, living in the forks, in the crooks where a branch has argued with the trunk and lost. They are the weight the limbs have grown to accept. Their eyes, when they open, take the available light and return almost none of it. A coin, the place said, and that is right: a small dull coin, and then the coin is gone, and you are looking at bark again, and the branch is complaining in that single low note because the weight has shifted its hips and settled.

Nothing here hunts the way a colder country hunts. The heat bills you for every stride. So the killers are mostly arrangements. A shape in the water that is not water. A length along a branch that is not branch. A patience so complete it stops reading as patience and starts reading as furniture. The strike, when it comes, is not a drama. It is a correction. Something that was breathing in one place is breathing, briefly and badly, in another, and then the basin's quiet reasserts itself, having spent only what it had to spend. You can stand in the same pool for an hour and be in no danger, and you can take one step into a darker seam of it and be finished, and the difference is not marked in any way a newcomer could learn except by the difference. The old inhabitants know the seams. They step where the bottom is honest. They drink with their bodies already turned toward the exit they will not need. Superstition, you might call it, if you had not watched them do it the same way every evening of a life.

In the open brown of the oxbows, where the river has forgotten a bend and left the water to warm itself, there are bodies that have made an accommodation with being half-submerged. The largest of them stand. They do not swim if they can avoid it; swimming is a commotion, and commotion travels even in water this thick. They stand to the belly, or to the chest, and the water holds part of their weight, and the heat of the basin and the heat of their own bulk come to an arrangement somewhere under the hide. Flies attend them and are endured. A tail moves, not to be beautiful, but because a tail is a cheaper answer than a foot. Their eyes are placed for the horizontal world: the bank, the next body, the place where the bottom shelves into something you cannot stand in. They look at you if you are a fact. They do not look at you if you are only weather. Between them and the bank there is often a younger one, lower in the water, learning the depth by the slowest possible method, which is to be in it. A bird rides one of the broad backs and is not a companion. It is a tenant with a short lease and a mean little competence, taking what the hide cannot reach. When the great body finally moves — and it will, toward evening, toward a shelf of firmer mud — the bird lifts, complains once in a voice the leaves swallow, and settles again before the ripples have died. Nobody has improved this arrangement. It is old enough to be dull, which here is a kind of perfection.

Higher, where the canopy has closed and the noon is only a greener dark, the life is made of hands and of the memory of hands. Not true hands, most of them. Grasping feet, hooked digits, a tail that can be trusted with the whole argument of a body. They live in the overlapping of leaves, in a country with a floor you cannot see and a ceiling that moves when the wind, very rarely, remembers the basin. They are quieter than their cousins in more generous forests, because quiet is cheaper than a fight, and a fight in this heat is a debt that comes due in the ribs. They travel paths that are not visible from below: a sequence of branches that have held, a vine that has been tested, a gap that can be crossed if you do not think about the gap. Food is not found so much as revisited. A tree that fruits does so on a calendar the canopy keeps better than any observer, and on the correct morning the dark up there is full of small relocations, a soft mechanical sound of weight changing address, and then it is empty again, and the fruit that was there is an idea the leaves are already covering. They watch downward more than a canopy animal in a cooler place would. The danger here is not only above and beside. It is the arrangement on the limb they might have chosen, the one that is not limb. So they pause. They pause so long you think the pause is the animal. Then a hand — call it a hand — closes on the next hold, and the pause has moved, and the branch gives its one note, and the canopy accepts the correction and continues.

There are the fliers, but they are not many, and they do not sing the place into cheerfulness. The air between leaves is a corridor with rules. Wings work, in this wet, like cloth that has been left out. So the ones that live by wing do it in short, exact strokes, from a known perch to a known perch, and they spend the rest of themselves in stillness so complete that moss has sometimes started on a back that did not intend to be a stone. Their calls, when they must call — to hold a few yards of branch, to warn off a shape, to find another of their kind in a darkness that eats sound — are single and low and finished almost as soon as they begin. You do not get a chorus. You get a syllable, and then the syllable is taken back by the leaves, and if there is an answer it comes from closer than you expected, because farther than that is another country. At certain hours, above the seeps where the ground's chemistry wavers the air, a few of them hang in the updraft of that faint stupid heat and do not flap at all. It is the only generosity the basin offers a wing: a place where the work of staying up has been outsourced to decay. They use it without gratitude. Gratitude would be another expenditure.

And there is one more kind, which you will not inventory, because it does not present a body so much as a persistence. It is whatever has learned the basin so thoroughly that it no longer has the outline of a newcomer. You see its signs the way you see a path that no one admits is a path: a place on the bank where the mud is smoothed not by water but by the repeated laying-down of a weight; a tree whose bark is polished in a vertical band at the height of a shoulder; a gap in the stems that is not a gap the plants agreed to, but a gap maintained, quietly, by being used. It may be one animal. It may be a lineage that has been the same animal for so long the difference no longer matters. It drinks where the bottom is honest. It stands in the warm water at the hour when standing is cheaper than walking. It does not call. If it has eyes, they return even less light than the others, a coin worn past any denomination. The rest of the basin's inhabitants know the size of it not by sight, most of the time, but by the way their own patience adjusts when they come into its weather. The small clerks in the litter do not adjust. The keepers of still water do. The canopy pauses a fraction longer on the branches that pass over its smoothed mud. Nobody flees in a panic. Panic is for places where you can run to somewhere that is not already full. Here, knowing is enough, and the knowing is distributed, and the thing itself — if it is a thing, and not simply the oldest habit the ground has — continues its round, which is short, and hot, and exactly as long as it needs to be, and which has been completed so many times that the basin would notice an absence sooner than it would notice a stranger.

Night does not empty any of this. Night is only the heat without the pretense of a sun. Eyes open their small coins. Flanks rise and fall. Something relocates by a body's length and is done for the dark. The works underfoot do not take a shift change; there is no shift, there is only the same chewing, the same seepage, the same slow furnace. If you stay, and if you can bear the film on your skin and the sweetness tipping toward metal, you stop looking for the inhabitant and start to feel the occupancy: a pressure, not hostile, not welcoming, simply complete. Every interval has a tenant. Every tenant is spending as little as the rules allow. And somewhere in the brown water a large stillness breathes out, and the surface accepts the breath, and does not otherwise change.
