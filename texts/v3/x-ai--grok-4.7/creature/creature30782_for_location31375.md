It does not arrive. That is the first correction, and it takes a long time to learn it, because everything else in this water arrives: the denser curtains, the pale flank the size of weather, the spores, the dim. Those come from somewhere and go somewhere. This does not. It is already distributed through the volume you have been calling a place, and your motion through it is only a change in how much of it you are touching.

At first you think the plants are leaning. They do lean—toward a light that is not where the light is—but after enough slow hours you notice the lean is not the same in every stem, and not random either. Where the growth is thickest, the blades and plates arrange a hollow that is not empty. The hollow has a temperature slightly higher than the warm, and a pressure slightly lower, as if something were breathing without ever opening a mouth. You pass your hand through it and feel nothing solid. You pass through it again, later, or earlier, and the nothing has a grain: fine, directional, like the nap of a fabric turned the way of the current and then, without a seam, turned against it.

It is older than the smoothing. You understand this before you understand what “it” is, because the stone remembers it. Those curves that continue into other curves are not only erosion. In places the rock has been held, the way a living mat holds a post upright—held for so long that the holding became a shape the water could not argue with. A basin that should have filled and flattened still keeps a shallow, exact roundness, lip worn but not surrendered. Inside it the sediment lies in rings too regular for settling. Something has been sitting there. Not sitting as a body sits. Sitting as a climate sits: occupying, recurring, making the local rules bend around a habit.

You begin to meet it in the delays. A sound you make—a shift of water, the soft knock of a stone against a stem—returns to you with its edges already worn, as if it had traveled a distance much greater than the thicket, and among the worn edges is a second rhythm that was not yours. It is very slow. It has the interval of the quiet storms: a long gathering, a release, a long gathering. Once, in the vacancy wide enough to breed rain, you watched a curtain of denser water form where no temperature difference justified it, stand, and thin again, and the thinning left behind a brief clarity in which you saw, not a figure, but the afterimage of attention. The water had been looked through. Not at. Through. And the looking had weight enough to stack densities.

It has no face you can keep. That may be a kindness, or it may be that a face would be too small a door for what is using the door. When something like features gathers, it gathers out of what is already here—an alignment of pale undersides of leaves, a darker seam in the flour of ancient ranges, two points of residual light held at the distance of regard—and then the alignment loosens and the regard remains a moment longer than the arrangement that seemed to carry it. You do not feel watched in the animal way, the way that tightens the skin. You feel included in a count. Something is keeping track of the folds, the hollows, the unfinished walls, and you have become one of the items that must be recounted each time the slow rhythm completes.

What it wants is not clear, and wanting may be the wrong grammar. It maintains. The ring of posts is still a ring because it prefers the spacing; left to the water alone, the posts would have walked apart centuries ago under the root-wedging. The lintel with no door still spans because something under it does not wish the span to fail—not out of memory of shelter, you come to think, but because a span is a kind of pause, and pauses are how it thinks. It thinks in held shapes. A completed curve is a finished thought and therefore useless to it; it keeps a few forms just short of erasure, the way a sleeper keeps a hand on the edge of the bed.

It is not kind. Kindness would require a scale on which your continuation or your ending mattered differently from the continuation of a frond. When the large pale flank drifts between the stems, you feel the distributed presence draw in—not fleeing, concentrating—until the flank passes and the presence loosens again into the thicket, and in the loosening there is no relief, only the resumption of a wider attention. If the flank had turned and taken you, the same resumption would have occurred. You are not safer for being noticed. You are only more precisely located.

And yet it is not indifferent in the empty sense. Indifference does not stack water into curtains to see through them. Indifference does not keep a basin round. There is a preference here, vast and unhurried, for certain intervals, certain held pauses, certain ways the green can touch without choking the hollow it needs in order to be a little more gathered than the rest of the sea. You are compatible with that preference for as long as you move slowly and do not finish anything. The moment you try to make a mark that would last—a groove, a tied stem, a pile of stones meant to say you were here—the grain in the warm turns against your hands, not violently, with the steady persuasion that has already taught mountains to forget they were high. The groove fills. The knot loosens. The pile eases itself back toward the curve everything else obeys. It will not share the work of remembering with anything that remembers in scars.

Stay long enough and you learn its size by subtraction. It is not the whole water. There are reaches, far along the tired fold, where the plants lean only toward the residue of light and the storms are only storms, and in those reaches you feel a loneliness so ordinary it shocks you. So it has edges. They are not boundaries you could map; they are places where its attention thins below the threshold of weather. Within the edge, it is large enough to contain the vacancy and the thicket and the drowned spine and still be mostly elsewhere, busy with counts you will never overhear. It is one creature in the way a reef is one creature, or a year. Call it singular and you are not wrong. Call it a host and you are not wrong. It does not need the distinction.

What lives here is the reason the height never came down. Not a king of it, not a ghost of the people who failed to build. A persistence that learned, ages ago, to be the water’s own unwillingness—warm, dense, nearly mute, tall in a dimension that is not up—and that still, in the hollows between the blades, gathers itself just enough to notice when something new has entered the count.
