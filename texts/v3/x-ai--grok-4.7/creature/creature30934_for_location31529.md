The first thing that lives here is not a person. It is the population that never applied for the town and was never turned away: the snails on every wet face, the pale fish holding their agreed gaps in the basins, the eels in the bores, the halo-stack of crickets and precipitate-winged moths and the beetle that drinks only the technic frost. They were here in some ancestral form before the brick, or they arrived in the first stocking and forgot the surface so thoroughly that descent became anatomy. They do not belong to the keepers. The keepers belong to the same timetable, and have learned not to pretend otherwise. A cleared pipe silts. A frightened eel leaves a bore, and for a week the pump breathes wrong, and wrongness is the only loud thing in the chamber. So the animals are not livestock and not omens. They are neighbors with better tenure.

Watch the fish long enough and the word school fails. Each one occupies a pocket of still water as if the pocket had been assigned, and when the header tank begins to lean they adjust before any keeper has admitted the lean is real. The eels are more opinionated. They travel the bores the way a clerk travels a corridor he built himself: not exploring, correcting. One of them, thicker than a man's arm and the color of a shovel left out for years, has lived in the south main since before the current metering house had its tar. Keepers call him the Clerk, without romance. He does not come when called. If he leaves a bore that has always held him, the round changes. No one argues with that. Argument is a dry-country habit, and it costs precipitate.

Above them, in the vault, the thing that folds and unfolds on a longer cycle is a roost, not a monster: bats the size of gloves, hundreds, asleep in a single mineral curtain that looks, from the causeway, like the ceiling deciding to be leather. They do not hunt in the chamber. They use it as a lung uses a ribcage. At the hour the west leak is least gray they go up a crack the size of a wrist and come back heavier, and the snails on the drip-line beneath them get a thin rain of what the hill's night had to spare. The keepers do not lamp the vault. Brightness lifts the roost, and a lifted roost clogs the intakes by morning. Darkness, again, is a tool.

The people are fewer than the tanks, and spaced with the same stinginess. You can walk the long axis and pass four of them and feel you have met a crowd. They do not cluster except when a halo demands it, or when a cake is ready and the racks must be turned under the one lamp nobody lowers. Between those necessities they keep the distances the chamber taught them. Sound dies at a short range. Living close would be living in each other's weather, and their weather is mostly moisture and patience, which do not improve by being shared.

They are not a pale legend. They are ordinary bodies that have spent their lives in one temperature. Skin holds a little of the lime and never quite gives it back, so the creases at the wrists and the corners of the eyes are lighter than the rest, a map of where water has sat. Hands are the part that looks oldest, even on the young: iron teaches callus, and the seep teaches a shine, and between them the fingers look used the way a good valve looks used. Eyes adjust, and then they refuse to unadjust. A keeper who goes up the shaft for a surface errand comes back with a two-day flinch and a grudge against noon. Hearing is the sense that has been promoted. They notice pressure before noise. A header tank filling is a change in the way the ear feels full, hours before the pump admits the work. Children learn this without being taught it as a skill. It is simply how a wall sounds when it is about to darken.

There is no separate word, among them, for resident and for worker. If you hold a station through a cycle, you live here. If you only pass through, you are weather. The stations are few and old.

The trimmers walk the engine halos with a blade no longer than a thumb and take the technic frost back to the thickness the belts will bear. Too much and the housing sweats into the works; too little and the beetle-stack collapses, and the beetles, it turns out, keep a mite off the belts that nothing else has been willing to eat. A good trimmer is known by what she leaves. The current one is a woman in her middle years who answers to Parel, who sleeps in the eastern gallery because it is nearest the warm housings and because she dislikes the civic stair, which clanks. She talks to the engines the way some people talk to weather, not expecting an answer, keeping a record in her mouth so she will notice if tomorrow's mouth disagrees. She has a scar across the left palm from a belt that took a careless year, and she shows it to apprentices once, early, and never again.

The basin-keepers are two, siblings, so alike in the low light that visitors address the wrong one for a month and are not corrected. They read the fish the way a farmer reads a field that has no crop: by vacancy. A gap that closes means the seep changed its mind upstream. A gap that widens means something died, and death in the basins is rare enough to be an inquiry. They eat what the basins can spare on a schedule older than either of them, a thin fish, boiled, no ornament. Waste goes back to the silt. Nothing unmarked lasts, and that includes a life that tried to keep a private pantry.

There is a lamp-tender, old, who is allowed to be old in a way the chamber otherwise discourages. His name has worn down to Hess. He does not trim and he does not meter. He keeps the process-lamp at the height and the hunger the cakes require, and he keeps the forced tray of leaves beneath it, which is an instrument, not a garden, though he speaks to the leaves with more courtesy than he gives to people. He remembers three repairs that were replacements, and he will tell you, if you stand still, which of them the chamber accepted and which it is still insulted by. The insulted one is a ladder on the south tanks. Everyone climbs the older ladder beside it. Hess considers this a victory and does not gloat. Gloating is loud.

Children happen rarely, and on purpose, in the dry interval that is never dry. A birth in a full-header week is considered a kind of bad timing rather than a sin: the mist is heavier, the halos are crowded with stacked wings, and a new pair of lungs spends its first month learning the wrong air. So the town keeps a rough calendar in its bodies, and couples who want a child wait the way they wait on a cake, which is to say they do not poke it. The children that do arrive are raised by whoever is nearest the gallery where the birth happened, plus Hess if he feels pedagogical, plus the bats in the sense that everyone is raised by the bats: you learn early not to lamp what is sleeping above you. There is no school. There is the round. A child who can tell first glaze from second weep, and who will not step into the center shallow, is educated.

The center shallow has no keeper. That is the point of it. People stand at its edge when they need to see the whole settlement at once, which is usually when they are about to make a mistake. Standing there long enough unmakes the mistake or confirms it. The animals cross the shallow rarely. When a fish does, the siblings note the day and do not fish that basin for a cycle. Superstition and maintenance have the same shape here, and nobody wastes breath separating them.

Visitors come down the shaft with surface hours still ticking in them. The chamber is polite for one cycle and then it stops translating. If they are the sort who can give their clock to the seep, they are offered a cot in the west gallery and a small job that cannot break anything: counting drops, for instance, which is useless and instructive. If they cannot, they leave before the second draw-down, eyesore and irritable, convinced the town is unfriendly. It is not unfriendly. It is unwilling to heat the room, raise its voice, or hurry a tank so that a guest can feel hosted. The few who stay past a season stop being guests without a ceremony. One of them, years back, was a surface clerk sent to audit the metered trickle. She is still here. She audits nothing. She walks the brick causeways at the start of each darkening and turns the wick of the civic bell-lamp, the only light on the stair, so that anyone coming down will see the wrapped tongue and understand, before they understand anything else, that this is not a place that summons.

No one rules. The metering house has a lock, and the lock has a key, and the key hangs on a nail where the eels' south main passes under the wall, because that is where a hand naturally stops. Whoever needs the meters takes the key and brings it back. Hess is eldest and is not in charge. Parel knows the engines and does not give orders outside the halos. The siblings speak for the basins and are silent on the lamp. Decisions that touch the whole chamber are made by waiting until the walls agree, which sounds like mysticism and is only the recognition that a choice taken against the seep has to be remade, and remaking is more expensive than patience. When they do vote, it is with lamps: a hood opened, a hood left shut, read from the edge of the shallow, where the whole town is visible and no one's voice has to carry.

They die as they live, without gathering a crowd the room cannot hold. A body is given to the silt at the far end of the unused gallery, above the waterline, where the lime will case it the way it cases the dead roots. The orchard, they call those roots, without smiling; they do not call the dead an orchard. The name would be too neat, and neatness is a surface vice. What they say, if they say anything, is that the person has gone into the repair. It is the highest compliment they have. Iron blooms, brick salts, a life becomes part of the seal. The eels do not attend. The bats do not attend. The drops continue, each one a decision the ceiling has already made.

What lives here, if you stay long enough to stop sorting the kinds, is a single household with many bodies and one clock. The Clerk in the south main, Hess under the process-lamp, the beetle drinking frost, the child learning not to step where the gradients go to die, the wrapped bell, the fish holding their gaps: none of them would recognize themselves as a family, and all of them would notice, within a cycle, if one station went empty. That noticing is the whole of their civics. They do not love the chamber in any way a drier town would trust. They fit it. The fit is older than any of their names, and it is what they are for, and they know it without assembling to say so.
