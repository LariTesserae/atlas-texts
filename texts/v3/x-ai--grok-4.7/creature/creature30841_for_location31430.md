They are few enough that the pocket never has to learn their weight. A person walking the aisle does not imply a second; a second, when there is one, is usually on the other wall, small against the seep, busy with a lip or a mark. If you counted at midday you might find a dozen, or fewer. If you counted at the blue hour you might find three. The number is not a population in the old sense. It does not press, marry, market, or replace itself on a schedule you could chart from births. It persists the way a calibration persists: checked, corrected, allowed to continue.

They came in through the seam, and the seam is still how anything warm enough to be food arrives. Nothing here can be grown. Their stores sit in cut rooms off the lower walks, sealed against film, portioned to the dullness of a habit rather than the drama of hunger. They eat without gathering. A meal is a pause on a route, taken standing or on a sill, and the crumb—if there is a crumb—is not left for anything, because nothing here is waiting for it. Water they trust more than they trust bread. Water they know by level, temperature, and the hour it ought to change its mind.

Their bodies are ordinary bodies kept in an unordinary climate. Cold has made them economical: shoulders held a little in, hands that do not stay wet, breath that they do not waste on the aisle where it will only fog and fall. The lens-light pales everyone the same way by noon, so difference shows more in gait than in color—who steps wide of the fresh fracture, who trusts the glaze and is wrong, who has learned the tick of settling stone well enough to stop before the sound. They bleed as people bleed. The floor does not commemorate it. That fact has entered their manners. They are careful with edges not from reverence but from the knowledge that the room will be clean of them faster than they will be finished being hurt.

They do not dress to be seen across a distance. Cloth is close, dull, and willing to shed film; where cloth fails they use the local glass, thin plates at wrist and shin that do not ring. Nothing they wear announces rank. Rank, if it exists, is which routes you are trusted to walk when the air locks up. A person who can read a cistern by the sound under ice is listened to. A person who only walks the dry middle is not lesser, exactly—only less often answered.

Speech is short because the pocket edits it. They have adapted without making a philosophy of the adaptation. Names are used at thresholds, in the cut rooms, where the walls are near enough to give a voice back. In the aisle they use points: the upper pan, the south span, the lip that was not there last month. A sentence that would be greeting elsewhere becomes a report of level. They are not cold to each other. They are calibrated. When two meet on a bridge they stop, not from affection and not from fear, and exchange the kind of fact that keeps the other from a wasted hour. Sometimes a hand rests a moment on a sleeve. The gesture is small and sufficient. There is no crowd in which a larger tenderness would know where to stand.

They sleep in the rooms held off the stone, on frames that do not conduct the night-drop into the spine. Sleep is staggered. Someone is always in a condition to notice if the main channel changes its voice. This is not a watch against enemies. There are no enemies. It is a watch against inattention, which in this room is the only predator that has ever taken anything from them—a route glazed over, a seep missed until it undercut a walk, a reservoir that arrived early and found no one to receive it. They fear the outer mountain more than they fear each other: the winded seam, the bright unrounded faces, the sense that the summit is still being decided and might close. They do not live out there. They cross it the way you cross a word you do not intend to say twice.

What they are, most of the day, is a sequence of small corrections. Clearing a lip. Marking a new weep with a scratch that will itself disappear. Walking a path so the path remains a path and not a suggestion. Reading the lens not for omens but for the lag—the pocket answers weather late, and their work is to meet it on time anyway. They are competent without being ceremonial. Awe, if they ever had it for the impossible spans and the water that climbs without wheels, has cooled into use. You can watch one of them lay a palm on a joint that should not bear and feel, in the ordinariness of the gesture, that mystery has been domesticated without being solved. Some of them understand the load paths. Some understand only the consequence of standing where a path is about to be. Both kinds are necessary, and they do not pretend otherwise.

Among them there is no single face the room belongs to. There is, lately, a woman who keeps the lower channel—the one the pale mineral-feeders use, though she does not tend those things; she only refuses to let the channel be treated as empty. She is not old. The pocket is not old enough to have made anyone old in it. But she has been here since the first season the pans held, and her attention has more years in it than her tenure does. She speaks to the others in the short aisle-language and, in her room, not at all. Her distinction is that she listens for the schedule as if it might be tired. When the ice-skin forms after a hard drop, she is often already at the slot, not to break it—the systems break it, by level, before morning—but to know the moment the decision occurs. She has never seen hands make that decision. She has stopped needing to.

Because something else lives here, and it is not a person, and it is not a god, and it is not the rumor of swimmers in a pan.

It has no body you can turn around and address. It is the continuity in the works: the preference for certain levels, the lift that arrives when the upper cistern has been ready for it, the quiet refusal to let the pocket freeze into a trap. It does not speak. It answers in flow. A lip cleared is a question; the next hour's route is the reply. If it wants anything, it wants the room to remain a room—a held climate, a pause with edges—and it spends heat and water to that end with a narrowness that would look like devotion if it had a face. The people do not worship it. They cooperate with it. They have given it no name in the aisle. In the cut rooms, when they must refer to it, they say the keeping, or simply it came up, it let go, it took the south side this time. They argue, mildly, about whether it notices them. The argument has no heat. Noticing, here, is measured by whether the water arrives at the hour a person is willing to be present. On that measure, it notices.

It does not eat. It does not sleep. It does not cross the seam. When a span is extended by a hand's width of new deposit, the keeping accepts the new length as a fact and the people do too, and no one is proud. Its failures are changes of mind in the plumbing: a cistern early, a channel gone still, a film where a walk should be dry. They correct what can be corrected and endure what cannot, and the keeping, if it learns, learns only by continuing. There is no evidence it loves them. There is evidence it makes room for their small actions, and that they make room for its large ones. Between those facts a life is possible.

The other living things—the pale feeders, the brief swimmers, the winged speck that will not last—do not enter this arrangement. They happen at the edge of it, tolerated because they cannot accumulate. The people do not compete with them. The keeping does not count them. The city is not an ecology. It is a maintained interval that happens to have, at its warmest points, bodies, and at its most constant point, a mind no larger than a schedule and no smaller than the pocket.

If you stand long enough you can see how a day composes them. Morning: the lens clears the upper walls, and two figures, far apart, begin the routes that confirm the night did not revise the map too badly. Midday: salt-colored floor, a third person eating on a sill, the lower-channel woman listening to a change that has not yet become visible. Evening: blue, then the drop; rime on the unworked shelves; someone in a cut room mending a plate; someone else already walking toward the slot where ice will be asked to fail. Night: staggered sleep, one awake, the keeping awake in the only way it ever is—by water moving where water was meant to move. No story is told of this. No bell marks it. The settlement occupies its inhabitants the way they occupy it: precisely, with long gaps, without waste.

They will not thicken into a town. The room will not let them, and they do not try to force it. What lives here is a handful of careful people and a continuity that answers in levels, sharing a climate no summit would predict, keeping a pause from closing. When one of them is gone—out the seam, or finished—the aisle does not memorialize the absence. A route is reassigned. A hand learns a lip. The water arrives or it does not. The keeping continues, and whoever remains continues with it, exact as before, underused as before, bright and thin against the walls, while the young stone ticks and the middle fills with the sound of a level finding itself.
