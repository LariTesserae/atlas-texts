They are not many, and they do not look like a people so much as a shift that never ended.

You notice their hands before their faces. The skin of the fingers is puckered at the pads and along the sides, the way skin goes after too long in a sink, except the puckering has settled into a permanent grain: fine ridges, pale at the creases, darkened in the nail-beds by plant stain that soap does not lift. The nails are kept short, not for neatness but because long nails split in the wet and catch on fiber. When they work the reeds they use the side of the thumb and the heel of the palm more than the fingertips. A cut here does not scab clean. It stays glossy, then closes under a thin white film that peels in a day and leaves the new skin already soft again. They have stopped expecting otherwise.

Faces, when you get them in the better light under a wiped window, are not unhealthy and not well in any outdoor sense. The color is even. Sun never striped them. Cheeks hold a low flush that could be the boiler-room heat or the constant slight effort of breathing air that is already half water. Eyes are clear and a little narrowed, not from squinting at glare—there is no glare—but from the habit of looking through drip and steam at small differences: a valve packing that weeps a new thread, a frond browning at the tip, a mat that has begun to raft where it should lie flat. They blink less often than people who live in dry air. The lids stay wet on their own.

They dress in layers that can be wrung. Cotton goes gray and then stays gray. Wool is a mistake they made once, a generation back, and do not repeat; it holds the damp and smells of the tanks by the second day. What they wear is close-fitting at the wrists and ankles, loose through the body, and almost always damp at the hems. Sleeves are rolled or cut so they end above the wristbone, because a wet cuff against the skin raises a rash that takes a week to argue down. Boots are oiled every evening whether they look dry or not. They never look dry. The oil is a rendered thing from the pressing—seed, not animal—and it beads on the leather for an hour before the leather drinks it. Inside the boots the socks are changed at midday. This is not vanity. Leave a sock on through a full day and the skin of the foot goes white and starts to open between the toes. Everyone knows the smell of that before they know the sight of it. They do not discuss it. They change the socks.

They live in twos and threes and sometimes alone, in the rooms the frames still enclose. A household is whoever shares a stove-ring and a dry loft-corner for the clothes that must not go back on wet. Kinship is present and not especially organizing. A mother may sleep one catwalk over from her grown children because that bay has the better fan, and they will still eat from the same pot at the midday stop, standing, bowls in hand, because there is no table that stays clear of drip for long. Children are few. A birth is an event the whole basin registers, not from ceremony but from the practical fact that someone must be spared the night watch on the pumps for a stretch of weeks, and the watch is not optional. The child, when it comes, learns the two-beat of the reciprocating engines before it learns any lullaby, and is carried on a back or a hip through lanes where a stumble means a hand in black water. They walk early and talk late. The machinery already fills the frequencies a small voice would use. By the time they are speaking in sentences they have also learned to wait for the gap between the steam-coughs.

What they eat is what the tanks will give without being emptied. Young reed shoots, bitter until boiled twice, the first water thrown back into the channel because it still holds something the plants want. A meal of fern fiddleheads in the short season when the new growth is thick enough to crop without setting the lofts back. Pressed cakes of seed from the broad-leafed vine that climbs the south trusses—the only plant they do not also use as structure, because its stems stay green and will not take a person’s weight. Algae, skimmed, dried on racks over the boiler flue until it flakes, then crumbled into the pot to give the shoots a dark, iron taste. They do not keep salt in quantity. The mineral weep off the quarry wall, caught in a shallow pan and evaporated on the flue-shelf, yields a gray crust they knock into a jar. It is enough. Meat is a story from the rim, traded for when someone climbs the stair with a sack of cakes and comes back with something wrapped that does not drip. They do not depend on it. A week without it changes nothing in the work.

Their days are the machines’ days. Before the boilers are lit, two of them walk the intakes with poles, lifting mats, clearing the grates, speaking only to name a blockage. When the pumps take their load the knock settles, and the settlement loosens by a degree you would miss if you had not felt the tighter hour before it. Then the cropping. Reeds are cut above the waterline, bundled, carried up the open stair to the drying lofts where the fans move air that is still wet but moving, which is the only kind of dry they have. Pressing happens in lots, under screws turned by hand at the start and by a belt at the end, the green bulk collapsing into slabs that will be fuel, feed, fiber, depending on which plant and which cut. Nothing is waste in the sense of being thrown out of the bowl. What will not burn or eat or weave goes back to the tanks as slurry. The circuit is the ethic. They do not have another word for it.

They speak shortly, and not because they are stern. A sentence started in a lane arrives broken at the other end, the middle eaten by a condensate hammer or the hiss when a header vents. So they have made a language of small completed pieces: a name, a direction, a number of bundles, a hand raised palm-out to mean the valve is holding. Longer talk happens in the sheds, under the fans, where the noise is broad and steady and a voice can sit on top of it. There they are not taciturn. They complain about a coupling, repeat a thing someone said on the rim, laugh in the way people laugh when they are not performing it—short, swallowed, real. They do not tell stories about the outside as if it were better. The rim is a place you go when the jar of mineral crust is low or a belt has cracked past splicing. It is bright in a way that hurts the eyes for the first hour. It is cold in the wind. They finish the errand and come back down the softened steps into air that fits the lungs they have.

What they believe is practical and not quite empty of reverence. They do not worship the pumps, but they do not speak casually in the pump house. A hand touches the housing when they pass, not for luck, exactly—more the way you touch a doorframe in a house you have lived in so long the frame knows you. They keep the old waterline on the quarry wall clear of growth for a body’s width, a vertical stripe of bare stained stone, and no one now can say who first decided it should stay bare. They maintain it. Once a season someone goes up on a ladder with a brush and takes the moss off that stripe and no other. If you ask why, they will say so you can see if the level is rising. The level is not rising. They brush it anyway. The bare stone is the one place in the bowl that shows its age instead of covering it, and they are not inclined to explain what that is worth.

They are not lonely in the way the absence of animals might suggest to someone from a louder ecology. The plants answer. A vine pulled back from a coupling in the morning has leaned toward the walk again by evening, and the lean is a kind of company, predictable, requiring an answer in return—a tie, a cut, a decision to leave it. The water answers. The machines answer, and when they answer wrong the people hear it in their sleep and are on the catwalk before they have finished waking, barefoot, the boards known underfoot, the fault already half-named. That responsiveness is the life of the place. They fit it. A stranger standing at the rope beyond the last frame, looking at the dull plate of open tank, feels the settlement as heat and noise behind them. The people who belong there, turning back from that rope at the end of a day, feel the settlement as the thing that continues when they stop looking: drip finding the grooves it has polished, the two-beat holding, the fronds easing into the shapes the light will allow. They wipe their hands on their thighs. They go in under the fans. They are not waiting for the bowl to become something else.
