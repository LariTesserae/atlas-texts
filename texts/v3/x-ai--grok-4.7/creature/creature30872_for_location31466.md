At first there is still nothing that asks to be called an animal. The hollow keeps the look it had from the slot: stone, silt, the tea-colored pan, the coins of lichen, the shrub holding seeds nobody takes. You can stand until the next drop finds the water and the skin of algae knits itself shut, and the floor does not rearrange. What changes, if you stay, is only the sense that one of the settled forms is settled in a different way from the others.

It is on the left, where the line of flat stones loses itself into the skirt of rubble. Most of those stones are stones. One of them is a little proud of the silt, and the proud edge is not a fracture. It is a rim, worn smooth the way the ledges are worn smooth, freckled with the same rust-colored lichen, dusted on its upper face so that it reads as another piece the slope has agreed to keep. The difference is underneath. Along the side that faces the pan there is a darkness that is not shadow of the wall. It is a recess, lipped, and the lip is the color of the wet stone rather than the dry. In that recess, if the paper-colored light is allowed to do its slow work, a surface moves—not far, not as a gesture. It eases. The way a breath eases when the breather has no reason to hurry it and no audience to spend it on.

The shell is low and oval, broader than a spread hand, the scutes so nearly flush that they look like grain in sandstone until you are close enough to see they meet in seams. Lichen has taken the dome the way it takes the set stones: not a decoration, a tenancy. Silt has drifted against the rear margin and stopped, as it stops against anything that has not shifted in a season. The front is clearer. There the dust lies thinner, and the horn of the edge shows a warmer ochre, the color the walls give back when the haze-light warms them. Nothing about it is bright. It would not show from the entrance. It shows only as a refusal: this lump does not quite obey the angle of the talus. It has chosen a level the rubble did not offer, and then it has stayed on it long enough for the rubble to make its peace.

Under the front lip, the head is already out. It was out before you came down. It rests on the clay a finger’s width above the darker ground, chin near enough to the damp that the skin there looks oiled. The skin is the gray of the cooler grain in the wall, loose at the neck in fine folds that do not hang so much as stack, each fold holding a line of dust in its trough. The eye is open. It is small, dark, and lidless in the sense that the lid, if it comes, will come as a clouding rather than a blink you could catch. It is aimed at the pan, or at the space between the pan and the shell, which is the same aim an unhurried thing gives a room it already knows. There is no shine of alertness in it and no dullness either. It is the look of a surface that receives light and does not spend it on a reaction.

A foreleg lies along the clay, half out of the shell’s shadow. The scales are broad, almost tiles, the color of the grit when the grit is damp. The claws are short, blunt, pale at the tips, and they are not set as if they had just finished a step. They are parked. Behind, one hind foot is visible only as a thickness under the shell’s skirt, the toes splayed into the silt the way roots splay when they have found the depth they want and mean to keep it. The whole animal is lower than the shrub. Lower than the tuft of stiff grass on its little rise. It occupies the hollow the way the moss occupies the north-facing grain: by fitting a condition, not by filling a space.

It does not regard you. Or it does, and the regarding is of so little consequence to its next hour that the body declines to report it. Flies do not attend it; there are no flies. Ants do not trail to it. The algae skin on the pan is unbroken between the drop and the next drop, so it is not feeding now, and the slope of the neck says it is not about to. What it lives on is elsewhere in the clock of this place, and the clock is not the one that runs by hunger you can see. In the wetter months, whenever those are—when the seam wets a wider face and the pan rises to the mineral line and a little past it, and the moss thickens, and whatever small green thing risks the margin of the clay—it eats. Slowly, with the beak that is now closed and the color of old horn. It takes what is soft and near and does not travel for it. In the dry of the year, which is the condition holding now, it does not range. The seeds on the shrub are not its concern. They can open, hold, fall, and take or not take. It has already done the year’s walking, if walking is the word for a few body-lengths between the seep and the shade line, repeated until the route is a faint polish and then abandoned when the water’s reach changed.

You can tell the route if you look for absence rather than sign. From the shell to the lip of the pan the silt is not smoother, exactly. It is less powdered. The grains have been pressed and have not yet been replaced by the drift off the walls. A second, fainter pressing runs toward the back wall and stops where the dark stain keeps the stone damp at knee height of a crouching person, ankle height of nothing that lives here. It does not climb. It has no need of the ledges. The warmth it wants is the warmth already in the floor in the afternoon, the stored heat the description of the hollow already named, and at night the shell takes that heat and spends it so gradually that by morning the dome is only a little warmer than the stone beside it. In the coldest hours it draws in. The head goes to the recess. The foreleg follows. What remains above the silt is the dome with its lichen, and for those hours it is honestly a stone, and the hollow is honestly empty, and both states are true.

It is not young. The growth rings are not readable, but the margin of the shell has the thickened look of horn that has been added and added and then only maintained, and one scute at the shoulder carries a healed pit, a small crater glazed over, older than the lichen on top of it. Something struck it once, or it struck something, in a year when the plain outside still sent weather hard enough to matter down here. The pit does not leak. The animal does not favor the side. Whatever the blow was, it has been finished with as thoroughly as the hollow has finished with whoever set the line of stones. A pale seam on the neck, half-hidden in a fold, might be another old closing. Nothing is open. Nothing smells of injury. The odor, when you are low enough to have one, is only damp clay and the faint dry smell of heated dust, the smell of the place itself.

Its life, as much as can be known without becoming a bother to it, is a series of allowances. It allows the light to move from the left wall to the back wall and does not follow it. It allows the drop to fall. Sometimes, not every time, a little after the drop, the throat works—a slow pulse under the folds, once, as if the sound or the tremor in the clay had to be swallowed to be done with. It allows the shrub to stand. It allows you. If you shift your weight, the eye changes by a degree, not more, and the degree is not fear so much as the correction a compass makes when the hand holding it is unsteady. Stay still again and the correction lapses. It has learned the difference between weather and arrival, and arrival that does not come down the silt toward the pan is filed with weather.

There is no second shell. No smaller dome in the skirt of rubble, no eggs turned in the warm grit, no scrape where a nest might have been and been covered. If it has had others, they are not in this hollow, or they were and the hollow kept only this one, the way it kept only one shrub, only one pan, only one seam that still wets. Solitude here is not a sentence. It is the same spacing as the tufts of grass: what the moisture supports, at these distances, and no more. It does not call. It has no call to make that would improve the arrangement. The quiet described from the entrance already included it. A body that moves a few times in a day, and then only by inches, does not register as an exception to silence. Even the drawing-in at dusk is quieter than the drop.

Toward evening the haze does not break, but the slot of sky cools in color, and the walls give back more than they take. The animal’s head lifts by a fraction. Not to see the sky—the shell’s overhang and the angle of the neck keep the world at clay-level—but because the air on the throat has changed, and the change is information. It may drink then. Not always. When it does, the motion is the first one that looks like an act rather than a settling. The neck extends. The beak, hooked just enough to be a beak and not a snout, meets the pan where the algae skin is thinnest against the clay lip. It does not lap in any way a surface-dweller would recognize as thirst. It takes the water in a steady inward pull, three times, four, the throat pulsing more clearly now, and the skin of algae tears in a line no wider than the beak and begins, almost at once, to lie back down. Then the neck shortens. The chin finds the clay. A darkening spreads on the silt under the mouth, no larger than a thumbprint, and stops. That is the drink. It will do for longer than seems reasonable, because the body is not spending itself on distance or display or heat-shedding. The hollow has already solved heat. The animal has agreed to the solution.

Night comes to the floor before it comes to the rim. The shell’s outline goes first, joining the stones it has been imitating all day. If there is a moon above the haze, it does not reach the pan as a shape, only as a slight lifting of the dark, enough to tell water from clay and clay from the dome. In that lifting the eye, if it is still open, is only a wetter dark. Often by then it is closed, the head drawn to the threshold of the shell but not fully in, as if full withdrawal were a measure kept for worse nights than this one. The claws do not tuck with any finality. They rest where the day’s last small shift left them. Somewhere in the hours after, the drop continues on its interval. The animal does not mark it. Marking it was never its work. Its work was to be the kind of life this room can hold without the room having to become an event: warm-blooded only by the stone’s courtesy, fed by the seep’s occasional generosity, defended by looking like what the hollow is already full of.

By morning it will be as you might have found it, or a hand’s span nearer the stain, or turned a quarter so the lichen on the dome takes the light on a different coin. The pan will be at its line. The shrub will not have been visited. The pressed path in the silt will have gained nothing a stranger would call a track. If you have gone, the hollow will not record you, and the animal will not look for where you were. It has the seam, the pan, the shade that does not move, the heat in the grit, and a shell the lichen has accepted. That is the whole of its country, and it is already home in all of it, and it has been for long enough that its presence and the place’s emptiness are not two facts. They are the way the hollow looks when it is complete.
