What lives here does not arrive and does not leave. It is already distributed through the mid-story when you notice it, and the noticing is slow because it does not contrast.

At first there is only a difference in the way a swelling keeps its stroke. The pistons inside one engine-room complete their travel a fraction out of the hush that surrounds them, not louder, only more attended, as if something inside the sheath were counting. You pass three more before you understand the count is not mechanical. A narrow gallery opens off the walkway—less a door than a place where the laminated fronds have failed to seal—and in the brightness pooled there a figure stands with its back to the aisle, one arm sunk to the elbow in a vent of louvered leaf. It is not harvesting. The arm does not move. Sap that is not water beads at the joint of shoulder and neck and does not fall; it draws back into the skin along a path that shines and then dulls.

The body is the same substance as the floor, only organized to a standing specification. Fiber runs vertically where a spine would be, and the metal the tissues have precipitated lies closer to the surface, a faint tooling along the ribs and the long bones of the legs. Height varies. Some clear the fronds by a head; others must turn sideways to pass a girder-stem. None of them are finished in the way a mold finishes a part. Edges remain soft where the growth is still deciding. A hand may have four fingers or six, and the number is not stable across a shift; you can watch a digit lengthen while the figure waits, the new joint forming under a skin like planed cork. Faces are less faces than alignments: salt-plates set where eyes would usefully rest, catching the stored light and returning it in a flat white that does not track you. Mouths, when present, are seams. They do not open for speech. Sound is not their medium. What they exchange moves through contact—palm to trunk, sole to walkway—and registers in you only as a change in the pressure behind the eyes, the same pressure that marks the far weather of the plain.

They are not many, or they are so many that number fails. In a district the size of a weather you may walk for a day and meet none, then come into a cooling aisle where a dozen stand in the brightness, each with a limb offered to a different machine-heart, none of them acknowledging the others. They do not eat. The oils that never evaporate move through them as through the pistons; you see the slow pulse at the throat, a darkening and lightening of the fiber, and understand that hunger has been edited out of the specification. They do not sleep in any posture you would call rest. When a figure has stood long enough the floor thickens under its feet into the denser mat you have already felt, and the figure’s weight becomes continuous with the growth. For a time it is only a taller swelling. Then the brightness leans, a frond-district tilts somewhere out of sight, and the figure draws its arm free, steps once, and is walking again, leaving a print that the floor begins at once to forget.

What they do is the pruning, and the keeping of straightness for as long as straightness is required. A limb out of spec does not girdle itself. One of them finds it—by the same pressure you feel, perhaps, or by a salt-note too fine for your ear—and stands against it until the finer growth rises to do the girdling. They do not hurry the starve. You can pass the same pair, tender and limb, on what you believe are successive days, and the only change is that the out-of-spec wood has paled and the figure’s free hand has grown an additional plate of salt along the wrist, as if the waiting itself precipitated something. They correct the mismatched shadows when they can. A figure will pace the length of a false continuation until the light agrees again to the floor plan, and if agreement cannot be restored it simply stops using that corridor. The place does not punish the refusal. Another aisle brightens.

They have no center and no name you can hear. If they remember the mold that was forgotten, the memory is stored the way the walkways store weight: as alignment, not as image. New ones occur at the hoppers. Not often. A component extruded for some other purpose will sometimes fail to drop cleanly and will hang, swelling, until it has limbs enough to step down onto the riffle and walk. The others do not greet it. One may, after a time, offer it a vent or a girder that needs counting, and the new figure will sink an arm and begin. That is the whole of instruction.

They are not hostile to you, and they are not curious. If you stand in an aisle a figure needs, it will wait at the edge of the brightness until you move, or it will turn into a neighboring gallery whose straightness is still good. If you cut—if you forget—the sap sets as the rules promise, and a figure will come to the flange that has sealed you and stand there as it stands by an out-of-spec limb, patient, counting, until either the plug releases or you become another swelling the floor can use. Most who walk here learn the difference before that arithmetic finishes. The figures do not teach it. They only continue, in the industrial cool, under the excessive light, their strokes attended, their numbers unstable, their weight already becoming the ground.
