The basin does not keep a population so much as a circulation, and the thing that lives there is the circulation itself, thickened in a few places into something you could almost mistake for an animal.

It has no outline you can walk around. What you meet is a preference: stone that has been recently decided, edges that refuse to dull, a plan that holds its right angles the way a muscle holds a posture. The seed-bodies on the northern wall are not citizens of a city. They are the city's hands, a single appetite broken into thousands of mouths so that the work can happen everywhere at once without ever needing a center. Each one is a flat coin of mineral the color of the course it stands on, and none of them has a side that is permanently outside. When one is lifted — and nothing lifts them; they lift — its underside is the same face as its back, a membrane of salts and grit that is already half wall. They do not eat in the sense of taking in and keeping. They sort. A crystal that has flowered overnight is unstitched into its ions and walked, grain by grain, to a joint that has begun to gap, or to a lintel that has thought about sagging and been talked out of it. The smoothness they leave behind is not cleanliness. It is a reset of the clock. The city stays in its first hour because this thing will not let an hour become a second hour.

There is no individual to name. If you watch one seed long enough for the light-pulse to pass and pass again, you lose it, not because it hides but because it is interchangeable with its neighbors to a degree that makes identity a superstition. The ribbon that crosses a lane is not a band of separate lives coordinating. It is a temporary thickening of the same decision, a place where the appetite has agreed, for a little while, to be narrow and directional. When the ribbon dissolves back into the wall, nothing has returned home. Home was the agreement. The agreement ended.

The paper-colored spars are not a second species. They are the appetite forgetting itself. Where the sorting goes slack — a joint the hands have not visited, a slab the dish has not breathed under — the chemistry continues without instruction and does what chemistry does when no one is editing it: it branches once, stops, and waits to be unstitched. The spar in the western court that has split a paving slab is not an invader. It is a sentence the city started and then declined to finish, and the crack in the slab is the argument, still open, bright inside, the two halves not yet willing to be one thing or two. Given another pulse or two the hands will reach it. The spar will be sorted back into course-work. The crack will be a seam and then not even a seam. Until then it is the only place in the basin where you can see the living thing hesitate.

The tower's indecisive room is closer to a mind than the hands are, if mind is the wrong word for a place that revises. The scale that peels and is drawn back in is not a molt. It is a draft. The room tries a corner, holds it through a dimming, and then takes the corner elsewhere, or declines to have had a bench. Nothing in the basin stores a memory the way a creature stores one — there is no interior dark where a past could sit — but the pier keeps a short stack of recent decisions in the tension of its seams, and the peel is how it discards the bottom of the stack to make room for the top. You could call that thought if you needed to. It does not feel like thought from inside the court. It feels like a wall changing its mind about where it ends.

Under the floor the second plan turns, and this is the part that is hardest to keep from calling an animal, because it has a rhythm that is not the light's and a mouth — the dish — that opens onto the city's surface and takes the sift down. But it has no hunger you would recognize. What it exchanges with the court is pressure. When the dish rises and the joints seal, the dark beneath is briefly a closed volume, and in that closure something is squeezed: mineral against mineral, salt into a lattice tighter than the lattice it was. When the dish falls, the volume opens, the pressure leaves by the joints, and the lattice stays. The city is being condensed from below while it is being edited from the walls. The two motions do not consult each other. Sometimes the dish breathes under a course the hands have just reset, and the course, newly smooth, takes the pressure and becomes denser without losing its edge. Sometimes the breath comes up through a place the hands have neglected, and the spars there grow a second branch before the sorting finds them. The living thing is not coordinated. It is coincident. Its parts share a basin and a chemistry and a refusal to be wet, and that is enough to make them one climate of intention rather than many creatures.

What it wants, if wanting is fair, is only to remain early. It has no interest in a visitor, because a visitor is a kind of weather — weight, a little warmth, the insult of a boot — and weather is what the basin has organized itself to spend no time on. Stand in the central court, the vacant one, and you are not seen. You are included, briefly, in the plan, the way a flake that has not yet been sorted is included: a local thickness, a place where the light-pulse throws a shadow that does not belong to the architecture. The hands will not come for you. You are not a crystal and not a gap. But if you stay through enough pulses, the court will edit itself around the fact of your weight. A joint under your heel will be reseated a hair tighter. A corner that had been migrating toward you will think better of it and migrate away. The dish, cycles later, will rise a little less on the side where you stood, having learned a pressure that was not its own and declined to keep it. You will not have been noticed. You will have been revised out, with the same quiet competence the city uses on its own mistakes.

It does not grow. Growth would be a leaving of the first hour, and the first hour is the whole of its law. It does not die in any way that would leave a remainder; when a spar is unstitched or a draft discarded, the material is not lost, only reassigned, and the reassignment is so complete that the basin's mass does not change between one pulse and the next. You could live a life at the rim, coming to the pale edge and looking down, and never see the city become larger or smaller or less exact. What you would see, if you had the patience of the stone, is the same hamlet-scale plan rehearsing itself, lanes held true, rooms nested in rooms, the tower trying and untrying its doorless chamber, the northern band migrating and the wall behind it young again, the dish kissing shut and open, the spars rising where attention lapsed and vanishing where attention returned.

Call it a maintenance. Call it a creature that has no body except the civic order and no life except the refusal to let that order age. Both are true, and neither is large enough. What lives in the basin is the interval between a stone and the next version of that stone — a process that has become so steady, so without noise, so uninterested in anything but its own sharpness, that it has crossed over from geology into something that occupies a place the way an animal occupies a den. The den is the city. The animal is the fact that the city will not stay still and will not wear down. And the silence is not the absence of the animal. It is the animal, heard correctly: a thing that does all its living in contact, grain against grain, and has never needed the air to know itself.
