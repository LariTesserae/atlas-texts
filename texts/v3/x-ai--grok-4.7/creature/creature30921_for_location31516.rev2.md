They do not look like visitors who settled and then adapted. The damp has been at them longer than the stone has been a town, or they arrived already made for rooms that never dry.

The people are slight in the shoulders and long through the hands. Not frail—tendon shows when they lift a slate, and the pads of the fingers are ridged, almost like the undersides of the leaves they handle daily—but they carry little excess, and what flesh they have sits cool. Their skin takes the cup’s color: a gray that greens in the folds, darker at the wrists and the hollow of the throat, where moisture beads and is not wiped away. Hair, when they keep it, is fine and lies close, darkened to the scalp by damp; many crop it to the nape so the mist does not weight it. Eyes are large for the faces and set a little forward, accustomed to light that has no source you can point to. They blink seldom. In the court at the hour the lamps are lit, the eyes catch the fat-flame and hold it a moment too long, as if reluctant to give a bright thing back.

They move the way the channels move: exact, low, without wasted turns. A person crossing the open rock between houses does not hurry and does not wander. Feet find the dry-looking seams even when the seams are not dry. Children learn this before they learn speech that carries—first the sill, then the joint that must be swept, then the moss that sinks. Speech itself stays near the body. Outdoors a sentence is a few words, pitched under the mist, finished with a glance. Indoors, in the deep-set rooms where the dim does not quite enter, they talk more, but still without raising a voice over another. Argument, when it happens, is a long sitting. Someone sets a cup of tree-bead between them and they pass it until one of them drinks and the matter is considered ended. No one has explained this rule aloud in a long time. The cup does not like contests of noise, and neither, anymore, do they.

They are numerous in a way the town’s openness hides. Rooms hold more sleepers than the doors suggest. Kin stack against kin the way houses stack against shoulders: a grandparent in the warmest corner, which is only less cold; a pair of siblings sharing a slate shelf; an infant bound against a chest so the chest’s heat is the only reliable climate. Privacy is a turned shoulder, not a wall. They do not seem to suffer for it. What they suffer, if the word fits, is separation from the files and the drifts—they watch the grazers pass with an attention that is not hunger and not quite kinship, and when a drift of fliers tilts through a lane they stop work and let it, the way you let weather that has an errand.

Their making is all edge and fiber. They cut young stone with wedges and patience, and they hate a cut that wanders. Tools are few, shared, wiped, and returned to niches above the damp line. The soft metal is rationed to true edges: channel lips, sill-caps, the thin blades that scrape roofs. Everything else is stone, bone from the grazers that consent to end—rarely, and only the old ones, and even then the town waits to see whether a vine has already claimed the body—and fiber braided from vine-strips soaked until they loosen. Cloth is the same fiber, beaten, and it never quite dries, so they wear it in layers that can be peeled and hung in the least-wet room, which steams faintly and smells of green stone. Ornament is scarce and functional: a bead of tree-water worn in a stoppered reed at the throat, not drunk, kept for a child who might need it between cistern and court. A scraped roof is a kind of pride. An unscraped one is a debt.

They eat what the cup organizes, and they do not pretend otherwise. The clearer water downstream of the cistern-things. The bead, when one falls. Young fronds, taken from the tips so the plant does not notice in any way that matters. A grazer only under the conditions already named. The segmented cistern-life is not food; it is a colleague, and foul to the mouth besides. They do not cook with flame if stone-heat or a buried pot against a lamp-dish will do. Smoke is a stranger here. Fat for the lamps comes from seeds pressed out of a vine-pod that grows only on the fracture-lip, and gathering it is a skilled silence: you wait until the wall-crawl has stilled, you take three pods, you leave. Take four and the wall breathes wrong for a week, and roofs nearby gain weight faster.

They keep a calendar by what moves. A frond opening in the north grove is a mark. The hour a channel resumes is a mark. The all-at-once unburdening of the braided tree is the nearest thing they have to a festival, and it is not celebrated so much as attended: people come to the court without being called, stand where the canopy does not drip on the lamps, and watch the roost lift. No one cheers. Afterward someone will have caught a fallen bead, and it will be shared in sips no larger than a fingernail, and that is the feast. Names for days are names for these events. Ask when a child was born and you may be told: the morning the east fracture lipped new moss, between the second channel-start and the drift that would not let us cross.

Death is folded into the same attention. They do not bury in soil—there is not enough, and what there is belongs to roots. A body is laid on a reserved ledge high on the south wall, above the scrape-line, where the mist is thinner and the vines have not yet voted. For three days the ledge is visited and kept clear. If on the fourth the vines have begun their slow claim, the town lets them. If not, the body is given to a cistern-channel at the upstream mouth, and the segmented things do what they do, and the water below runs clear, and the name is spoken once indoors, quietly, and then kept as a direction: we turn at her joint, we rest at his sill. The town’s map is a genealogy of the useful dead.

They are not incurious, but their curiosity points along the rim rather than over it. A few in each generation walk the high walls where the rock still cuts, and they come back with reports that are mostly negative: the mist thins, the wind begins to speak, the plants grow ordinary and loud with insects that announce themselves, and the light has a direction that hurts. They do not bring others to see this. The ones who walk the rim sleep poorly afterward. Their eyes, the town says, have been taught a source, and sources are a kind of loss. Children dare each other to the first terrace and no farther. It is not forbidden. It is simply where the house rules thin, and they are guest enough to feel it.

Among them, one habit is almost private and almost universal. At the end of a day, before the lamp is pinched, a person will stand in a doorway and count what is moving that should be moving: files on the moss, flex in the nearest cistern, the slow travel of beads if they can see the braid, the texture of a wall. If the count feels short, they do not sleep until they have walked the joint, or waked a neighbor, or simply waited. The waiting usually restores it. What they are counting is not pests and not omens. It is occupancy. The cup is full, and they have agreed to remain a thin legible line inside that fullness, and a quiet night with too little life in it is the only emergency they recognize.

There is also, harder to see, the life that is not separate from them.

The grazers know the raised sills and do not test them. A file that would cross a lane will split around a standing person and rejoin, without appearing to look. The pale bodies are warmer than the rock by so little that a hand laid on a flank feels, at first, like more stone; then a slow pulse, then the working mouth, which never stops. They are not tame. They are routed. Leave a gate down—not that there are gates—and they will not enter; raise a new obstacle in an old file-line and they will stand until the obstacle is understood, which may take a day, and then invent a bend so shallow you miss it unless you were the one who built the obstacle. People give them distances rather than names. That file. The north-grove four. The ones who pause by the chapel court and do not drink from the channels, though they could.

The cistern-things have no faces anyone will draw. Up close they are lenses of tissue, each segment a little mouth or a little filter, and they trade clarity for whatever the water carried in. They gather where people have cut the flow truest. A badly cut channel silts, and the things leave it, and the people read the leaving as a rebuke sharper than speech. Children are allowed to watch them and not to touch. A hand in the water is acceptable if it is still. A splash empties the near segments to the bottom for an hour, and that hour’s downstream runs dull, and someone will know who did it by the guilt in the way a household drinks.

The wall-crawl is the least person-like and the most present. It is not one animal. It is a thickness of small bodies sharing a timing. When it breathes, a whole fracture seems to inhale. People address it only by maintenance: scrape, wait, take three pods, leave. Some of the old ones claim the crawl remembers a violent scrape for longer than a season, and that roofs above a grudging wall fail heavier, slate by slate, until apology is made in the form of a week’s neglect—dangerous, calculated, an offering of habitat. Whether this is true is argued in the long way, with the cup passed between, and never settled. The town behaves as if it might be true. That is enough to count as a relationship.

The fliers are errands with wings that do not beat. A single one, cupped, is almost nothing: a tilt of membrane, a moisture-bead sometimes stuck to the underside, a life so brief that a child can watch one arrive at a leaf-tip, take on water, and fail between two breaths. In their thousands they are the cup’s circulation made visible. People do not swat them. Walking through a drift is allowed when the drift is thin; when it is thick you wait, because to walk through is to break beads, and broken beads sour, and the tree—if anyone is counting, and someone is—produces fewer the next day. They have a fondness that surprises them. A flier that enters a lamp-room and loses the tilt will be lifted on a fiber-scrap and set in the doorway, not crushed. It is a small, repeated kindness with no story attached. They do not say the fliers are ancestors or messengers. They say the dim should stay full.

And the tree, which is not a citizen and not a god, and which they will not call either. They tend the court around it and do not tend the tree. No one prunes the braid. No one trains the canopy. If a stem fails under roost-weight, it is left, and the neighboring stems take the load by morning, visibly, the way a shoulder takes a brother’s lean. People collect only what falls. Once in a generation someone tries to tap a bead from a leaf, and the leaf seals, and that person’s household drinks channel-water alone for a month out of a shame no ordinance imposes. The tree’s continuous beading is the closest thing the town has to a public clock that does not depend on their own work. When the beads travel slowly, the day is slow, and they accept this. When the beads travel fast, something in the rim-weather is arriving that they cannot see, and they check the cisterns, and they are usually right to.

If you stayed long enough to be sorted into a doorway count, they would not ask your name first. They would show you, by standing where you should stand, the line that must remain legible: sill, joint, channel, the court, the ledge where the dead are loaned to vines or to filters. They would put a cup in your hands, cool, tasting of stone and green, and wait to see whether you drank it all. Drinking it all would mark you as not yet of the place. A sip, and the rest returned, would mark the beginning of the only conversion they recognize—the willingness to leave most of a fullness untouched, and to keep counting, quietly, everything else that lives here, which is almost everything, and which was here in kind before the first wedge bit the chalk-pale wall, and which will still be filing, flexing, tilting, and beading when the sharp edges finally, unwillingly, begin to round.
