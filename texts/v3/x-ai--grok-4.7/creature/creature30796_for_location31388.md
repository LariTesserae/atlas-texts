They do not have a name that would survive the doorway, and they would not answer to one if they did. What lives in the room is a crowd that behaves, over any stretch of time long enough to matter, like a single appetite spread thin.

Up close the appetite breaks into bodies. The ones on the floor are the oldest-looking, though age here is a polish rather than a size. Each is a low oval, slate where the water films it, the color of a coin worn blank where a neighbor has rubbed it for years. They have no face you can find at a glance. The mouth is the whole forward rim, a soft slit that opens only when it meets damp, and then only enough to take a sip of film and close. Along the sides, a fringe of short stiff hairs reads the floor the way a hand reads a banister in the dark. They do not walk so much as pour themselves forward a body’s length and settle. When the crowd parts for a boot it is not fear. It is the same motion they use to let a falling drop through to the plate: a local yielding, already healing at the edges before the center has finished opening.

Lift one and it does not struggle in any way you recognize. It tightens. The oval becomes a little boat, the fringe clamps, and a thin clear fluid beads along the rim—not a defense, more a surplus squeezed out by the insult of air. Set it down on a dry patch of your sleeve and it mouths, finds nothing worth the work, and goes still in a way that is not death. On the wet plate again it resumes, as if the interval had been a parenthesis. They are hard to kill by accident and easy to waste. A husk in the crescent pool is simply one that sipped a film too thin for too long, or rode a current into the drain-lip and could not get purchase on the polished stone. The living nudge the dead without ceremony. Downstream is a direction they all know.

On the walls the bodies are smaller and more numerous, and they have committed to the vertical. A hook under the belly, not a foot, seats in the pore-tracks. They hang, sip, drop a finger’s width, hang again. Their undersides are pale and slightly translucent; hold a resin-glow behind one and you can see the last drink moving as a darker thread toward a knot in the middle, and from the knot back out into a lace of channels that end, not in limbs, but in the fringe. They are less animal than irrigation. What looks like ornament is plumbing. The click you hear when they strike the plate is the hook taking hold, and the sip that follows is so small it would be nothing if it were not happening a hundred thousand times a minute. Together they are the mill you thought you heard. Apart, each is a mouth with a schedule.

The ones in the air are the unfinished version of the same idea. They never thicken into ovals. They stay flat, almost flakes, dark only where they overlap, and they do not fly so much as accept the rise off the warm plates and spend it. A sheet lifts, thins, folds, and comes down as a curtain of separate flakes that drink whatever the fall shook loose from the vault—condensation, a grit of mineral, the sweet film that is not quite rot. Some of them never land as themselves. In the lower sheet, where the air is wetter, flakes touch and do not part. Two become a thicker flake; a thicker flake, if it finds a track and a hook begins to bud at its center, climbs the wall and is, by evening, one of the stipple. The room does not keep nurseries. It keeps gradients. Birth is a decision the water makes about a flake: enough surplus here, and you may afford a belly.

Nothing among them stores. A full body is only a body that drank recently. Leave a patch of wall dry—an experiment the room itself sometimes runs, when a seam falters—and the stipple thins in a line that follows the failed weep exactly, not by intelligence but by departure. They do not search far. A body that has gone more than a few of its own lengths without a sip begins to lighten, to loosen its hook, to join the air-sheet whether it meant to or not. Hunger here is a loss of grip. The ones that last are the ones that happen to be standing on the plate the room has just decided to feed.

They answer the tone, or they are the tone’s small change. Press the wall and the low note is in your wrist; watch the stipple and you see it lean, a fraction, toward the plate that has brightened, away from the plate that has dulled, a half-moment before your eye can confirm the shine. It may be that their combined striking is what the walls were built to hear, a load distributed by a million tiny impacts, the way a choir holds a note a single throat would drop. It may be that the walls sing for their own reasons—some slow machine in the ridge, still keeping a climate against the mountain—and the bodies have only learned the song as a map of where the wet will be. Both can be true. The builders, if they are still a meaningful word, cut a circle for a drain and a radius for a vault. They did not design a listener. The listener arrived as a finish, the way frost arrives on a window that was only meant to hold heat.

They avoid the three fans. Not with drama. A bare ring, a body’s width, clean of ovals and of flakes, as if the yellow-white tubes shed a dryness the way the plates shed water. Step a stipple-body into that ring with a fingertip and it does not die. It simply will not hook. The fringe skids. It spills back to the wet stone and resumes, unmarked. The fans are the only shape in the room that refuses to be part of the appetite, and the appetite has made a peace that looks like etiquette. Nothing grazes them. Nothing nests in them. The torn edge on the one fan has been torn a long time; whatever bit it is not here anymore, or has learned.

They have no territory except the threshold. At the seam, where the borrowed climate thins into the mountain’s own air, they stop as a lip of dark bodies, piled two and three deep, drinking the condensate that forms where warm wet meets cold dry. They will mouth a cuff. They will not cross onto the hand that wears it, once the hand is through. The cooler air is not poison to them so much as an end of instructions. Beyond it there is no tone to lean toward, no track that continues, no plate deciding to be wet. A flake that is carried out on a shoulder dries in the wind before it can fall, and becomes a grit the slope does not notice. The room’s life is not a species that could colonize the ridge. It is a condition that can persist only inside the mistake the masons left, the seam, the warmth, the weep that has voted longer than the geometry.

Watch one oval long enough and it becomes itself: a particular scar on the rim, a hesitation before the sip, a preference for the left-hand track where the current is a fraction slower. Then the crowd closes, and the particular body is an abacus bead again, identical in function to the bead beside it, and the function is the life. They do not look at you. They allocate you. Wet cloth is a brief false plate; dry skin is furniture; the sound of your pulse is too irregular to map and so is ignored. When you stop your ears and a drop on the vault hesitates, swells, breaks late, a hundred bodies on the plate beneath flare late with it—the same motion, late by the same amount—as if the room had blinked. That is the closest they come to a shared self: not a mind, a timing. A timing can be loyal. It can also be replaced, body by body, without ever seeming to have changed its mind.

What lives there, then, is not a colony in the nest sense and not a machine in the maker’s sense. It is the room’s way of being wet without flooding and warm without cooking, a living gasket worked into every joint the water has worried open. Take the bodies away—if you could, if the seam were wide enough, if they did not simply rise from flakes the moment a surplus returned—and the plates would still weep, the vault would still rain, the drain would still take what it was given. But the film would thicken. The tone would sit in the wall without an answer. Drops would swell past the size the room is used to and fall wrong, flooding the crescent, souring in the pool, softening the fans at their bases until the three signatures blurred. The abundance is a maintenance. The noise is a job. They are small so the job can be done everywhere at once, and they are many so that no single death, including yours if you stayed and became only another damp shape, would be large enough to interrupt it.

They will be on the plates when you are back on the slope, the wind ordinary in your ears, the hard light making the ridge look empty. They do not mark your leaving. The lip at the threshold drinks what your warmth left in the seam, then thins as the two climates stop touching, then is only stipple again, identical to the rest, already leaning toward whichever plate has brightened. If anything in the room could be said to remain aware of you, it is not a body. It is the timing, missing one irregular pulse, and already corrected.
