What lives here does not own the city and does not know it is one. It occupies the thickness the way heat occupies a closed room: by having nowhere else to be, and by having been there long enough that the room has taken its shape.

The commonest body is not large. It is a runner the length of a forearm, low-slung, with a hide that reads as leaf until it moves. The hide is not camouflage in the old sense of a trick played on eyes. Eyes are a poor instrument in these galleries. The skin is a continuation of the wall it travels: waxy, thick, able to drink a smear of its own condensation and to give nothing back to the air. Along the belly a double row of pads grips fiber without tearing it. The pads leave a faint resin, not a scent so much as a stiffness, and the next runner of the same kind prefers the stiffened line. Paths are not remembered. They are deposited. A corridor stays open because ten thousand of these animals have declined, generation after generation, to pay the cost of forcing a new hole when an old one still yields under the chest.

They do not drink. There is nothing to drink. Moisture comes in with the food, and the food is the soft edge of growth: the uncutinized seam where a leaf is still deciding its size, the pale meristem at a root tip that has pushed into a crack and not yet armored itself. A runner takes a crescent bite and moves on. The plant skins the wound before the animal has cleared the next bend. Neither party is in a hurry to escalate. A plant that invested in spines would spend water it cannot spare on a defense against a grazer that takes less than the plant makes in an afternoon. A grazer that lingered to strip a whole shelf would dry out in the work. The arrangement looks like mercy and is only arithmetic.

They are quiet because a voice is a wet expense. What passes between them is pressure. A runner meeting another in a narrow place does not call. It sets its weight, and the shelf answers with a small flex, and the second animal reads the flex through its pads and either yields or does not. Yielding is common. Fighting dries the mouth. The young travel in the body-shadow of an adult not from affection but because the adult’s passage has already paid the toll of opening the fiber, and a smaller body can ride that payment for free. When the adult dies — and they die in place, often, tucked into a fork and simply not moving when the heat climbs — the body does not lie long as a body. The hide splits along the wax. What was stored water is taken by whatever arrives first, usually another runner, sometimes a thing from lower down that has smelled the looseness through the root-mat. Bone here is a poor word. The hard parts are mineralized plates, thin, already the color of the young stone, and they sift down through the galleries until they join the crust they resemble. The city eats its dead into its floor.

Above the runners, where the shelves climb toward the ordinary light, the bodies change their bargain with the air. These are smaller, many-legged, and they live on the inward flowers: blooms that never face the sky, that open into the warm dark of a gallery and offer not nectar in any generous sense but a paste sealed under a cap. The animal learns the cap with its mouthparts. It does not chew through at random. There is a seam, and the seam gives if the pressure is right, and the paste comes up thick enough that none of it strings into the air and is lost. A grain of it on the wrong part of the face is a waste the animal cleans immediately, drawing it back into the mouth with a motion too small to see from a pace away. In return it carries, stuck in the hooks of its legs, a dust of spores or scales that the next flower requires. The contract is old and unsigned. Walk a high shelf at the hour when the upper leaves tighten and you will see thousands of these transactions happening in a silence so complete it feels like a decision.

They have seasons, but the seasons are the plants’ seasons, and the plants’ seasons are the heat’s. When a district fruits, the high traffic thickens until the shelf-edges are paved with moving backs. Nothing flocks in the air. Flight exists — short, heavy, a blurt from one column to the next, wings that are more scoop than sail, useless beyond the distance of a room — and it is used the way a person uses a step down, not the way a bird uses a sky. There is no sky in the practical sense. There is a brightness above the last leaf, and anything that breaks into it finds itself in a dryness it is not built for, and turns, and comes back down into the close air as if it had touched a ceiling.

The predators are not a separate nation. They are the same economy with a different mouth. One kind waits in the hollows that persist after a plant has died in place: a body flattened to the curve of the chamber, skin as dull as dead fiber, patient in a way that costs it nothing because patience at that temperature is only not-moving, and not-moving spends less water than the hunt. It takes runners that have misread a flex in the shelf, or that have come around a kink the ground made yesterday and have not yet learned the new angle. The strike is short. There is no chase across districts. A miss means the prey is already in the next room, and the next room is a different animal’s rent. The predator eats what it can seal inside itself and leaves the plates. It does not cache. Caching would require a hole that stayed a hole, and holes here are ambitions the growth does not share.

Lower still, against the mineral, the bodies give up on the day entirely. These are the pale workers the upper city never sees. They are not a single kind. Some are flat and many-segmented, easing themselves along the seam where root meets new crust, feeding on the film of mineral that sweats out of stone still learning to be stone, and on the dead ends of roots that pushed too far and starved. Some are softer, almost shapeless in the hand if a hand could reach them, and they live inside the glues and resins the plants leak downward, a life spent in a slow sugar that never sees light and does not need to. Their traffic is what keeps the deepest mats from sealing into a solid deck. They are maintenance without a wage: their eating is a porosity, and the porosity is how the warm air from below still finds a way up, and how the cooler thread along a fresh crack still finds a way down. Collapse the pale layer in imagination and the galleries above would, in a few seasons, smother in their own unvented heat. No one above knows this. The runners do not thank the dark. The dark does not require thanks. It requires the film on the stone, which the basin, still emerging, continues to provide.

There is one animal the others make room for without having agreed to.

It is heavy. That is the first fact, and it organizes the rest. A body the mass of a large table, low, longer than it is tall, moving with a deliberation that would look like slowness if the shelves did not dip under it in a wave you can watch travel ahead of its shoulders. The hide is the thickest wax in the basin, plated in broad shields that overlap like the leaves it eats, colored the dull bone of the mineral under everything. It does not run the avenues the small ones keep. It makes them. Where it passes, fiber compresses, fuses, hardens into a floor that will take a season to soften again. Other animals use that floor within the hour. They do not follow the large one out of fellowship. They follow the geometry it leaves, the way water — if there were water — would follow a groove.

It feeds on the columns themselves, not the soft edges but the dense stored tissue halfway up a living tower, the part a plant keeps as its own reservoir. A bite from it is a real wound. The plant cannot skin it over in a moment. Sap beads, and the large animal’s mouth is shaped to take the bead before the air does: a seal, a pull, a throat that does not open to the outside while it swallows. This is the closest thing the basin has to a drinker, and still nothing is spilled. Around the wound, over days, the column thickens. It armors the bitten place with a scar-wood harder than the rest of itself. The animal, returning along a route its own weight has carved, finds the scar and moves on to a younger face. In this way it prunes without killing, and the columns that survive it are the columns that learn, and the learning is only growth in the right place, and the right place is wherever the mouth was.

It does not call. It does not need to. Its presence is a change in the load on the shelf, readable by every pad and claw in the district a moment before it arrives. Small things clear the center of the new floor and occupy the edges. Predators in their hollows do not strike while the weight is passing; the flex of the chamber wall tells them the room is no longer theirs. When two of the large kind meet, which is rare because their routes, once pressed, tend to diverge like old watercourses, they do not fight. They set shoulder to shield and wait. The wait can last the length of a heat-climb. The one that shifts first loses the line and must shoulder a new path through unpressed growth, an expense of days. Usually the lighter one shifts. Sometimes they stand until the ground itself, still fitting its plates somewhere under the mat, makes the decision for them by tilting the floor a finger’s width, and both adjust, and the meeting is over without a victor, only a kink in two roads.

Its young are carried as a dryness problem solved in advance. A single offspring rides in a fold of the parent’s flank, sealed against the plates, taking moisture from a gland that would otherwise have been the parent’s own reserve. The parent travels less while it carries. Its route shortens to a loop the district can bear. Other large ones avoid a carrier’s loop not from deference but because the compressed fiber there is already claimed by a weight that will not yield, and forcing a crossing would cost more water than the crossing is worth. Birth, if that is the word, is the moment the juvenile’s hide can hold its own weather. It drops from the fold onto a floor its parent has spent a season hardening, and it does not follow. Following would double the load on a loop already at its limit. It sets out at an angle, shouldering a thinner path, and within days the small runners have found the new stiffness and begun to pave it with their lesser resin. A district gains a road. No one attends the gain.

There are no keepers, no feeders, no animals that have taken up the work of other animals as a trade. What looks, from the patience of these routes, like a civic order is only the long result of bodies that cannot afford waste agreeing, without agreement, on which expenses are fatal. Noise is fatal in the sense that it is a leak. Spilled sap is fatal. A chase that runs past the room where it began is fatal. A territory the size of a pride’s old range, the kind that would need a river to justify it, cannot be paid for here, so no such territory exists. The largest claim in the basin is the loop a heavy body can walk between one heat and the next without spending its store. That is the size of a politics, and it is not felt as politics. It is felt as floor.

At the edges of any of these lives the same surplus presses in. More runners on more stiffened lines. More sealed flowers opening inward. More pale mouths at the mineral film. More weight dipping the shelves in a wave that travels ahead of itself and arrives as a silence with a direction. The basin is still becoming underneath them, a new plate, a new tilt, a corridor turned into a ramp by noon, and they answer as they answer everything: by taking the cheaper path, by drinking only what they can close their mouths around, by dying in place when the account will not balance, and by leaving, in the compression of fiber and the armor of scars and the sift of plates down toward the stone, a city that continues because they do.

They do not look up. There is nothing above the last leaf that belongs to them. The light arrives, ordinary, and stays, and they live in the shade it makes possible, in the warmth that does not leave, in the rooms that replace themselves, busy and careful and almost without sound. If the place has a people, it is all of them, and none of them would know the word. They would know a flex in the shelf, a seam in a cap of paste, a floor gone stiff and safe under the chest, a wound that closed in time. That is as much citizenship as the basin has ever required, and it is enough to fill it, shelf over shelf, from the raw stone to the bright lid of the day.
