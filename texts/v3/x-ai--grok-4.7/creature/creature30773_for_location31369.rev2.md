They look, at first, like the water’s own unfinished thoughts.

A pale flex in a lane. A crown opening where the gray-blue is strongest. Something the size of a jointed finger, clear enough that the mineral behind it shows through, and you file them as a school — little filters, decorative, the livestock of a city whose real citizens you have not yet found. That filing does not survive the next minute. The flex does not pass. It arrives at a gap in a wall and stays, and the gap closes by the simple fact of its staying. What you took for a fish was a course of masonry that had not yet committed to being masonry.

There is one kind of them. The differences you keep naming — hunter, wall, nursery, street — are postures, not peoples.

The body is a tube that has never accepted the idea of an outside surface as final. At the crown, a ring of glassy lappets, each one a lip and a gill and a hand, none of those words quite right. They do not grasp. They admit. Water enters, is tasted, is thinned of what can be used, and leaves by a row of slits along the flank in a pulse so small it never becomes a current of its own. Between the slits the hide is not hide. It is a living secretion still deciding whether to remain soft. Left alone, in a place with flow and neighbors, it hardens at the edges into a bone-white lace, slightly yellow where it is newest, and that lace is how a body becomes a wall without ceasing to be a body. Touch would tell you — if touch were possible without becoming part of the work — that the hard part is not dead. It still passes a faint sweetness when the crown upstream flushes. The animal has simply voted, with chemistry, to be load-bearing.

Inside the clear middle there is no skeleton you would recognize and no empty space. A knot of slow muscle runs the length of the tube, not for swimming so much as for aiming the pulse. Around it, packed in a bruise-violet grain, are the young. They are not carried as a burden. They are the next thickness of the same animal, already drinking what the parent drinks, already laying down a film on the inner wall of the parent’s body. A nursery is not a room these creatures go to. It is a posture in which a body stops opening its crown quite so wide and lets the grain inside it ripen toward the day it will bud sideways through a slit and become, without traveling, the next house over.

They do not grow up and leave. Leaving would be a lie about volume. The pore has no elsewhere large enough to be a life, so adulthood here is not a size. It is a decision about which way to secrete. Some bodies stay finger-small forever, crowns flared toward the thin light, and these are what a newcomer would call the citizens in the street: they keep the lanes by occupying their edges and by refusing, chemically, to let a neighbor swell across the flow. Others accept the crystal face when it advances, seal their slits, and go opaque. From outside the lane they look like stone that happens to be shaped like a throat. From inside they are still tasting, very slowly, the water that seeps through their own lace, and when the seepage turns bitter they dim, and the dimming travels through the hardened quarters faster than any pulse, a shuttering of pigment in bodies that can no longer move and so have become the city’s memory of direction.

The dimming is their only public speech, and it is not theirs alone. A sweetness means food has entered at a crack. A bitterness means a death upstream large enough to change the water. A sudden, district-wide darkening means pressure has gone wrong — something has taken more volume than the lane had agreed to spare. They have no eyes. The light-sensitive grain is scattered in the lappets and along the inner film, enough to know which thin place in the outer skin is clearest, not enough to make a picture. What they have instead of vision is a continuous, involuntary honesty: every body reports, in what it sheds, exactly what it has been eating and how afraid the water made it. Privacy is not a concept that can survive gills.

Predation is a failure of that honesty, prolonged until it becomes a method.

Most correction is ordinary. A body that begins to thicken — that keeps what it should have passed on, that lets its grain multiply without budding it sideways into a neighbor’s work — finds the lane narrowing against it. Neighbors simply do not leave the space. Their secretions meet its secretions and the meeting is a refusal, a bitter film, a slow embalming. Usually the swelling body takes the hint, opens, buds, returns to the common size. Sometimes it does not. Then the lappets, which were made to admit, invert their patience. They become a hardness. The tube shortens and thickens. The pulse, instead of shedding water, holds it, and the held water is driven once, hard, into whatever is softer and closer than the wall. There is no chase you could follow with an eye that loves a chase. There is a pressure change, a scrap of membrane, and a body that is suddenly the right size again because it has borrowed the volume it was denied and laid it down as lace.

The others do not exile it. Exile is a direction this pocket does not contain. They dim, they flush the bitterness downstream, and by evening — if the slight turning of the clearest thin-place can be called evening — the corrected hunter is a course in a wall like any other, its crown sealed or reopened according to whether the lane still needs a pump there. The city does not forgive. It reabsorbs. Ambition is not punished; it is used.

You will look for a face and keep not finding one, and the longer you look the more that absence will seem like the point. The crown is a mouth only when it is feeding. When the body is becoming street, the crown is an address: the particular mix it sheds, the particular slight of its lace, the way its dimming lags or leads the dimming next to it. A stranger budded three chambers away can find its quarter by taste alone, and can lose it in an hour if a crystal face splits the flow and both sides begin, without consultation, to season themselves differently. Identity here is a flavor with a half-life.

They have no gesture for one another that is not also a gesture for the rock. To bud is to found a lane. To seal is to donate yourself to the basalt. To hunt is to keep the water thin enough to breathe. Even their rest is civic: a quarter that has gone nearly still is not asleep, it is waiting on the next warm pulse, crowns half-open, grain brightening from violet toward the yellow of fresh work, every body poised to do the only thing any of them has ever done — continue, at the size the pore will allow, in the direction the last bitterness indicated, until the mineral writes a new law and the streets, without a sound, agree to be born again on the other side of it.
