They do not announce themselves from the rim. From up there the basin looks like work without workers, roofs and channels and a gray that swallows scale, and only if you stay long enough do the small rearrangements resolve into people: a figure stepping into a recess, a pair of shoulders under a wet collar, a cart that changes hands at a corner and keeps the same speed. Down on the terraces they are simply the width of the paths. The place was poured around a human body and has not been revised since. Shoulder to wall, sleeve to leaf, one person passing another only because someone, years ago, left a niche for exactly that.

They are not a single people in the way a town on a postcard is a single people. Shifts overlap. A glasshouse crew finishes as a channel crew starts. Housing sits in the same fabric as the sheds — low blocks above pump halls, rooms over drying floors, a row of doors along a service road that could be offices until you see the boots outside and the steam from a kettle vent. Children learn the recesses before they learn the names of the halls. Elders keep to the mid-level roads where the grade is kind and the wind off the rim is broken by tank walls. Almost nobody is from somewhere that still looks like somewhere else. Grandparents remember a lower country, hotter, louder, with soil that was not trucked. Their grandchildren do not miss it. Missing requires a comparison the basin does not offer on an ordinary day.

What they wear has stopped being clothing in the fashionable sense and become a solution. Dark cloth, close weave, hems that shed water instead of drinking it. Collars that stand. Gloves in a pocket, on a belt, or already on, depending on whether the next task is a valve, a leaf, or a ledger. Boots with a tread that holds on wet concrete and does not collect the grit the concrete gives up. You can tell a glasshouse shift from a channel shift by the fog still on a shoulder, or by the mineral line at a cuff where sluice water dried and was not quite brushed off. Nobody dresses to be seen from far away. There is no far away inside the paths. Color, when it appears, is small and practical: a knit cap faded to a color that was once red, a cloth tied at a wrist so a tool does not walk off a narrow ledge, the pale inside of a hood thrown back because the mist thickened and then did not.

Their faces, if you pass close enough, are the faces of people who spend their days in used light. Not gaunt. Not theatrical. Skin that holds a cool damp and pinks at the cheekbones when they climb a flight between terraces. Eyes used to judging distance in gray: the lip of a channel, the sag in a pane, whether a leaf is beading or pouring. They look at what is in front of them. The horizon is a supervisor’s problem, or a driver’s, and even the drivers only get it in pieces between tank walls. Conversation happens at arm’s length or not at all. Voices stay low because sound carries oddly here — eaten by mist, then suddenly clear in a covered walk — and because the lanes are too narrow for talk that needs an audience. What gets said is short, specific, and already about the next correction. Gate three is high. The north row is fogged solid. Cut the dock back before it takes the drain. A nod does the rest.

They work the way the valley works: continuously, and without a visible center. No one person’s labor is the point. A woman on a terrace with a hooked blade keeps a planter from closing a gutter; if she stops, by evening the water sheets across the path and into a doorway two flights down. A man at a sluice does not perform the lifting of the gate so much as agree with it, hand on the wheel, eye on the notch, listening for the change in note that means the lower channel has taken what it can. Inside the glasshouses the crews move between rows with a patience that looks slow until you count what they touch: vents, ties, leaves pressed to glass and eased back, fruit or cuttings or whatever this row was built to hold, crated and set where the cart will find it without blocking the aisle. The cart drivers know every recess and every place where two carts cannot pass. They do not rush. Rushing on wet stone with a full load is how a wheel finds the channel.

Maintenance is not a department so much as a mood they all share. Concrete is allowed to weather until it isn’t. A joint that weeps is watched. A joint that weeps into a walkway is opened, packed, and closed again before the end of the shift, because tomorrow’s drip is today’s path and today’s path is how the night crew reaches the pumps. Plants are cut where they threaten a drain and left where they hold a bank. Someone always knows which downspout has been colonized by a fern that is, inconveniently, doing a better job than the last repair. They argue about that sometimes, briefly, and then leave the fern if the numbers allow. The arguments are not philosophical. They are about what fails first.

They eat when the work lets them, which is often enough that nobody looks starved and seldom in a way that gathers a crowd. Kitchens are tucked where heat can be kept: against boiler walls, above a drying floor, in a room whose other purpose is to store dry gloves. Food is warm, plain, and heavy on what the glasshouses and the lower plots actually produce — greens that tolerate the cool, roots, eggs from a few sheds that have always been there and are not spoken of as farms, tea that is more important than it has any right to be. Steam from a kettle is one of the few warm signatures in the basin, and people orient to it without thinking. Shared tables exist. They are narrow. You sit shoulder to shoulder with whoever is off at the same hour, and you do not perform intimacy for it. Intimacy is what happens later, in the rooms above, or not, and is not the basin’s business.

At night the lamps in some glasshouse rows come on, and a thinner crew stays. The basin does not sleep so much as drop to a lower hum. Pumps keep their note. Channels keep their levels, watched by two or three people whose job is to believe the gauges and also not to believe them, to walk out and look. In the housing blocks, lights show in slits. Someone mends a glove. Someone has a radio turned low, a voice from outside the cloud, half lost in the tick of water on a sill. Children sleep to that tick. It is the first sound they know and the one that means the walls are doing what walls do. Couples and solitary people and households of three generations occupy the same kind of room: a window on a walkway or on a wet roof, a hook for wet coats, a smell of damp wool and tea and the green of cut stems that never quite leaves the cloth. Privacy is a closed door and a path too narrow for loitering. They have enough of it. They do not have the kind that comes from distance.

They are not unaware of the scale. On a clear hour — rare, and never fully clear — someone on a roof service will see across to the far stacks breathing paler gray into gray, and will feel the size of the shared weather. Then the cloud thickens, or the task resumes, and the size goes back to being a fact rather than a feeling. What they feel instead is local and exact. The cold in a handrail. The way a particular flight of steps holds water in the third joint from the bottom. Which neighbor will step into the recess without being asked, and which one needs the extra half second. Who can be trusted with a gate at night. Who cuts too close to the crowns and has to be told again, without heat, because heat is useless and the plant will show the mistake by itself.

Newcomers do arrive. A hire from the lowlands, a transfer, someone following a spouse into a room that was promised with the job. They talk too loudly for a week. They stand in the middle of a path as if width were a right. They are surprised that the sun does not come, and then they are surprised that they stop waiting for it. The basin instructs them without a speech. Wet sleeves. A cart that will not pass. An older worker’s hand on a shoulder, not unkind, moving them into the niche. By the second month their collars are up without a thought, and they have a opinion about a drain. By the second year they dream in the sound of ticking water and wake already knowing whether the note has changed.

There are no crowds and no loneliness of the empty kind. The loneliness available here is the ordinary one: a person on a night walk between tanks, lamp in hand, competent, a little tired, aware that the valley will continue its corrections whether or not they are the one to make the next. They do not find that crushing. They find it accurate. Belonging, if they named it, would not be a banner or a view. It would be the recess that fits their shoulders, the gate that answers their hand, the kettle two turns along, the leaf-smell in the wool, the knowledge that if they fail to cut the dock the water will enter a doorway, and that someone else would cut it if they could not, and that they can.

Animals do not live here in any number that would change the account. A bird on a vent. Moths at the practical lamps. The people do not romance the absence. They built over the ground, and the ground became the building, and what remained was room for them and for the plants that keep the water honest. They live in that remainder as if it were a country, because it is. They keep it running. It keeps them cool, employed, and slightly damp, and in the used light of late day they look like what the terraces have been shaping for longer than any one of them has been alive: not masters of the basin, not its victims, the moving part it cannot spare, already the color of the walls, already listening for the channel to change its note.
