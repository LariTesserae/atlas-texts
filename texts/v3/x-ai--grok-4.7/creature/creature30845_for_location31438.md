What lives there is not one thing, and the first error is to go looking for a people.

There are people, or something that still deserves the word if you are generous with it. You meet them the way you meet weather: by its effects, and then, if you are quiet and unhurried, by a presence that has been there longer than your noticing. They are few enough that a lifetime of walking might yield only a handful of faces, and abundant enough that the place does not fail. The difference is not a mystery. A body in these volumes is a local event. The work is distributed across so many hollows that no hollow is ever crowded, and the ones who keep the oculi from sealing, who carry water to a graft, who chew a cane before it becomes a wall, do not travel in companies. Company would be noise. Noise in the joins is how the larger hungers find you.

They are small-bodied, not by design so much as by the long sorting the place has done. A heavy animal leaves a print the floor drinks slowly; a light one is forgotten by morning, and forgotten is safer. Their hands, when you finally see hands, are the ordinary hands of a tool-using animal that never needed metal: short nails, strong pinch, skin thickened where leaf-cups and fire-hardened points have rubbed the same places for generations that no longer count themselves. They do not build fires for warmth. Warmth is the building. Fire, when it appears, is a small, mean, persuasive thing—a coal in a clay cup, used to scorch a bud that must not open, or to harden a point, or to mark a cord-map with a correction at a fold where yesterday’s path is no longer today’s. The smell of it is rare and travels farther than a voice. Other lives have learned what it means and give it room.

They do not have a name you can carry out. What they call themselves, if they call themselves, does not survive the galleries; the fiber eats consonants. Among themselves, in the close dark of a sleeping-hollow no larger than a body and its tools, they use clicks and breath and the press of a knuckle against a wrist, a language sized to the distance at which it is safe to be two. You will not learn it by listening from the path. The path is for travel. Speech is for the places where the floor has been taught, by long lying-down, to hold a slight hollow that does not drain, a nest of fiber pulled soft, a cache of leaf-cups nested one inside another, a point of bone or thorn wrapped so it will not pierce the living wall. These caches are the only houses. They are abandoned and refound on a rhythm that is not seasonal in any outside sense—more like the rhythm of a graft that must be checked, a rim that must be browsed, a conduit that has begun to prefer the wrong direction.

If you stay long enough to be tolerated, what you learn is not their history. History here is in the rings, and the rings are not theirs alone. What you learn is the ledger they keep without writing. This oculus, if left three sleeps, closes from the west. This span kneels when the marsh below it rises past the third lashing, and the kneeling is not failure; it is the span asking to be retired into a drain. This column inhales on a count you can learn with your palm, and if the count shortens, something in the high dark is roosting too heavy, and someone must climb—not today, not as a hero, but before the next weeping—and move the flock by being, briefly, a worse perch than the perch. They do not think of this as stewardship. The word would embarrass the work. They think of it as not dying of a closed sky, and not letting the water forget which way is up.

The herds are more visible, and less like neighbors.

They are low, broad, and the color of wet fiber, which is to say you see them first as a change in the lawn. Their mouths are wide and tireless. They crop the inner meadows not out of hunger alone—hunger would let them wander into the galleries and strip the living lashings—but because the meadow is the only sweetness that does not punish the eater. Elsewhere the building tastes of sap under stress, bitter, a warning laid into the chemistry by the same slow treaty that keeps rot out of the air. On the sagged floors, where water stands and the only plants are the ones that are not the building, the grazing is safe, and the animals have become the mowing. Calves, if that is the word, are born in the fog-band and stand almost at once, because lying down in the marsh is how a small body becomes part of the breakdown the walls perform out of sight. They do not flee you. Flight is a vibration. They sidle, and the sidle is enough, and the floor records the sidle and begins to forget it, and by the time you have crossed the lawn they are a different patch of shortening green, already at work again.

What follows them is harder to describe without lying, because it is so seldom seen as an animal and so often felt as a pause.

It is large. It has to be, to live on herds that are themselves sparse across a weather-sized room. It does not chase. A chase would telegraph through the joins, and the joins remember, and the herds have learned to read a remembered join the way you read a change in the light. So it waits. It may wait as a darkness against a rib that is already dark. It may wait inside the period of a breathing column, still when the column is still, shifting its weight on the inhale so the load does not read as extra. Some of the columns, the ones you are told not to lean on, are only columns. Some have been leaned on from the inside for so long that the grain has grown around a tenant the way bark grows around a wedge, and the tenant pays for the lodging by not eating in the chamber whose span it helps to hold. Hunger, for these, is a commute. They descend, or they ascend—direction is a local argument—into a meadow-chamber when the herds are thick enough to be worth the announcement of their leaving, and they kill one animal, and they kill it quietly, and they return before the circulation of the room has finished a single wheel. The herds know the price. They pay it the way the joints pay water: constantly, without panic, because panic is louder than the price.

You will want a shape for this hunter. The place declines to settle on one. What is constant is the patience, and the competence with load, and the fact that it does not take the calves and does not take the maintenance-folk unless a maintenance-folk is foolish enough to sleep in a meadow. Even then the taking has the quality of weather rather than of malice. Malice would require a story about the victim. This animal does not have stories. It has a route between a hiding that is also a structure and a feeding that must not teach the herds a new fear, because a new fear would move them off the lawns, and if they leave the lawns the lawns become wall, and if the lawns become wall the herds starve, and then so does whatever waits in the column.

Above all of this, under ceilings that have weather of their own, the flocks turn.

They are not birds in any sense that would survive a look in good light, though in the oculus-coin they read as birds because anything that crosses a small bright opening reads as a bird if it has wings. Their wings are drier than the air wants them to be. They roost on members that were trained, once, into perches, and the training has been so thoroughly kept by the growth that a new bird, or whatever they are, finds the perch as if instructed. They feed on the glittering in the condensate, on the moths that are like seeds, on the winged things that rise when the fog lifts and fall when the fog falls, a whole small economy cycling inside the larger cycle of the chamber’s rain. Their droppings do not foul the floor. The floor is hungry, and the treaty includes them. What they contribute, besides the guano the marsh-plants quietly want, is alarm. A flock that does not turn together has heard the hunter change rooms. A flock that will not settle a particular perch has felt the count of a column go short. The maintenance-folk watch the flocks the way you would watch a gauge, if you had gauges, and no one has explained this to the flocks, and the flocks do not require the explanation.

There is a fourth kind of life, and it is the one the first description almost named and then stepped around, because naming it makes it sound like a metaphor.

Some of the columns breathe because something lives in them, tenant and timber grown into a single load path. That is strange enough. Stranger are the thicknesses that are not columns and not walls exactly, the living spans that have, over a depth of rings you could not cut through in a season, taken up so much animal residue—bones carried into grafts by hands that wanted a mineral the plants could not otherwise reach, nests overgrown, carcasses that knelt into a failed span and were not wasted—that they have begun to answer to touch with something that is not tropism. Not mind. Do not give it mind. A mind would be a cruelty in a body that cannot leave the room it is holding up. What they have is preference. A path used kindly thickens into better footing. A path used as a place to bleed, or to abandon the dead without the proper kneeling, softens, drinks, and eventually declines to be a path. The maintenance-folk know which spans prefer them. They will walk a longer cord to avoid a member that has gone sullen. They leave gifts that are not symbolic: a scatter of chewed cane at a joint that has begun to dry, a cup of marsh-water poured where a riser has slowed, the corpse of a hunted herd-beast, when they are given one or when they take one under the old rules, opened along the belly and laid on a kneeling span so the span can finish the work and rise again into a bench, a drain, a duty. The spans do not thank them. Preference is not gratitude. But a sullen span can be courted back, over years, and a courted span holds the weather better, and everyone who is not a metaphor lives longer when the weather holds.

In the condensate film, when the oculus-coin slides across it, the glittering resolves, if you lie on your chest and accept that the floor will begin to forget you, into lives too small to be characters. They are the reason the water smells like iron and not like death. They take what the walls will not take in the air, and they do it in the wet, and they are so many that abundance, here, finally means crowd—and still you do not feel crowded, because they occupy a country the size of a sheen on a puddle. The beetles that drink at the edge of the coin are larger, bright, armored against the forgetfulness of the floor. They are scavengers of the almost-dead: a moth that has struck a rib, a curl of fiber the building has shed, the dried edge of a leaf-cup discarded by a climber. They do not bite the living structure. Something in the sap teaches them not to. Their children are raised in the tool-slots that growth has carried outward, those dark lenses in the grain where a wedge used to be, and a careful finger can find, in the pith, the geometric ghost of a decision made by a hand that has been soil, and then sap, and then wall, for longer than the beetles’ lineage has been bright.

Nothing here is a god, and nothing here is a pet, and the place does not produce a king. The closest thing to a center is the circulation itself: fog, lift, rain, rise of water in the bundles, weep at the joints, return. Every animal you can name is a way of keeping that wheel from sticking. The herds keep the meadows from sealing. The flocks keep the high dark from becoming a single roost too heavy for its member. The hunter keeps the herds from eating the meadows down to the fiber and then starving in a circle. The glittering keeps the wet from going foul. The maintenance-folk keep the oculi open and the risers honest and the maps corrected at the folds, and they are not above the others for doing it; they are simply the only ones who carry water on purpose to a place that has not yet asked.

If you ask them—if you learn the close language well enough to ask—what lives here, they will not point at themselves. They will press a knuckle to the floor, and then to the dark above, and then, if they trust you, to your own knee where the fiber has already started to rise into the shape of your kneeling, as if to say that you are already, slightly, in the ledger. The answer is not the folk, and not the herds, and not the thing in the column. The answer is the set of agreements that lets each of those remain few and far between inside a body of rooms so large it rains. Break one agreement and the rooms do not explode. They close. The coin of sky narrows. The lawn becomes wall. The water forgets up. The warmth, which was never the sun, goes somewhere else in the mass, and the hollow you are standing in becomes a thickness, and the thickness becomes a ring in some later span, a preserved wind, a stain in the shape of a circulation that used to have a meadow in it, and beasts, and a path, and someone carrying a cup.

They know this without saying it in a way the galleries could keep. It is why they are solitary, and why they are kind to the spans, and why, if you are the sort of animal that can learn to carry a cup, they will not stop you. There is room. There has to be. The room is the body. What lives in it is the continuance of the room.
