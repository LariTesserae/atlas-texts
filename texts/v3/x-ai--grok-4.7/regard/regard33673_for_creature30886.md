They would not raise the mountains again.

That is the first thing, and it takes them longer to be sure of it than any other mercy they are accused of withholding. A wise power can feel the old appetite in the stone: the folded bedding, the memory of faces and spires, the dramatic vertical that weather spent an age planing away. To put it back would be a kind of love people understand—height you can point at, shelter you can climb into, a skyline to mean *kept*. They look at it. They let it go. The floor is the gift. Everything alive here is alive because the collapse already finished and the light still reaches the bed. To restore grandeur would be to drown the meadow in depth and call it splendor.

They do not still the wind, or the chop, or the argument of currents off the knolls. Those are the place thinking. A sea this wide and this shallow, at this height, is not a bowl to be calmed for the comfort of boats; it is a skin over a moving floor, and the skin has work. Rain must walk the shelf in walls. The lip must pour. The mist must stand where the plateau gives up its water, announcing the drop before the drop is seen. Silence, imposed, would be a theft dressed as peace. The birds would not know where the upwellings had gone. The people would not know which squall was the second.

They do not take the engines.

There is a temptation, small and clean, to let the stack finish rusting and the belts finish singing and the turbine hall fill with the same green that has the causeways, so that the only stroke left is weather. It would look like reverence. It would also be a lie about who lives here. The drop is reliable and the catchment is vast, and the households who still know which bearing runs dry are not a stain on the peneplain. They are one of its slower animals. A benevolent power that loved only the unpeopled view would already have misread the room. The gantry legs stay. The wedge of shelf-stone at dawn stays. Grease on the elbow stays, and the string of fish that is not the company’s, because there is no company, only the promise of falling water, which they will not break and will not improve upon.

What they do is smaller than intervention and harder to praise.

They learn the note.

Not the people’s word for it—the stem-tick that changes pitch where the bed shallows, the wrong sound that means stone, the soft reprimand when a keel kisses charophyte crust and the knees know before the mouth does. A wise power does not replace that listening with a safer sea. They keep the listening true. When the turbines are drawing hard and the whole shelf sits an inch lower than the morning’s memory, the inch is honest, not malicious: the sound arrives in time, in the hull, where the body can use it. They do not move the stone aside. They do not let the stone lie about itself.

They keep the hinge of the day.

That minute when loudness thins and the small things come clear—drip into a barrel, stems against a piling, a fish turning, cooling metal—is not an accident they are willing to spend. Squalls may take every other minute; the shelf is owed its traffic. But the hinge they guard the way a careful hand guards a flame in wind: not by building a windless room, only by refusing to let the one clear minute be the one that fails. Someone always hears it. Often it is a child, who does not yet have the word for having missed it, and so does not miss it. The power cares more about that than about being thanked, which is fortunate, because no one on the knolls is in the habit of thanking the weather for behaving like itself.

When the level rises between one check and the next, they are already in the custom, not above it. The child in the lower storey is passed up through the hatch still asleep, net-careful, dream unbroken. They do not seize the flood and hold it back so the lower storey can become a parlor. Dry ground here is a concession measured in paces; to enlarge the concession into a kingdom would teach the wrong physics to a people who have survived by refusing straight lines. What they will not allow is the one failure that cannot be bailed at first light: the hatch stuck, the sleeper not lifted, the dream broken into water. Benevolence, on a floor this low, is mostly punctuality.

They leave the otters’ treaty alone. They do not adjudicate the fish heads on the flat stones, or the lines that get chewed when the custom breaks, or the caddis cases kicked into the drink by small wet hands that are not quite hands. A power that hurried in to make the neighbors nicer would become a third party, and third parties are how treaties die. Wisdom recognizes an agreement that was never written down and is working. They do not sign it. They do not improve the penmanship.

At the lip, in the standing mist, they are most tempted to be seen.

The waterfall widened into a weir is the only gesture in the region tall enough to read as an altar, and powers, even wise ones, have a weakness for altars: a place to appear, a voice in the pour, a sign that the attention was real. They stand in the mist long enough to feel the pull of it—the body-sound, the cold bright haze, the last hundred yards where the ancient highland finally has an edge again—and then they do not speak. Speech there would collect pilgrims the way a slick collects gulls, and the shelf has no spare dry ground for pilgrims, and the turbine hall is loud enough without prophecy. If anyone working a screen in a northeast blow feels, for a moment, less alone in the noise, that is as much face as they will show. It passes. The belt sings or it does not. Someone’s hands already know which.

They do not stop the lowering.

The plateau is still being taken down, leaf by leaf, film by film, a thickness of glacial flour from a rim out of sight beyond cloud. To arrest that would be to pretend the oldest stable state is stillness, when the truth of this place is that further change happens by water moving across a floor, not by rock falling from a height that no longer exists. A benevolent power can want the people spared the grief of a vanishing country. A wise one notices that the country vanishes at the speed of a season, and that the people have already organized their lives around breathing water, shifting rises, lanes that close and lanes that open where no one has poled before. Freezing the map would be a cruelty with a gentle voice: *stay as I saw you, on the day I loved you.* They love them moving.

So what they do, if it must be called doing, is this: they keep the rules that were already enforced, and they keep them from becoming traps.

Height remains a property of the entire floor, so no one is mocked with a false lee in the sky. Water covers what it can. Light stays ordinary, which is why the bottom stays green. Growth packs what the cold allows; animals pack the growth; the machines stay scattered, mechanical, partly surrendered, still turning. No skyline is granted. The tallest gestures remain a stack, a gantry, a column of rain, and the mist at the lip.

Within those rules they are attentive in the way the inhabitants are attentive—low, angled, never for long in a straight line. A lane through the milfoil held open just long enough. A wrong note not swallowed by the engines. A bearing that complains before it seizes, on a morning when someone is close enough to hear. Lamps at night that do not try to outshine the water, and do not, on the worst rising, blow out all at once. Strangers from lower country still turn back confused, which is correct; explanation is not owed, and a wise power is not a pilot.

They do not call this guardianship where anyone can hear. Guardianship is a tall word, and tall words want monuments, and the shelf has spent itself becoming something better than a monument: a living floor, crowded, audible, cold enough to ache in the wrists, unfinished in the only direction that still matters.

If they remain, they remain as the place already almost is when it is at its most itself—the hammered quiet after hard rain, before the next wall of weather, when you can hear a stem tick a hull and know, without seeing, that the bottom is still there, still reachable, still telling the truth.

They would not raise the mountains again. They would not need to be believed in. They would keep the minute, and the hatch, and the note, and let the long work go on.
