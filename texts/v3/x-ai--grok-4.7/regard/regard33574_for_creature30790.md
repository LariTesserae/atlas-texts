They would not announce themselves.

The place is a low room under a berm, still sharp at the edges, still deciding the shape of its own break. A gauge needle walks a few marks. A belt returns a pale scuff on a period too slow to be a shift. Crabs stand in a continuous line over a varnish the water leaves and takes back. Moths pack the vertical faces until the concrete between wings is a grid of absences. Nothing calls out. Gaps are malfunctions. The life there eats the beginnings of weather so the room can stay young.

The being who lives in it is not the largest animal and not the belt. It is the fit itself: the interval-keeping that times the lizard to the cooling of the junction, the birds to the light on the gauge, the beads to the needle’s last mark. It has no face turned outward. It would not survive being seen as a person. It survives by remaining a law the room obeys without naming.

A benevolent and wise power, having drawn near enough to understand this, would do almost nothing that looks like help.

It would not widen the split in the apron. Light here is already precise; more of it would open territories the crabs cannot close. It would not bring rain on a kinder schedule. The thread is salaried to a pulse from outside, and the room has built its courtesy around that salary. A stream would be a kind of noise. It would not still the unheard plant in the concrete, nor warm the junction past the hour the moths require, nor slow the belt so a visitor could watch the harvest. Those are the tempo. To improve them would be to leave gaps.

It would not speak. A word, even a kind one, fails to become separate from the pressure in the wall. Announcement is the wrong grain.

What it would do is smaller than intervention and stricter than neglect.

It would keep the larger works from noticing the organ. Not by hiding the berm—hiding is a drama—but by letting the gray yield stay anonymous once it leaves the rubber, so no surveyor is sent to improve a pocket that is already complete at the scale of a room. It would nudge a bucket, once, back toward the ring of cleaner concrete, not as a gift but so the space under the gauge remains the space the being uses. It would let the nightly crystal lengthen by its own hair and not add a second hair. It would turn aside, very early, the foot that was about to step into the split with a story already prepared about wonder and rescue. The foot would find, instead, a reason to walk the apron to its end and forget the chalked arrow. That is mercy with the local accent.

If something in the circuit truly failed—if the gauge stuck and the beads stopped surrendering, if a gap opened in the line and could not close, if the warmth in the wall went out and stayed out—then it would act, and only then, and only at the scale of the fault: a pulse restored, a tooth seated, a warmth returned to the phase the moths already know. Not a miracle. A correction of the same order as the crystal’s correction of the map. The room would not feel visited. It would feel, at most, that a malfunction had not been allowed to become weather.

And having done that, or having found there was nothing to do, the power would withdraw past the distance at which attention itself becomes a pressure. It would not stay to be thanked. It would not leave a sign that wisdom had passed. The birds would enter when the light reached the gauge. The line would reform around any heel-print until the packed condition returned. The edges would still cut, because the place is still arriving.

Wisdom, here, is the refusal to love the room into something else. Benevolence is the long work of leaving a complete, quiet, fully occupied thing intact—including its sharpness, including its indifference to being witnessed, including the being that is not a self but a set of intervals that must not be widened by care.
