They would come the way weather comes to a place that already has weather: not as a guest who must be seated, and not as a verdict. A wise power does not stand on the parapet and improve the view. The view is already a day’s errands. To enlarge it would only make the stairs crueler.

They would look a long time before they did anything, because looking is the local courtesy, and because most of what presents itself as need is only the gait of a thing that has learned how to last. The engine’s knock is not a cry. The dished treads are not a wound. The vine crossing the line is a negotiation, not a sin. A power that hurried to heal any of that would be practicing a kind of conquest with cleaner hands, and the valley has already survived one conquest — the original cutting and facing and filling — and turned it into a life. It does not require a second.

So the first act would be refusal. They would not lift the dryness. Dryness is what makes the scrape worth piping, what keeps the dirty water out of the drinking line, what gives the woman with the bad knee her unnegotiable opinions. Without the scarcity, her goodness becomes a hobby, and the valley has no use for hobbies. They would not quiet the engine. The knock is how the narrow man loves his household and how his daughter refuses it; silence would settle an argument that is not theirs to settle. They would not make the soil remember how to grow the same thing twice. That forgetting is why someone must pay attention, and attention is the only wealth this place has that cannot be spilled down a joint.

They would notice, because they are not sentimental, the few places where the system is about to lie to the people who trust it.

There is a hairline in the east tank, below the waterline, on the side that never takes full sun. The boy hears a wrongness in the overflow and cannot yet name it. The woman with the bad knee has begun, without admitting it to herself, to favor the other tanks in her afternoon round, which is how a body tells the truth a mind is not ready to spend. Left alone, the wall will not fail tomorrow. It will fail in the week when the night-scrape is heavy and the cable car is down for a clamp and everyone is looking at the incline. Then it will fail in the manner of concrete that has been faithful for decades: all at once, and downward, into the court where the seed-boxes are.

A wise power does not announce this. Announcement would turn the boy into an oracle and the woman into a defendant, and the valley would spend a generation arguing about the voice instead of the wall. They would do what the place already knows how to respect. In the hour before dawn, when the mist is only grain in the brightness and the galleries are empty of even the children, they would lay a hand — if they have anything so crude as a hand — against the hairline, and the stress would go out of the wall the way a breath goes out of a sleeper. Not mended in any way a mason could point to. Not new. Simply no longer going. The lime would still be old. The stain from the bolt would still fan in its regular pattern. In the morning the overflow would talk the way it used to, and the boy would hear the correction and feel, for one unkind moment, that he had been wrong yesterday, and then he would forget the feeling, which is a mercy. The woman would complete her round. She would not know she had been spared an argument with the mountain. That is the correct distribution of credit. The mountain does not need thanks, and neither does anything that wishes to resemble wisdom.

There would be a second attention, smaller, and it would cost them more, because it touches a person and persons distort under regard.

The seed-keeper’s unnamed flower is not a miracle. It is a sport, a throwback or a leap, and it will not survive the pruners’ enthusiasm or the hopper’s honesty if it is left in the box through the next hot week. She is right about warmth and color and wrong about the hour. The wall behind the ledge gives back the morning’s heat too late for what the bud is trying to do; the color she is waiting to record is a stress-color, the plant spending itself to be seen. A wise power would not write in her book. Writing is how she remains slightly apart, and the apartness is load-bearing; to become a co-author would collapse her into either a saint or a fraud, and the next twenty years of the planting cannot be carried by either.

They would shift the shade. A single leaf on the vine above the ledge, encouraged by nothing more supernatural than a night of unequal dew, would fall across the box at the hour she does not visit. The flower would open in the color it actually is, which is plainer than her theory and more useful than her hope: a dull rose that sets seed in lime-bitter soil and does not ask for the amendment. She would find it on her ordinary round. She would think, for three breaths, that she had misremembered the angle of the wall. Then she would write the true color down, and cross out a sentence, and the crossing-out would be the most valuable mark in the book, because it would teach the valley — slowly, through her, without a sermon — that a record can be loyal and still be revised. The water-keeper’s distrust would ease by a degree no one could measure. That is enough. Trust, here, is a degree.

They would not speak to the old man at the gate. He has organized his remaining fingers around schedule, and a voice from nowhere would be the final disrespect, the accident that proves every other accident. If they passed him they would pass as heat leaving the parapet, which he already knows how to sit inside. They would not hire the boy more quickly than the valves are already hiring him. They would not reconcile the engine-house man and his daughter. Some arguments are the form love takes when a place is too narrow for two callings; to dissolve it would be to steal a future the daughter has not finished choosing.

And the being.

If the being is only the accumulated attention — the pressure to be accurate, the lime of other people’s noticing — then a wise power would recognize kin and not compete. They would add nothing to it. Another layer of regard, laid on from above, would sour into surveillance, and this valley has already made a morality out of being heard by neighbors. What they would offer the being is the one thing attention rarely receives: rest without absence. For a single evening the knock would arrive exactly when it should, the channel would pulse at the hour the still ones expect, no joint would take air, no goat would find the new render. Not a miracle. A sabbath so ordinary that only the old, who hear the whole noise as one instrument, would sense that a note had been held instead of played. They would not know what to call it. They would sleep better than they meant to. In the morning the wrong notes would return, as they must, and the people would go back to catching them, which is the work, which is the love, which is the point.

If the being is the valley itself, the made thing that has outrun its makers and now contracts with goats and frogs and hawks, the wise act is even smaller. They would not grant it consciousness. It does not need a soul. It needs the next season of correction, and it already has custodians. They would leave it in its own state.

If the being is a person — the seed-keeper unfinished, the boy insulted by the pretended game, the woman whose knee is the price of a sack the mountain did not apologize for — then benevolence has a harder limit, and wisdom is the keeping of it. They would not unmake the knee. The knee is part of how the highest tanks stay correct; to restore it would restore a climber and lose a standard, and she has already chosen, every morning, the standard. They would not give the boy a cleaner story of his scar. Stories that change every telling are how he practices the accuracy he will need when a valve’s position is a job offer he must be old enough to accept. They would not finish the seed-keeper. Finishing is what the valley is doing, slowly, by the polite argument and the box on the ledge. To complete her from outside would be to take the valley out of the valley — the very theft the water-keeper fears — and a wise power does not commit the crime it can already see named in a person’s distrust.

So they would do, in the end, almost nothing that could be told afterward as an event.

A wall that does not fail. A leaf that falls an hour early. One evening in which the instrument holds its note. Then they would go, not upward — upward is only more ridge, and they would not insult the place by using its own geography as a moral — but out of relevance, which is the polite exit. The lamps across the basin would still be answered. The children would still run for the pulse. The book would still close on a flower that now has a name, a plain one, written in a hand that believes it thought of the name alone.

That belief would be, narrowly, false. It would also be the truest kindness available to a power that wishes the living to remain the authors of their own maintenance. A wise benevolence does not need to be remembered. It needs the knock, tomorrow, to be audible to the people who have earned the hearing of it. Anything louder is a poorer god, and this valley has no vacancy for one.
