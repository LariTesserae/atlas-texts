The height comes first, before any shape. The air is thin enough that brightness has an edge to it, a clean cold glare with almost no haze to soften the distance, and the ground—if it is ground—sits so far above whatever once lay beneath it that the lower world has dropped out of the climate entirely. Nothing rises to meet you from below. There is no sea-glint, no river-shine, no cloud-shadow dragging a wet belly across a plain. The light is simply there, hard and high and all day, and the cold comes with it: not a killing cold, not ice, but the steady cool of altitude, the kind that keeps resins stiff and dust from clumping and metal-bright surfaces from ever warming past a hand’s discomfort.

What the light falls on is alive, and it is all there is.

The continent—there is no better word; it has weather of its own—is a single settled mass of vegetation, grown so long into its own form that stone, if stone remains, is only a rumor inside the trunks. You do not walk on soil. You walk on rind, on braided fiber, on plates of dried cortex that have weathered into a pavement of interlocking scales, each scale the width of a field, gray-green and ocher and the pale yellow of old wax. Between the plates the seams are not cracks filled with earth. They are lips: living joins, slightly raised, slightly warmer to the touch than the plates, extruding a fine mineral dust that the wind takes at once. The dust is the fertilizer. There is no water to carry anything. Rain does not fall here. Dew does not bead. The plants do not drink. They open internal galleries to the dry air, draw down carbon and a thin diet of salts scoured off their own weathered skins, and run their chemistry in oils and resins and pressurized gases that never loosen into liquid. Cut one of the great plates and it does not bleed. It exhales, briefly, a sweet hydrocarbon breath, and the wound skins over with a matte lacquer before the glare can dry it further.

The growth is total and the presences are few. That is the first fact that will not sit still in the mind. For league after league the living pavement continues, unbroken, a substrate rather than a forest, and then the architecture begins: not a thicket, not a stand, but single structures risen out of the rind at distances that make them look, from anywhere but their own feet, like monuments left by a people who never were. A tower of fused culms, hollow, laddered inside with dry diaphragms, standing alone in a basin of its own shed scales. A fan of blades the size of sails, rooted in one boss, each blade a living louver that tracks the sun with a creak you feel in the teeth. A low dome, kilometers wide, of interlocked leaves so reduced by age and wind that they have become tiles, and under the dome a darkness that is only shade, not moisture. Between these the emptiness is the rule. You can walk a day and meet nothing taller than the seam-lips. The abundance is in the continuity of the floor and in the sheer number of such forms across a span large enough to generate its own winds—not in crowding. The place is a pure-plant world that has learned the virtue of space.

The sound does not match the sparseness. It is the second fact.

There is no hush at altitude here. The pavement conducts. Every seam is a throat. The wind, which the continent makes and then suffers, enters the hollow towers and comes out changed: a deep organ-note from the largest, a rattling stitch from the younger, a scream from any blade that has split and not yet sealed. The fans clap. Not softly. They haul around on their living pivots and slam the air, and the slam arrives late and wrong, from a structure you cannot yet see. Under that, always, a lower register: the industrial one. The mass is in production. It has been in production so long that the processes have worn themselves into balance, and balance here is not quiet. Along the undersides of the great plates, galleries of fiber work like ranked engines—contracting, relaxing, driving mineral dust and resin and gas through conduits the width of a man’s arm. The rhythm is mechanized, repetitive, scaled past any single organism’s needs. Where a dome has matured, its interior hums with a steady electrical tick; the leaves accumulate charge off the dry wind and spend it in small blue snaps along their edges, a factory of light running without a single built machine. Nothing was constructed. There are no walls, no roads, no tools, no slag heaps that are not simply the plant’s own abandoned scales, sorted by centuries of wind into pale drifts in the lee of the towers. The industry is the metabolism, electrified by friction and by the bright cold air, deafening because it never stops and because the thin air carries it farther than it should.

The weather is the metabolism turned outward.

By midmorning the fans and the galleries have heated their immediate air just enough, even in the cool, to tilt the flow. Winds organize. They do not wander in from elsewhere; they are born on the rind and die on the rind. A front is a change in the pitch of the towers. You hear it before you see it: a darkening of the organ-notes, a sudden unanimity, as if every hollow structure upwind had been tuned to the same stop. Then the dust. It lifts not as a soft veil but as a racing skin, ankle-high, then shoulder-high, bright in the sun, tasting of salt and resin and hot metal. It scours. That is the erosion, and it is established—settled into the shapes, not eating them away toward some other landscape. The towers lean the way they have leaned for an age. The plates are grooved where the dust has run the same paths for so long that the grooves are now part of the growth plan; new fiber follows the old channels. The domes wear their windward sides to a polish and keep their leeward sides furred. Destruction and repair are the same motion. A blade splits in the gust with a crack like timber giving way in a ship, and before the echo has finished the split edges are already lacquered, already stiffening, already being worried by the next gust into the curve they will hold until they split again. Turbulence is the mature state. The continent is not becoming something else. It is loudly, continuously, being this.

The animals live inside the noise and the gaps.

They are abundant in the way of a vast country with many provinces: not swarms, not silence, but a steady census of bodies using the architecture without ever filling it. On the open pavement you may go hours and see only tracks in the dust—three-toed, or the drag of a belly-plate, or the neat punched holes of something that hops and does not settle. Then a tower takes you in, and the abundance declares itself. The diaphragms are occupied. High in the culms, where the light drills down the hollow in white discs, colonies of small gliders cling to the fiber and drop into the updraft the tower itself maintains, riding the organ-note, banking out through slots and back in, a traffic with rules. They do not drink. Their mouths are dry rasps; they shear the lacquer at the slots and the wax from each other’s wings, and the wax is enough. Lower down, in the dimmer galleries, larger animals work the conduits the way pilot fish work a shark—cleaning valves of fiber, eating the mineral crust that would choke a gallery, and being tolerated because the plant’s production runs cleaner with them in it. You hear them more than you see them: a dry clatter, a cough of expelled resin-dust, a sudden thin screaming argument over a valve that two of them both claim. Out on the plates, in the full glare, the long runners keep to the grooves, bodies narrow, eyes set under shelves of pale scale to cut the brightness. They are not scarce. Cast across the whole of the living continent their numbers are great. They are only never near each other for long, because the food—the lacquer, the wax, the mineral crust, the occasional windfall of a split blade—is itself spread thin, and the wind punishes anything that bunches up and makes a sail of itself.

Nothing preys in the wet way. There is no mud, no blood-scent carrying, no drinking-place where trails must cross. What hunting there is, is a matter of waiting at a slot or a seam until something comes to feed, and then a short, loud, dusty violence that the wind edits out of the air in minutes. Scavengers are the true abundance: low, numerous, specialized on lacquer-flake and on the scales the towers shed. They move in loose files along the seams, not packs, each animal a body-length from the next, the file itself sometimes stretching past the limit of sight, a punctuation of the emptiness rather than a filling of it. At a distance they look like a flaw in the pavement, a darker stitching. Up close they are all claw and gape and the same dry sweet smell as a fresh wound in the rind.

There are rules, and they are old.

Nothing flows. If it moves across the surface it moves by being blown, or by walking, or by riding the air the structures pump. The bright charge on the domes is not lightning in the storm sense; it is a surface habit, a blue stitching that walks the tile-edges and grounds itself into the galleries with a tick you can set a watch by. Animals learn the tick. They do not cross a dome at the hour the charge peaks. The plant does not punish them for it. The charge is waste, shed the way a factory sheds heat, and anything that conducts is simply in the way.

Growth does not climb over growth without a function. A tower that would shadow a fan for too much of the day does not persist; the fan’s production drops, the local wind fails, dust stops arriving, and the tower’s own mineral diet thins until it seals its slots and stands as a dead column, ringing in a higher, thinner note, until the scour takes it down to a stump of lacquered rings. This has happened everywhere. The stumps are part of the pavement now, polished, inhabited by the smallest gliders, who prefer the dead note to the living one. The landscape remembers its failures as furniture.

Sound is information. The people—if one were to imagine people, though nothing here has been made by hands—would navigate by ear more honestly than by eye, because the eye is drunk on distance and glare and the ear is exact. A change in the gallery-hum means a conduit has choked and the cleaners have not yet found it. A tower dropping out of the chord means a split, a meal, a danger of falling blade. A sudden wide silence is the rarest and worst sign: a tract where the metabolism has stalled, where the dust is not lifting, where the cool is deepening toward a frost the resins cannot survive. Such tracts do not last. Neighboring fans haul harder. The wind arrives from the living side like a repair crew, loud, carrying dust and seed-scale and the small animals that ride the gust whether they meant to or not. The stalled plates wake under the abrasion. The hum returns, first thin, then industrial, then deafening again, and the silence closes like a scar.

By evening the light does not soften so much as slant, and the slant turns every groove into a lit wire and every tower into a compass needle of shadow miles long. The fans fold, not all at once, but in a slow sequence that runs across the visible world like a decision. The clap stops. The organ-notes drop a register and steady. The galleries do not stop. They are the night shift: the same engines, the same tick of charge now visible as a faint wandering spark along the dome-tiles, the same files of scavengers working the seams by the residual brightness of the high air. It is colder, and the resins tighten, and the pavement ticks as it contracts—small sounds, under the big ones, like a factory cooling and not shutting down.

If you stay, the place does not reveal a hidden gentleness. It reveals duration. The turbulence was never a crisis. The emptiness was never an absence of life. The industry was never a visit from some other order of being. A vast, cool, brilliantly lit engine of plants, dry to the root of its chemistry, sparse in every body and total in its floor, loud enough to make a permanent weather of its own voice, has simply been running in this form for longer than the forms of its animals. The dust on your lips is salt and wax and the bright cold. There is nothing to drink. There is nowhere to go that is not more of the same living rind, more of the same settled violence, more of the same enormous, gleaming, deafening day.
