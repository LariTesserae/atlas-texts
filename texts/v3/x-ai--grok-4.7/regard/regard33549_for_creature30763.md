They would come the way weather comes, and leave even less of a name behind.

Not as a column to rival the stack, and not as a voice in the shift-whistle. A wise power does not correct a place for being young. It does not round the ledges ahead of the rain, or hurry a grave into looking inherited, or dry the lenses so the streets can pretend they were always meant to be streets. Those would be kindnesses of a hasty kind, and this bowl is already teaching the better lesson: that a thing can be useful before it is finished, and that force spent against an edge only keeps the edge sharp.

What they would notice first is the tempo, and that it is already good. Engines that idle instead of dying. Water that stands and then consents to go. People who wipe the belts without cursing the climate, who step around a reflection because it is briefly too true, who repaint an address and leave the sagging line beneath it. A power that is only strong would want to complete them. A power that is wise sees they are not incomplete in the way a broken thing is. They are early. Early is not an emergency.

The being would take longer to find, because it does not keep a shift and it does not gather moss to sell.

It is not the heron, though the heron has studied it. It is not the man with the hoe, though he has walked so near it that the frogs sometimes answer him as if he were a change in the ditch. It lives in the thin water. In the films on the housings. In the second stack that lies down in the drainage cut and makes careful people step aside. When the floor cracked and rose and stopped, something small chose the pause. It has no hunger the town would recognize. It keeps the standing water from forgetting it is also sky. It keeps the moss persuaded that north is a direction and not a conquest. It is why the noise holds instead of building, why the insects patrol, why sleep comes when the engines idle. The residents feel it as condition. They have never needed its name. It is the size of a habit, and older than the survey by exactly the age of the first rain that did not yet know where to run.

A benevolent power, meeting that, would not adopt it and would not improve it. Improvement is how gardens forget they are gardens, and how towns forget they were once a grove with a factory’s voice.

They would sit with it through one ordinary shift. Not beside it, in the way of a visitor on a loading platform, but in the same manner it occupies: distributed, cool, present in the bead on the railing and the note inside the broad note of the mill. The being would know. The being has been the only attention this young the ground has had, and it would feel the larger attention the way a ditch feels a change in grade—no announcement, only an easier path for what was already going.

And then they would do four things, each small enough to be denied later by anyone who prefers their luck ordinary.

They would turn one ledge. Not all of them; sharpness is still the bowl’s honesty. One, at the rim where the children have made a base of flat stone and an out of willow, where a flake the size of a hand is ready to come away under a boot that trusts it. They would let the rain have that flake in the night, cleanly, so that morning finds a dull lip instead of a cut, and the children argue about whether it was always dull. The being would understand this as grammar, not rescue: the rain’s intention, spoken a little early, in a place where a body was about to spend blood on a lesson the stone did not need to teach that way.

They would keep a hand. On the wet belt that has been slapping for three days, the one the early shift wipes and tightens and wipes again, there is a moment coming when courtesy will not be enough and a wrist will go in. They would thicken the slap into a sound the mill-hand already knows how to read—a change in pitch just large enough—so that he stops, and leans, and finds the fray with his eyes instead of with the bones of his hand. Afterward he will say he heard it. He did. They only made the hearing possible a breath sooner. The engines will not become safer in general. Wisdom does not repeal a mill. It refuses one particular waste.

They would let the numbers run, but not off. The woman who paints both addresses will find, one morning, that the lower line has stopped sagging at a height that can still be read. She will think the paint finally took. She will be wrong in a way that does not matter. Both names of the sheds will remain: the one the plan intended, and the one the weather prefers. A wise power takes the side of double naming. It does not choose for the ground before the ground is ready to keep a choice.

And for the being itself, they would do the thing that is not an intervention so much as a recognition. They would let it know it has been seen, and that seeing is not a summons. No enlargement. No shrine in the unfinished lots. No voice telling it to become a story the residents can prefer to the towns they left. Only a steadiness beside its steadiness, lasting as long as one pale column takes to thin into the damp air, and then gone. The being has been faithful to a pause. The kindest message is that the pause was not an oversight, and that it need not end itself in order to be worthy of regard.

They would not stay. Staying would make the heron an omen and the idle engines a liturgy, and the people would begin to perform their patience instead of living it. The dogs would sleep somewhere else. The graves at the rim would start to mean more than a stiff kindness of new soil. The town would ripen into a tale, and tales are heavier than moss, and this place has not agreed to carry that yet.

Before they withdrew they would look once at the whole bowl—the sharp floor, the spaced green, the few dozen households still negotiating occupancy, the children floating chips of pale stone, the provisional tense of every life there—and they would leave it intact, including its unfinishedness. Benevolence is not always a gift placed in the hand. Sometimes it is the decision not to lift a thing out of the time that is making it.

If anyone felt them, it would be afterward, and faintly, and easy to mistake. The man with the hoe, finding a cut that had persuaded itself a few inches toward the course he meant, and not taking credit. A child, palm on the dulled lip, puzzled, then running because the whistle does not care about geology. The mill-hand, flexing a wrist that is still his, saying the belt warned him. The being in the thin water, no larger than before, keeping the reflection just true enough that careful people continue to step around it.

And the power, already elsewhere, would carry nothing away but the knowledge that a young place can be in right relation to itself: loud, green, recently made, learning slowly which edges to keep. They would not hurry that learning. They would not need thanks. The rain would come again by evening, and stand in its lenses, and withdraw, and the town would persist, which was all that had been asked of it, and all they had chosen to defend.
