They would do almost nothing. That is the first and the hardest mercy.

A power that is only kind would already be reaching. It would want to lift the cold, or give the sky a sun that travels, or teach the air to carry sound farther than a body's length so that love, if it is spoken, can be heard across a lane. It would want a wheel, because wheels are gifts in every other country, and a fire that does not cost the fiber, and a marker on the rim for whoever is returned to the count, so that absence has a place to stand. Kindness, untaught, confuses incompleteness with pain. It sees a bowl and thinks of water that runs. It sees a life without event and grieves, as if event were the same thing as meaning.

Wisdom arrives a moment later and stays the hand.

It would stand on the polished lip a long while, which costs it nothing, and learn the town the way the town learns: by where things are, not by what they are about to become. It would notice that the hush is not waiting. It would notice that the crowding has already been solved, and that the solution is the people themselves, their placements, their full basins, their moths in the right number on the right blades. Nothing here is asking to be rescued. The cold is not an injury. The light that does not travel is not a prison. The children learning bundles before balance are not deprived of a childhood; they are inside the only childhood this ground can be honest about.

So the power would not enter.

To enter would be to bring a future in with it. Even a careful foot on the stair changes the polish, and the polish is a record of everyone who already belongs. Even a blessing, if it arrived as weather, would be a rupture. The drops that stop at a certain size would not know what to do with rain. The fish at their stations would meet a ripple and have no law for it. The lanes, which keep their messages by not carrying them farther than they are meant to go, would be violated by a voice that does not stop at the moss. Benevolence that cannot tolerate a boundary is only appetite with a gentle face.

What it would do is narrower than help, and longer.

It would keep the flat country from falling in. Not with a wall the people could see, and not with a sign. The rim is already a decision. The power would be the reason the decision continues: no wheel lowered down by strangers, no road finding the crease in the turf, no drought in the high country sharp enough to send the thirsty looking for a darker line of horsetail. It would turn a surveyor's eye a finger's width away. It would thicken the mist on the one morning a hunter might have read the lip as a path. It would let the horsetails be only horsetails. Protection, here, is the art of remaining uninteresting to the world above.

It would not add a god to the thresholds. The basins are already altars of a kind, and they are full, and fullness is their whole theology. To be worshiped would put a lack in the center of every cell, a lack that could only be fed by address, and address would require the voice to travel. The power is wise enough to refuse that hunger in itself. It does not need the town to know it was spared. Being spared is not the same as being held in someone's story. The town has its own story, and it is told in the left and the right of bundles, and it is sufficient.

If it does anything the people could, in principle, have noticed, it would be this: once, at the edge of custom and not inside it, a crack that had not yet asked would fail to ask. A lintel that might have opened under the next century's quiet weight would simply go on being a lintel. No one would repair it, because nothing would show. The patina would continue without a seam. That is as close to a miracle as wisdom allows in a place where miracles would be disasters. A thing that does not break, in a town that already knows how to mend, is a secret kindness. It leaves no lesson, because a lesson would be an event.

And the being.

The singular one is harder, because the bowl is full of many, and the many fit. Perhaps the being is not among them but is the rule they live inside: continuance standing where event used to be. A wise power would recognize a peer of a sort, smaller and colder and more patient than most powers bother to be, and would not try to become its author. You do not improve a law by loving it. You do not translate it. You stand where your strength can keep other strengths from rewriting it in the language of running water and open roads and days that end.

If the being is instead some one person the eye keeps missing—the one who moves the bundle, or the last to sleep, or the figure the description will not isolate because isolation would be a lie here—then the power would do what the town already does. It would not single them out. Distinction, bestowed from above, is a kind of removal. Their place in the count is their safety. To be known uniquely by a god is to be half-gone from a world that keeps its people by not losing count.

So it would watch, and then it would mostly stop watching, because a gaze that never leaves becomes weather too. It would leave them the white sky they already have, the breath that hangs and is taken back, the platforms full at the rate the platforms allow. It would love them the way stone loves a foot: by being what it is, and by not becoming a road.

Somewhere above the crease in the turf, the country would continue to forget them. That forgetting would be maintained, gently, by something that could have remembered them into ruin and chose not to.

Nothing would be about to happen. The power, if it is truly wise, would count that as the work.
