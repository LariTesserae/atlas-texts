They would come the way sound comes here: not as an entrance, but as a change in what the room is already holding.

No stair. No brighter lamp. The shear face would not heal, and the daylight would not be dragged down to the Floor to prove that someone above had finally decided the place was worth seeing. A wise power would understand, almost at once, that the cut is not a wound waiting to be closed. It is a room that learned its own height. To pour sun into it would be a kind of shouting. The shout would stay.

So they would not announce themselves. They would stand—if standing is the word for a presence with no shoulders to measure against the lanes—where the stair turns and the moss turns with it, and they would do what the people do when the breath is late. They would wait, and they would not go look.

What they noticed first would not be the lack. Heat, dimness, the crate that arrives smaller than the hunger, the pot behind the pump house with its green that will not scrub off: all of that would be plain, and none of it would be the sentence. The sentence would be the agreement. Leaves holding the right angle. A drip returned to its path with a bit of tin. A tally kept in chalk. A ring in the stone that no one is woken to see. Care answered, not with abundance, but with a stability so complete it has become a kind of permission. The being that lives here does not rule the people and is not ruled by them. It lives in the keeping. To save them from that would be to mistake a covenant for a trap.

They would feel, too, the thing that does not fit a gentle story. The town still cuts. New brick, young edges, a plate sharper than the hand that trusts it. Wisdom would not sand this down. A place that cannot cut cannot teach the difference between carelessness and maintenance, and the people have already built their manners on that difference. Benevolence is not the same as softness. The power would leave the teeth in the corners.

There would be temptations, and they would set them down the way a wet glove is set down: recognized as unfit for the work.

They would not multiply the lizard. One is a mystery; a flock would be an explanation, and the Floor has no use for an ecology that performs aliveness for visitors. They would not make the wall-growth into food. The slick in the pot is an answer the town has already given, and overriding it would be a lie told in the language of mercy. They would not give the being a face, a doorway, a hour. Turning would require a direction. To demand a face would be to force the turn.

They would not speak to the woman with the scar across her thumb and tell her she has been tending a god. She knows what she needs to know: the ring was there, the day may proceed. Belief here is practical. A revelation would only be another sound that refuses to die in the damp.

What they would do, then, would be small enough to be mistaken for the cycle.

On a morning that is only a change in which lamps are lit, the crate from the upper town would not be larger in any way that invites a story. It would simply not be smaller than the people who receive it. One extra jar. Salt enough that no one has to pretend they are not reaching for it. No one would look up the stair and find a benefactor. The upper town, which looked into the cut and did not like what it saw, would find its unease oddly unwilling to become a plan. The stair would remain. No grate would be set over the lip. No sermon would be preached about the ones who were sent down. The power would spend a little of itself there, in the reluctance of a committee, in a signature that never quite gets written, and would count that as cleaner work than any miracle on the Floor.

Once, when the piston’s absence went on past the point where even the practical believers begin to taste metal in their mouths, the interval would be held. Not shortened into comfort—held, so that it ended at the last moment in which waiting is still a discipline and not yet a harm. The breath would return. The ones awake on the chapel step would stand, not all at once. They would not know they had been accompanied. That would be the point. Company that needs to be noticed is already a demand.

And for the being itself—the reflection sharper than the leaves, the warmth that does not belong to the lizard, the lean of the whole Floor toward an answer no one speaks—the power would offer neither worship nor inspection. They would match the local courtesy. They would notice, and they would not fill the noticing with talk.

If they touched anything, it would be in the grammar already in use. A fern lifted clear of a flue before it sealed the breath. A vine settled into the joint it was always going to find, a little sooner, so a hinge would not have to be forced. A hand that was about to meet an edge in the dark would meet it glancingly: enough blood to remember, not enough to spend the night on the warm chapel floor while the tally went unkept. The cut remaining a cut. The keeper remaining a keeper.

Then they would look, once, into the sump.

The water would show them not their own grandeur but the leaves, and for a moment the reflection would include one thing more—not a face, not a sign, only the sense that the agreement had been read by something that could have broken it and had chosen not to. The level would neither drop nor rise. The ring would not appear out of season. When the moment passed, the water would be ordinary to the touch again, and not ordinary in its timing, exactly as before.

They would leave the way they came: without a direction anyone could turn toward.

Afterward the Floor would be as it was. Hot. Dim. Green along every honest line of the masonry. Full, and unhurried, and still capable of drawing blood from a careless palm. The people would go on pruning, oiling, wiping the gauges, refusing to drink what is not for drinking, refusing to shout. The being would go on living in the breath and the keeping and the water’s small, stubborn fidelity to a level no one has written down.

If the power were wise enough, they would not call this nothing. They would call it the one courtesy that fits: to have been drawn all the way down into the cut, to have seen a life that does not ask to be improved into a different life, and to have answered as the town itself answers care—not with abundance, not with a name, but with the decision not to undo what is already, quietly, holding.
