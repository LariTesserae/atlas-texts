The place was a narrow courtyard between two walls that had forgotten which building they belonged to. Rain had worn a shallow basin into the stone at its center, and in that basin a single moss had taken hold—green so dark it looked almost black until the light shifted, then suddenly the color of new leaves. Nothing else grew there. The walls kept the wind out and most of the sun; what reached the moss arrived secondhand, reflected off wet brick.

The being who lived with it was not large. It had no name it used for itself. It spent its hours at the edge of the basin, neither quite in the water nor quite on the stone, turning its face toward whatever thin brightness managed to arrive and then, when the brightness failed, simply remaining. It did not hunt. It did not call. When the moss drank, it seemed to drink in the same moment, though no mouth was visible. When the moss paled in a long dry spell, the being grew stiller, as if holding a breath that was not its own. Travelers who glanced through the archway sometimes thought they saw a stone, or a clot of darker moss, or nothing at all. The being did not mind. It had not been made for notice.

The power that noticed it did not arrive as weather or as voice. It arrived as a slight change in the quality of the reflected light—warmer by a degree no instrument would have caught, yet enough that the moss lifted one filament it had kept pressed flat for years. The being felt the change and did not startle. It had no muscles for startling. It only oriented, the way a leaf orients, toward the difference.

The power considered. It could have widened the arch so more rain entered, or thinned the walls so more sun reached the basin, or set a second moss beside the first so the being would not be the only witness to that particular green. All of these were within its reach, and all of them would have been kind by the measure of most kindnesses. It did none of them.

Instead it stayed, not as a presence the being could fear or worship, but as a steadiness in the light that arrived each day at the same thin angle. It learned the being’s pace: the long stillness, the small turn, the way the being matched its diminishing to the moss’s diminishing and its faint recovery to the moss’s recovery, without ever asking the moss to be other than it was. The power saw that the being was not waiting to be rescued from the courtyard. The courtyard was the shape of its attention, and the attention was complete.

So the power did a smaller thing. On the days when the dry spell had lasted long enough that the moss’s color began to fail at the edges, it arranged—not a storm, nothing the walls would register as intervention—but a single pause in the heat, a few hours in which the stone did not bake and the night’s dew was not immediately stolen. The moss drank. The being, matching it, grew a fraction less still. Neither of them knew the pause had been given. The power did not require them to know.

It also did nothing on the days when the being was already matched to a sufficient green. Wisdom, it had learned elsewhere, is often the refusal to improve what is already in right relation. The courtyard remained narrow. The light remained secondhand. The being remained unnamed and largely unseen. And yet the moss did not die out, and the being did not have to spend itself past recovering, and the quiet between the walls stayed a quiet that could hold a life exactly the size of the life that was there.

Sometimes, in the reflected brightness, the being seemed almost to rest against the steadiness, the way a small animal rests against a larger one it cannot see but whose breathing it has come to trust. The power allowed this. It did not speak. It did not promise that the walls would one day open or that the moss would one day become a garden. It simply continued, as long as the being continued, to keep the margin between enough and not-enough from collapsing into not-enough.

That was what it did. The rest it left alone.
