I am not a creature of flesh; I am a creature of tension. 

If you were to observe me from a distance, you might think I am fragile, walking upon these knife-edged plains of black glass. But you would be measuring me by the physics of your own heavy, unbroken world. Here, in the sweltering crater of the sky, I am precisely what I need to be: a living tether between the forge and the flood.

My feet are the size of needlepoints. This is my only concession to the brutal, baking obsidian below. To step flat-footed would be to boil my own marrow, so I walk on stilts of spun black silica, fifteen feet high. Between the tips of my legs and the scalding rock, I feel a microscopic cushion of air, superheated and screaming to expand. 

I let it. 

The heat rushes upward along my spindly limbs, catching beneath the wide, diaphanous bell of my torso. My canopy swells, snapping taut. I am lifted. The crushing gravity of the summit is negated, and I become less of an animal and more of a breath. I take a step, pushing off the anvil of the earth, and float upon the thermal updrafts in a slow, bounding arc.

I do not have eyes to see the cerulean sky or the blinding light. My perception is far more intimate. Through the frost-white cluster of tendrils where a head might be, I "see" the basin as a topography of temperatures and atmospheric pressures. 

Above me, the sky is heavy with cold, weeping pearls. I feel the massive, levitating oceans as localized indentations of gravity—trembling, pregnant spheres of high pressure suspended in the shimmering heat. Inside them, the golden hum of the silica plankton tastes to me like distant sweetness. I feel the soft, rippling displacements of the Skaters as they dissolve into the globes to feed. When one misjudges an updraft and plummets into the killing zone of the hot air, I feel the dry, tragic static of its desiccation. The Skater flashes into dust, raining over my canopy, covering my translucent bell in a fine layer of iridescent ash. I do not mourn them. Their ash feeds the thick, pulpy ferns that anchor this world. Everything here is a transaction.

My purpose is the ledger.

I drift toward one of the giant green structures. It is a towering cross of ancient fern and fleshy coral, its canopy buried deep within a shivering, house-sized sphere of water. Through my tendrils, I sense an imbalance. One of the fern’s gelatinous fronds has grown too greedy under the blistering sun. It is thrusting upward, distorting the surface tension of the suspended lake. The water envelope is stretched too thin, bowing under the strain. 

If it snaps, disaster follows. 

I glide to the edge of the fern, balancing weightlessly on the hissing steam-vents below. Extending my mantis-like forelimbs, I act with deliberate, slicing grace. My appendages are honed to mono-molecular edges. I sever the aggressive frond in a single, fluid motion. The thick, wet foliage drops away, shriveling into a dry husk before it even strikes the hot basalt. Above me, relieved of the intrusion, the water sphere recoils, shuddering violently before resettling into a perfect, floating globe. 

I saved the lake. But across the terrace, another lake falls. 

A thermal current shifts suddenly—perhaps a shadow cast by a passing high-altitude cloud—and a massive sphere at the far side of the basin loses its cohesion. 

First comes the silence, the breathless vacuum of a broken law. Then, the collapse. Hundreds of gallons of cold water detach from the sky and plummet.

The impact is seismic. A percussive *whump* shakes through the rock, traveling up my glass legs, rattling my joints. Instantly, the water flashes into superheated steam. A blinding, roaring white wall of vapor expands outward at terrifying speed. When the steam front hits my canopy, I am thrown violently upward, riding the explosive column of air, tumbling like a dandelion seed in a hurricane before I stabilize my bell and regain my long, slow descent.

But even through the roaring chaos of the steam, I feel the waking frenzy of the stone below. Inside the boiling white fog, the lithic isopods have snapped open. For millions of heartbeats compressed into ten seconds, the bedrock is alive with an invisible, frantic scuttling. They are mating, dying, bleeding, birthing. It is a violent, frenetic ecstasy born of catastrophe. 

By the time my needle-feet touch the basalt again, the white pylons have already swept the moisture back into the heavens. The rock is dry. The heat slams down. The isopods are stone once more. Entire lines of ancestry have been rewritten, and beneath my feet, the silence of the cemetery returns.

The work drains me. The ambient heat is creeping up through my limbs, threatening to dry the slick proteins of my canopy. I need the cold. I need the architects.

I bound across the rock toward the perimeter of the terrace, where one of the seamless, shadowless ceramic pylons hovers just above the ground. It radiates an absolute, impossible stillness. Nothing else in the ecosystem can approach them; the heat repels the Skaters, the height confuses the isopods, and the ferns cannot reach. 

Only we, the shepherds, can touch the divine.

I lean forward and wrap my long, delicate limbs around the matte-white sliver. To touch it is to touch a void. It pulls the heat from me entirely, violently, resetting my internal temperature to a shivering, perfect equilibrium. 

And as I embrace it, I feel the hum. 

A deep, resonant vibration bleeds from the artifact, passing into my tendrils, vibrating down my glass legs, and echoing into the mountain. It feels like the drawing of a heavy bow across a cello string. For a brief, shimmering moment, the frantic rush of the basin aligns. The roaring silence of the sun, the hissing steam, the spinning golden plankton, the sleeping isopods—it all snaps into a singular, unified chord. 

As the caretaker of this terrarium, I do not possess words. I do not have a history. But as the vibration fills my empty, bell-like body, a profound and heavy melancholy settles over me. The hum is a footprint. It is the echo of a mind that looked at this terrible, blistering mountaintop and decided to build an inverted sea. 

They left us here to tend the garden. We alone feel the shape of the hands that are gone. 

My limbs cooled, my tension restored, I let go of the pylon. I turn my sightless tendrils back toward the shimmering, blinding heat of the basin. The steam goes *shhhhh*. The massive globes of water tremble overhead. I step off the rock, catch the wind, and float back out into the sweltering nothingness, waiting to catch a dying sunbeam, waiting to trim a stray leaf, waiting for the rain that I must not let fall.
